I'm one of nine members on the board of a regional transit authority — metro area of about 700k, so not a big city but not tiny either. For two years we've been circling a decision about whether to put a variable-price congestion cordon around the downtown core, and we have to vote in October because the federal grant that would pay for the gantries and cameras lapses if we don't. One of my colleagues, who came to the board from a fintech company, has brought forward a proposal a foundation would partly fund: instead of voting on the cordon directly, we'd open two conditional prediction markets, one paying off on a composite "cordon welfare index" measured two years out if we implement, the other paying off on the same index if we don't, with the unchosen branch settled by called-off bets so nobody has to fund the thing that doesnt happen. Then we'd pass a resolution committing the board to vote yes if the implement-branch price sits above the don't-implement price on a named date. She's framing it as the futarchy idea — the appointed people decide what we value, the betting only decides what will work. I'm sympathetic in principle and I read a fair amount of this stuff, but I think the proposal as drafted has problems nobody on the board is naming out loud, and the member who wants to kill it is doing so for the wrong reason (he thinks it's a stunt timed to a press release, which is... partly fair).

Three things are bugging me and I'd want the memo to actually engage them rather than wave at them. First, our bylaws require every resolution to carry a written statement of findings, and a market price is not a finding — our attorny has already said we can't just staple a price chart to the resolution and call it done. Is there any way to turn a price into reasons that the losing side could answer, or is that pure decoration? Second, the index the consultant drafted is basically aggregate vehicle-hours of delay saved plus net operating revenue, but we adopted an equity policy last year saying impacts on the roughly 9,000 lower-income residents who currently drive into the cordon have to be weighted explicitly, and nobody seems to have noticed that picking the index settles that fight in advance — which is supposed to be the part we vote on, not the part we bet on. Third, and this is the one I can't figure out: our attorney pointed out that if we reserve any discretion to override the market, then what traders are really betting on is our board's politics rather than the cordon's effects, and the price stops meaning the thing the whole proposal needs it to mean; but if we commit fully we've handed a decision affecting a quarter million daily trips to a market with maybe a few hundred participants, two of whom — a downtown business association and a hospital system with a big campus inside the cordon — have obvious reasons to trade it rather than forecast it. On top of that, only one branch of the conditional ever gets realized, so we'd never learn whether the comparison was any good, and the foundation's money is contingent on us adopting an innovative decision procedure, which means the people funding the pilot want us to adopt it whatever the market says.

Could you draft the memo I'd circulate to the board about two weeks out? It needs to land somewhere — adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions — and give a concrete bottom line on the agenda item, not a survey of considerations. If you land on conditions, spell out what they'd actually be: who sets the index and by what vote, what kind of commitment we can and can't credibly make, what we do about the interested traders, and what the resolution text says to satisfy the findings requirement. I'd also like a short set of criteria the board could reuse the next time somebody proposes outsourcing a decision to a market, because this won't be the last time. And please give the strongest honest case for the side you're arguing against: the point that our current process just aggregates whoever shouts loudest at public comment, with nobody ever on the hook for being wrong, is genuinely strong, and I don't want a memo that pretends otherwise.
