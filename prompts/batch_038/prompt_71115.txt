I am working through a well-reviewed popular introduction to the philosophy of science, and its chapter on causation contains the following passage, which I have transcribed for you:

"Lewis and Woodward are both counterfactual theorists, so it is easy to mistake their disagreement for a family quarrel. Lewis asked what would have happened had the cause not occurred; Woodward asks the same question but insists that the answer be read as a claim about what an intervention would produce, which is why interventionism is best read as Lewis's theory updated for the practice of statistics. On Woodward's account, X is a cause of Y just in case some possible intervention on X would change the value of Y. Two difficulties follow at once, and they are fatal. The first is that the account is circular: an intervention is itself a causal process, so the analysis defines causation in terms of causation; and if one tries to escape by reading interventions simply as the things experimenters do, causation turns out to be relative to our manipulations, which is another way of saying subjective. Woodward's own reply — that whether X causes Y is relative to the set of variables one has chosen — concedes precisely this point, since it makes causal claims hostage to a conceptual scheme. The second difficulty is that Lewis's programme had already collapsed. He admitted that his analysis cannot handle cases in which the effect occurs anyway, whether because a second cause was waiting in the wings or because we are asking about an effect rather than a cause, and his later refinements only restated the original failure in a more complicated notation. The upshot is that the whole dispute concerns how to interpret statistical evidence, and not what causation is."
