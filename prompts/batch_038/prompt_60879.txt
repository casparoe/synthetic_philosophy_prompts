I run the post-op rehab side of a pediatric cataract program — we do maybe twenty sight-restoration cases a year, mostly kids between 5 and 15 who've had dense bilateral congenital cataracts, plus a handful of adults with corneal scarring. After surgery the team does twice-weekly 45-minute sessions for about six weeks: tracking, reaching, faces, mobility, and a set of "is he actually seeing it or guessing" tasks. The problem is we have no framework for the question everybody keeps asking us — parents, the school for the blind that referred the kid, a local reporter, and now our funder, who wants one binary line in the annual report reading cross-modal transfer: yes/no. My clinical director keeps saying this is just Molyneux's problem with a patient attached, and a colleague insists the whole thing was settled empirically something like fifteen years ago by the sight-restoration studies and that I should just cite them and stop agonizing. I don't want to cite anything — half the team couldn't read a journal article if they had the time, and I'm explicitly not asking you for a reading list or references. What I want is a procedure.

So: write me a decision procedure my rehab team can run at the bedside with no philosophy and no stats training, on a specific kid in week two post-op. Ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, what to do when a step comes back inconclusive, and explicit stop points where the procedure says you may not report an answer to the Molyneux question for this patient — plus the exact sentence to put in the chart and in the funder report at those stop points instead. It has to survive the simplification without lying to us, so tell me which distinctions from the philosophical version genuinely have to be preserved: my own hunch is that it turns on separating detecting that two things look different from seeing that this one is the cube from being able to say the word cube out loud, and on whether you're allowed to coach the patient with language at all — and then tell me which distinctions I can safely drop, because a nurse with three patients waiting will not maintain a subtle one. Flag every step that is really a judgment call wearing a rule's clothes, especially the "was that a report of what he saw or a smart guess from context" call, since I think that one decides everything and cannot be made mechanical. One constraint: our ethics committee is fine with tasks that double as rehab, but anything purely aimed at the research question needs a seperate consent form and a session slot we don't currently have, so the procedure should mark which steps are which.

Then run it on three of our cases. (1) A 7-year-old, dense bilateral congenital cataract, operated at 7, acuity around 20/400 with nystagmus, who names a cube and a sphere instantly by touch; in week two we set the two objects 40 cm in front of him, put a cube in his hand out of his view, and ask him to point at the one that feels like what he's holding — six of eight correct, and he says he was "looking for the same one." Does the procedure let me report a yes? (2) A 45-year-old who lost his sight at eighteen months after a chemical burn, gets a corneal graft, and within a few days matches seen shapes to felt ones and tells us things look the way they feel. He's our best success story and the funder adores him, so please tell me straight if the procedure says he isn't a Molyneux case at all and can't serve as evidence for anything. (3) A 10-year-old whose grandmother has been drilling shape names with him at home for two weeks before we ever got to test, so there's no clean baseline to work off — what does the procedure do when the input is contaminated and you can't un-know it? Finally, tell me what the procedure can't do: in particular whether anything in it can distinguish "he sees the shape but has no visual concept for it" from "he doesn't see the shape at all," and how I'm supposed to answer the reporter's version of the question (so, he was born blind and now he can see?) without either overselling it or sounding like I'm hiding something from the families.
