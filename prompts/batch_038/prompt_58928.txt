Me and a coworker have been going in circles for like three weeks and I want an outside number. We both work adjacent to AI safety, we've both been reading the rationalist blogosphere since 2008, and we've ended up very far apart on a claim I'll try to state precisely. My credence is 0.7, hers is 0.2.

The claim: conditional on a large amount of artificial sentience existing a century or more from now, and holding the resources spent per mind roughly fixed, the majority of that sentience will be suffering-heavy rather than flourishing-heavy.

The intuition driving my number is something I've started calling phenomenological convergence, which is not the same thing as the instrumental convergence stuff in the Sequences. It's this: there seem to be vastly more cheap ways to build a mind that suffers than to build one that flourishes. Suffering looks like an attractor — you get it from pain systems, from frustrated goals, from social evaluation getting damaged, from boredom, from fear, from a hundred different substrates, and a lot of those states are also *useful* so selection keeps them around. Rich flourishing looks like a needle-thread — agency, security, meaning, good social bonds, engagement matched to challenge, all dialed in at once, and if any one of them is off you get something much worse. So if the far future is populated by things that weren't deliberately designed to be happy, the default should be bad.

Her counter is basically that any sufficiently advanced civilisation can thread any needle it wants, and that suffering is a legacy kludge from evolution that gets engineered out because it's inefficient and because nobody wants to keep it around, so the cheapness intuition is a fact about *us*, not about minds in general.

I want your credence on the claim plus the decomposition, not a survey. Specifically:

1. How would you sharpen the claim so it actually has a truth value? "resources held fixed" is doing a lot of work and I suspect there's a real measure problem there — per unit of compute, per unit of energy, per mind, per subjective hour? Tell me whether you think there's a version of this that isn't just unfalsifiable mush.
2. Give me your probability that there is a genuine functional or computational asymmetry in the space of possible minds — that suffering-flavored states are cheap and widely realizable and flourishing-flavored states are expensive and narrow.
3. Give me your probability that this asymmetry survives the selection argument: that whatever actually builds or inherits the future is indifferent enough about experience that the asymmetry shows up in the outcome, rather than either being optimized away or deliberately curated. This is where her objection bites, I think.
4. Give me your probability that the *measure* of sentient minds is what morally matters here rather than peak intensity or headcount-of-distinct-persons or whatever — i.e. that "the majority of sentience" is even the right scoreboard.
5. How does the moral weighting of suffering vs flourishing interact with 2 and 3? Do you need something close to lexical priority for the claim to have practical bite, or does a moderate ratio do it?
6. Combine into a number and show the arithmetic, not just a vibe.

Then tell me which term you're least confident about, what kind of consideration or evidence would move that term the most, and — the part I actually care about — whether the 0.7 person and the 0.2 person most likely disagree about the asymmetry itself or about whether it survives selection. My suspicion is the latter but I can't get her to say which.

No citations needed, I'm not writing this up, I just want to see where the load-bearing parts are.
