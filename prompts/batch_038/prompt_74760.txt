I am trying to settle what I think about infinite processes completed in a finite time, and I want to do it by going through my verdicts on particular cases rather than by adopting a theory.

Zeno's runner, and Achilles after the tortoise: no problem whatsoever, I feel no tug at all. Thomson's lamp, switched on after half a minute, off a quarter of a minute later, on an eighth later, and so on: at the minute there is no fact about whether it is on — not that I cannot tell, but that there is nothing to tell — and I also think no such lamp could have been built. The urn: at stage n, which takes half the time of the stage before, I put in the balls numbered 10n−9 to 10n and take out ball n; at the end the urn is empty, and I am confident of that, even though the number of balls in it rises at every finite stage. Change the rule so that I take out ball 10n instead, and I am equally confident that infinitely many balls are left, so the answer turns on which balls go and not on the arithmetic. A machine that prints one digit of π per stage and shows me the millionth digit at the end: possible, if deeply unsettling. But the wheel train — an infinite row of wheels, each half the diameter of the last and touching it, so that each turns twice as fast as the one driving it, the first being turned for one second — described backwards, so that the row is already turning with no first wheel to start it: impossible, not merely unsettled. Likewise a squad of killers, the nth of them timed to strike at noon plus 1/2^n minutes if the victim is still alive: nobody strikes, and yet he is dead by a minute past, and I judge that impossible too.

My tentative principle is that the state at the end is whatever is fixed by the fates of the parts, provided each part's fate is settled at some finite stage, and that where no part's fate is settled there is no fact at all. It seems to handle the lamp against the urn, but nothing in it makes the wheel train or the squad impossible rather than indeterminate, and I cannot tell whether to add a clause or to give up those two verdicts. Test the principle against every case above, repair it or replace it, and say what the result delivers where I have no verdict of my own.

Answer with exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each and nothing else — no preamble and no closing remarks: (1) the principle you settle on, stated as precisely as that length allows; (2) what it makes of the lamp and the urn, which look structurally alike but which I judge differently; (3) whether any clause of it can render the wheel train and the squad impossible rather than unsettled, and if not, what has to be added; (4) its verdict on a case of this kind that I have not listed, chosen because you think it is diagnostic; (5) which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon.
