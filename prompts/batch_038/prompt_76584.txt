Last night a friend and I argued until nearly two in the morning about moral testimony, and he finished by telling me that what I have is not a position but a heap of intuitions that collapses the moment you stack them. I run a reading group next week and I had planned to present this material as my own settled view, so before I do, I would like a sober audit. I genuinely cannot tell whether he was right or merely scoring points, and I am not going to tell you which commitment I am most attached to, because I suspect I do not actually know.

Here is the package, stated as carefully as I can manage. (1) I am a pessimist about moral testimony in the epistemic sense: however reliable the testifier, believing that some act is wrong purely because a trustworthy person said so does not amount to moral knowledge — although I cheerfully grant that testimony delivers knowledge in history, in medicine, and even in mathematics, so the peculiarity I am positing is specifically moral rather than a general feature of testimony. (2) What is missing, on my view, is understanding: I take moral understanding to consist in grasping how moral facts depend on non-moral ones — seeing why a given act is cruel *because of* these features — and I hold that it is understanding, not mere true belief or even knowledge, that makes someone a competent moral agent and a proper subject of moral praise. (3) The badness of relying on another's moral word is not merely instrumental for me. It is not only that the testifier might be mistaken; it is that a relationship in which one party takes the other's word on what matters most is second-best, lacking the connection to the value that makes the relationship worth having. I want to insist this is a non-derivative objection, not a hedged bet on reliability. (4) And yet I think that in an emergency you may permissibly act on a trusted other's moral advice. My case: I am the physician on duty, one ventilator remains, and a colleague I trust implicitly tells me that allocating it to patient A rather than patient B is permissible; I have neither the time nor the clinical information to work the matter through myself, so I follow her advice. I do not think I have done anything wrong, and I think a colleague who froze while trying to reconstruct the moral reasoning from first principles would have done worse. (5) I also think culpable ignorance is real: someone who could have informed himself and did not is blameworthy for a wrong act done out of that ignorance, even when the ignorance was produced by testimony he had no particular reason to distrust. (6) Finally, I think parents properly instil moral beliefs in small children by testimony alone — a child who believes hitting is wrong because his mother says so is exactly where a child ought to be — and that a parent who declined to say anything until the child could grasp the underlying reasons would be doing something seriously wrong.

The argument turned mainly on (3) and (4), but my friend also made a more general charge: that (1) is doing secret normative work that I have mislabelled as epistemology, and that once you see that, several of my other commitments quietly change sides. Please go through the set and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do — I am fairly confident that not all of them are in trouble, and an audit that flags everything is useless to me. For each genuine tension, state how severe it is, identify precisely what is incompatible, and give the cheapest available revision together with an honest account of what that revision costs me, since I suspect some of them are not cheap at all. Pay particular attention to whatever criterion I would need in order to treat the colleague in (4) as blameless while still holding the line in (5), and tell me whether adopting that criterion is consistent with my insistence in (3). Then close by naming the single revision that would clean up the most damage overall, and say whether any version of (3) survives it.
