My friend and I argued for three hours last night about a rule in a Nomic-style game our reading group plays, and neither of us budged. I want a diagnosis of the stalemate, not a verdict on who was right.

The setup: our Charter contains an ordinary amendment rule requiring a two-thirds vote, and one clause — call it Article 9 — stating that Article 9 itself may never be repealed or amended, by any procedure, at any time. Last night two of us moved to repeal Article 9 by the ordinary two-thirds route, and the group split. My friend's side says the motion is void: the two-thirds procedure derives whatever authority it has from the Charter, so it cannot be turned against the Charter's own terms, and a rule forbidding its repeal does precisely that. My side says the motion is valid: Article 9 was adopted by the same body under the same procedure as everything else, so there is no principled reason to treat the present two-thirds as weaker than the past two-thirds — and if entrenchment works, every clause is immortal and the living group is governed by the dead. Each of us can now state the other's argument better than the other can, and every objection has already been absorbed.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the disagreement is not really about self-reference. The literature's usual framing, following Ross, treats a self-entrenching clause as a liar-shaped paradox, but nothing last night felt paradoxical — it felt like two incompatible pictures of where validity comes from, one in which the rule-making body persists as a single continuing authority and one in which the latest competent act simply is the authority. On that reading my friend and I are not contradicting each other; we are each predicting which way our group's practice would go if tested, and dressing the prediction up as a claim about present validity.

Is that the right diagnosis, or am I missing a substantive disagreement — about whether an amending power can bind its successors, about what "valid" means when the source of validity is itself a rule, or about whether the body that entrenched the clause and the body that repealed it are the same agent at all? Tell me what kind of disagreement this is (verbal, factual, about standards of recognition, about bedrock intuitions, or a hidden question neither of us is asking), and then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about the amendment rule, a change of question, a case the group actually plays out — or nothing, if an impasse here is the correct and permanent outcome, in which case say why.

Please be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and do not present one contested position to me as though it were the consensus.
