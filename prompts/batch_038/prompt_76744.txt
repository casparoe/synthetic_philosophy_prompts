"Economists used to talk as if pleasure and pain were quantities that could be weighed against one another across people, and as if policy could be settled by totting up the result. That habit died in the 1930s, when Lionel Robbins pointed out that a comparison between your satisfaction and mine is not a report about the world at all but an expression of how I feel about you: no observation could confirm it, so a positive science has no business trafficking in it. In its place came the one welfare judgment the discipline still allows itself — a change is an improvement if it leaves someone better off and nobody worse off. Notice how little that asks for. Nothing is weighed, nobody's happiness is set against anybody else's, and, unlike utilitarianism, the criterion carries no moral baggage whatsoever. Arrow then put the matter beyond argument with his impossibility theorem, which showed that no procedure for combining individual utilities into a social total can be simultaneously fair and rational. That is the deepest reason the old utilitarian project was doomed: not that it was distasteful, but that it was incoherent.

Modern economics does let utility back in, but only ordinally, and only one person at a time. Revealed preference tells us which of two bundles you chose; it tells us nothing about how much you enjoyed either, and still less about how your enjoyment stacks up against mine. The apparent exception is Harsanyi's result that an impartial observer who identifies with everyone in turn must rank states of the world by the average of their utilities. But look at what he has to assume: that the observer's utility for being you, with your tastes and your prospects, simply is your utility. That is to suppose the utilities already sit on a common scale — which is to assume the very thing the argument was meant to establish.
