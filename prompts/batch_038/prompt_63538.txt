I am drafting the conceptual foundations chapter of a master's thesis in political theory on the ethics of public office, and I have hit a snag I cannot reason my way out of. Working through the dirty-hands literature, I keep encountering the same deliberately paradoxical formulation: the officeholder who authorizes torture to locate a bomb, or who lies to the electorate to keep a negotiation alive, is said both to have done wrong and to have done what he ought to have done. Walzer's version of the claim is that the politician does wrong in order to do right, and that the justification does not wash the wrongness out of the act; on my reading he also holds that an officeholder who reports feeling no residue in such a case has missed something rather than achieved moral clarity. My supervisor's comment on the draft was that whether this is a genuine paradox or a confusion depends entirely on unstated assumptions about how moral requirements combine, and that I should get clear on the logic before I get clear on the politics. That is what I need help with.

Could you explain the conceptual issue by laying out what has to be true for both verdicts — 'the act was wrong' and 'the act was justified, all things considered' — to be assertible without contradiction? I would like the explanation organized around the alternatives rather than around authors. One line of thought is that the act really is right all-in, so nothing survives the justification except the bad outcome it was necessary to produce, and the air of paradox is an artifact of confusing the balance of reasons with the moral quality of the act. A second line holds that moral requirements are irreducibly plural and do not aggregate into a single overall verdict, so the officeholder can simultaneously be under an obligation not to torture and under an obligation to obtain the information, with no further fact about which obligation wins. A third holds that an overridden requirement is not thereby cancelled: the pro tanto duty continues to press even after it has been outweighed, and that residual pressure is what the dirt consists in. What I especially want to understand is the role of what is sometimes called the agglomeration principle — the principle that if you ought to do A and you ought to do B, then you ought to do both A and B. Why does accepting agglomeration, together with something like 'ought implies can', seem to force you onto the first line? And what is supposed to go wrong if you reject it: that the agent would then be under literally contradictory obligations, that he would be rationally criticizable no matter what he does, that deliberation loses its point because there is no single thing to work out, or something subtler about the nature of practical reasoning? Please be concrete about where the pressure comes from rather than simply naming the positions.

The second thing I need clarified concerns the shift from acts to agents, which I suspect is doing quiet work throughout the literature. Some writers concede that the justification defeats wrongdoing at the level of the act but insist that a residue remains at the level of the agent, and they cash that residue out as agent-regret rather than guilt — the attitude Williams illustrates with the driver who runs over a child through no fault of his own and who, though he is not to blame, cannot adopt the standpoint of a bystander toward his own causal history. Please explain what distinguishes guilt from agent-regret conceptually (what each presupposes about wrongdoing, about causal involvement, and about who is entitled to feel it, including whether a third party can appropriately feel it), and then work through what follows for the dirty-hands case: if the correct residue is agent-regret, can the officeholder still be liable to blame, censure, or punishment, and does the answer change if the residue is understood as guilt? A worked example of your own devising — a mayor, a central banker, an intelligence chief, whatever makes the machinery visible — would help considerably. I am after the structure of the reasoning rather than a survey, and I would rather you flag the points at which reasonable theorists split than present any single line as settled. No references or further reading are needed; the concepts are what matter here.
