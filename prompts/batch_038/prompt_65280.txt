I TA a second-year seminar called "Who Makes History?" — twelve students, history majors mostly, one or two philosophy people, none of them specialists in the period. For the Weimar week I want to run a sorting exercise rather than a discussion that collapses into "structure versus great men, go." So: write me a ten-question diagnostic quiz built on the counterfactual of whether there'd have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler, where the point is to sort students by what they actually say about cases, not by which side they announce they're on. No item is allowed to just ask "do you think individuals matter?" — every question has to be a concrete counterfactual or a concrete claim about one, two or three answers to pick from, phrased so a student can answer without knowing where the choice leads. You can hand me the scenarios if you like, but a couple I'd want in there: he never recovers from the gas attack that blinded him in the last weeks of the war; or the 1923 putsch gets him a capital sentence or a long stretch instead of the comfortable months in Landsberg, where he had the time and a scribe to write his book. And I want at least one item where an available answer is that things get worse rather than better without him — martyrdom is a real option and my students never reach for it.

Things I want the quiz to pull apart, because in my experience they blur:

- whether the regime type survives his absence — authoritarian, revanchist, anti-parliamentary — versus whether the specific programme does, industrialised racial genocide and a war of annihilation in the east; plenty of them will say yes to one and no to the other and never notice they've done it;
- whether they treat him as a necessary condition, a sufficient one, an accelerator, or a selector who picked one outcome out of a set the structures had allready made available — these feel like the same claim to a twenty-year-old and aren't;
- whether the counterfactual is even well-posed, or whether "Nazi Germany" names something that can't survive the substitution, in which case the honest answer is that the question has no truth value; I want that leaf to exist even if nobody lands on it;
- and whether their answers are stable across cases or whether they're structuralists about everything except the one they feel worst about. So put in at least one parallel case — Stalin, the Meiji oligarchs, Toussaint, whatever you think holds up — scored so that a student who is a structuralist there and a great-man person about Hitler gets caught by it rather than flattered.

Also give me at least one item where every answer option is something a structuralist could sincerely say, and one where every option is something a Carlylean could sincerely say, so the instrument isn't just measuring which pole you gravitate to.

Then the scoring key. Name the leaves where they have names — the Carlylean end, the hard structuralist end, the "structures set the stage and individuals write the script" compromise, the accelerator view, the ill-posed-question view, anything else you think belongs — and mark the rest "unnamed" with a line on whether it's unoccupied because it's incoherent, because nobody wants to hold it, or because nobody's thought of it yet. Flag any two leaves your ten questions can't actually seperate, and say so plainly instead of papering over it. And if a leaf can only be reached by an inconsistent set of answers, say that too — that's a finding about the student, not a defect in the instrument.

Format: this is going to the convenor as an email and she'll typeset the handout herself, so no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered sub-lists anywhere in the reply — continuous prose only, the way you'd write a letter. One paragraph per question with its options written out as ordinary sentences, then the key as a run of paragraphs, one per leaf, and whatever you need to say about the unoccupied and the inseparable cases at the end. It'll be long. That's fine.
