I am putting together a case study for a graduate seminar that sits awkwardly between political theory and history — its nominal subject is the invention of consumer activism — and I want to run one eighteenth-century episode through Adam Smith's moral psychology in a disciplined way rather than the loose way it is usually done. The episode is the British abstention from West Indian sugar and rum that began in 1791. The facts I propose to work with are these, and if you think I have got any of them wrong please say so before proceeding. Wilberforce's bill to abolish the slave trade was defeated in the Commons in the spring of 1791. Within months a short pamphlet by William Fox was in circulation arguing that the consumer is not a bystander to the crime but a participant in it, and pressing readers to do the arithmetic of that participation: if a given quantity of sugar requires the labour of a given number of enslaved people, then a household consuming its ordinary share bears a corresponding fraction of the guilt, and ought to be able to say what portion of a person's suffering its own teatable is answerable for. The pamphlet was reprinted again and again within its first year and reached far more readers than any speech in Parliament did. Abstainers switched to East India sugar, which shopkeepers advertised as produced by free labour; women, who had neither votes nor seats, were among the most active organisers; and the West India interest replied in print. The following year brought the enormous petition campaign and a Commons resolution for gradual abolition. Smith himself died in July 1790, a year before any of this, and never commented on it. Since contemporary estimates of how many households actually abstained vary a good deal, I would rather the analysis did not depend on a figure; treat it as settled only that the movement was large enough to be a matter of serious alarm to the planters and their agents.

What I want first is the mapping, done properly, and only then the verdict. Take the load-bearing pieces of Smith's account: sympathy as an act of imagination by which we place ourselves in another's situation rather than as a contagion of feeling; the decline of concern with distance, as in the passage where a humane European learns that an earthquake has swallowed up the whole vast population of China, moralises briefly on the precariousness of human life, and then dines, sleeps and goes about his business as undisturbed as if nothing had happened, whereas the loss of his own little finger tomorrow would cost him his night's rest; the impartial spectator as an internalised audience built up out of actual spectators and then corrected for their partiality; and the distinction between the propriety of an action and its usefulness. Then say, for the boycott, who the spectator is and for whom he is standing in, and what precisely is the sentiment being approved — the abstention itself, the pamphlet's calculation, or the motive of the person who abstains. What does the theory predict about whether such a movement could get started at all, given that Smith takes our sympathies to be calibrated to nearness and to what we can vividly picture? And what does it explain better than the rival readings I keep hearing from colleagues: that this was utility-maximising behaviour dressed in piety, that it was expressive politics with no genuinely moral content, or that it was Quaker and Evangelical conscience and had nothing whatever to do with the philosophical theory of the moral sentiments.

Then the harder half, which matters more to me. Three features strike me as places where the theory either breaks or has to be stretched, and I want each of them tested rather than waved at. First, abstention is invisible: no one can observe that you did not buy sugar, and Smith's spectator is trained and corrected by an audience that sees what you do, so the movement seems to have had to manufacture its own visibility out of pledges, pamphlets, shop signs and teatable talk. Does that show spectators doing something Smith's account cannot accommodate, or only the same thing in an unfamiliar medium? Second, the enslaved could never be actual spectators of a British consumer's conduct and could neither confirm nor correct his judgment, yet the abstainers behaved as though there were a spectator standing where they stood. Is that a resource latent in Smith, who does allow the imagined spectator to be corrected beyond the company actually around one, or is it an import from elsewhere — a religious or rights-based notion of standing that his theory never grounds? Third, the pamphlet's argument is a calculation of causal shares, and Smith is uneasy about approving of actions on account of their consequences; so when we approve of the abstainer, are we approving of the propriety of the motive or of the utility of the act, and can his framework keep the two apart in a case like this at all? It is also worth addressing that Smith's own case against slavery was largely economic — that work done by slaves ends up costing more than any other, since a person who can acquire no property has no interest in acquiring anything beyond his subsistence — which is not remotely the argument that moved anyone in 1791. Where Smith is simply silent, I want you to say so plainly rather than recruit him; and if you think the exercise fails at the outset, that his moral psychology is the wrong instrument for this case and some other would do better, say that early and say which. I am not after a bibliography or a reading list, and I am not asking you to adjudicate the numbers. Please write in continuous prose, in the manner of a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables — and in British English.
