I'm two years into a PhD in political philosophy and the third chapter is meant to be about the legitimacy of choice architecture, but I've hit a wall, because the debate looks like it's been going round in circles since about 2008 and nobody has moved an inch.

Here's roughly how I'd set the two sides out. Critics say nudges are objectionably manipulative, or at any rate that its a threat to autonomy: a nudge works precisely by exploiting biases and heuristics instead of engaging the person's reasoning, so a well-designed default or a graphic warning gets you to do something you never endorse, and the problem is as much about self-government as about individual liberty — a population that's steered isn't a population that's ruling itself. Defenders' standard moves: choice architecture is unavoidable, there's no neutral baseline to measure against, so the real question isn't whether to nudge but which ones; the right standard is the person's own considered judgement, so a nudge that helps people do what they'd say they want on reflection isn't doing anything morally objectionable; and there's a transparency requirement, that a legitimate nudge has to be able to survive being announced openly and argued with. Then a third camp who think all of this is a distraction and that we should be building capacities instead — boostings, basically — so people aren't reliant on the architecture at all.

My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the two sides aren't really disagreeing about manipulation or autonomy, they're disagreeing about what the null case is. Defenders take 'no intervention' to mean 'whatever the default architecture already happens to be', critics take it to mean 'no attempt to shape anybody's choices', and the arguments keep going past each other because those two baselines never actually meet. If that's right then the dispute is partly verbal, or rather it's substantive but mislabelled: the live disagreement is about the baseline, not about the psychology.

I'm not confident that's right, though, and I suspect some of it is a hidden empirical mess — namely whether disclosure kills the effect. My reading of the disclosure evidence is that it seems to come out differently depending on the nudge: defaults more or less shrug off being announced, graphic warnings and framing effects look a lot more fragile. If so, the transparency requirement isn't a conceptual fix at all but an empirical bet that varies case by case, and both sides have been arguing as though one answer covers everything.

So: diagnose this. Is it a verbal disagreement about the words 'manipulation' and 'autonomy', a substantive disagreement about baselines, a hidden empirical question, three different questions going under one heading — legitimacy, psychological mechanism, democratic authorship — or is it just genuinely hard and I should stop expecting a resolution? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says rather than giving me the survey, I've read the survey. And tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that the chapter should end in a stalemate and why.
