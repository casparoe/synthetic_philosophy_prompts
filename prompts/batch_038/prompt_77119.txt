I convene a small reading group on population ethics — six of us, mostly graduate students plus two people with no philosophy training at all — and next Thursday we close the semester with a session on the mere addition paradox. Rather than summarise, I would like to end the meeting by reading a poem aloud, and I am hoping you can write it for me. The form I have in mind is a sestina: thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six lines with the end-words rotating in the traditional permutation (if it helps, the second stanza takes the first stanza's end-words in the order 6-1-5-2-4-3), and a three-line envoi that gathers all six up. I want the form to be doing argumentative work rather than decorating a paraphrase. A sestina's rotation is very nearly the shape of the puzzle itself: the same handful of ingredients return in a new order each time, nothing genuinely new is introduced, every individual turn is innocuous, and yet after six of them you are standing somewhere you never agreed to go — which is how a sequence of additions that no one in the room would call a wrong ends in a world of very many lives each barely worth living. The envoi should be where that destination finally becomes audible, and where the six words that have been trading places all term arrive in the same breath at once.

A few constraints on content. Choose six end-words of your own, but keep them plain and monosyllabic where possible — the sort of words that already carry the argument without sounding technical — and please do not let the words *utility*, *total view*, *person-affecting*, or *welfare* appear anywhere in the poem, and do not name Parfit or use the phrase everyone reaches for to describe the bad outcome at the far end. Render the whole thing in concrete images instead: a valley, a town that keeps building, a ledger, a queue at a well, a table with more chairs brought to it, whatever you prefer, so long as the two non-philosophers in my group can see what they are looking at while a specialist can read the structure of the argument off the stanzas. Specifically, a philosopher in the room should be able to tell that the first stanza performs the step of adding lives worth living to a world that is already good, and that no later stanza performs a step anyone present would judge wrong. I would also like one of my group's own obsessions built in, because it is what our discussion kept snagging on: nobody in our version of the case chooses. The additions happen because people go on having children and nobody ever vetoes anything, so there is no decision in which to locate the wrongness — which is why the obvious response, that we should simply choose otherwise, does not touch the version we care about. At least one stanza should make the absence of a chooser felt, and I would rather the poem did not supply one at the end either. My own private name for this, which the poem is welcome to echo in imagery but should not state, is the escalator: every step is level, there is no riser anywhere, and yet you end up on another floor. Leave the tension open. There should be no stanza in which the poem tells us what to think, and the last should be at least as unsettled as the first; where our discussion ended is precisely that we could not find a step to refuse.

Since this will be read aloud to six people in a seminar room, it needs to work in the ear: I do not care about metre, but I do care that the lines are roughly even in weight, that no line ends on a weak preposition just to satisfy the rotation, and that the returning end-words feel like a refrain gaining menace rather than like a puzzle being solved correctly. Give it a title, but not one containing the word *sestina*. Please send the poem and nothing else — and if you feel you must explain a choice you made, put a short paragraph of continuous prose after it, in the manner of an email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italicised emphasis, no tables, and no suggestions for further reading or sources, which I already have.
