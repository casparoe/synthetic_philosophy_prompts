I'm chairing an adversarial collaboration between two historians who've spend a year tearing into each other, and I want to make them produce the document they should have written in month two.

Side A is a Braudelian: geography, climate, demography, the slow stuff; events are foam riding on a deep current and individuals are basically weather vanes. Side B is an events-and-agents historian: structures are just names we give to the accumulated results of what people actually did, and any explanation that can't be cashed out in choices and contingencies is decoration.

Their running case is the aftermath of the Black Death. In England and much of western Europe, serfdom and labour services crumble over the next century and a half; east of the Elbe, the same labour scarcity is followed by lords *tightening* the bonds instead. A insists the divergence is a slow-variables story: town density, peasants' exit options, how much political muscle the nobility had, land-to-labour ratios. B insists the identical structural setup got pushed in opposite directions by statutes, revolts, and particular lords and kings — which for B proves the structural story can be made to fit any outcome after the fact.

Two reframings of mine that I want pressure-tested and written *into* the document rather than argued around:

- A's real claim, as I'd put it, is that structures don't cause so much as license: they fix the space of outcomes that are even available, and events pick inside it. Would A sign that? Would B? If B signs it, what's left of the fight?
- Part of the dispute may just be about which decomposition you use — how much of the variance in outcomes across regions and centuries is attributable to persistent variables versus fast ones. If both accept that as the shape of the question, say so plainly, then say what's left over that a variance decomposition can't settle.

What I want back: a numbered list of claims both can sign, where nothing is vacuous — every item should be something a third party could plausibly have denied. Then a disagreements table giving each remaining disagreement in exact wording, phrased so each side would accept the statement of its own position. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling it would actually move somebody. For each crux, give the case, argument, or kind of evidence both accept as bearing on it, plus each side's prediction, recorded in advance. My hunch is that two of them are (i) whether persistence is a mark of causal depth or just of slowness, and (ii) whether a counterfactual that deletes an entire geography or an entire pandemic is a legitimate contrast at all, given no one could ever have run it. Replace or add as you see fit.

Close by saying which parts of this dispute are verbal and why, and by naming one claim each side is committed to that it would refuse to sign — the thing the other camp has been accusing them of all year. No sources or reading list, just the drafting.
