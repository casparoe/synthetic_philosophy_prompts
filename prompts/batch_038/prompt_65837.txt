I facilitate a philosophy discussion group that meets inside a state correctional facility, and next month we begin a six-week unit on free will and moral responsibility. One of the regulars — a man in his late twenties, several years into a sentence, who has completed two court-ordered treatment programs as a condition of parole eligibility — proposed that we screen Kubrick's *A Clockwork Orange*, and the group agreed. He came with an interpretation attached: the film, he said, proves that a man who cannot choose to do wrong is not thereby good, and that the state, in trying to make him harmless, had made him into a thing rather than a person. That is very nearly a statement of the principle of alternative possibilities, and it is precisely the principle Frankfurt's 1969 paper is famous for attacking. I want to walk into that room able to say something more careful than either "the film proves the prison chaplain right" or "philosophers have since shown the chaplain wrong," so I would like you to read the novel and the film as a piece of philosophy of action, taking the technical literature seriously rather than treating the movie as a mood piece.

Here is the puzzle as I have come to see it, and I would like you to test it rather than simply ratify it. Frankfurt's counterexample is engineered so that the intervener never has to act: Jones deliberates and acts on his own, and Black, who would have forced the outcome had Jones wavered, is left on the shelf. The agent lacks alternative possibilities, yet his action issues from his own reasoning, and that is supposed to suffice for responsibility. Alex looks superficially like that — the Ludovico conditioning is the would-be intervener, and in the ordinary run of things it never fires, because Alex does not get as far as choosing. But I think the resemblance ends there. Alex is not a case in which the actual sequence is normal and only the alternatives have been quietly removed; the mechanism is doing the work inside the actual sequence, and he knows it, because on the demonstration stage he is taunted and struck by an actor and finds he cannot raise a hand, and later, when the vagrant he once beat sets upon him, he cannot defend himself. If that is right, the film is not a Frankfurt case at all — it is far closer to the manipulation cases Pereboom and Mele use to argue that how an action comes about matters even on a purely historical or actual-sequence account of responsibility. Which would mean the work does not vindicate the principle of alternative possibilities but rather the claim that an action must issue from the agent's own未被-manipulated reasoning, and that the chaplain's objection reaches the right verdict by the wrong route. Tell me whether you agree, and if you do not, show me exactly where my description of the case goes wrong.

Please work through the following, in this order. Begin with a short brainstorming pass — set out three or four candidate readings of Alex's post-conditioning conduct (a Frankfurt-style removal of alternatives; a manipulation case in the Pereboom/Mele sense; a case about praiseworthiness for *refraining* rather than blameworthiness for acting; a case about the value of virtue rather than about moral responsibility at all) — and say which you will develop and why before committing to it. Be precise about which version of the principle is in play: Frankfurt targets a claim about blameworthiness for actions, whereas most of what Alex does after the Ludovico technique is not act, and there is a live question how far those arguments carry over to omissions — the locked-door cases, the agent who could not have done otherwise even if he had tried. Does the chaplain's objection depend on a version of the principle that Frankfurt's argument never touched? Then handle the two endings, because most of my group will have seen only the film. Burgess's novel runs to twenty-one chapters in three parts of seven, a structure he chose with the age of majority in mind; the American edition dropped the final chapter, Burgess signed off on that cut at the time, and Kubrick followed the truncated text, so the film ends where the twentieth chapter ends, with the conditioning reversed and Alex insisting that he was cured all right. In the restored chapter, Alex, now eighteen, finds his new gang tedious, runs into an old droog who has married and settled into ordinary work, and decides he is finished with the whole life and means to look for a wife. Take seriously what each ending contributes: does Kubrick's cut-off hand Alex back his alternatives at the exact moment the question of responsibility reopens and then refuse to say what he does with them? Is Burgess's chapter an argument for the principle of alternative possibilities, or an argument that what matters is the slow acquisition of reasons-responsiveness over a life — which would put him nearer to Fischer and Ravizza than to the chaplain? And is it fair to say that the film is the better Frankfurt test-bed precisely because it stops before the answer? Finally, attend to the way the work manages its audience: Alex narrates to us directly in a manufactured slang and is charming, and his worst acts are scored with music he adores. If our sympathy is engineered, are our intuitions about whether he is responsible — or about whether the chaplain is right — usable as evidence at all? Say whether the novel and film corrupt their own status as intuition pumps, and whether that counts for or against screening it in a room of people with a direct stake in the answer.

Approach this as a philosopher of action who also does careful film criticism, and think hard before you start writing. Give me the reading in structured prose, with a short table if it helps to line up the Frankfurt structure, the manipulation structure, and Alex's case side by side. End with two or three concrete questions I could put to the group that would force the distinction between "he could not have done otherwise" and "what he actually did was not really his own doing." I do not want a reading list, bibliography, or citations — I want your reasoning, and where you are unsure of a detail of the plot or the publishing history, flag the uncertainty instead of filling the gap with an invention.
