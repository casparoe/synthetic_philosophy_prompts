I am the teaching assistant for a first-year seminar, cross-listed between the history and philosophy departments, on what we can claim to know about periods that predate any surviving written record, and I need a diagnostic instrument the students complete on their own before our second discussion section. Please build me a decision tree whose leaves are named positions in the epistemology of knowledge about the distant past — roughly eleven branch nodes and nine to eleven leaves. Two structural requirements. First, construct the tree out of the logical space before you start placing views in it: the nodes should be questions about what a person is committed to when they weigh evidence about the past, and only afterwards should actual positions be located at the leaves, carrying a recognized label where one exists (inference to the best explanation about the past, source-critical or Rankean method, Collingwoodian re-enactment, coherentism about historical warrant, testimony-based realism, an archaeology-first view on which material traces carry the weight and narratives merely decorate them, and a skepticism on which the surviving record underdetermines the past) and the label "unnamed" wherever no one actually defends the combination in question. Second, no node may be answerable only by someone who already knows the labels, and no node may use a term of art; if a node has to turn on something technical like the common descent of manuscripts, phrase it in plain language. Assume the students have not read Herodotus or Thucydides and have never encountered the Ranke–Carr–Elton lineage, so anything of that sort belongs in the leaf descriptions rather than in the branching questions.

The nodes should bite on commitments that genuinely divide people rather than on historiographical taste, and I would like you to make sure the tree turns on most of the following axes, choosing whichever are load-bearing: whether the absence of a mention counts against a claim, and how preservation conditions and the expectedness of a mention modulate that; whether two accounts are independent because different people wrote them or because their chains of transmission are separate; whether material remains can confirm a narrative at all, or only confirm what they are themselves about (a coin dated to a reign versus a battle described in a poem); whether reliability attaches to a source or to a source-and-claim pair, since the same scribe may be scrupulous about grain deliveries and worthless about dynastic murder; whether an account must be roughly contemporary with what it reports to carry much weight; whether requiring the past to resemble the present licenses us in discounting miraculous or divinized content outright, or merely in bracketing it; whether convergence among accounts that all descend from one lost original adds anything at all; whether a wildly improbable event can be established by testimony that is otherwise excellent; and whether the target of inquiry is that something happened or what it was like for it to happen.

So that the tree can be demonstrated rather than merely asserted, here is a wholly invented dossier — please do not attempt to identify it with any real site, and treat every item below as stipulated. The question is whether the city of Orenth was destroyed in a single military event in the middle of the first millennium BCE. We have: a destruction layer at Orenth with ash, collapsed masonry, and a scatter of arrowheads of a type manufactured by a neighboring polity, but no mass graves and no hoard of valuables left behind; a victory stele of that neighbor's king, dated by its own regnal formula to his ninth year, boasting that he leveled the walls of a hundred cities without naming any of them; a long poem composed perhaps four centuries later in a third culture, describing a great fire in a city whose name is close to but not identical with Orenth and whose plot is saturated with divine machinery; a fragmentary administrative tablet from Orenth itself, from a later phase of occupation, using the year of the fire as a date formula; two much later prose histories from that third culture which agree on the story and share phrasing in enough places to suggest dependence on a single lost original; and total silence from the extensive merchant correspondence of a fourth polity that was trading actively in the region at the time, though that correspondence is itself only a small fraction of what was presumably written. Run your tree on this dossier twice — once for a student who takes the arrowheads and the stele to be doing the real work, and once for a student who takes the silence of the merchants to be decisive — and show where each lands, whether the tree's verdict is stable under small changes in their answers, and whether the dossier is one on which your instrument should be expected to yield agreement.

Please deliver: the tree node by node, with each branching question and its options; a table mapping answer patterns onto leaves; one sentence per leaf naming its most awkward commitment or the case that most embarrasses it; at least three unoccupied or thinly occupied regions of the space, each with a diagnosis of whether the gap exists because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; and an explicit list of any pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate, with an explanation of why the questions fail to distinguish them rather than a pretense that they do. I would rather you flag a genuine non-separation than manufacture a distinction to fill out the grid. No bibliography, no reading list, and no citations — I want the instrument to stand on its own as a handout, and the students will not have library access during the week they use it.
