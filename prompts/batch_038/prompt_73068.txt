So I was listening to a philosophy podcast on whether you're allowed to say things you don't know. The host has a PhD and doesn't seem like a crank, but here's roughly how he set up the view — transcribed from memory so the exact wording may be slightly off, the substance is his:

"There's an influential view that assertion is governed by one rule and only one: you should assert that p only if you know that p. Think of it like a game. Chess is constituted by its rules, so if you move your bishop in a straight line you haven't made a bad move in chess, you haven't made a chess move at all. Same here — asserting something you don't know isn't a defective assertion, it's not really an assertion. You can hear the rule at work in ordinary talk: when someone tells you something surprising, the natural response is 'how do you know?', and that challenge only makes sense if knowledge is what's demanded of them. It also explains why 'it's raining, but I don't know that it's raining' sounds absurd even though it could easily be true — if the rule were just 'assert only what's true,' that sentence would be perfectly in order whenever it is raining and you happen to be ignorant, so the rule can't be truth, it has to be knowledge. Anyway the refutation came fast. People pointed out that we assert things we don't know constantly and nobody bats an eye — you glance at a departure board and tell a stranger the train leaves at 6:15; that's a perfectly good assertion and you've done nothing wrong. So at best the knowledge rule is an ideal nobody meets, a picture of how we'd talk if we were much better epistemic agents than we are, not a rule our actual practice follows."

It sounded airtight at the time and now I'm suspicious, partly because I vaguely remember Gettier cases being used to argue *for* this view, and the departure board thing doesn't sound anything like a Gettier case. Also: which counterexamples would a defender actually worry about, and why?
