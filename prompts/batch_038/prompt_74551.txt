I spent thirty-five years running an electrophysiology laboratory, a good deal of that time on sleep, and it is only since retiring that I have turned seriously to philosophy of mind. There I was disconcerted to find a respectable tradition denying a presupposition my working life took entirely for granted. Norman Malcolm argued that a dream is not an experience at all: the sleeper has no experiences, the only thing that genuinely exists is the impression one has on waking, and a sentence of the form *I dreamt that such-and-such* is therefore not a report of a past occurrence. Dennett pressed the point further and suggested that a dream may be more like a recording loaded into the machine at the moment of waking than a performance anyone was present for. My local reading group is watching Christopher Nolan's *Inception* next month, and I should like you to write the piece of criticism I am not yet equipped to write myself.

The film's machinery appears to require precisely what Malcolm denies. The dream is a place one is in while asleep; its events run at their own rate relative to the sleeper's body, so that an overnight flight contains a week of happenings; a falling body can interrupt those happenings at a determinate moment; and a spinning top is supposed to tell the dreamer, from the inside, whether there is an inside to be in at all. Dream-sharing goes further still, making a dream a public environment that two sleepers can occupy and a third can furnish in advance — which looks like a flat contradiction of the claim that nobody is ever in a position to observe what a dreamer is having. Is the film accordingly a refutation of Malcolm, a piece of question-begging, or simply not about dreaming in his sense, so that the dispute never really engages? I would like you to work through the details that seem to me to pull in opposite directions: the architecture built before anyone sleeps and the projections that startle their own dreamers, both of which make the dream look authored rather than undergone; the decades Cobb and Mal pass in limbo and return from with the memories of having been there, which seem to demand that those decades were experiences; and Mal herself, undone not by a dream but by a proposition about dreaming that survives waking — an idea planted in a dream and retained in the world, a possibility Malcolm's account appears to leave no room for. Then tell me what you make of the last shot, in which Cobb walks away from the top before it settles. My own suspicion is that the film's real interest lies not in whether he is dreaming but in the confabulation of an answer to that question, which would place it nearer to Dennett than to Malcolm, though I may be flattering it.

Where the film is a heist picture treating dreams as stage machinery rather than thinking about them, say so plainly: I would rather have an honest account of what it ignores than a tidy allegory. Please do not feel obliged to quote the dialogue; describe the scenes as you remember them, and be candid wherever your memory of a detail is uncertain. Write to me in continuous prose, as one writes a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold type, no tables, no numbered sections. I should like a genuine verdict on the central question, however heavily qualified it must be.
