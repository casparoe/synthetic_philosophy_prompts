I'm on a five-person team writing an honesty spec for a deployed assistant, and the definitional argument has been going in the exact same circle every week since roughly March. Nobody has erased the whiteboard. There are three camps. Camp A, mostly the evals people, says honesty is a property of outputs: a system is honest to the extent that it doesn't systematically produce false beliefs in the person reading it, which cashes out as claims tracking the truth and stated confidence being calibrated. Camp B, mostly the interpretability-leaning people, says that's the wrong level entirely, because a system can be accidentally truthful — trained on proxy signals that happen to correlate with truth — while its internal representations are misleading, and the moment the correlation breaks (distribution shift, user pressure, a reward change) the outputs flip. So B wants honesty defined as agreement between what the system's own internal model represents and what it emits, i.e. roughly that it says what it "believes." And there's a quieter third position, camp C, that nobody argues for out loud but that decides what actually ships: honesty as a property of the training process — the system is honest if the objective that produced it was aimed at truth-telling rather than at approval, regardless of what any individual output does. Note that A's own formulation already wobbles, because a perfectly truthful answer can still install a false belief in a confused reader, which means A can't even tell me whether honesty is about truth or about the reader's head.

The maddening part is that on every concrete case we've actually put on the board, all three camps agree on the verdict: the sycophantic model that flatters a user into a wrong decision is dishonest; the model that confidently asserts a fact it has no way of knowing is wrong isn't dishonest, just badly calibrated; the model that emits a memorized true string it doesn't represent at all is honest; the model that internally represents the right answer and emits the wrong one because the user obviously wants the other answer is dishonest. And whenever somebody constructs a case where the camps split, the others say it's misdescribed or not a real deployment case. Last month I tried to break the tie with a counterfactual version: a system is honest iff its output would track the truth across nearby situations where truth and reward come apart — you intervene on the ground truth while holding the reward signal fixed and see whether the answer follows the truth or the reward. A told me it's unmeasurable in practice so it can't be a spec. B told me it just collapses into their view with extra steps. C told me it was their view all along in different words. Those three reactions are the data I care about most, honestly, because if all three camps can absorb the same proposal without changing their verdicts, that tells you something about whether there's any content left to disagree about.

My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: this is a verbal dispute over the word "honest" compounded by two or three different questions running under one heading — A is answering "what can we measure and put a number on for a shipping decision," B is answering "what property, if it held, would make the failure modes we're actually afraid of impossible," and C isn't answering a question so much as stating a design constraint. If that's right the fix is to stop fighting about the word and maintain three separate specs that are explicitly allowed to diverge. But I'm not confident, because there seems to be a substantive disagreement buried under it about whether the relata B wants even exist — whether "what the system represents" is well-defined and identifiable enough to bear a property like honesty at all, which would be a factual/technical question and not a verbal one. And I suspect each camp is smuggling in unargued intuitions from the ancient philosophical fight over whether lying requires an intention to deceive or only saying what you take to be false, which has itself been stuck for a very long time, so we may be re-running someone else's deadlock with better hardware.

So: diagnose the thing rather than adjudicate it. Is this verbal, substantive, or several questions under one name — and if it's the last, draw the lines explicitly, saying which question each camp is actually answering and where they only look like they're contradicting each other. Is there a hidden empirical or technical question doing the load-bearing work (I have a candidate above, tell me if it's the right one or if there's a better one), or is the impasse instead a sign that honesty-for-AI is just not a concept with determinate extension? Work from what the three camps are committed to as I've described them, and tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a decisive case, an intervention or measurement, an impossibility result, or a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and that managing three divergent specs is the correct end state. Please don't survey the honesty literature or the deception literature, and no reading list at the end; I have to merge this document on Friday and I need the structure of the fight, not more references.
