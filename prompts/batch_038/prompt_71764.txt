Been going round in circles on the "separateness of persons" complaint about utilitarianism, and I want help sorting two versions of it apart.

Version one is the Rawls line, as I understand it: utilitarianism takes the principle of choice that makes sense for a single person weighing costs and benefits across their own life and just scales it up to society, as though the many where one big person. The complaint isn't that the sums come out wrong, it's that the loss lands on someone who never receives the compensating gain — nobody is made whole.

Version two is Parfit's reductionist spin: if personal identity isn't what fundamentally matters and the boundaries between persons are shallower than common sense assumes, then the objection loses its sting, since the fact that a cost and a benefit fall on different people is a less deep fact than we thought. Nozick's version sits somewhere near one of these, though I'm not sure which, so correct me if I've got that muddled.

Here's the bit I actually made up, and it's what I want your view on. I think the two versions are running on different tracks. Version one is about what I've started calling compensation reach — whether the gain can be a gain for the very person who bore the cost. Version two is about boundary depth — how metaphysically significant the line between persons is. Those come apart, and I think a fission case shows it. Say Ana undergoes fission, and Lefty and Righty both come out fully psychologically continuous with her: boundary depth between them is minimal at the point of division. But then they live fifty years of separate lives with no contact, and some scheme hands Righty a large benefit for a modest cost to Lefty. Boundary depth says aggregate away; compensation reach says the cost to Lefty is never made good to Lefty, because sharing an origin with Righty doesnt do anything for her now. So Parfit's blunting move seems to evaporate exactly where the Rawlsian objection bites hardest.

My tentative view is that Parfit isn't answering Rawls at all — he's changing the subject from justification to metaphysics — and that once you split compensation reach off from boundary depth, the objection is left untouched.

Do you buy that? I'd like you to actually pick a side and argue for it rather than setting out the considerations on both ends and handing me back a menu: is compensation reach versus boundary depth a real distinction doing real work, or just re-labelling with extra steps? And if my gloss of Rawls or of Parfit is straight-up wrong somewhere, say so plainly before you get to the argument. British English, please.
