---

The supervaluationist starts from the idea that *bald* is semantically underdetermined. Our use of the word settles that a man with no hair is bald and that a man with a full head of hair isn't, but it doesn't settle the middle range. So we look at all the ways of making *bald* fully precise that are compatible with that use — the admissible precisifications. Each precisification has a sharp cutoff, and different admissible ones put the cutoff in different places. A sentence is supertrue iff it comes out true on every admissible precisification, superfalse iff false on all of them, and otherwise it has no truth value at all. So "Patch is bald" is neither true nor false when Patch is borderline, while "Patch is bald or Patch is not bald" is supertrue, because on any given precisification one of the disjuncts holds.

That's how the argument gets blocked: premise (2) fails to be supertrue. Notice that no single instance of (2) is superfalse — for any n you pick, there are admissible precisifications on which "if a man with n hairs is bald then a man with n+1 hairs is bald" comes out true — but the universal generalisation is false on every precisification, since every precisification has some cutoff at which one of the instances fails. The reasoning is still perfectly good, though. Classical logic is left completely untouched on this view: bivalence, excluded middle, and all the familiar validities survive intact, precisely because supertruth is just truth across all admissible precisifications. The argument is valid but has a premise that isn't supertrue, so the conclusion isn't licensed.

The costs are real. *Definitely* becomes a genuine operator with its own semantics, and then "definitely definitely bald" makes sense, so the problem seems to reappear one level up — the notion of an admissible precisification is itself vague, which pushes you toward precisifications of precisifications. And critics object that drawing the line between admissible and inadmissible precisifications is just a hidden sharp boundary of the sort the view was meant to avoid.

The epistemicist, by contrast, denies there is any indeterminacy to explain. "Patch is bald" is straightforwardly true or false, there is a unique n that is the last number of hairs a bald man can have, and premise (2) is simply false — the instance at that n is false. Bivalence and classical logic are preserved wholesale, with no new operator doing any work. The remaining task is to explain why we can't locate n. Williamson's answer is that the cutoff is fixed by our use of the word, but fixed in a way we cannot track. The margin-for-error principle does the heavy lifting: a belief counts as knowledge only if it would still be true in nearby cases, and any belief about the exact cutoff would have been false had our practice been slightly different, so no such belief amounts to knowledge. In principle, though, the cutoff is a fact about our practice rather than a gap in the world, so this isn't mysterious — we just haven't done the survey. A complete enough account of every disposition to apply *bald*, in every context, would let us read the cutoff straight off the data.

The costs here are that the boundary looks arbitrary, since nothing about the process of going bald makes n rather than n+1 the special one, and that our ignorance of it is necessary rather than contingent, which strikes a lot of people as an ad hoc price to pay for keeping bivalence. The epistemicist also owes us an account of why the tolerance intuition is so compelling if the premise expressing it is flat false.
