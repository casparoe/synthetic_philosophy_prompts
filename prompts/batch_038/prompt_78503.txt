**Almost One: What the Problem of the Many Can Teach the Problem of Universals**

§1. The standard one-over-many argument. Two things, a and b, are both red. The realist says there's something they share, and that if nothing were literally identical between them, their agreeing in colour would be a brute fact with no explanation. So: a universal, redness, wholly present in both. Note the shape of that argument. It runs from *they agree* to *therefore something is numerically one*, and it treats the only alternative as a shrug.

§2. Now Unger's problem of the many. There's a cloud over the field. Take any aggregate of droplets in the vicinity that's a decent candidate for being the cloud: there are enormous numbers of them, differing by a droplet or two at the fuzzy edges, and none of them is metaphysically privileged. So either there are many clouds where we thought there was one, or there is no cloud. Note the shape: from *no candidate is uniquely privileged* to *the counting goes wrong*.

§3. My claim is that these are the same argument run in opposite directions, and that both rest on a bad assumption. The assumption is that a qualitative specification — being red, being that cloud — has to be discharged by a counting answer: how many things are literally identical here, or which aggregate is it. The realist demands a unique answer, fails to find one among the particulars, and posits an entity to supply it. The problem of the many finds too many answers and panics. In both cases the panic is about the *number*, not about the *character*. And in both cases the character was never in doubt: we know exactly what it's like for something to be red, we can point at the cloud, sort the droplets, predict the rain.

§4. The response I want to import. Lewis's handling of the cloud, if I've got the spirit of it right, is neither to pick a winner nor to deny the multiplicity. It's to say: yes, there really are many candidates; they overlap so nearly completely that for every question you actually want answered the choice between them makes no difference; and so we speak, correctly or near enough, as if there were one. Many, but almost one. So apply it to properties. There is no single thing wholly present in all and only the red things. There is a vast overlapping cluster of resemblance relations among particulars, whose members are near-indistinguishable for every purpose the concept of a property serves. Which cluster-member you mean by redness is like which aggregate you mean by the cloud: underdetermined, and harmlessly so, because everything a property is supposed to do is insensitive to the choice. This isn't ostrich nominalism, because it doesn't wave the question off. It answers it. The answer is that the question has many almost-equally-good answers, and that's all it ever needed.

§5. The pressure point I already know about. Armstrong's real target is laws and causation: what makes it a law that Fs are G is supposed to be a relation between the very universals F and G, which is precisely why a mere regularity over resembling particulars won't do the job. If F is indeterminate among near-identical candidates, the law is indeterminate, and the counterfactuals and causal explanations riding on it go indeterminate with it. My reply is that the insensitivity premise does the work: for clouds the choice doesn't matter because candidates overlap something like 99.9% by volume; for laws it doesn't matter because laws get stated at a level of coarseness at which all the candidates agree. I'm not confident that's right, and I expect a realist to say the margins are exactly where the empirical content lives — a law that's indeterminate about the borderline cases has surrendered the thing that made it worth positing.

§6. A prediction, mostly as a promissory note. If the diagnosis is correct, the two debates should have parallel pathologies and parallel fixes. Resemblance nominalism's classic troubles — imperfect community, where things can resemble each other pairwise without sharing a genuine property, and the companion-property problem — should structurally mirror the problem of the many's troubles, vagueness at the edges plus the arbitrariness of any precisification. And the repairs should look alike too. I haven't worked this out at all.
