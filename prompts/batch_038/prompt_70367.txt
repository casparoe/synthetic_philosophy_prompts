I am auditing a graduate seminar on skepticism this term, and for the reading group I tried to reconstruct Moore's proof of an external world in a form I could defend. What I ended up with is a template that seems to settle far more than Moore ever claimed it would, and my seminar leader's verdict — that the template begs the question in every application — does not look right to me, because the shape of the reasoning is identical in applications where I am confident the conclusion is true. I want to know which step to give up and what giving it up costs. Here is the argument as I have it written out.

(1) Some ordinary empirical claims are things I genuinely know, not merely believe or find compelling: that I have two hands, that there is a table in front of me, that I had breakfast this morning, that my roommate was annoyed with me yesterday when I broke her mug.
(2) Knowledge is closed under known entailment: if I know p, and I know that p entails q, then I know q.
(3) Therefore, for any philosophical thesis T such that some proposition of the ordinary kind listed in (1) entails not-T, I know not-T, and T is refuted. (This looks like a direct consequence of (1) and (2), not a further assumption, which is part of why I am stuck.)
(4) Application one, the external world: that I have two hands entails that at least two objects outside my body, present in space, exist; so I know an external world exists, and any idealism that denies it is refuted. This is essentially Moore's own proof, and it is just (3) with a particular T plugged in.
(5) Application two, the simulation and Boltzmann-brain hypotheses: the hypothesis that I am a briefly existent simulation, or a fluctuation with hand-like impressions, entails that I do not have two hands; I know that I do; so I know that hypothesis is false, without needing any further evidence about my origins.
(6) Application three, determinism: I know that when I raised my hand a moment ago I could have kept it down — nothing was holding it, I had the ability and the opportunity, and I would have done it had I decided to. If determinism is true, nobody ever could have done otherwise. So I know determinism is false.
(7) Application four, other minds: the thesis that we can never know anything about the mental states of others entails that I do not know my roommate was annoyed; by (1) and (3), that thesis is refuted.
(8) So (3) has at least four unproblematic applications, and a range of philosophy's negative theses is settled by ordinary knowledge rather than by argument.

If I am forced to choose, I would rather keep (2) and restrict (1) than abandon closure, since closure looks like the engine of every anti-skeptical argument I have met and giving it up seems to make knowledge systematically unsafe. But I suspect that restricting (1) to just those propositions a Moorean proof is allowed to use would be arbitrary, and I cannot say what the restriction principle would be. A friend of mine who works on language models says the same template turns up in arguments about whether such systems understand what they produce, which is why I care whether the template itself is broken rather than merely misapplied.

So: which step do you reject, and is the rejection uniform across the four applications or does it bite only on some of them? If your answer is that (3) does not really follow from (1) and (2), show me where the gap is, and explain why the reasoning looks airtight on the surface. If your answer is that (2) must go, tell me what becomes of applications one through four without closure, and whether anything recognizable as anti-skeptical argument survives. If your answer is that (1) must be narrowed, give me the narrowing principle and tell me whether it is anything other than a redescription of the conclusion. I would especially like a separate verdict on (6): is "I could have kept my hand down" the same kind of item as "I have two hands," and does it matter for the template if it is not? Finally, tell me what Moore's proof is left being if (3) is illegitimate — a proof of something, a diagnosis of the skeptic, or a mistake — since that is the part my reading group will actually have to present.
