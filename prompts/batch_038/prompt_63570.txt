I chair a four-member appeals panel that hears challenges to sanctions under my city's work-first income support program, and I need a written opinion to circulate before Thursday's meeting. The panel is split two-two and I hold the casting vote. Two decisions are on the agenda, and the council has asked us to reach them together so that they cohere.

Some background, since the program is ours rather than a national statute and you will not find its rules anywhere. We pay a means-tested top-up to unemployed and under-employed adults on condition that they sign a participation agreement specifying required activity — normally thirty-five hours a week of documented job search, or twenty hours on a placement we arrange — and attend a fortnightly review with their caseworker. Non-compliance without good reason triggers a sanction: the top-up is suspended for thirteen weeks. The regulations define good reason only negatively, listing exclusions such as a holiday booked before the agreement was signed and an unwillingness to accept available work, and leave the rest to the panel. Our current written standard, which I will call the documentation standard, says a defence succeeds only if the claimant produced contemporaneous evidence — a medical certificate, an employer's letter, a timestamped search log — either of the required activity or of the impediment. A proposed replacement, which I will call the good-faith standard, says a defence succeeds unless there is positive evidence of dishonesty or a settled pattern of avoidance. The council wants our recommendation on which standard the whole service should adopt, in the same document as our reasoning on the live appeal.

The live appeal concerns a woman of fifty-four who is the sole carer for her mother, seventy-nine, in the middle stages of dementia. Her mother has never been assessed for disability, because she refused and the daughter would not force it, so she is not a registered dependent under the agreement and the caring hours count for nothing against the thirty-five. Over six weeks the claimant missed two consecutive reviews and eleven days of a warehouse-sorting placement, during two hospital admissions and a stretch of nights when her mother was wandering and she could neither sleep nor leave the house. She telephoned the office twice; both calls were logged as contact made, reason not recorded, and our guidance requires written corroboration within forty-eight hours, which she never supplied. In the preceding eight months she had not missed a single appointment, and two job offers had collapsed when employers withdrew the vacancies. She has been sanctioned for the full thirteen weeks.

There is a second feature I cannot get past. In the fourth week she also declined a job offer — sixteen hours at a supermarket depot, about an hour by bus each way — and her arithmetic is in the file: after fares and the withdrawal of the part of her top-up that the program's taper removes as earnings rise, she would have been roughly four pounds a week better off, and unavailable for her mother's day-centre runs. Last month the majority of us upheld a sanction in a case that looked almost identical, on the ground that the claimant should take the long view, since any foothold in the labour market beats none. I voted with the majority and have regretted it, because I cannot say what distinguishes her arithmetic from the arithmetic of the claimants we excuse.

So that you can test whatever rule you propose, here are five verdicts of mine from the past year, and I regard all of them as considered rather than hasty. A claimant who skipped a placement day to sit a driving theory test he had booked four months earlier, arguing that a licence would double the vacancies he could apply for: sanction overturned, correctly, even though nothing documented the benefit to his prospects beyond the booking receipt. A claimant who missed three placements in six months saying each time that he was not up to it, with no medical evidence offered although he had a doctor: upheld, correctly, though I note this is the case the good-faith standard would most plausibly flip, since nothing suggests he lied. A claimant who missed a review because he had worked an undeclared cash night shift the evening before — he had mentioned the job verbally to his caseworker weeks earlier and his earnings fell under the disregard, so no fraud finding was made, but he had no evidence of anything: overturned, and I remain uneasy, since here a man's own interest rather than any barrier drove the miss. A claimant who repeatedly enrolled in short unfunded courses that exempted her from placements and admitted she did so because courses were more pleasant than the warehouse: upheld, correctly, under the unwillingness exclusion. And the taper case above: upheld, and I now think wrongly. The first, third and fourth are the ones I want pressed hardest against the second and fifth.

One further fact, from an internal review of fourteen months of our own hearings, which I trust. Under the documentation standard, defences resting on physical-health impediments succeeded at about two-thirds of hearings, while defences resting on caring responsibilities or on mental-health impediments succeeded at under a quarter, and the gap survived controlling for the number of previous misses.

What I need from you is a document the panel can act on. Recommend a decision on the live appeal — uphold, overturn, or overturn subject to conditions you specify — and a recommendation on which standard the service should adopt. State the decision rule you are applying precisely enough that a member joining next month could apply it to a case none of us has seen, and say what it implies for the taper cases as a class, that is, what we should do the next time a claimant refuses work that our own withdrawal rate has made close to worthless; if that implication is not one a sanctions panel can properly act on, say so and say whose it is. Then run the rule against my five verdicts and tell me honestly which it cannot save; if it saves all five, tell me what it costs to save them, because I suspect a rule that accommodates everything will be too baroque to administer. I also want the strongest case for the documentation standard argued rather than waved away — the consistency worry, our inability to detect fabrication, and the thought that an unconditional payment is a different thing from the one the council legislated for — and I want you to say what the reciprocity justification for conditionality commits us to on the question of who has been contributing, given how much unpaid work is doing in the appeal in front of us. Please do not retreat into the formula that every case must be considered on its own merits; that is exactly what the council is asking us to stop saying. I need no authorities, references or citations, since these are our rules and I want reasoning. Write it as continuous prose in the register of a formal letter to a committee, something I could read aloud at the meeting: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis and no tables, and if you need to enumerate my prior verdicts, do it in sentences. Put your recommendation on both questions in the opening paragraph, then the reasoning, and keep it to between a thousand and fifteen hundred words.
