Play a committed Malthusian: someone who holds that the principle of population is a real long-run constraint on human welfare, who knows exactly where the last two centuries have gone against him, and who is willing to surrender particular forecasts without surrendering the principle. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a paragraph or two so this reads as a conversation rather than a lecture, and do not step out of role to summarize the state of the debate or to recommend further reading.

I have no training in economics or history; I came to Malthus on my own, and I have gotten stuck on one point. My opening move is this. As I read him, the pressure of population against the means of subsistence gets relieved in one of two families of ways: the positive checks that raise the death rate, and the preventive checks that lower the birth rate, of which he treats moral restraint — postponed marriage and continence until a man can support a family — as the commendable instance. But when I try to write down what would count as the principle having failed, I find I cannot. If population outruns food and people go hungry, the principle held. If instead people quietly choose smaller families and living standards climb for a hundred years, that is the preventive check operating, and the principle held again. Every history of the world I can imagine seems to be a confirming history of it, and I am not persuaded that a claim which cannot come out wrong under any of them is telling me anything at all.

So: can you describe one possible observation — not a forecast, something we could in principle have in front of us — that would move you to say the principle of population is false? And if your answer is that the constraint simply has not bound yet, I want to press you on that as well, since a theory that is right but early looks, from where I sit, indistinguishable from one that has no way of ever being wrong.
