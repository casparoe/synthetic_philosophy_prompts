I'm on the grants committee of a small private foundation — 14 of us, mostly faculty plus a couple of program officers, around 70 applications a cycle, and we fund 8. Our old process was, honestly, a mess: everyone read what they wanted, we went proposal by proposal with a show of hands, and the chair broke ties. After the last round two members admitted separately that they'd voted against papers they actually liked in order to keep a competing application alive, and one of them seemed proud of it. The new chair wants a written rule adopted before next cycle, published to applicants up front, and defensible if someone appeals — which is apparently why I'm doing this, because I took a social choice course a decade ago. My brother, whos done some game theory, told me not to bother: that Gibbard-Satterthwaite proves any non-dictatorial rule over three or more alternatives is manipulable, so whatever we write is a polite fiction, and we should just have an outside reviewer score everything and draw lots among the top three.

I don't buy his argument entirely, but before I go shopping for rules I want to know what I should even be demanding of one. So write the desiderata: what an adequate selection procedure for a committee like ours has to deliver — the properties it must have, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, the formal results it has to be consistent with — with the consideration or the concrete committee example behind each item, and mark which ones are genuinely non-negotiable for us and which are just tastes of one camp in the literature. The ones I most want pressure-tested are the ones that sound self-evidently good. Is strategy-proofness really the target, or is the underlying worry that sophisticated members exploit honest ones — and does it matter whether we get dominant-strategy protection or only no-gain-given-plausible-beliefs, and whether it's one member who can profit or a bloc of three? Are anonymity and neutrality requirements or ideals? What work is "onto" actually doing? Is demanding a complete ranking of all 70 from every member itself a mistake, given that I genuinely cannot compare two applications in a field I've never worked in? And since our output is a set of eight rather than a single winner, does the impossibility framing even apply to us, or is there some different notion of manipulation for that job?

Then flag any subset of the desiderata that can't be jointly satisfied given what's known — I suspect thats the interesting part and my brother is half right, but I want to know exactly which requirement does the damage and what giving it up would cost us, including whether restricting the admissible preference profiles or allowing randomized rules buys anything real, and whether the fact that we'll all be colleagues again next cycle changes whether manipulability matters at all. Tell me which single desideratum knocks out the most otherwise-attractive candidates. Finish with a very short scorecard, a few lines each, for what people would realistically propose to us: Borda, instant runoff, approval, score/range, plurality with the chair breaking ties, and my brother's lottery. No history and no proofs, please — just the spec.
