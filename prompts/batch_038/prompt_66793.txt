Our metaphysics reading group has been chewing on the identity of indiscernibles since September and I think we've hit the point where nobody's even pretending to listen any more. The setup everyone agrees on is Black's: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres — same size, same temperature, same colour, same everything — and absolutely nothing else in existence. Camp A says the case fails. Depending on who's talking, that's because the spheres do differ (each of them has a relational property the other lacks, or each carries a primitive thisness that makes the principle true but trivial), or because you can't stipulate "two" without already smuggling in a background space that does the individuating for you, so the world described isn't a coherent possibility at all. Camp B says the case succeeds and A is flailing: numerical difference can just be brute, there needn't be any qualitative ground for there being two things rather than one, and every move A makes either collapses into "they're different because they're different" or quietly assumes what it needs to prove. We've had this exact exchange maybe four times now, with nobody conceding a single premise.

Then I floated a variant last week and it made things worse, which is why I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Keep the two spheres but add one distinguished feature to the universe — a lone point-particle, or a global orientation, some kind of cosmic wind that gives space a direction. Now the spheres sit at different distances from it or point differently relative to it, so they're plainly discernible by relations. Camp A immediately beamed and said see, that's what we meant all along, discernibility was never in question once the world has any structure in it. Camp B immediately shrugged and said nothing has changed, the symmetric two-sphere world was only ever the limiting case, and adding a wind just relabels the brute difference rather than grounding it. Same sentence, opposite reactions — that's the thing that made me suspect we're not having one argument. My hunch, and it's only a hunch, is that two questions are being run together: a grounding question (what, if anything, makes two things two?) and a modal-evidential question about how much weight a stipulated scenario can bear, since Camp B treats "I can coherently describe it" as doing all the work while Camp A thinks description is cheap and the real issue is whether anything is individuated in the world so described. But I honestly can't tell whether that's right, or whether the whole thing is verbal — a fight about what counts as a property, whether haecceities are properties, whether relational ones are allowed, whether we're quantifying over properties intensionally or extensionally — or whether there's a substantive disagreement buried under all of it about what a metaphysical explanation has to do.
