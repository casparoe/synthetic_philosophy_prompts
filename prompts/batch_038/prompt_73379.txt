Six months ago I took charge of a small municipal museum and archive whose remit has just been widened to include listed buildings and public statuary. A volunteer committee meets monthly to allocate a modest budget, and almost every proposal that reaches us is in truth a claim about how the town should stand to its own past. I should like to stop settling these by temperament.

I have been rereading the second Untimely Meditation, where Nietzsche distinguishes monumental, antiquarian and critical ways of drawing on history, and argues that each becomes a burden rather than a resource for life once it exceeds its proper measure. Please convert that argument into a procedure my committee can run in two hours, with no philosophical training: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; how to identify which of the three attitudes a proposal expresses, given that they rarely announce themselves and one often masquerades as another; the signs that an attitude has tipped into its diseased form; and the point at which the procedure stops with a refusal or hands the matter back as a question of values rather than of fact. Say how the modes are to be weighed against one another when they conflict, and where Nietzsche's criterion of plastic power — a culture's capacity to absorb and remake what it inherits — carries weight that no question on the list can.

Then apply it to three live cases: stabilising a terrace of 1840s weavers' cottages with no realistic public use; reinstalling a Victorian mill owner's statue with a panel naming the conditions in his mills; and demolishing the square's listed bandstand for a skate park. Tell me which verdicts I shall dislike, and where the procedure will misfire in practice.
