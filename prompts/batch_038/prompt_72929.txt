I'm an empirical social scientist — I run variance decompositions on earnings for a living, and my philosophy background is one semester of undergrad Rawls that I've mostly forgotten. So I want the meritocracy-and-desert debate translated into the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can manage, and then I want to know what fell out in the translation.

Here's roughly what my output looks like for, say, log annual earnings at 40: about 10% attributable to family background (parental income and education), 8% to measured cognitive ability at 16 net of that background, 15% to credentials and schooling, a couple points to geography and cohort, and then a big residual — 60%ish — that in my papers usally gets waved at as "effort, luck, and unmeasured stuff." I do Shapley-style allocations when the components overlap. I know full well that a variance share isnt a causal contribution and that the residual is a dumping ground.

So translate these, one at a time: the Rawlsian claim that native endowment and social starting position are morally arbitrary; the luck-egalitarian split between brute luck and option luck; Nozick's entitlement objection that a free exchange system doesn't need desert to justify its outcomes; and the meritocrat's reply that contribution and effort can ground desert even if talent can't. Render each in terms of shares, mediation, residual variance, selection, measurement error, counterfactuals.

Specifically — is my instinct right that once you can measure a "circumstances" share, the moral arbitrariness argument becomes an empirical question I can go out and answer? Or is that instinct itself a smuggling operation, reading a normative premise off a decomposition? And on the other side: is the residue a genuine limitation of variance decomposition, or a sign that the philosophers are arguing about something less substantive than it looks?
