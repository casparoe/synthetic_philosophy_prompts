I teach philosophy of mind and moral philosophy, and for a book chapter on risk and responsibility I have had to work through the literature on what probability claims mean. Something in that literature recurs often enough that I have come to think of it as a single recognisable pattern rather than a set of unrelated slips. I do not know whether it has a name, and the vocabulary my own field supplies for argumentative faults does not seem to cover it.

The pattern is this. A theorist adopts one account of what a probability claim is about — say, that it reports a limiting relative frequency across a long run of repeated trials. Pressed with a case the account struggles to handle — this particular atom, this one defendant, this single storm — the theorist replies that the account was never in the business of saying anything about single cases, so the objection misses its target. Fine. But a few pages later, when the theorist wants a substantive conclusion, the very case just placed outside the theory's scope is being characterised in terms the retreat had disowned: the figure now functions as a fact about a tendency or disposition in the thing itself, or as the confidence a well-informed agent ought to have, and the conclusion is drawn in precisely that register. Pressed there, the theorist returns to the original account; and when a different pressure point appears, usually the question of which trials count as relevantly similar, a third account is deployed that had not been the theory at all.

One instance stayed with me. A writer on extreme weather is asked what it could mean that a particular flood had a three-in-ten chance of occurring. The answer is that the figure belongs to a hypothetical ensemble of similar climates and no claim is made about the one actual event. The same figure then reappears, in an argument about a utility company's negligence, as a fact about the region's standing tendency to produce such floods. Asked which of the indefinitely many ensembles of similar climates is the relevant one, the writer explains the figure as the degree of confidence a well-informed analyst should have had. Three incompatible accounts, one paragraph each, no note of the transitions.

Two features distinguish this from the faults I know how to name. None of the accounts invoked is consistently the weaker or the more cautious one, so there is no defensible core being retreated to; each is the bold position exactly when it is the one in use. And each individual deployment is internally coherent and often right — a frequency claim is the right thing to say about an actuarial class, a dispositional claim about a half-life, a confidence claim about a body of evidence. The failure lies in the unmarked switching, which ensures that no account ever pays for the cases another account was invoked to avoid.

I have considered equivocation and the family of ad hoc rescues. Neither fits. Equivocation assumes one term used in two senses where the argument needs them to be the same; here the senses are avowedly different theories, and the switching happens between arguments as readily as within them. An ad hoc rescue assumes a theory being patched; here nothing is patched, since the theory is simply not the one doing the work at that moment.

So: is there an established concept for this, in the foundations of probability or in informal logic? If there is, tell me what it is and how it is characterised. If there is not, coin one, and do more than supply a label. I want the diagnosis in conditions I could apply to a passage of text — what must be true of an argument for the charge to be correct, and what would look identical on the surface without being it. Mark the edges for me, in particular against the reply that probability simply has several legitimate senses and a writer may use whichever the context licenses: what, if anything, separates that legitimate practice from what I have described, and is the difference detectable from the text or only from the author's further commitments? Finally, and this is what I most want thought through rather than asserted: does the prevalence of this pattern suggest that the dispute between the interpretations of probability is merely verbal, or does it cut the other way, indicating that each interpretation is capturing something the others must borrow?
