My school's honor council — three students, two faculty, six or eight cases a year, any of which can put a suspension on a transcript — sets sanctions from a published tariff that maps each violation and each prior offense onto a penalty. I am a junior serving on it, and I have come to think the tariff is wrong for the reason particularists give about principles generally: the same feature keeps changing its valence. Passing work to another student aggravated one case, where it was paid for, and mitigated another, where the student had been out three weeks caring for a younger sibling; open remorse moved us toward leniency twice and toward severity once, because in that case it arrived only after discovery and attached only to having been caught. The generalists on the council reply that without the tariff we are merely arbitrary, and that a student sanctioned more heavily than a classmate for what looks like the same act has a real complaint regardless of the merits — a complaint sharpened by the fact that our charter publishes our reasons with every decision and permits appeal on grounds of inconsistency with an earlier case.

Could you convert that standoff into a procedure the council can run inside a ninety-minute hearing, written for members with no philosophy background and for two faculty advisers who have to be willing to sign the reasons it generates? I would like the questions in order; each step marked as either statable as an exceptionless conditional or not; what to record, and re-check, once a feature has flipped polarity somewhere in our own record; where the tariff must be overridden, and precisely what the published reasons must then say in order to survive an appeal alleging inconsistency; and which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure. Then run it on three cases, including one pair that looks identical from the outside and that your procedure ends up deciding differently — I expect to dislike that outcome. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by either "generalist" or "particularist", identifying which of the two the procedure you have built actually is, even if you believe it borrows from both.
