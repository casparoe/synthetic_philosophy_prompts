can you build me a decision tree for a first-year philosophy of physics seminar? Half the room has done a bit of GR, half has done intro metaphysics and never heard of a manifold, so the tree has to work for both. It starts from something everybody in the room will grant — you take general relativity seriously, the field equations don't care which coordinates you use, and so there are mathematically distinct models, related by a diffeomorphism that's the identity everywhere outside some bounded region, that agree on everything outside that region but disagree inside it — and it walks them down to a named position on whether spacetime is a thing in its own right, or nothing over and above fields and matter, or something else again.

some rules i care about:

- every branch node must be a question a student can answer on the merits without knowing which label it's steering them towards. No node is allowed to contain the words substantivalism, relationism, structural realism, haecceitism, or gauge. Those belong on the leaves or in the key only.
- somewhere between six and eight nodes, six to nine leaves. Leaves have to include, at minimum: the naive version that just accepts the two models describe two different worlds, the more careful version that keeps spacetime points but refuses to ask which is which across models, relationism in whatever form actually survives the argument rather than the straw version, a structuralist option, and at least one corner labelled 'nobody defends this' if the logical space turns out to have one.
- these are the questions my mate and I kept talking past each other on, and i want them to be the branch points: (i) do the diffeomorphically related models describe two genuinely different physical situations or one situation written down twice; (ii) does it even make sense to ask, of a bare manifold point, what the metric is there, independently of any model; (iii) is the indeterminism the argument generates something to be avoided at all costs, or just unfamiliar; (iv) is the metric field itself substantial enough to be the spacetime, so that giving up the manifold gives up nothing; (v) is treating isometric models as the same situation a discovery about the world or a decision about how to read the maths. Some of these may turn out to be one question wearing five hats — if so, say which, merge them, and explain why they feel different when you argue about them at two in the morning.
- my mate's actual claim, which i want you to test rather than take on trust: he says that once you accept the diffeomorphism freedom as a redundancy in the description, the hole argument is dead on arrival and there is literally nothing left to choose between, so the tree ought to have one leaf. Is he right? If he isn't, put the fork that seperates you from him on the tree explicitly and say what substantive question is still open after the redundancy has been conceded.
- flag every pair of leaves your tree can't actually distinguish, and say what extra question would be needed to pry them apart.
- for each leaf: the position's name as the literature has it, one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on which branch question did the work of landing you there.
- then list the combinations of answers the branches make available that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the gap is there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or simply because nobody has bothered.
