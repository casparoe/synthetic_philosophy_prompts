You're a political philosopher who works on the liberty debate — Berlin's negative liberty, Pettit's non-domination, the self-mastery stuff. Please think carefully and brainstorm a bunch of candidate principles before you settle on any. No reading list, I've got the readings; I want the principle.

I'm in a political theory course trying to work out what I actually believe about when someone is unfree, going off my gut on cases rather than off which author I like. Here they are:

1. The company that made my smart lock can disable my front door remotely. They never have, they've said publicly they never will, and my lease makes them liable for a fortune if they do. I'm free.
2. My friend's landlord can raise the rent at the end of any month, at will, for any reason, and can do it without losing anything himself. My friend has never once complained about the mold. Nothing bad has ever happened. He's unfree.
3. Same landlord, same power on paper, but now there's an enforceable cap and a housing board my friend can actually go to. He still never complains. Free now.
4. A prisoner whose cell door isn't locked and whom nobody would stop, but who was told it's locked and believes it, so he never tries. Unfree — even though no one is doing anything to him.
5. Someone with every option open who can't stop checking their phone. Free, but doing badly. Not unfree.
6. An alcoholic who says they don't want to want the drink. Unfree. I know 5 and 6 have the same shape and I can't say why I split them.
7. Me under laws I voted against, in a place where I get one equal vote and the majority has to answer for what it does. Free. But my classmate whose group has lost every single vote for thirty years: unfree, and no interference has ever been aimed at him personally.
8. A citizen of a genuinely benevolent dictator who keeps every promise and has never restricted anyone. I keep flipfloping — some days freest person alive, some days exactly as unfree as the slave with the kind master our TA kept bringing up.

My tentative principle: you're unfree to the degree that you have to keep tracking another agent's will in order to know what your own options are. It handles 1 (I don't have to track the company, the law does that for me), 2, 3, 4 and 7. But it makes 6 come out free, which I don't want, and it can't tell 8 apart from 2.

So: give me a principle, or a small set of competing ones, precise enough that I could apply it to a new case without asking you. For each clause, say which of my cases bought it. Then run it on two cases I didn't list, ones you pick becuase they're informative. And be honest about the cost — which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and if 5 and 6 have to collapse or 8 has to get pinned down, tell me which way to jump and why. Also: what's the real lever between 2 and 3 — arbitrariness, control, the cost to the interferer, or something else? I can't tell whether my gut is tracking non-interference, non-domination, or that dominium/imperium split, and I'd like to know which.
