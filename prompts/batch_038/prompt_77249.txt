Before I commit a chapter of my dissertation to it, I want a position I have been developing on moral testimony stress-tested as hard as you can manage. Please answer as a moral epistemologist who has argued on both sides of this debate and has no stake in being polite about either.

The position is this. I grant that taking a moral claim on someone else's say-so can produce true belief and, in favourable circumstances, knowledge; I do not want the sceptical version of pessimism. What I claim is that the defect in moral testimony is not alethic or justificatory but dispositional. Moral understanding — a grasp of why a moral claim holds that lets an agent deploy it in structurally novel cases, weigh it against competing considerations, and keep holding it under social or rhetorical pressure — is not transmissible by bare testimony. So an agent whose moral beliefs come from authority is, in the ordinary case, in a defective position not because those beliefs are likely to be false or unjustified, but because they are inert: they do their work only where the situation matches the one the agent was told about, and the resulting right action lacks the sensitivity to moral reasons that I take to be required for an action to have moral worth. I think this relocation is what allows the pessimist intuition to survive the familiar objection that testimony can transmit knowledge after all. I already know two places where it looks fragile: if testimony accompanied by a full explanation of the reasons transmits understanding, the view collapses; and if the defect is purely dispositional, it may apply equally to factual beliefs taken on authority, leaving me either with a general scepticism about testimony that nobody holds or with an unprincipled stopping point at the moral.

Tell me what would count against it, in the strict sense: not objections that have been made, but states of affairs — arguments, cases, empirical findings, results in neighbouring debates — that would, if they obtained, show the position false or empty. I would like the following. First, before ranking anything, reconstruct the view in your own terms and identify its load-bearing assumption; if you think I have mis-stated my own position in a way that makes it easier or harder to attack than I realise, say so. Second, construct the sharpest counterexample you can — a short vignette of an agent who plausibly acquires moral understanding from testimony alone — and say whether you think it is fatal or absorbable, and on what grounds. Third, work through the generalization worry: is there a principled line between moral testimony and testimony about, say, a mathematical proof, a medical prognosis, or the merit of a novel, and if the defect spreads across that line, does my view become trivially true or unacceptably revisionary? Fourth, tell me what my view predicts that its optimist rivals do not — I suspect it predicts an asymmetry in how well testimony-acquired beliefs generalize to new cases, how they survive counter-pressure, and how they support explanation — and say what evidence, empirical or otherwise, would bear on those predictions, including whatever you think the record on moral expertise, and on the behaviour of people who are supposed to be moral experts, actually shows. Fifth, consider the class of cases where moral deference looks not merely permissible but required: the young child, the agent raised in a corrupt community, the person deferring to those better placed to see a harm they have been trained not to notice. Say whether these break the view or merely embarrass it.

Sort everything you produce into fatal, serious, and merely awkward, and say which of the fatal and serious items a committed defender of my position would themselves concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss as a misdescription of the view. Then give me your honest assessment of whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability: I have already conceded knowledge and relocated the defect, so tell me whether anything is left that it genuinely risks, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind or an acceptable feature of a claim about a dispositional capacity.

A few constraints on the answer. I want commitment, not a survey: rank the defeaters, name the single one you think should make me abandon the view, and give your reasons for putting it there; if your honest conclusion is that none of them is fatal, say so flatly and then tell me what the view is worth holding if it cannot be shown false. Distinguish throughout between what is settled in the debate, what is contested, and what you are extrapolating, and where you appeal to empirical findings, describe them substantively enough that I could evaluate the reasoning without looking anything up, marking your confidence that the finding is as you describe it. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and invoke philosophers' names only where a name is doing argumentative work rather than gesturing at authority. Think carefully before you begin; if it helps to brainstorm the space of possible defeaters before narrowing to the ranked list, do that first and leave the brainstorm visible — I would rather see what you considered and discarded than receive a tidied-up result.
