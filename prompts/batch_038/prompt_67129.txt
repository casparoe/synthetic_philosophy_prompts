Here is the passage:

> When we ask whether someone was entitled to say what they said, we are not really asking whether what they said was true. Those who study assertion have converged on a different answer: you may assert only what you know. To say that the meeting starts at three is to present yourself as knowing that it starts at three, which is why the natural response to a flat assertion is the challenge "how do you know?", and why the only answer that satisfies the challenge is one that exhibits knowledge rather than a hunch, a report of what somebody told you, or a statistical likelihood.
>
> The same rule is said to explain lying. A lie is an assertion made without knowledge, so lying is not a special wrong needing a special explanation; it is the ordinary wrong of asserting what you do not know, in a particularly blatant form.
>
> The difficulty is that almost nothing meets this standard. I do not know that it will rain tomorrow, or that my friend's flight has landed, or that the sun will rise, in the strict sense the rule requires, since each of those could easily have turned out otherwise. Were the rule really governing our speech, ordinary conversation would be impossible: we would have to spend our days saying "I have strong grounds for believing, though I do not know, that the flight has landed." Nobody talks that way, and that is the best evidence that this is not the rule we actually follow. What its defenders are left with is the claim that we breach a norm of assertion thousands of times a day and are not to be blamed for it, which is less a theory of our practice than an admission that the practice is something else.
