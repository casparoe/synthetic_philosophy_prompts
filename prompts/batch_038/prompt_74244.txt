**Error Budgets for Belief**

*1. The operational version of the puzzle.*

My team runs a service with a 99.9% monthly availability target, which gives us an error budget of about forty-three minutes of downtime per month. We ship roughly sixty deploys a month. Call the probability that any given deploy causes a user-visible incident about 2%. Reviewed one at a time, every deploy clears our bar: 98% confident that this one is fine. Reviewed as a month, the same numbers give 0.98^60 ≈ 0.30, so about a 70% chance that at least one deploy causes an incident. We accept of each deploy that it will not cause an incident; we accept of the month that it will. Both clear our threshold, and both cannot be true.

That is the preface paradox with a pager attached. The author who has checked every assertion carefully, and who nonetheless writes in the preface that there are errors in the book, is running my monthly review.

*2. Two paradoxes, two anatomies.*

The standard telling treats the preface paradox (Makinson, 1965) as the lottery paradox (Kyburg, 1961) with non-uniform probabilities. In Kyburg's version there is a fair thousand-ticket lottery with exactly one winner and a rule of acceptance permitting you to accept any proposition whose probability exceeds 0.99. You may accept of each ticket that it will lose. Conjoin a thousand such acceptances and you get "no ticket wins," contradicting "some ticket wins." Kyburg's own move was to refuse the conjoining step: keep the threshold rule, keep the prohibition on accepting known inconsistencies, deny that rational acceptance is closed under conjunction.

I want to argue the two puzzles are not the same puzzle, and that treating them as one is what makes the shared solution look unsatisfying.

In the lottery, the joint claim is *derived* from the item-level claims by a logical rule. Nothing else produces it. Reject adjunction and the contradiction has no route into existence; what remains is only the question of what you may say out loud.

In the preface, the joint claim is *estimated* from a different source. The author's belief that there is an error is not an aggregate of their confidence in individual assertions. It comes from a base rate over books, authors, and their own track record — a process-level estimate produced by a different instrument than the item-level estimates. So rejecting adjunction cannot touch it. The author never used adjunction to get there.

*3. Exogenous and endogenous probabilities.*

Suppose the author assigns 0.999 to each of three hundred assertions. Independence would give 0.999^300 ≈ 0.74 that the book is flawless, yet the author's process-level prior says something closer to 90% that it is not. Two estimators of the same quantity, disagreeing, with no logical rule connecting them. That is the real content of the preface paradox, and it is a fusion problem rather than a logic problem.

The asymmetry matters. In the lottery, the 0.999 is exogenous: it is a fact about how the lottery was constituted, known independently of the person doing the accepting. In the preface, the 0.999 is endogenous: it is produced by the very checking process whose reliability is at issue. The author's confidence in each assertion is a self-report filed by the defect-prone component. When an item-level estimate is generated by the process being estimated, it is not an independent measurement, and it should be shrunk toward the process-level estimate rather than aggregated with its peers. This is also why errors in a book are positively correlated — a bad chapter, a tired fortnight, a disciplinary assumption that is simply wrong — and correlation makes the naive independence arithmetic optimistic in exactly the wrong direction.

*4. Thresholds should be batch-relative.*

Here is the proposal I actually want to defend. Acceptance thresholds should not be constants. If you intend to assert N propositions and want the joint assertion to carry no more risk than a single assertion at threshold t, then per-proposition risk must be (1 − t)/N, so the threshold is t_N = 1 − (1 − t)/N. With t = 0.95 and N = 300, that is 0.99983 per claim — a bar no author clears, which is arguably the point: the author who writes a book has overspent, and the preface sentence is the confession.

The consequence for Kyburg is that a batch-relative threshold is not merely "restricting aggregation." It changes what you may accept one at a time, and it makes acceptance non-local and history-dependent: what you may accept next depends on how much you have already accepted. I expect that is the part a philosopher will find most objectionable, and I do not know what the standard objection is.

*5. Query-relative confidence.*

Finally, in a system there is no such thing as "the system's confidence in P" independent of the query. Ask my service about deploy 471 and it answers with 98% confidence. Ask it about the month and it answers that an incident is 70% likely. Both answers are calibrated when each is scored against its own ground truth. If belief works this way, then "S believes that p" is incomplete without specifying the question p answers, and the appearance of contradiction in both paradoxes comes from treating outputs of different queries as entries in one ledger.

I anticipate the reply that I have changed the subject: the paradoxes are about what a person is entitled to believe all things considered, not about what a system emits when prompted. My only answer is that "all things considered" is doing unexamined work in the standard formulations.
