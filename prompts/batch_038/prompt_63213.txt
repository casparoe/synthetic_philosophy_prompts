I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy, and in six weeks I will present a forty-minute paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on the relationship between formal impossibility results and normative institutional design. What I need is a question-period preparation sheet, not a defence of the argument, which has by now settled in my own mind.

My thesis, in the form I intend to state it from the podium, comprises four claims.

First: the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is routinely described in political theory as showing that every non-dictatorial voting rule with at least three possible winners is manipulable, and thence that manipulation is ineradicable from democratic choice. But what the theorem actually constrains is the shape of the choice problem. It presupposes a resolute rule returning a single winner, an unrestricted domain of voter orderings, and a range of at least three alternatives. Each of these is an assumption about how determinate and how rich the outcome space is, not an assumption about anyone's incentives. On my reading the result is therefore best understood as a richness theorem — a claim about which kinds of collective choice problem can be made self-enforcing at all — and its philosophical moral has been mislocated.

Second: the two standard appropriations of the result, the quietist one (all systems are manipulable, so comparative institutional design is idle) and the permissive one (manipulation is unavoidable, so strategic voting cannot be a vice), both rest on a quantifier error. The theorem guarantees, for each admissible rule, the existence of some profile at which some voter can gain by misreporting. Existence is perfectly compatible with such profiles being rare, dispersed, difficult to locate, or mutually defeating once several voters act on them, and the theorem says nothing whatever about any of that.

Third: the correct normative target is consequently not the binary axiom of strategy-proofness but a graded family of anti-manipulation properties — the proportion of profiles at which a beneficial misreport exists; the magnitude of the available gain at such profiles relative to the spread of outcomes the rule can produce; and the degree to which those gains are concentrated in a small number of pivotal voters. Nothing in the theorem excludes a rule that performs well on all three measures while remaining non-dictatorial, and I take the restricted-domain results (single-peaked preferences together with the median-voter rule) and the randomized variants (random dictatorship, which survives only by adopting a particular extension of ordinal preferences to lotteries) as evidence that the space of such rules is considerably larger than the theorem's reputation implies.

Fourth: reform debate should accordingly be re-posed — not as the question which rule is strategy-proof, to which the answer on the standard assumptions is none, but as the question which rule minimizes the graded measures subject to the other constraints we independently have reason to care about.

The audience will be about thirty people. Roughly half are economists and computer scientists working on computational social choice who know the theorem and its variants technically; several of them work on the complexity of computing a beneficial misreport and are likely to regard my measures as naive about that literature. Two are mathematical political theorists who have written on restricted domains and will regard claim one as either trivially true or as a misunderstanding of what domain means in this context. Three are political philosophers in the deliberative-democracy tradition who cite Gibbard–Satterthwaite as a sceptical premise against electoral reform and will have little appetite for measures of any kind. The chair is a philosopher of mathematics who works on the epistemology of impossibility theorems and enjoys asking what such theorems license us to call possible.
