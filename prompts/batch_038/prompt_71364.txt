I'm building a problem set for a grad seminar on decision-making under extreme uncertainty, and one week is spent on Pascal's-mugging-type cases. Every year the seminar turns into people lobbing the canned responses at each other — bounded utility functions, discounting tiny probabilities, "a claim with that much information content deserves a tiny prior," Hume-style suspicion of testimony about marvels — and nobody actually has to do any work. So I want an exercise that forces the issue by construction instead of by lecture.

Build me a matched pair of cases.

Case A is a mugging proper: someone approaches my agent with an absurdly large promised payoff or threatened loss in exchange for a small, ordinary cost, and the agent's own credence in the claim is genuinely tiny.

Case B is something completely mundane, the kind of thing a normal person runs into a few times a year, where the agent also has a tiny credence in an enormous-stakes outcome and also faces a small cost to do something about it.

The point is that in Case B everybody agrees the agent should act. So the class can't wave Case A away with a blanket rule about tiny probabilities without saying what breaks in B, and that's exactly the conversation I want them to have.

Specifications, and this is the part that matters:

- No sci-fi. No gods, no simulators, no predictors better than a smart well-informed human. The stakes in A can be big but they have to be stakes that could conceivably be on the table in a real conversation, and the cost has to be something a broke grad student could actually pay.
- Please don't use insurance, cancer screening, or asteroid deflection for Case B. Those are the ones they've all seen and it turns the exercise into recall.
- Match the two cases as tightly as you can on: subjective probability of the high-stakes outcome, size of the payoff or loss, cost of acting, reversibility, and how confident the agent is in her own reasoning. Give me a short table of the matched values and be upfront about anywhere you had to fudge.
- The residual difference between A and B is the real deliverable. Name it in one sentence, then argue for it. Is it that A's probability comes out of somebody's mouth while B's comes from background statistics? That A is unverifiable in principle? That A was generated by someone who chose to make the claim to her? Something else?
- Then run the test. State the principle a student would need in order to discount A but not B, and say honestly whether it can be stated without either (i) collapsing into a general rule that also forbids acting in B, or (ii) smuggling in the very intuition it's supposed to explain. If it can't be stated cleanly, say so and tell me what we're left with.
- Finish with a paragraph on how a defender of probability-discounting would attack the pair. The most likely move is that once the agent conditionalises properly, the credence in A isn't tiny in the same way the one in B is. Either show why that fails or concede it and say what survives the concession.

About a page and a half total. I'm handing students the two cases with no analysis attached, so the cases need to be self-contained and the analysis has to be clearly seperated off after them.
