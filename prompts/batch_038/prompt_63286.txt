Next term I am convening an upper-level seminar on the ethics of climate change, and one session will be devoted to what my students will find the most deflationary material in the course: the inefficacy objection. At a departmental workshop last month a colleague in political theory pressed that objection with an image that has become almost proverbial in our building. One person's annual emissions, she said, are like a cup of water poured into the ocean: the sea is not raised by any amount that could be detected, so the individual act harms nobody, and a moral theory that nonetheless finds wrongdoing in it has confused aggregation with contribution. She reinforced the point politically, noting how much of the personal-footprint vocabulary we all use was popularized by a fossil fuel producer's advertising campaign in the mid-2000s, and arguing that urging students to fly less and eat less beef is therefore not merely futile but counterproductive, since it lets the significant agents off the hook and supplies the conscientious with a sense of having done their bit. Her conclusion was that normative attention belongs exclusively at the level of institutions and policy. Another colleague replied that this line of reasoning proves far too much, because it would equally condemn voting: in a national election no single ballot is remotely likely to be decisive, yet nobody concludes from this that voting is pointless, and most of us think there is something like a duty to vote. The structure of the two cases, she said, is identical, and if the inefficacy objection does not defeat the duty to vote it cannot defeat a duty to cut one's own emissions. Since it is that analogy, and not the bare claim that aggregate effects matter, that is supposed to carry the refutation, I would like to audit it properly before putting it in front of students.

What I want, then, is an assessment of how far the voting analogy actually holds. Please begin with an explicit mapping: what in the target case corresponds to the ballot, the tally, the decisive threshold, the electorate, the losing alternative, and the duty to vote itself — a table of correspondences would be perfectly welcome. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for this particular use from those that are merely decorative. I expect the interesting ones to cluster around questions like these, though I would rather you tell me which of them are load-bearing than assume that all are: an election is a deliberately designed aggregation device with a public, countable tally and an (at least in principle) determinate pivot point, whereas market production is buffered by inventories, lumpy reorder thresholds, and supply chains that may make an individual purchase more or less likely to be traced back into production; the cost of voting and the cost of a life reorganized around emissions are wildly unlike; the duty to vote is usually grounded in fairness to identifiable co-participants who are also bearing costs, or in expressive and associative value that does not depend on causal efficacy at all, and it is not obvious that either ground has an analogue for the person deciding about a long-haul flight. I would also like you to consider whether any of the disanalogies run against the analogy's user rather than for her — in particular, whether the market case is if anything more hospitable to individual difference-making than the ballot is, given that a purchase carries some small probability of triggering a large reorder, which is a structural feature elections lack. Having laid all this out, tell me plainly whether the colleague's conclusion survives: not that voting is impermissible, but that individual emission-reduction duties exist on the same grounds as the duty to vote. If the analogy fails, say what better one would take its place, and whether the cup-of-water image fares any better under scrutiny — including whether "makes no detectable difference" is even the right thing to say, given the old Parfitian point about the mistake of ignoring effects merely because they are imperceptible or improbable. Finally, and briefly: if there is a residual grounding for individual duties that does not depend on the analogy at all — complicity, integrity, participation in structural injustice, norm-shifting, political signaling — does it rescue her practical recommendation while abandoning her argument, and is that a rescue or a change of subject? I care far less about the state of the literature than about whether the analogy itself can bear the weight placed on it, so please do not close with a non-committal summary; give me a verdict I can defend to a room of skeptical undergraduates.
