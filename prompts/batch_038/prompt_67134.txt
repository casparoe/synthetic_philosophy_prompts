I am drafting the methods chapter of a dissertation in nineteenth-century intellectual history, and my two readers have given me instructions I cannot both follow. One says that any evaluative verdict I pass on a historical agent is presentism in disguise — that I have substituted my department's standards for the ones in force, and that the resulting judgments belong in a footnote at best. The other says that a history which refuses to call anything wrong is not modest but unintelligible, since my own account of why these episodes are worth studying already depends on evaluations I am not allowed to state. Before I pick a side, I want to know what I would even be asking of one. So: write the specification. I want the desiderata that any account of the historian's evaluative stance toward past agents must meet in order to count as adequate, where adequate means strong enough to be stated in a methods chapter and then actually applied at the desk, case by case, without the historian having to reinvent the principle each time. For each requirement, give the phenomenon, puzzle, or case that motivates it — I want to see why it is a requirement and not just a taste — and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference belonging to one camp. No bibliography, no names, no history of the debate; the specification itself is the deliverable, and I will do my own reading afterward.

To keep the list honest, it has to survive contact with cases like these, which are close to the ones in my chapters. Case one: a physician whose standard practice we now regard as gravely harmful, working at a time when a vocal minority within his own profession had already published objections, and when there is evidence he read them and dismissed them as sentimentality. Case two: a magistrate of the same decade who enforced a statute he privately believed unjust, in a jurisdiction where no organized criticism of that statute existed and where refusal would have cost him his position and his family's security. Any account that returns the same verdict for both, or that returns the verdicts it does merely because criticism "existed" or "did not exist" somewhere in the world at the time, has missed something about the reachability of dissent rather than its existence; I want that something named as a requirement. Beyond those, the account must at minimum handle the fact that the standards of a period are never unanimous and are largely recoverable only through the writings of the people my case two's magistrate was answerable to; the distinction between evaluating an act, evaluating an agent, and evaluating a character; the fact that present-minded historians distribute praise as well as blame, and that the pressure toward exculpation runs in only one of those directions; the reflexivity problem, namely that whatever shields my actors must also yield a verdict about me without assuming that my successors' standards are the correct ones; and the worry that an account anchoring all evaluation in what an agent could have recognized leaves the past normatively inert, unable to correct us in turn. It should also say something about whether the historian's verdict may depend on how things turned out, and about how the account is supposed to be operationalized — what in the archive settles which standards were in force for which people.

Then do the two things I most want and least expect. First, sort the requirements: which are genuine desiderata that any candidate must satisfy, and which are actually the doctrinal commitments of one side wearing the costume of a criterion — I suspect at least two of the things routinely demanded in this area are of the second kind, and I want to know which. Second, tell me whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. If, say, immanence to an agent's recognizable standards, the preservation of genuine criticism, and a non-question-begging account of whose standards count cannot all be had together, say so plainly and say which one should go, because that result would settle my chapter for me better than any choice of camp. Order the requirements by how much exclusionary work each does — which single one knocks out the most positions — and close with a short paragraph on how the main available stances (unrestrained contextualist restraint, unabashed present-minded judgment, a two-tier separation of explanation from evaluation, and a culpability-relative-to-accessible-criticism view) fare against the list. A numbered layout with a negotiability marker on each item is fine; a table is also fine.
