I am at work on a novel one strand of which concerns an invented state's campaign against a linguistic minority inside its own borders: mass internment of adults registered as belonging to that minority, the closure of every minority-language school, the banning of its use in public administration and on the state broadcaster, and the removal of some thousands of children to boarding institutions in the majority region, from which they do not return. Nobody is massacred. The state's public justification is assimilationist, and its internal correspondence speaks of correcting a demographic imbalance.

For a late chapter I need a dialogue of roughly nine hundred words between two characters: a prosecutor preparing a filing who argues the campaign is genocide, and a retired appellate judge of equal ability and equal good faith who argues that it is not — though he would convict without hesitation for persecution and forcible transfer as crimes against humanity. Give me only the two voices, with no narration between the lines.

Neither of them may win by caricature. The prosecutor must meet the objection that dispersal and assimilation are not the destruction of a group, however grievous, and must explain why the clause of the Convention that lists the forcible transfer of children as a genocidal act in its own right does the work she needs. The judge must confront that clause directly rather than wave it away as drafting debris, and must say what he thinks it does cover if it does not cover this. I would like the exchange to pivot on a shared piece of drafting history read two ways: the drafters rejected a broader cultural-destruction provision yet kept the children clause, and each character takes that as evidence for his own side. I would also like one turn to be evidentiary rather than interpretive — what a prosecutor can and cannot prove about a special intent to destroy the group, in whole or in part, from documents that never once use the word — and I would like them to disagree about whether Lemkin's own broader conception of the term, which took in the dismantling of a group's institutions, language, and cultural life, should have any authority over a court applying the narrower treaty text.

After the dialogue, please append a brief note in your own voice: which of the two you have inadvertently made the stronger, which of their claims rest on contested readings rather than settled law, and what argument or piece of evidence would change your mind about how the case should come out.
