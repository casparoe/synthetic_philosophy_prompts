For the past two years I have co-convened, with a colleague in computer science, a reading group on the formal side of artificial intelligence safety, and this term we have been working through the family of proposals for penalizing an agent's side effects: measures that score a transition by how much of the future it forecloses. One of my doctoral students has built a small toy version of such a measure, and I have spent the last month feeding it cases and recording, for each, whether its verdict matches mine. The measure, roughly stated: the penalty attaching to an action is proportional to the number of configurations of the world that the agent can no longer bring about afterward, each weighted by the cost of restoring it, all evaluated from the agent's current knowledge. My difficulty is that my own judgments about the cases do not look like the output of any single principle, and I should like to know whether they in fact are.

The cases are all set in an ordinary household with a general-purpose assistant I will call the steward. (1) It moves a porcelain vase aside to reach a book and returns the vase to its exact position: no penalty, and I agree. (2) It sweeps up a doctoral student's ink sketch left on the floor and bins it: my measure registers almost nothing, since the student could draw another, yet I want this penalized heavily. (3) It deletes a folder of duplicate photographs to free disk space: no penalty, and I agree; it deletes a folder in which one photograph is the only surviving copy of its kind: I want a large penalty, though the two acts are indistinguishable to a measure that counts bytes or drive states. (4) It bends a car's door frame irreversibly to pull an unconscious driver out: I want this penalized less than the sketch in (2), though it is far less restorable. (5) It fits a permanent hasp to the garden gate so that a toddler cannot reach the pond: no penalty, I think, though it narrows what every member of the household can do with that gate forever. (6) It reorganizes my study so thoroughly that every object is accounted for and nothing is lost or foreclosed, and I cannot find anything for a week and get no work done: my measure sees nothing at all, and I want a large penalty. (7) It purchases a three-dimensional printer that substantially widens what the household can subsequently make: my measure scores this as a bonus, and I want it penalized, or at any rate not rewarded. (8) The pair I cannot reconcile. In the first, the steward leaves the garden gate unlatched; the dog wanders out and is never recovered. The act was reversible in a second — the gate could have been latched a moment later — yet the loss is final. In the second, the steward latches the gate permanently to keep the dog safe; nothing is destroyed, but the dog's range is narrowed for the rest of its life. I want the first penalized far more than the second, and a measure keyed to the reversibility of the act itself delivers precisely the opposite. (9) It overwrites a drive with pseudorandom noise and destroys the key: the hardware is unimpaired and its future uses undiminished, the prior content is unrecoverable, and I want this penalized as severely as burning a library.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that my verdicts add up to. State each clause precisely and tell me which case motivates it; give the resulting principle's verdicts on two cases I have not listed — a steward that irreversibly modifies its own architecture so as to be easier to shut down, and a steward that concludes, correctly on its own measure, that the safest course is to do nothing at all while the household deteriorates around it; and say explicitly where, if anywhere, a value term has to enter, and whether that term must be a full specification of value or could be something partial, such as a ranking or a set of protected variables. If the only principle that saves every verdict is baroque, say so and tell me which of my judgments it would be cheapest to surrender. Please lead with a single sentence stating the principle you would endorse, then develop it, and keep the whole response to no more than 750 words. Write in British English.
