I'm in a seminar on the history of casuistry where each of us drew a figure, and we have to hand down rulings on cases the prof invented and then defend them under cross-examination. I got Augustine on lying, so I've been working through On Lying and the later one he wrote to Consentius — the one about whether you may pretend to accept Priscillianist teachings in order to get inside the sect and expose it — taking notes on every case he actually rules on rather than just his general principles. My table so far, working from memory of the notes:

- a joke or an obviously invented tall story: not a lie at all, since nobody is meant to take it as true;
- someone who asserts what he believes to be false, and it turns out to be true: he lied anyway;
- someone who asserts what he believes to be true, and it turns out false: no lie, just a mistake;
- the Priscillianist infiltration plan: flatly forbidden, and it sits in the worst of the eight grades he ranks lies by;
- lying to a would-be killer about where his intended victim went: still forbidden, but way down near the bottom of the eight; what you're supposed to do instead is stay quiet, refuse to answer, or say something true along the lines of knowing where the man is and not handing him over;
- the midwives in Exodus who told Pharaoh the Hebrew women were too quick for them: he won't call that a good lie — as I read him, God repaid their mercy and overlooked the lie, which stays a sin, just a light one — and I'm pretty sure he treats Rahab the same way, spared for her faith and hospitality with the lie forgiven rather than commended. (There's also a bit where he insists Jacob's trick on his father wasn't a lie at all because it was a sign of things to come, and I may have mangled that one — tell me if it doesn't fit the pattern.)

The case I have to rule on Thursday. A woman has fled her husband and is upstairs in the house of the neighbour sheltering her. The husband comes to the door, armed, and asks whether she's in the house. The neighbour answers that she hasn't seen her since Tuesday — literally true, she hasn't laid eyes on her since Tuesday, because the woman arrived after dark and went straight upstairs — and she fully expects him to take that as meaning the woman isn't there. She also knows she could instead just refuse to answer, or say she isn't going to discuss it, and she has reason to think either of those would cost her nothing he'd dare do in front of witnesses.

So what does he rule, and I want the machinery, not just the verdict: is her answer a lie, a sin that isn't a lie, or clean? Which rule is he actually running, given that at least four seem to fit everything in my table — the heart-mouth match (a lie is only a mismatch between what you hold and what you say, so steering someone into a false belief by way of a true sentence is off the hook), or the will-to-deceive (so a true sentence engineered to plant a false belief counts), or something about what speech is *for*, or a scalar reading on which the eight grades are degrees of badness that can in principle be outweighed, which a classmate swears is all the midwives case amounts to. I keep going in circles about whether to read him with something like implicature — she says something true and implies something false, so maybe his test should be applied to the implied content — or whether that's an anachronism that quietly swaps his position for a nicer one. Then: which of her two live options he'd rank better and why, and what further case, one I haven't listed, you'd put to him to decide between the candidate rules. And if any entry in my table is wrong as I've stated it, flag that first — I'd rather fix it now than get caught on it in front of everyone.
