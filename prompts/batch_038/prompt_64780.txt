I am drafting a position paper for a debate about reforming the allocation of competences in the European Union, and the central argument I have to answer rests on an analogy I would like audited before I engage with it.

The argument comes from a senior colleague. He says subsidiarity should be understood as a corporate delegation rule: a well-run multinational decides at head office only what its subsidiaries genuinely cannot do as well themselves, leaves everything else where the knowledge and the customers are, and settles disputes about where a decision belongs empirically, by comparing performance. Article 5(3) TEU, he says, is the same test in constitutional dress, since the Union may act only where objectives "cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States ... but can rather, by reason of the scale or effects of the proposed action, be better achieved at Union level." He draws two conclusions. First, subsidiarity is at bottom a principle of organisational efficiency rather than of liberty or self-government, so comparative evidence of performance is what settles competence disputes, and objections rooted in national identity carry no independent weight. Second, the early-warning mechanism is an anomaly on this reading: national parliaments may transmit reasoned opinions objecting that a draft act breaches subsidiarity within eight weeks, and where those opinions reach one third of the votes allocated to them (one quarter for proposals in the area of justice and home affairs) the Commission must review the draft — which has happened a handful of times, including on the proposal for a European Public Prosecutor's Office. A firm, he notes, has no comparable procedure; no subsidiary gets to file a reasoned objection to a head-office decision. He treats that as a defect in the EU's machinery rather than as a clue about the principle.

Another colleague offers the opposite analogy. For her, subsidiarity's home is the household rather than the firm: its origins lie in the thought that a parent should not take over a task a child can manage, because the child's doing it is itself part of what is being protected, and the higher level's business is to support rather than to replace. On that reading comparative performance is beside the point — head office may be measurably better at the job and still be properly excluded from it — and the efficiency reading simply deletes the normative content of the principle.

What I need is an audit of both, with most of the weight on the first. Specifically:

(i) An explicit table of correspondences for the delegation analogy: head office, subsidiaries, board and shareholders, the firm's single objective and its articles of association, the standing test of who does it better, the power to re-centralise, the possibility of exit, and the party who decides where decisions belong.

(ii) The disanalogies, sorted into those that actually bear on my colleague's inference and those that are merely true of firms and therefore inert here.

(iii) What the source quietly supplies that the target lacks — in particular whether a firm has anything corresponding to the question of who holds authority to decide who decides; whether it has constituent units whose standing does not derive from their contribution to the whole's objectives; and whether "better" is measured on one scale in a firm while the treaty's test is conjunctive, requiring both insufficiency below and improvement above.

(iv) Whether the absence of any analogue to the early-warning mechanism counts against the EU, as my first colleague says, or shows that the analogy has already misdescribed the target — and what the mechanism's existence tells us about the direction of the subsidiary relation, whether the whole exists for the units or the units for the whole.

(v) The same treatment, more briefly, for the household analogy: where it does better, where it fails, and whether it captures anything the delegation analogy cannot.

(vi) Whether either analogy fares differently against the other federations we keep invoking in the same debate. In Germany the Länder administer federal law and their governments vote as instructed units in the Bundesrat; in the United States the states both legislate and execute; in Switzerland the cantons retain everything the constitution does not expressly confer on the Confederation. Say which of these, if any, is genuinely firm-like, and what the variation implies for treating subsidiarity as one principle across systems.

(vii) A verdict: does the efficiency conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if neither analogy carries it, what work is the principle actually doing? If you think a better comparison exists — the allocation of jurisdiction in private international law, the matching of benefit areas to deciding jurisdictions in fiscal federalism, or a default rule of residual competence — say what each gets right and what each still distorts.

Finally, if any of the institutional facts I have recited are wrong or out of date, please flag them rather than build on them: my colleague's argument leans on them, and mine will too.
