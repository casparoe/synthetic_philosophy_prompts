I'm a self-employed electrician, and once a week I host a card game at my shop for a group that includes Ray, who has taught high school physics for thirty years, and my sister Nadia, who works as a pharmacy technician and reads steadily on her own. I've been working through philosophy books by myself in the evenings for a few years, so I normally enjoy these arguments, but last Thursday's one ran close to two hours and ended with both of them asking me to settle it, which I can't. I don't know which of them I agree with, and I've tried below to report the exchange fairly rather than in my own favored order. Part of my difficulty is a fact from my own trade that Ray pulled into the argument: I was trained to think of current as flowing from the positive terminal to the negative, even though the charge carriers in a copper wire actually move the other way, and in twenty years of wiring that has never once led me to a wrong connection. I can't tell whether that fact helps Ray, helps Nadia, or helps neither.

Here is how it went. (1) Nadia opened by saying she has stopped believing that any current scientific claim is literally true, because the history of science is a history of confident theories being thrown out: burning was explained by a substance called phlogiston, heat by a fluid called caloric, light by waves travelling through an all-pervading ether, and every one of those posits is now gone. Her conclusion was that the sensible expectation is that today's best theories will look equally silly in a century, so we should treat them as useful instruments and not as truths. (2) Ray answered that her examples are misleading, because those theories were never discarded wholesale. Combustion still involves oxygen combining with fuel, the quantitative predictions about heat survived, and the mathematics of light waves is still used daily by engineers who don't believe in the ether. What gets dropped, he said, is the part that wasn't doing any real work: the ether was there to make the story hang together, not to generate predictions, and when it went, nothing anyone had actually measured went with it. (3) Nadia said that distinction is only available after the fact. At the time, the failed interferometer experiment was treated as a genuine crisis precisely because people thought the ether was load-bearing; you only learn which parts were load-bearing when the successor theory arrives and tells you. So the ledger Ray wants to keep can't be filled in in advance, which means it can't guide us now. (4) Ray said it can, at least partly: some claims are tied directly to instruments and to things we build, and those are the ones you can identify ahead of time as likeliest to survive. This is where he brought in my trade, and said that the direction of current is a false detail in an otherwise reliable model, and that an electrician can tell which is which without waiting a hundred years. (5) Nadia said my example proves her point instead of his. "Atom" originally meant something that cannot be cut; we found out atoms can be split, and rather than say the old theory was refuted we kept the word and called it roughly right. Whether an old claim counts as surviving or as overthrown depends entirely on how generously you redescribe it, and the same goes for current flow: is the direction a false detail, or did the theory simply never make a claim about it? There's no neutral way to decide, so saying "the successful part always survives" can't be tested. (6) Ray said her list is cherry-picked in the other direction. Germs cause disease, the earth goes around the sun, matter is made of atoms, blood circulates: none of these have been overthrown, and if you count the survivors alongside the casualties, the same induction runs the opposite way, toward science closing in on the truth. (7) Nadia said the survivors only look like survivors because the name stayed put while the content changed underneath it. What "atom" means now would have been nonsense to the man who coined the term, so her claim that today's science will be redescribed rather than believed is exactly what the record shows. (8) Ray said her position is then unlivable: she fills prescriptions all day, and if she really believed modern pharmacology was false in the way phlogiston chemistry was false, she wouldn't hand the drugs to anyone. Nadia said she trusts the drugs completely, because they work, and that working is precisely what doesn't require truth. He said that's a distinction without a difference; she said it is the entire difference. (9) Ray then turned her own method against her: her pessimism is itself an inductive generalization from a handful of past theories, so by her own standard she should expect it to be overthrown, and she has no way to exempt it. (10) Nadia replied that hers is not a claim about how nature is but a claim about the track record of claims about how nature is — a map of failed maps is not itself a map of the territory. Ray said that is just a way of putting her claim beyond the reach of evidence, which is the same complaint she made in move (5), and neither of them would concede that.

Please go through this move by move rather than giving me an overall winner. For each of the ten numbered moves, rate it somewhere on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say briefly why, and where a move is partly right, say which part. Then tell me specifically: whether the distinction Ray is relying on in (2) and (4) between the load-bearing content of a theory and the decorative content can genuinely be drawn in advance, or whether Nadia is correct in (3) that it can only be drawn afterward; whether Ray's charge in (9) that her argument undermines itself actually lands, given her answer in (10), or whether her answer is a legitimate difference in kind rather than an evasion; whether the "we kept the word atom" point in (7) shows that survival claims are unfalsifiable, as (5) alleges, or something weaker; and where my conventional-current example belongs — with Ray, with Nadia, or in neither camp. Finally, say what the two of them are both missing, and give me a practical answer to the question that started it: how much confidence a person in my position should place in what current science says, and whether Nadia's stance — trusting the drugs because they work while denying that their working shows anything true — is a coherent place to stand. I'd rather have your own reasoning than a list of who has written about this; please don't send me sources or reading lists, and don't feel obliged to split the difference if one of them is simply wrong about a given move.
