I worked for five years as a registered nurse on a medical-surgical ward before starting an MA in philosophy, and I am trying to turn the practice that most unsettled me on the job into a seminar paper for a philosophy-of-mind course in which we spent two weeks on the private language argument. The practice is the numeric pain scale. Every patient I admitted was asked for a number between 0 and 10, where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain imaginable; the number was taken at triage, at every set of observations alongside pulse and blood pressure, before and roughly an hour after each dose of analgesia, and at every handover. Scores were banded — mild, moderate, severe — and standing orders were keyed to the bands, so the number determined whether a patient got paracetamol or an opioid. A fall of about two points, or about thirty percent, was the accepted marker of a meaningful response, and it was documented as such in the notes and in the discharge summary. For children there was a faces scale; for patients who could not speak — infants, the intubated, people in advanced dementia — a behavioral observation scale, in which the observer scores breathing, facial expression, body tension, consolability and vocalization and sums them into a 0-to-10 total on the same ruler. And running alongside all of this was the maxim we were taught and audited on: that the patient's self-report is the most reliable indicator of pain, and that pain is whatever the person experiencing it says it is, whenever they say it exists — which is why the chart reads *patient reports 8/10* rather than *pain 8/10*, and why a nurse who privately thought the number was inflated still had to record it and, in most systems, still had to act on it.

What does this whole apparatus presuppose? On one reading, the number is supposed to be the read-out of an inner magnitude that each patient identifies privately and matches to a numeral, in which case I cannot see what makes my 7 the same 7 as yours, or what could make any particular use of the numeral correct rather than merely sincere. On another reading, the numeral gets its entire meaning from its place in the public practice — the thresholds, the re-dosing rules, the comparison against yesterday's charted score, the accompanying grimace and guarding — so that the scale is precisely the shared scaffolding without which talk of pain intensity would have no sense at all. Tell me which of these, if either, the practice actually runs on, and whether the self-report maxim commits the institution to an incorrigible inner gaze or can instead be read as a rule about how the game is played: the avowal is not a report that could be checked and corrected, so writing *patient reports* is a way of recording that no further evidence is admissible. If that deflationary reading is available, say whether it is really the reading the ward operates with, given that nurses plainly do form independent estimates of patients' scores and that our documentation conventions were built to distinguish the reported number from the observed one. I am especially stuck on the apparent split at the center of it: for the speaking patient, the inner state is authoritative and the behavior beside the point, while for the non-speaking patient, the behavior is the only evidence there is — and yet both are supposed to produce a number on one shared scale, anchored at the top by a "worst pain imaginable" that has to mean the same thing to everyone, and evaluated by a response threshold that has to mean the same thing across time within one person. Is that one coherent picture or two incompatible ones bolted together?

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and keep a firm line between what the practice strictly presupposes — what it could not survive without — and what it merely sits comfortably with. Tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined: where a use-theoretic account, a behavioral or dispositional one, a straightforwardly representational one on which the number tracks a magnitude in the patient, and a multidimensional one on which the number is a summary index rather than a measurement at all would each license exactly the same ward routine. Then commit. I am not after a balanced survey that leaves the verdict to me: tell me which account the scale in fact rests on, whether the two halves of the practice are reconcilable, and which parts you think are indefensible as written — and if your conclusion is that the whole thing is innocent, that a public technique needs no inner metaphysics, argue for that rather than offering it as one option among several. Say concretely what would have to change on the ward if the presupposed view were replaced: what would stop being askable, what would have to be charted differently, and what would become of the faces scales and the behavioral observation scales. Flag any point where your reconstruction leans on a contested reading of the private language argument rather than on the practice itself — I would rather you mark those seams than smuggle them in. For what it is worth, I know the campaign to score pain as a fifth vital sign is often blamed for part of the opioid prescribing disaster; I am not asking for that policy history, but if your reconstruction explains why a number of this kind came to be felt as an obligation to treat rather than a description, I want that too.
