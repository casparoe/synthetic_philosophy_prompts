I cover science and ideas for a broadsheet, and I've just filed a long piece about why social scientists still can't agree on whether you explain things at the level of groups or whether you have to come down to individuals. Trouble is I've noticed I've been smugling my own position into the copy, and I want to know whether it hangs together before the subs get at it. Here's what I actually believe:

1. I'm a methodological individualist, roughly in Elster's sense — anything said about "society" has to cash out in what individuals do, believe and want, plus the situations they're in.
2. That said, when I write I treat things like norms, institutional trust or the legitimacy of a body as genuine causes, doing work that individual psychology can't do by itself.
3. I think the best social explanations are mechanism-based: you trace the chain from individual actions up to the outcome, like Coleman's boat diagram.
4. I think Durkheim was basically right about suicide rates — the rate isn't derivable from individual motives, and that's a real argument for explanation at the social level.
5. I don't buy rational choice theory as a predictive tool and I'd never defend homo economicus.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix costs. Then tell me which single revision would clear up most of the mess — and what I'd have to stop saying in my own prose if I made it.
