I cover psychology, neuroscience, and philosophy for a national magazine, and I am midway through a reported feature on the institutional management of resentment: de-escalation protocols in psychiatric emergency departments, compassion-fatigue curricula in oncology wards, the neutrality training that restorative-justice facilitators complete before they sit in a circle with someone who has destroyed a family. The philosophical spine of the piece is Strawson's 1962 essay 'Freedom and Resentment', and my editor has asked me not to paraphrase it but to give readers a single case in which the machinery is visibly at work — or visibly breaks down. I would like you to construct that case to specification, and I would ask you to treat each specification as binding rather than advisory. No references or citations are needed; I will handle attribution myself.

Here are the constraints. The scenario should run to roughly one paragraph, in a realistic contemporary institutional setting, with no fictional neuroscience, no imaging results, no children, no florid psychosis, and no legally cognizable duress. First: Strawson's own criteria must license the objective attitude toward the wrongdoer — one of the suspending or exempting conditions he catalogues, such as a recent and relevant emotional shock, unusual stress or strain, compulsion, conditioning, intoxication, or moral underdevelopment, must genuinely apply, and a conscientious Strawsonian reading the case must be forced to concede that it does, not merely that it looks as though it does. Second: the reader should nevertheless come away thinking that treating this person as exempt is a moral loss rather than a liberation, and that the exemption is being claimed because it suits the institution — cheaper than conflict, tidier than grievance — rather than because it is the truth about the agent. Third: the cost must land on an identifiable, named person who is not the wrongdoer, someone whose resentment is officially ruled out of order; and there must be a second, smaller cost that lands on the wrongdoer himself, of a kind that Strawson's framework has no obvious vocabulary for. My own half-formed candidate involves a recently widowed department head, six months out, who humiliates a junior colleague in a staff meeting, and a human-resources director who instructs the team that the man is not presently a person anyone should expect anything of. Please diagnose that seed honestly against the specifications: if it fails one or more of them, say precisely which and why, and build a better case; if it survives, tell me what is load-bearing in it and refine it rather than replacing it.

After the scenario, show me the work. State which of Strawson's conditions you are invoking and defend its application. Then say what the case is a counterexample to — that exemption tracks the capacities of the agent, that the objective attitude is always an appropriate response to such capacities, that the participant and objective stances exhaust our options, or something narrower — pick one, argue for it, and explain why the alternatives are the wrong target. Identify the single feature of the case doing the work, and tell me what happens if I delete it: does the case collapse into an ordinary exemption, or into a straightforward complaint about a bad employer? Sketch how a Strawsonian would redraft the framework to absorb the case while keeping the distinction intact, and say whether that redraft concedes my thesis or defeats it. Finally, because this is destined for a general-interest magazine, flag every step of the analysis that depends on terminology I cannot use — objective attitude, participant reactive attitudes, exempting conditions — and offer a plain-language substitute for each, or tell me plainly that the case does not survive translation into ordinary prose and where the loss occurs.
