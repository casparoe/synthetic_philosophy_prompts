Last night my friend — he does research for a grantmaking body that funds across several very different cause areas — and I argued until past one in the morning about whether compromise between different value systems generates genuine gains from trade, and we ended up exactly where we started, which is the fourth time in two years. The shape of the argument is this. His side runs what I would call the moral trade argument: where two parties with different values each control resources and each ranks outcomes by its own lights, a deal in which each funds part of the other's project, or agrees not to obstruct it, can leave both better off by their own measure than mutual non-cooperation, for reasons structurally like the ones that make specialization and exchange productive in ordinary trade theory — the gain does not come from anyone changing their mind or from splitting the difference on a shared question, but from the parties' claims falling on different margins. He insists, and I think correctly, that the two parties need not agree about anything substantive for this to happen. My side — well, I keep switching sides, which is part of why the argument never ends — has three objections that he says are three different confusions. First, that trading a moral claim treats it as a preference, and a duty or a prohibition is not the sort of thing that has a price. Second, that even where the arithmetic works out, entering the deal makes you a participant in what you take to be a serious wrong, so the gain is bought with your complicity. Third, that saying both parties are better off already presupposes cardinal comparability across value systems, and if you grant that much, the trade picture has conceded the interesting question rather than answered it.

His response to the third objection is the one I find hardest to answer, and it is where I suspect the whole stalemate lives. He says: fine, grant me comparability as a hypothetical — take two parties who already measure outcomes on a scale they agree about, say two utilitarians with different weightings over the same goods — and the gains-from-trade result still goes through, with no controversial assumption about comparing incommensurable values. I said that at that point there is no compromise between value systems at all, only a coordination problem inside a single one, and he said that was precisely the point: the cases that matter are the ones where the structure is that of a trade, and whether you also call the underlying values one system or two is doing no work. Then we noticed something neither of us could explain. We both agreed about a narrow case I will call asymmetric abstention, in which neither party has to do anything the other considers wrong and each merely declines to press a claim it could have pressed; we agreed the deal is available and unobjectionable there, and we still disagreed about whether that case counts as moral trade or as something else entirely. Agreement on the case, disagreement on the description, no movement on the principle. I keep thinking that pattern is diagnostic, but I do not know of what.

So here is what I actually want, and it is not a verdict on who is right, and it is definitely not a survey of the field — I have read enough of it, and I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind, since I have no way of checking them tonight and invented references would be worse than none. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is it verbal, in the sense that gains from trade is being used sometimes for the existence of an improvement each party recognizes and sometimes for the permissibility or advisability of pursuing it, with each side attacking a claim the other is not making? Is it substantive but located somewhere neither of us has been looking — for instance in what a moral claim is, a weight that can be traded off against other weights versus a constraint that cannot be weighed at all, with all the trade talk a surface manifestation of that deeper rift? Is the complicity objection secretly an empirical or dynamic claim, about how permitting trade erodes one's standing to object or licenses bad-faith deals over time, that could in principle be settled by evidence rather than argument? Or is the question ill-posed, such that no progress should be expected? Work through what each side actually says in the case above rather than in the abstract: I want the diagnosis to have to deal with the complicity objection and the duties-are-not-preferences objection specifically, and to explain why the two-utilitarians move either rescues or collapses the argument. And tell me what would count as progress given whatever diagnosis you land on — a distinction, a reframing, a formal result, a case both sides are committed to accepting, or nothing at all, with reasons. Please write in American English.
