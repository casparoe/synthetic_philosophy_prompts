Every autumn the blogs I have read for twenty years rediscover Keynes's 1930 essay on his grandchildren's economic possibilities — a four- to eightfold rise in living standards within a century, fifteen hours a week of work sufficient, and the problem left over not how to make a living but how to occupy the leisure that science and compound interest have delivered. The threads invariably argue about whether the forecast came true. I want the conceptual difficulty underneath it instead. Keynes expects abundance to be enjoyed by those who have kept alive the art of life and do not sell themselves for the means of life — but that reads like a habit formed under, and perhaps dependent upon, the very struggle it is supposed to survive. Call this the bootstrapping worry.

Give me an example: a concrete life, invented is fine, particular enough that I can see how the person spends a week and, more important, where the capacity came from — someone who genuinely has the art of life in Keynes's sense. Then answer whether that capacity requires the economic problem to remain live, for them or for the people around them. If it does, what becomes of the picture when abundance arrives for everyone at once and no stratum is left under scarcity to cultivate the art and pass it on — and is the early ethics he criticized in My Early Beliefs (states of mind, personal affections, aesthetic enjoyment, a repudiation of general rules of duty) a resource against the worry or a symptom of it?
