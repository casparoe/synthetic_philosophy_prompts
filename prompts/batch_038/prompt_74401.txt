Thirty-five years I ran an analytical chemistry lab, been retired four now, and I've fallen into philosophy roughly the way other retired people fall into golf. Current obsession is Grice and implicature. My problem with it, stated the way I'd state it if it were an instrument:

The cancellability test. If a bit of meaning survives being explicitly denied without the result turning into contradiction, you call it pragmatic; if denying it wrecks the utterance, you call it semantic, part of what was literally said. In my old life I would never have signed off on a diagnostic like that without knowing its false-positive and false-negative rates. I'm pretty sure it has both, and I don't think anyone has ever bothered to characterize them.

So I want your actual credence, a number, in this:

"For any proposition Q that a competent hearer reliably takes away from a sincere utterance of P, where Q is not entailed by P: if appending an explicit denial of Q to the utterance produces contradiction or incoherence rather than mere oddness, then Q is part of the literal semantic content of P."

That is, the test never misfires in the "it's semantic" direction. I'm not asking whether the test is useful, or whether Grice was broadly right — just this one-way reliability claim.

Now decompose it the way I'd want a measurement decomposed. Here are the knobs I can see, add or throw out as you like:

- how often "contradiction" versus "that just sounds weird" is really a clean binary — I suspect its graded all the way down, in which case the test is reading a dial with no markings
- whether the context-sensitivity crowd are right that nearly all semantic content is hostage to context, which would make the test unfalsifiable rather than wrong, and I want to know if you count that as helping my proposition or killing it
- whether so-called conventional implicature is a coherent third category that breaks the binary the test assumes, and if so how often it actually shows up in ordinary talk versus in philosophers' toy sentences
- whether the test is being applied to sentences or to utterances-in-a-context, and whether those give different answers

Give each a rough probability and one line of why, then show me how they combine into the final number.

Then tell me which knob the number is most sensitive to and what would move it — I want an argument or a thought experiment, not a pointer to something I should go look up. And if you think I've mis-stated the proposition so that it comes out trivially true or trivially false, say so, fix it into the strongest non-trivial version you can, and give me your number for that one instead.
