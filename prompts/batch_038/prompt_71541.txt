I'm in a reading group on self-deception and we keep going in circles, so I want to settle one thing concretely: can the strict version be built without cheating? Can you construct me a case to these specs?

- The person has to genuinely intend to get themselves to believe something false. Not drift into it because they'd prefer it to be true — if all that's happening is motivated evidence-gathering, the case fails for my purposes.
- No little people. Nothing like a seperate unconscious module with its own beliefs and desires, no censor, no "part of me knows". If the mind gets split, split it along a line you can defend without handing either side its own psychology.
- It has to dodge both of the usual puzzles: how they can form the intention at all without the intention itself giving away the truth they're hiding, and how they end up both believing the thing and not.
- Ordinary life. A job, a marriage, a body, a kid. No hypnosis, no drugs, no head injuries, no sci-fi memory tech.
- The deception lands — by the end they sincerely believe it, and would say so on the stand.
- One or two paragraphs for the scenario, no more.

The point of the exercise is to test whether strict self-deception actually requires a homunculus, so please don't just stipulate that the person both knows and doesn't know — I want to see the machinery turning. After the scenario, do the check: state exactly what the person intends and in what terms they can intend it without representing the truth to themselves; say where the split in the case lives and what kind of split it is; and walk through how the two belief-states coexist without contradicting. Then tell me which single feature is doing the heavy lifting, what breaks if I delete it, and how someone who thinks self-deception is nothing over and above biased belief formation would redescribe your case as a plain old preference-driven error. What would you say back to them, and is there any version of the case that survives that redescription?
