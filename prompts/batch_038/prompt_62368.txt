I run a tiny philosophy-of-psychiatry podcast and next month's guest is a philosopher who's spent her career extending Hacking's looping-effects picture. Her twist: a classification only really loops when it hands people first-person vocabulary for their own experience, so purely administrative or behavioral labels loop weakly or not at all. On top of that she's lately been argueing we're in a period she calls loop exhaustion — the big DSM categories have stopped generating new kinds of person because everyone already has them, so the loop still runs but nothing new comes out the other side, and its not clear what observation would show otherwise.

Write me eight numbered questions, no softballs, that engage that machinery instead of asking her to explain looping effects again. At minimum: one on the self-hiding problem — if a category loops perfectly it becomes invisible, so how does she tell an exhausted loop from a wildly successful one; one on falsifiability, i.e. what finding would actually kill loop exhaustion; one on transmission channels (clinicians, patients, insurers, the symptom-checkers and comment threads people diagnose themselves with) and whether divergent selves produced under the same label count as one loop or several; and one on what looping does to prevalence estimates and to trials, where the measuring instrument is being rewritten by the thing it measures. For each question add the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, plus one clause saying what she'd have to concede for that follow-up to land.

Last line should begin "Verdict:" and give just the number of the single question she can't get through without giving something up, followed by a reason of five words or fewer.
