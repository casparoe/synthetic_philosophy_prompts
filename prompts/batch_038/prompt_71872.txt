ok so my friend and I have been going in circles on the is-taste-objective thing, and both of us keep reaching for Hume's true judges as the tiebreaker and then quietly assuming our own side is the true judges. I'd rather stress-test the machinery on a real case than keep trading thought experiments, so: Paris, 1863. The Salon jury turns down something like two thirds of the submissions, the rejected artists complain loudly enough that Napoleon III has their work hung in another part of the building so the public can judge whether the complaints were fair, and Manet's picnic with the naked woman and the two dressed men becomes the thing everybody showed up to laugh at. Two decades on Manet is cannonical and the jury's taste is a punchline.

Run that through Hume's essay properly. First the mapping: who in 1863 is even a candidate true judge -- the jury, the newspaper critics, the laughing crowd, Manet's fellow painters, Zola -- and which of his five qualifications each one actually had and which they lacked. Then the fight we're having: he says the jury's disgust is precisely what practice and endless comparison produce in people steeped in the tradition, so it isn't prejudice and Hume has to side against Manet; I say a sensibility that reliably rejects what later ages canonize is prejudice wearing a qualification. Who can make that stick without special pleading? And what's the procedure for a critic standing in that room in 1863, given that Hume's evidence for a standard -- durable admiration across countries and centuries -- is only visible from the future? Finally, be honest about where the case hurts him: if the consensus on Manet got built by dealers, museums and a few very loud critics rather than by qualified perception, is the standard doing any work at all, or is it just a name we give to whatever won?
