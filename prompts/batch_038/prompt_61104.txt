I write literary fiction, and the essay on craft I have been drafting for four months has turned, without my noticing the turn, into an indictment of craft. My editor tells me, gently, that I cannot publish an argument whose conclusion is that I do wrong to my readers several hundred times per book. I cannot find the flaw myself. It is possible there is no flaw and I must simply swallow the conclusion; but before I do, I want the thing taken apart joint by joint.

Two stipulations first, since the argument depends on them and I do not want answers to a different question.

By a technique I mean any controllable feature of a work — the order in which information is released, whose perceptions the prose is filtered through, the length of the sentences, where a chapter breaks, what is omitted — that a writer chooses in order to produce a specified effect in a reader.

By non-recognition I mean the reader's failure to register, at the moment of uptake, that the feature is operating on her as a technique. A reader who is being carried along does not think about the withheld information; she thinks about the character.

The argument:

(1) A communicator manipulates her audience rather than persuading it just in case she deploys a technique whose efficacy depends on the audience's non-recognition of it.

(2) If the efficacy of a technique depends on non-recognition, then recognition defeats it: a reader who sees the machinery at work is no longer moved by it.

(3) What recognition would defeat, the reader cannot assess. What she cannot assess, she cannot consent to.

(4) What she cannot consent to, she cannot be persuaded by; it can only be done to her.

(5) Therefore every deployment of such a technique is manipulation and not persuasion.

(6) The techniques that do the principal work in a novel are of this kind. Suspense requires that the reader not register the withholding as a withholding. Sympathy produced by filtering the prose through a character's perceptions requires that she not register the filter as a bias. The force of a chapter's last sentence depends on the chapter's having ended there, and on her not registering the placement as a placement.

(7) Therefore a novel manipulates its reader continuously, and persuades her nowhere, at every point where it succeeds.

(8) To work upon a person in a way she cannot consent to is to use her as a means only, and that wrongs her.

(9) Therefore I wrong my readers continuously, and in proportion to my skill: the better the book, the larger the portion of it I have to answer for.

What makes me distrust the argument rather than accept it is that it does not stop with me. Substitute for (6) any case in which an effect depends on the audience's not tracking the machinery — the timing of a punchline, the ordering of chapters in a work of history, the placement of a photograph on a page, an image in a scientific paper that makes an abstraction stick, the sequence in which a physician mentions two treatments, a confession arranged so that the apology arrives before the offense — and (1) through (5) convict all of them, and (8) makes them all wrongs. Jokes become a species of trespass; so does teaching. I cannot believe that, and I do not think I am merely flinching from a conclusion I dislike, because the generalization outruns anything I actually hold about manipulation.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look sound when I wrote it down? Identify what the step equivocates on, or what reading of it the neighbouring steps require but do not supply. If instead you judge the argument valid, with the only question being which plausible premise is cheapest to surrender, say that plainly and price the options. Three exits in particular:

- If (1) must go, what replaces non-recognition as the mark that separates manipulation from persuasion? Does the replacement still capture the cases I am certain about — the memoir whose events did not occur, the urgency manufactured purely in order to be exploited, the appeal that shows a particular child's face at the precise moment it asks for card details? If it captures them only incidentally, tell me so.
- If (3) or (4) must go, what sort of consent could cover what (6) describes, and what prevents that consent from being so blank that it covers everything and therefore licenses nothing?
- If (8) must go, what is required beyond influence the recipient cannot consent to in order for someone to be used as a means only — and is that further ingredient present in the cases where I am confident manipulation genuinely occurs?

Two constraints on the repair. I want to keep (6): it is a straightforward description of what I do, and I am not willing to redescribe my craft as something more scrupulous than it is. I also want to keep whatever in (1) makes the fabricated memoir and the manufactured urgency manipulative rather than merely forceful. Tell me whether those two wants are jointly satisfiable, or whether holding both is itself what generates the trouble. And if your repair depends on drawing a new distinction, do not merely name it: tell me where it bites, where it fails to bite, and what it costs me on the day a reader notices exactly what I am doing and is moved anyway.
