I am preparing a graduate seminar session on decision problems with imperfect recall, and before I draft the handout I want to pin down where I actually stand on the absent-minded driver problem. I will state the setup in full so that nothing in your answer depends on recalling the literature, and I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or any history of who argued what — I will supply that myself.

**The setup.** A driver is heading home along a road with two intersections that are indistinguishable to him. His memory is such that when he arrives at one of them he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, and he retains nothing of any decision he made at the previous one. Exiting at the first intersection leaves him stranded in a bad part of town: payoff 0. Exiting at the second gets him home: payoff 4. Driving past both strands him in the middle of nowhere: payoff 1. He can commit to nothing in advance; all he has is a behavioral rule — at whatever intersection he finds himself at, he continues with probability p and exits with probability 1−p — and because he cannot carry information from one intersection to the next, the two randomizations are independent.

Two things then follow, and they are in tension. Ex ante, his expected payoff is 4p(1−p) + p², maximized at p = 2/3, worth 4/3. But at the intersection, if he assigns credence 1/2 to being at the first and 1/2 to being at the second, then at p = 2/3 exiting has expected value 2 while continuing has expected value 3/2, so exiting looks strictly better; and the same 1/2–1/2 reasoning is unstable in the other direction too, since if his plan were to exit always (p = 0), it would tell him at the node to continue instead (2.5 against 2).

**The response I find myself drawn to**, which I will call the fixed-point response. The 1/2–1/2 credence is not one the driver is entitled to. His only route to an opinion about which intersection he is at runs through his own plan: he reaches the first intersection with certainty and the second with probability p, so conditional on being at an intersection his credences should be 1/(1+p) for the first and p/(1+p) for the second. At p = 2/3 that is 3/5 and 2/5, and exiting and continuing each come out at 8/5 — exactly indifferent, which is what one should expect at an interior optimum. Nor is this a knife-edge artifact of the particular numbers. If the payoff for passing both intersections is q rather than 1, then ex ante optimality requires p = 2/(4−q), and indifference at the node under the self-consistent credence requires the very same p, for every q for which the ex ante optimum is interior. I have done both derivations by hand; if you think either is wrong, say so before answering the rest.

**The proposition I want a number on.** Call it P: the fixed-point response fully resolves the absent-minded driver problem. Unpacked: p = 2/3 is the correct action-guiding verdict for the driver once he is at the intersection, not merely the correct ex ante plan; the case poses no genuine difficulty for ordinary Bayesian expected-utility decision theory; and every argument for a different verdict at the node rests either on a credence assignment the fixed-point response rightly rejects or on conflating the value of a plan with the value of an act at a node. What would count as P's being false: an argument, or a variant of the case, on which a rational driver at the intersection is required — or is clearly better off — doing something other than continuing with probability 2/3, where the argument does not depend on a credence the fixed-point response deems illegitimate.
