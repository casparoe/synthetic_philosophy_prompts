I am the teaching assistant for an upper-level metaphysics seminar, and I am responsible for marking the take-home portion of our midterm. The question was issued to the students in the following form.

**Question.** The principle of the identity of indiscernibles (PII) states that if two things are distinct, there is some property that one has and the other lacks. Consider a universe containing exactly two iron spheres, *a* and *b*, alike in every intrinsic respect and lying exactly two metres apart. Every qualitative feature of the situation appears to be shared: the spheres are numerically two but qualitatively one, and PII seems to fail.

Now consider the following rescue. Call two objects **weakly discernible** when some irreflexive and symmetric relation holds between them. "Lies exactly two metres from" is irreflexive (neither sphere lies two metres from itself) and symmetric (if *a* lies two metres from *b*, then *b* lies two metres from *a*), and it holds between *a* and *b*; so *a* and *b* are weakly discernible, and PII is not violated. Call two objects **strongly discernible**, by contrast, when there is some property that one has and the other lacks, where that property is expressible without using names or other referring expressions that pick out either object individually.

(a) Explain what the weak-discernibility rescue is meant to establish, and say whether it establishes it.
(b) Say whether the rescue begs the question against the two-sphere case, and defend your answer.
(c) Suppose instead that the universe also contains a single cube, exactly three metres from *a* and exactly five metres from *b*, with everything else unchanged. Does the modified universe strengthen or weaken the rescue, and what does that imply about the role symmetry plays in the original case?

**Student answer.**

PII says that if two things are distinct, there must be some property one has and the other lacks; its converse, the indiscernibility of identicals, says that if two things are identical they share all their properties. Only the first is at issue here, since no one is disputing that *a* and *b* are each self-identical. Black's two-sphere universe is meant as a counterexample: *a* and *b* are distinct but share every qualitative property, so PII is false.

The rescue claims that PII survives because *a* and *b* are weakly discernible. I think that claim is correct, though not for the reason usually given. The relation "lies exactly two metres from" holds between them, and it does so asymmetrically, in the sense that *a* bears it to *b* while *b* does not bear it to itself. But more is available than that: *a* has the property of lying two metres from *b*, and *b* lacks that property, since what *b* has instead is the property of lying two metres from *a*. These are different properties, because their definitions contain different relata, so the spheres are not merely weakly but strongly discernible on the definition given in the question. Weak discernibility is therefore not a special third category; it is just strong discernibility described from a distance. Once this is seen, the two-sphere universe was never a counterexample to PII, because the spheres were discernible by relational properties all along.

On (b), the rescue does not beg the question, since it appeals only to the distance relation, which the description of the universe grants us: the spheres are stipulated to be two metres apart, so the rescuer uses nothing that Black's opponent is not already entitled to. The objection that the rescuer must have the two spheres in hand before applying the relation proves too much, because the same objection would apply to Black's own claim that there are two of them. One might instead hold that the spheres differ by primitive thisness, but that is not needed: the distance relation already does the work.

On (c), the cube makes the situation clearer rather than different. With the cube present, *a* is three metres from it and *b* is five, so the spheres are strongly discernible without any appeal to the two-metre relation. This shows that the rescue never depended on weak discernibility at all; it depended on there being something in the universe to which the spheres are differently related. But the same holds in the original case, since the spheres are differently related to one another. The modified universe therefore adds nothing except a more convenient marker, and it follows that any universe containing two objects contains some asymmetric feature. Symmetry is never doing real work in these cases, and Black's example is best read as one in which the stipulations were not carried out carefully.
