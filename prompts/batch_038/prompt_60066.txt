I teach a ten-week upper-level undergraduate seminar on conditionals, and in week six I have fourteen students for a single hundred-minute session. They have had one term of probability: they are at home with conditional probability, independence, the law of total probability, and updating by conditionalization, but they have had no measure theory and no possible-worlds semantics. Almost none of them have encountered Lewis's triviality results.

Design a structured in-class activity that walks them into the triviality squeeze from the inside, so that they meet it as a failure of their own bookkeeping rather than as a theorem announced from the front of the room. The target experience: the students try to treat an indicative conditional as the sort of thing that has a probability — operationalized as a price they are prepared to post for a bet — while simultaneously holding that price equal to the corresponding conditional probability, and they run into what holding both costs. Their conditional bets should collapse, and the antecedent should stop doing any work, before anyone names what has happened.

Hard constraints on the design:

1. Integer arithmetic only. Every price must be a whole number of tokens out of a fixed pot, and the space of worlds must be small enough — I suggest eight — that nobody reaches for a calculator. The three propositions involved should be physically represented on cards or chips that students can sort and stack by hand.
2. No labels before the collision. The result, its author, and the phrase naming the identification must not surface until the debrief; the students should not know what they are supposed to be discovering.
3. At least one round must involve the group learning new information and being required to re-post its prices, since the squeeze depends on the pricing rule surviving conditionalization rather than holding at a single point.
4. The stakes must be trivial and slightly comic rather than monetary, but there must be a genuine decision point at which a group chooses between abandoning the identification and keeping it while conceding that the bets it is posting are worthless.

Alongside the activity, give me: the rule set exactly as I would hand it to students (one page, no notation beyond what they have already met); a minute-by-minute timeline; a cover story for the worlds in which the antecedent intuitively matters to the consequent, so that its elimination stings; a debrief that maps each stage of the game onto the corresponding step of the derivation, identifying precisely where the total-probability expansion bites and where the assumption about conditionalization does the real work; and the two or three escape routes available once the squeeze is visible, each tied to the specific move in the game that taking it amounts to refusing. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list — a name and a one-line label is all I need for any response you mention.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. I expect someone to deny the identification within five minutes, and I expect arithmetic drift in the middle rounds; say how to keep the game alive when either happens. Say also whether the exercise is worth running at all for students who have met the result before. And give me an honest account of which feature of the theorem the activity cannot reproduce: my suspicion is that any finite set of posted prices can be patched up by hand, whereas the result ranges over an entire family of probability functions closed under conditionalization. Tell me whether that gap is fatal to the exercise, or whether a facilitator can close it in the debrief, and if so, how.
