I have been invited to submit a proposal for a 4,000-word chapter in an edited volume on the philosophy of commitment, and I want the chapter to turn on a question that philosophers and lawyers alike tend to assume away rather than argue about. The question is whether a promise binds only insofar as the promisor retains the genuine possibility of breaking it.

My material is three settings in which the possibility of breach has been deliberately engineered out of the arrangement. First, an escrowed smart contract that transfers the funds automatically if the deliverable is not logged by a given date, so that the obligor cannot withhold performance even if they come to want to. Second, a psychiatric advance directive in which a patient, while well, consents to compulsory treatment during a foreseeable future episode and asks the clinical team to hold them to that consent over their own later refusal. Third, a deterrent posture designed so that retaliation is triggered without any individual retaining the discretion to call it off. In each case the point of the device is to make one's word good; my suspicion is that in doing so it may destroy the feature that made the word a promise rather than a mechanism, leaving the other party with a reliable process instead of an obligor.

Please turn this into a chapter proposal that does the following.

1. State the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer rather than a smear of readings. In particular, separate a conceptual version (something ceases to be a promise once breach is impossible), a normative version (it remains a promise, but its binding force is weakened or transformed), and an evidential version (the obligation is untouched and the device merely changes what the promisee has to go on). Say which version the chapter should take up, why, and what turns on the choice.

2. Explain why the standard families of account of promissory obligation leave the question open instead of settling it. My hunch is that they pull in different directions here: an assurance-based or second-personal account seems to require the promisor's ongoing exercise of will, a practice- or convention-based account seems indifferent to it, and an expectation-based account seems to be strengthened by the removal of the option to breach. Assess whether that asymmetry is the real content of my question or a distraction from it, and say so plainly if it is a distraction.

3. Lay out the approach and defend it against the obvious alternative, which is to treat all three settings as instances of precommitment and settle the matter with the game-theoretic analysis of credible commitment. Say what that analysis cannot register, if anything, and what conceptual work is left for the chapter to do.

4. Address how the question interacts with the legal view that a contracting party is at liberty to breach provided they compensate: does that view already concede that bindingness does not require the impossibility of breach, or does it instead show that the duty created by a contract is not the obligation created by a promise?

5. Describe what a successful result would look like, including whether a single uniform verdict across all three settings would count as success or as evidence that the question was ill-posed, and specify what would count as the chapter failing.

6. Give an honest assessment of scope. At 4,000 words, which of the three settings should be cut, and what is lost by cutting it?

Do not include a bibliography or a list of works cited; I will supply references myself. Referring to positions and arguments by their content rather than by author and title is exactly what I want.
