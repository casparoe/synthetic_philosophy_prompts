Help me settle an argument with a colleague before next week's seminar. I'm running a third-year option on the philosophy of forecasting and we've walked straight into the reference class problem — the Hájek version, so not merely a headache for frequentists: any single case sits in indefinitely many classes, and the base rate swings wildly depending on which one you grab. It's basically Hume with a spreadsheet.

Our running case is deliberately unglamorous: how much longer the European Union lasts. But I've nailed down the failure criterion so nobody can slide around it. For our purposes the EU counts as having ceased to exist when either fewer than fifteen member states remain bound by the treaties, or the Court of Justice of the EU has lost jurisdiction over the territory of at least a third of the current members. Treaties renegotiated, competences clawed back, a state or two walking out — none of that counts. Only that.

I then put this list of candidate reference classes up on the board and asked the students to rank them. Do the same for me, sorting them into tiers from "I would stake a real forecast on this base rate" down to "rhetoric dressed as data":

1. Gott's Copernican argument — ignore all content and let the EU's age so far do the predicting.
2. Ibn Khaldun's dynastic cycle, treating the Union as a dynasty whose asabiyyah thins out over roughly three generations.
3. Taagepera's growth–decline curves — duration read off maximum territorial extent.
4. Turchin-style secular cycles / structural-demographic modelling: elite overproduction, popular immiseration, state fiscal strain.
5. Composite or supranational unions voluntarily constituted since 1815 — the German Confederation, Austria-Hungary, the Scandinavian Monetary Union, the Swiss federal state of 1848, Yugoslavia, Czechoslovakia, the USSR, the United Arab Republic, the West Indies Federation, the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland — survivors and non-survivors alike.
6. Weak-centre, treaty-constituted polities with legal pluralism and no territorial sovereignty of their own: the Hanseatic League, the Holy Roman Empire, the Dutch confederation, the old Swiss Eidgenossenschaft, that sort of thing.
7. Spengler/Toynbee morphological cycles of civilisations.
8. The inclusive-versus-extractive institutional account in the Acemoglu–Robinson vein.

Two constraints I'm imposing, and I'd like you to work inside them rather then argue with them. First, what I've been calling the conquest filter: when you compute a base rate from any class, drop the members whose end came from outright external military destruction, because our stipulated failure mode is internal — a polity that was simply overrun tells you nothing here. Second, no hedging by averaging everything into one number up front; the whole point is to see whether the ranking holds together once you apply the filter.

So: one sentence per item on why it landed where it did, and then — separately — the single probability you'd actually defend that our criterion is met within the next fifty years. A number, plus a sentence naming which class it came from. Not a range of ranges.

Oh, and please write the whole thing in British English; the students will be reading it and half of them have started mirroring my spelling anyway.
