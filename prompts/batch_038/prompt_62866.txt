I write the case scenarios for the ethics sessions that all our nursing and junior medical staff have to do at an acute trust — about 3,000 people, six new scenarios a quarter, each one run in a 45 minute ward-based session by a facilitator who usually isn't a philosopher and hasn't had much training. Sign-off is a panel that meets for an hour: a matron (final say, and she wants a yes or a no, not an essay), a consultant, me, and a philosophy lecturer from the local uni who does a couple of hours a month.

What I want is a vetting procedure my panel can run on a draft scenario in about fifteen minutes, grounded in whatever is actually known about how thought experiments persuade people and lead them astray — not a generic "is this a good teaching example" checklist. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a decent answer to each, which questions can end the thing right there with a reject, and which ones the procedure can't settle, where it has to stop and say this is a judgement for the matron rather than a test anyone can pass.

We already have two house rules I invented and I'd like you to either build them in properly or tell me they're no good. First, the flip test: if changing one detail — a name, a number, who's doing the acting — reliably changes the answer people give, then that detail is doing hidden work, and either the hidden work gets made explicit in the debrief or the scenario gets dropped. Second, nothing ships alone: every scenario goes out with its nearest twin, the version that points the other way. Problem is we can't survey staff to find out whether a detail is load-bearing, we haven't the time or the approval for it, so the flip test mostly gets run by four people arguing in a room. Tell me what to do when we can't settle that.

One more thing: some of the movement is deliberate. We're not trying to be neutral about everything, so the procedure can't just amount to "don't nudge anyone". It has to tell the difference between moving people on purpose about something we're prepared to defend in the debrief, and moving them by an accident of wording.

Then run it on three drafts:

1. A nurse can stay twenty minutes past the end of her shift to sit with a dying patient who has no family, or go and help a colleague finish an incident report due by midnight. The family turn up fourty minutes later and say they wish somebody had been with him.
2. A band 5 nurse is asked by a consultant, Mr Ashworth, to give 30mg of a drug she thinks is the wrong dose. He says he's done it a hundred times and it's fine.
3. This one is my favourite and it always kicks off a proper argument: the last dose of a drug can go to a 34 year old mother of two who is herself a nurse, or to a 78 year old man who has made his peace with things.

Flag where the panel is really making a values call in a test-shaped costume, and tell me what the procedure can't catch at all — I suspect it's the scenarios that pass on the page and then go wrong in the room.
