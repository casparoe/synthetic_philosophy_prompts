I retired four years ago after thirty-three years in a physiology department, the last two decades of them spent largely on proprioceptive afferents and the vestibular reflexes, and I have taken up philosophy since with the habits I had in the laboratory: I want to know what a claim risks, and how it could be shown wrong. For a year now I have been reading the Arabic tradition with a small Tuesday group, and I have arrived at a definite view about Avicenna's flying man which I should like you to attack as hard as you can. The view is this. The scenario — a man created complete and adult all at once, suspended in air, eyes veiled, limbs kept from touching one another, who in one standard English rendering of the passage would affirm his own existence "although not affirming the reality of any of his limbs or inner organs, his bowels, or heart or brain or any external thing" — is not a poetic illustration but a sound argument. From the fact that he would affirm himself while affirming none of his organs, the brain expressly included, it follows that the self's awareness of itself is not the operation of any bodily organ, and thence that the soul is neither a body, nor a faculty seated in a body, nor a temperament or proportion of the body's mixture. I hold further that the familiar physicalist rejoinders do not touch it: appeals to interoceptive signalling, to the brain's internal body-model, or to what people actually report from flotation tanks and from total deafferentation are all claims about what such a man would experience, whereas the premise Avicenna needs concerns the givenness of the subject to itself, which is not itself an item of experience and therefore cannot be a state of an organ. I note in passing that the list in the passage includes the brain, so the reply that the suspended man would in fact be aware of his own cerebral states strikes me as either an empirical claim nobody has made good on, or a confusion between being the organ by which one is aware and being given in awareness.

What I want is the exposure map of that position, sorted from what would be fatal to what would be merely awkward, with reasons at each step rather than labels. Begin with the inference itself: does the man's failure to affirm his organs license the conclusion that the self is not those organs, or does it move illicitly from separability in thought to separability in fact — and what would each reading require, the stronger claim that self and body can exist apart, or only the weaker claim that one can conceive the self without conceiving any body? A member of my group insists the passage is not offered as a demonstration at all but as a kind of alerting device, a prompt to notice something already true of oneself rather than a proof of anything; tell me how damaging that reading would be to my claim, and whether it can be met without conceding that the flying man proves nothing. Then take up internal consistency: Avicenna's own psychology assigns the internal faculties bodily seats in the cavities of the brain, treats the soul as the body's form and perfection, an instrument-using principle that comes into existence with the body and is individuated through it — is my immaterialist reading compatible with that apparatus, and if not, which side of the tension a defender of the view should give up? Ask also whether the argument reaches as far as I want it to: does it establish an individual substance capable of existing apart from the body, or only a bare first-personal givenness that is quite consistent with being a highly integrated, self-monitoring physical process?

On the empirical side I would like you to speak to someone who spent a career measuring the very systems my opponents invoke. Which findings would genuinely bear on the claim that self-awareness is unmediated by an organ — not the reports of what deprivation subjects say, which I have already discounted, but such things as conditions in which first-personal givenness appears absent or structurally altered while the organism plainly persists, anesthesia, profound depersonalization, and the loss of the sense of owning one's body; and is there any experiment in principle, however far beyond present technique, that could falsify the claim, or does the content-versus-givenness distinction I lean on make my position compatible with every possible result? If the latter, say so plainly and tell me whether that is a defect for a view of this kind or whether it belongs with conceptual, mathematical, and transcendental claims, for which empirical unfalsifiability is no indictment at all; and if it is a defect, tell me exactly what I would have to give up for the view to risk something. I also want to know which objections a committed Avicennian would himself concede as counting against him, as distinct from those he would wave away, and whether the sharpest of the tradition's own internal criticisms of the argument — describe the objection rather than merely crediting it to someone — cuts deeper than anything a contemporary physicalist has managed. Close with an honest verdict on how exposed I actually am, and with the single result or argument that, if it came to my attention, ought to move me most. Please argue with me directly; I have no use for a reading list, for historical praise of the thought experiment's importance, or for the observation that the issues are subtle.
