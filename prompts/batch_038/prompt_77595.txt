Me and a bunch of people in a Discord I've been in for about three years have gotten into an argument that just will not die, and I want to kill it by making a flowchart people can fill out — like those "which political compass are you" things, except for this question: do video game characters matter morally? Not in the fandom sense, I mean actually matter. Like, is there something you could do to an NPC that would be wrong for its own sake, and not just wrong because it makes you a creep, or because you wrecked somebody else's save file, or because you're disrespecting the developers. For context I'm not academic at all, I'm a pharmacy tech and I think about this stuff on my commute, so please make the thing readable by someone who has never read any philosophy — but don't dumb down the actual distinctions, that's the whole point.

The reason the argument wont die is we keep arguing past each other with different examples, so build the tree around these four cases (or better ones if you've got them, but they have to be concrete enough that a person can just react to them). One: a 20-year-old JRPG with completely scripted villagers, where one of them begs you not to kill the final boss because he's her brother, and the game lets you do it anyway with no consequence. Two: a survival game where your horse has a fear stat and a bond stat and visibly panics and bucks — everyone in the server agrees it's a lookup table with animations on top. Three: a mod I run where one townsperson is driven by a local language model hooked to a persistent memory file, so she remembers what you did three sessions ago, and she has said, unprompted, that she doesn't want the program closed. Four: a permadeath roguelike where a companion you carried for 40 hours is deleted permanently and the game makes you type their name onto a memorial wall that other players can see.

So the deliverable: a decision tree of maybe 10 to 14 questions, each one a case or a claim with two or three answer options, and every branch labeled with the question that actually splits it — does felt experience matter, does it matter that the character is *about* someone, does the player's relationship to the thing generate duties, does it matter whether the character could have turned out different, is deleting someone a different kind of thing from hurting them, does it change anything that it's fiction, would it matter if the program were paused forever instead of switched off, that kind of thing. Each branch question should be answerable by a layperson with no jargon, and then in parentheses name the technical distinction it corresponds to if there is one. Every leaf gets a name — if a position doesn't really have a name anywhere, just call it "unnamed" and coin a handle we can use in the server, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then two things I especially want: flag which pairs of leaves my questions cannot actually separate (I suspect the "what matters is the player's own character, not the NPC" view and the "relationships create obligations" view are going to collapse into each other, tell me if I'm wrong), and for every combination of answers your tree allows that nobody in the server actually holds, say whether that slot is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's thought of it yet.

One last thing, and this is me being selfish. My own position, which I have never been able to defend properly, is something I call the surprise test: a character matters if it's the sort of thing the world could still surprise, meaning its own future isn't fully settled in advance by what it was made to be. I know that sounds mushy and I know it might just be a repackaged consciousness criterion or a repackaged "has a stake in its own continuation" criterion. So put my view somewhere on the tree, tell me which questions route to it, and tell me honestly whether it lands on a leaf that already exists under another name or whether it's genuinely a different spot. And no disguised-label questions — nothing like "do you think games can be conscious," because everybody will just answer with their team's flag and the whole thing is worthless.
