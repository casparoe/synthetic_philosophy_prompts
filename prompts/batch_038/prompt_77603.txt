I am in my final year of secondary school in England, and I am writing an independent project, supervised by my history teacher, on how archaeologists claim to know anything about the beliefs and institutions of people who left no writing. In July I spent two weekends on a community excavation of a causewayed enclosure in the south of England. The interesting material was not the postholes but the ditch deposits: complete pots set upright, the articulated skeletons of cattle and pigs at what appeared to be regular intervals, some of the bones bearing butchery marks and some entirely unmarked, and very little that anybody would call domestic rubbish. When I wrote up my enthusiasm — that these were offerings, that they discharged some communal obligation, that the enclosure was where a dispersed population periodically enacted its membership of something — my teacher told me that this belonged to a genre of talk he called structured deposition, and that everything in it was inference rather than observation. I asked him what the inference consisted in, and, trying to answer for myself, I produced a chain of reasoning I cannot break and a conclusion I am not prepared to accept.

(1) To pass from a pattern of artefacts to a claim about what those artefacts meant to the people who deposited them, one must first establish what they meant.
(2) What an object meant to its users can be established only by comparison with cases in which the meaning is independently attested — by writing, by imagery that is itself explicable, or by a continuous practice surviving into the ethnographic present.
(3) For societies without writing there is no such independent attestation, so the only comparanda on offer are societies whose meanings one has already assumed in order to select them as comparable.
(4) Hence every claim about the meaning of a pre-literate deposit is either unsupported or circular.
(5) A claim that is either unsupported or circular is unjustified.
(6) Therefore no claim about the beliefs or the institutions of a pre-literate society is justified: neither the offerings, nor the obligation, nor the belonging.
(7) Yet the same reasoning would condemn any inference from a trace to an unobserved intention: the historian reading a medieval charter whose formulae are known only from other charters; the magistrate inferring an agreement from a pattern of conduct; a friend inferring from my face that I am cross.
(8) Such inferences are sometimes justified.
(9) So at least one of (1) to (5) is false, and my teacher's readings of the ditch may stand.

I want a diagnosis rather than reassurance. My own view is that the argument is valid and that a premise must go, but I cannot tell which premise I would sooner lose than (8); and (8) looks like the cheapest, being little more than confidence in ordinary practice — except that losing it seems to leave me unable to trust testimony anywhere, which I would rather avoid. So: which step do you reject, why did that step look respectable when I wrote it, and what does rejecting it cost you elsewhere? If instead you think the argument is invalid, identify the transition and say what reading of which term makes it fail. Finally, tell me what survives of the sceptical worry once your repair is in place — what constraint on my claims about the ditch the argument was right to press for, even though its conclusion is false. Please address the unmarked bones and the regular spacing explicitly, since those are the details I do not know what to do with.
