I am in my third year of a sociology PhD, and the chapter I am drafting concerns the moral vocabularies people recruit in low-stakes institutional conflicts. My committee has asked me to argue with the standard typology rather than simply deploy it — the tripartite scheme running from honor cultures to dignity cultures to victimhood cultures, distinguished by where moral worth is taken to reside and by how much of a dispute is handed over to third parties (Berger's dignity, Campbell and Manning's victimhood). My worry is that the scheme reads like a developmental sequence, when at least two of the three are better described as standing options that people toggle between inside a single dispute, and that the moral ranking the sequence quietly implies is therefore unsupported.

To test the worry, I would like a dialogue — a dialogue, not an essay about one — staged over a case I have constructed and that I do not believe appears anywhere in the literature.

The case: a works-in-progress colloquium in a philosophy department, about thirty people attending, including two visitors from another university. N. is a second-year doctoral student; R. is a fifth-year with three publications and a reputation for being rigorous and blunt. During the Q&A, R. objects that N.'s central claim is not original and that N. has failed to cite its evident antecedent. R. is substantially right: the antecedent exists. But it is a chapter in an edited volume rather than a journal article, N.'s version departs from it in a respect R. does not acknowledge, and R. presses the point twice after N. has begun to answer, in front of the visitors. Three days later N. files a complaint with the graduate ombudsperson describing a pattern of dismissive and hostile treatment, and requests that R. be struck from the colloquium's roster of respondents and recused from commenting on N.'s work. Three students submit statements corroborating the pattern; two submit statements saying that R. raised the identical objection to their own work and that they experienced it as exacting rather than hostile. R. responds by demanding a hearing at which R. may answer each allegation in person, and posts a written account of R.'s side in the department's shared drive. The chair proposes to settle only the scholarly question — whether the antecedent needed citing — and to decline jurisdiction over the interpersonal claims. Both parties reject that proposal, for different reasons.

Please write the dialogue between two interlocutors who are fully informed about this case, about the typology, and about each other's positions:

— A., who holds that what is called victimhood culture here is honor culture in new institutional clothing: the currency is still public standing, the dispute is still about who gets to define whom in front of the tribe, and the only genuine change is that the accuser's kin and seconds have been replaced by an ombudsperson and a roster. A. holds further that, understood this way, the honor norms are the morally better ones on offer, because they price an accusation — the accuser must stand in the open, be answered, and bear the cost of being wrong — whereas the procedure N. has used makes accusation nearly free and defense nearly impossible.

— B., who holds that victimhood culture is genuinely new, because it moralizes cumulative harm rather than momentary standing, and because it deliberately substitutes institutional procedure for the personal risk that honor demands. B. holds that this substitution is the moral advance: it extends standing to people who cannot afford honor's prices, and A.'s fondness for costly self-vindication is a preference for a system that filters out precisely the claimants who most need it.

Neither may be permitted to lose by caricature, and neither may be permitted to win by restatement. The requirements:

1. They argue over the same facts, not over different facts selected to suit them.
2. Each explains at least three specific features of the case under their own account — and they must not select the same three.
3. Each states what their account predicts about how the case proceeds from the chair's rejected proposal, and each names the feature of the case that would falsify their reading of it.
4. Each answers the other's strongest point about a feature they have just explained, rather than moving on to fresh ground.
5. Both address the chair's proposal: whether dignity is a genuine third option available here, or a self-description that both parties reach for and both betray.
6. The dialogue does not end in agreement. It ends on the single crux — one fact about the case, or one normative commitment — that would decide between A. and B., and it is named by whichever of the two is less comfortable naming it.

Hard limit: one thousand words. Speaker labels only; if you use stage direction, one sentence at a time.
