**Question.** An op-ed about artificial intelligence contains the following passage: "Gödel's first incompleteness theorem proved that any consistent formal system capable of expressing arithmetic contains statements that are true but cannot be proved within the system. It follows that the human mind cannot itself be any formal system, since we can recognize the truth of statements that the system cannot prove; and it follows more generally that no formal theory of the physical world can ever be complete." State as precisely as you can what the first incompleteness theorem establishes, including the hypotheses it requires, and then evaluate each of the op-ed's two inferences.

**Student answer.**

The theorem is not about "any consistent formal system," and the op-ed's first sentence is loose in three respects. The system must be (a) consistent, (b) recursively axiomatizable — there must be an effective procedure listing its axioms, so that the relation "x is a proof of y" is decidable and therefore representable inside the system — and (c) strong enough to represent a modest amount of arithmetic, enough to formalize its own syntax. The strength condition is not vacuous: Presburger arithmetic, which admits addition but not multiplication, is consistent, decidable, and negation-complete, and so is a counterexample to the op-ed as written.

Given (a)–(c), one arithmetizes syntax, defines a formula Prov(x) saying that x codes a provable sentence, and applies the diagonal lemma to obtain a sentence G provably equivalent in the system to ¬Prov(⌜G⌝). If the system proved G it would also prove Prov(⌜G⌝), since a sufficiently strong theory proves every true Σ₁ sentence about codes; combined with the equivalence that yields a contradiction, so consistency alone rules out a proof of G. The other half — that ¬G is unprovable too — needs more, which is why Gödel's original hypothesis was ω-consistency; Rosser's trick trades plain consistency for a more complicated sentence. This matters for the op-ed's word "true": the equivalence together with consistency gives that G holds in the standard model, because Prov is correct about actual proofs. So G really is true and unprovable. What the theorem does not show is that arithmetical truth outruns theory as such: the set of all true arithmetical sentences is both consistent and complete. It simply admits no recursive axiomatization. "Truth outruns proof" is therefore correct only for proof from an effectively specifiable axiom set.

The inference about physical theory fails, though not for the reason usually given. Incompleteness is a result about axiom sets and their consequences rather than about the world, and a physical theory need not be arithmetically strong at all. Theories of continuous magnitude are theories of the real numbers, and Tarski showed that the first-order theory of real closed fields is complete and decidable, so a genuinely physical language of that kind escapes Gödel's hypotheses entirely.

The inference about minds is the Lucas–Penrose argument. It is weaker than the op-ed suggests, but I think it goes through. Suppose a given mathematician were the formal system F. Then F's Gödel sentence would be unprovable in F and hence unprovable by the mathematician, while remaining true; and the mathematician, reflecting on F from outside, can see that it is true. The only premise needed beyond the theorem is that the mathematician is consistent, and we may grant that, since we do not find ourselves asserting contradictions. So the mathematician is not F; and since F was arbitrary, no formal system captures the mind.
