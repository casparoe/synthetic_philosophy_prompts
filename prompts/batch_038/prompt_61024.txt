Second-year decision theory module, and I've built an argument against risk-weighted expected utility that I half believe, but it seems to prove far too much and I can't find where it breaks.

Take a Buchak-style agent: utility linear in money, risk function r(p) = p², so every payoff gets weighted by the squared chance of getting at least that much. Coin 1 is fair; if it lands tails we flip coin 2. Let E be "coin 1 lands heads". Two acts:

A: heads → $30; tails-heads → $0; tails-tails → $100
B: $22 come what may

REU(A) = (3/4)² × 30 + (1/4)² × 70 = 21.25, and REU(B) = 22, so she prefers B. But conditional on E she prefers A ($30 beats $22), and conditional on not-E she also prefers A, since A is then a 50/50 shot at $0 or $100, worth (1/2)² × 100 = 25.

The argument:
(1) If you prefer A to B given E and prefer A to B given not-E, you cant rationally prefer B to A outright — your rankings have to compose.
(2) This agent's dont.
(3) So a bookie who knows her can charge her ε to swap A for B before anything is flipped, then charge her ε again to swap B back for A once she's been told whether E happened but before she's told how coin 2 went. She takes both trades, for any ε below $0.75. She ends up holding A with 2ε gone, which is worse than doing nothing in every single state.
(4) None of this depends on her being confused or ignorant: she knows her own preferences, the acts and the the bookie's whole plan.
(5) A theory of rational choice that lets an agent knowingly pay to end up poorer isn't a theory of rational choice.

Here's my problem. (5) looks like it generalises into something I'm sure is false — that it's irrational to let new information change what you want at all, since a bookie can always charge you at each change of mind, so you'd have to settle everything now as one big plan before you know anything. Ordinary risk-averse expected utility types change their minds on information all the time and nobody calls that irrational, so either my argument has a hole in it or it's proving something much bigger than "REU is wrong" and I can't tell which.

Which step goes, why did it look fine going in, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I'd like to keep the example and (3) if I can. And please don't just say "deny that dynamic consistency is required for rationality" and stop — tell me what that denial costs her, since she still has to say what the theory tells her to actually do at each choice point. British English, please.
