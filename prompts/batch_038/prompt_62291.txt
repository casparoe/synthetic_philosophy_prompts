I co-teach a first-year typography studio with an engineer and we're about to do the week on what makes an 'a' an 'a' — the one where students stare at a single-storey 'a' from a geometric face next to a double-storey 'a' from a text face and ask how those are the same letter. We've gotten into a standing argument about how to teach it, and we're both reaching for analogies to win, so I want an audit before I write the handout.

His line: letters are like species. Each letter is a kind with a fixed underlying structure — a skeleton, a set of diagnostic features — and every typeface is just variation on it. Get the feature list right and identification is a solved problem. That's why he thinks the whole business can be handed to a classifier, and why he wants the week to end with a checklist the students apply mechanically.

My line: letters are like a family in the Wittgensteinian sense — a network of overlapping resemblances with no single thread running all the way through, no essence, no definition. I use that to argue the opposite: identification is connoisseurship, judgement all the way down, no checklist can be complete, so the week should be taught by drilling their eyes rather then by handing out features.

Act as a professional philosopher — ideally one who has thought about categories and artifacts and who also knows something about type — and think this through carefully. Before you answer, brainstorm out loud for a bit: what does each source domain actually drag in with it? For one, reproduction, lineage, populations, descent with modification; for the other, criss-crossing similarities and the absence of a common core. I'm aware typefaces do have a genuine descent history (punches, revivals, digital redraws, a genealogy you can actually trace), so don't just wave that away — tell me whether it rescues his analogy or wrecks it.

Then I want:

- a correspondence table for each analogy: what plays the genome, the phenotype, the breeding population, the species boundary, and on my side, the family members, the overlapping resemblances, the thing that has no counterpart.
- the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually undercut the conclusion each of us draws and the ones that are merely decorative. Be blunt. I suspect at least one of us is being carried by the analogy instead of supported by it, and I'd rather hear it from you than from him.
- a verdict on each conclusion. Can a species-style picture deliver his checklist? Can family resemblance deliver my "no complete checklist, ever"? My own worry is that the Wittgensteinian point is about definitions in terms of necessary and sufficient conditions, its not a claim that recognition is hard or non-mechanical — so tell me straight if my inference is a non-sequitur, since a resemblance structure with no center might be perfectly capturable by something like a similarity metric.
- a third option, if you think there is one. Letters sit inside a system of mutually contrasting forms, so maybe the better source is something whose identity is fixed by role in a system rather than by shape — a phoneme, a chess piece, a banknote, a pitch-class realized on different instruments, whatever you think fits best. Propose one and then stress-test it yourself: where does it break, and does it handle the hard cases better than the other two?

Hard cases I want run through explicitly, since this is where an analogy earns its keep or doesn't:

- single-storey versus double-storey 'a', where in some faces the two share almost no strokes at all
- I / l / 1, and dotted versus dotless i
- a blackletter 'a' that most of my students will not call an 'a' until they've read the rest of the word
- the same shape counting as different letters in different alphabets
- ligatures, and whether æ is one letter, two, or neither
- a form that is an 'a' inside a wordmark and pure ornament in a border
- a half-eroded letter in a scan where the one deciding stroke is exactly what's missing

Say which of the three pictures predicts each of these and which is embarrassed by them.

Two ground rules. No bibliography, no reading list, no page numbers — I can't look anything up while writing this and I don't want you guessing. If you want to gesture at Letter Spirit (the old Hofstadter-group project on rendering the 26 lowercase letters in many internally coherent styles) or at anything else, do it in prose and only where the argument needs it. And if you catch yourself writing a paragraph that would apply equally well to chairs or games or any category whatsoever, delete it — I want the specifics of letterforms doing the work.

Length: whatever it takes, but I'd like to read it in ten minutes. Flag anywhere you're unsure of a typographic fact as opposed to making a conceptual point.
