I run a Tuesday-night reading group — mostly non-specialists: two nurses, a software guy, a retired Classics teacher, and a couple of grad students from unrelated fields — and this term we did al-Ghazālī's *Incoherence of the Philosophers*, mainly the stretch where he argues the fire doesn't burn the cotton by any necessity belonging to its own nature, and then we finished with Hume on constant conjunction for contrast. The problem is that afterwards, when people in the group say "so basically I'm an occasionalist," they mean at least four completely different things, and I've given up trying to sort it out by talking. So I want to make a one-page handout that's a decision tree — call it six to nine branching questions — where you answer plain questions about cases and end up at a named position, and where the whole point is that you can't steer yourself to your preferred leaf by recognizing the vocabulary.

Hard rule for the questions: no branch node may use a term of art. Not "occasionalism," not "secondary cause," not "disposition" or "power," not "necessity" or "contingency" — write "has to happen" instead of "is necessary." Every node should be a question about a concrete situation that someone who's never read any of this can answer off the top of their head, and each node should say which claim splits it from its siblings. Cases I'd like you to work from, in whatever order the logic wants (add more if you need them): dry cotton dropped on a flame, and it chars; the same cotton on a flame a thousand times running, all charring — and what you'd say would have to be different for it not to char the thousand-and-first time; bread that has nourished everyone who's eaten it up to now and then one day fails to; a magnet dragging iron filings across a table; a woman who decides to raise her arm and raises it — is the deciding doing anything, or is it just one more thing that happens alongside; and a case where the sequence breaks outright, the cotton sits in the flame and doesn't char, or the arm doesn't go up. On that last one especially I want the tree to force an answer about who or what is responsible for the break.

For leaves, at minimum I want: (a) God is the only genuine agent and the created thing is just the occasion; (b) God is the only genuine agent, but the sequences are stable enough as a matter of His custom that predicting them is a real achievement and not a lucky guess; (c) created things have causal powers of their own, which God maintains but doesn't have to step in and operate; (d) nothing anywhere has causal powers — not God, not objects — there's just regular succession plus our habit of narrating it as causing; (e) objects have real powers, period, and no theological term is needed; (f) causal necessity isn't a feature of the world at all but of how we carve the world into causes and effects. Also flag every pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I already suspect (b) and (d) are going to collapse into a single node, and if they do, say so out loud and tell me what question would have been needed to pull them apart. One sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment. And if some combination of answers is reachable in your tree but corresponds to no position anyone has actually defended, leave it on the tree anyway and diagnose why it's empty: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Last thing — I know people argue about whether al-Ghazālī himself was an occasionalist or only someone who denied that causal connections are necessary, so please don't build the tree so that his view is definitionaly one of the leaves; it should be placeable by the instrument, not baked into it. If you think I've mis-specified a leaf or that two of mine are really one view, tell me that rather than silently merging them.
