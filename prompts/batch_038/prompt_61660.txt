I am a final-year undergraduate in a seminar on the ethics of war, and for my end-of-term paper I am trying to determine whether the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants has a genuine early-modern ancestor, or whether what looks like an ancestor is doing something structurally different. My assigned reading this week was Grotius, De Jure Belli ac Pacis, Book III, and I have been working intensively with chapter iv, the one on the right of killing enemies in a public war and on other violence against the person. I have only been able to work with a library copy, and I do not want to garble a translator's wording, so below I have set out the opening argument entirely in my own words rather than quoting. I would be grateful if you could treat what follows as a reconstruction that may well be wrong in places, and tell me where it goes astray.

My reconstruction of the opening moves of the chapter:

(i) Grotius begins by observing that in a public war the right to kill the enemy belongs to both sides alike. The liberty of slaughter is held by each party to the same degree, and this holds even where the cause of the party exercising it is not a just one.

(ii) The ground he offers, as I read it, is not that both parties can be in the right about the same disputed matter. Rather, in war between sovereigns the ordinary legal remedies have ceased to operate: because there is no common judge with authority over both parties, each is thrown back into the position it would occupy if it had to pursue its claim by its own hand. Consequently, acts of violence that would be crimes within a civil society are not treated as crimes here.

(iii) What the law of nations therefore confers in this situation is, in effect, impunity. The killing of an enemy is not actionable, no penalty attaches to it, and the party who kills cannot be called to account before any human tribunal. It is this, and not an equality of desert or of cause, that makes the right mutual.

(iv) He is careful, alongside this, to insist that the permission is not a permission in conscience. What is exempt from human punishment may nonetheless be a sin, and the requirements of justice and of the law of nature continue to bind the party whose cause is bad. The chapter thus seems to run on two levels: an external one on which the parties stand as equals, and an internal one on which they plainly do not.

(v) The upshot he draws, as I understand it, is that the equality of the two sides at the external level is no reason to abandon restraint at the internal level. The remainder of the chapter then works to narrow who may licitly be killed and to separate what the law of nations permits from what justice or humanity counsels.

With that reconstruction in hand, I have six questions, and I would like them answered in order.

1. Is the outline faithful? In particular, is (ii) really an argument from the unavailability of adjudication, or is the deeper ground something about sovereignty, or about the parties standing to one another as individuals do in a state of nature? I cannot tell whether the closure of the courts is the premise that does the work or merely an illustration of it.

2. What kind of normative position is the mutual right? I have been trying to analyse it in Hohfeldian terms, and my current guess is that it is a privilege plus an immunity from sanction, but not a claim-right against the enemy, since the enemy remains perfectly free to resist. If that is correct, then the so-called equality is not an equality of standing between the two belligerents at all, but only an equality of non-accountability to third parties. Please assess whether that reading is coherent, and tell me what turns on the difference between these three possibilities.

3. Is the equality epistemic or deontic? That is, is it generated by the fact that no one is in a position to adjudicate which side has the just cause, or by the claim that both sides genuinely have the same permission? I ask because if it is epistemic, I am not sure it can be recruited as a foundation for the modern moral equality of combatants, where the combatant is usually said to be permitted to fight even when the injustice of his own side's cause is plain to him.

4. Who holds the right? Does it attach to the sovereign or the commonwealth, with the individual soldier deriving it from the public authority under which he serves, or does it attach directly to the soldier? This is the hinge of my paper: a state-level permission is not an ancestor of an individual-level one, and I suspect Grotius may only be offering the former.

5. Does the two-level structure leave a residue of wrongdoing, and can it express liability? Grotius keeps internal justice in play, which suggests he thinks the unjust combatant acts wrongly even where he cannot be punished. I want to know whether that amounts to the revisionist claim that unjust combatants are liable to be killed, or something weaker. Please be explicit about the difference between liability and mere permissibility here, and about whether a framework that speaks of impunity on one level and sin on the other can even formulate liability as a distinct notion.

6. What work is the qualifier public doing? If the mutual right is available only in a formally public war between sovereigns, what happens in an undeclared conflict or a civil war? Does the equality evaporate, and if so, is that a problem for treating this chapter as the origin of a general doctrine?

Please also tell me which of these questions cannot be settled from this chapter alone, and where in the wider work I should look — I have noticed that Book III contains a later chapter dealing with cautions regarding things done in an unlawful war, and I assume the earlier books on the causes of war are relevant, but I would rather not chase the whole treatise. Finally, please do not trouble yourself with page references, footnotes, or a bibliography; I have the edition in front of me and I will supply all citations myself. What I need is the argumentative analysis, together with a clear indication of where you are uncertain or where you think my paraphrase has quietly imported a modern assumption.
