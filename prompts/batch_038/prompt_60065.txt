I am drafting a section of a book chapter on constitutional precommitment, arguing that the "dead hand" objection is in fact two distinct objections that are routinely run together, and I need a case that pulls them apart. My readers are legal theorists and political philosophers who have already met every standard illustration — the mast and the sirens, Jefferson's arithmetic about generations, sovereign debt, the waste repository, entrenched rights — so the case must not be a reskinning of any of them.

Please construct a paired thought experiment to the following specification.

**The two accounts the case must adjudicate, as I define them here:**

- *Continuity account*: an earlier decision binds a later body as an exercise of self-government only if the later body is, in the relevant sense, the same agent — continuous membership, continuous projects, continuous identity. If that continuity has broken, enforcing the earlier decision is not the polity governing itself; it is one set of people governing another, and the fact that the first set is gone removes the legitimating force.
- *Revocability account*: a precommitment is genuine self-binding only if the bound party retains the capacity to undo it by a decision of the same kind as the decision that made it. What matters is not who the members are but whether an exit of the same democratic type remains open. Delays, supermajority thresholds, and second votes are forms of the same decision and so are compatible with self-rule; a provision that no future decision of that type can reach is an imposition, however stable the membership has been.

**The scenario requirements:**

1. Setting: a small-scale public body at which the whole electorate could plausibly vote on a charter amendment — a water district, a transit authority, a soil and water conservation district, a public hospital district. Not a nation-state, not a crisis.
2. The entrenched provision must be fiscal or procedural, not a substantive right, so the case does not quietly become a debate about the judicial enforcement of rights.
3. No court may enforce it, and no third-party contract may survive its repeal. The provision should bite administratively — the state simply declines to certify the levy or approve a bond issuance if the district acts contrary to it without meeting the charter's threshold — so that repeal genuinely would undo the constraint if it could be achieved.
4. The later majority must be reasonable: their grounds for reversal should look at least as good as the grounds for the original provision. No short-termist mob, no obvious villain.
5. The original decision was well informed and aimed at a predictable failure — but the failure did not materialize as predicted, so the provision now binds for reasons its authors could not have endorsed. It must not have been foolish when adopted.
6. Membership must have been substantially replaced between adoption and the present, and the replacement must itself be a consequence of the provision's operation, not an accident of the local economy.
7. In the base case the two accounts must give opposite verdicts on whether enforcement is legitimate.
8. Then supply a twin case differing in exactly one feature — whether a future vote of the district's own electorate can reach the provision at all — with setting, personnel, arithmetic, and motives otherwise held fixed, such that the revocability account changes its verdict and the continuity account does not. State in one sentence which feature you varied.

**The demonstration.** For each case, give each account's verdict on enforcement and the reasoning that generates it from the account as stated above, not from a general sympathy with it. Then answer:

- Which single feature of the case is doing the work?
- What happens to the pair if you delete the membership replacement in the base case while leaving revocability exactly as specified? Do the accounts still disagree, and if not, does the case still serve the purpose I described?
- Say which of the two accounts you think the pairing is designed to embarrass, and then give that account's defender the best available attack on the case — the strongest argument that it has been rigged, for instance that a supermajority repeal requirement is not really a decision of the same kind as the original adoption, or that continuity is being asked to bear normative weight it cannot bear.
- Audit my specifications. Identify anywhere the target pattern has been forced by how I described the case rather than generated by the accounts, and say what would have to be loosened for the case to be honest.

Each scenario should be a single paragraph; keep the whole response under roughly 900 words.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material. Flag in particular whether the two accounts as I have stated them correspond to positions anyone actually holds, whether either is a straw man I have built for the occasion, and where the pairing depends on a contested reading rather than on common ground. Do not present a disputed view as though it were the consensus.

End with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed only by a short phrase naming the single feature of the case that does the work.
