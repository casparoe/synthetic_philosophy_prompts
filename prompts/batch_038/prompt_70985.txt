hey — slightly odd request. I write a science-and-ideas column for a magazine, and for the last few summers I've also run a two week workshop for our fellows, early career reporters, most of whom have never taken a psych course and none of whom have read any philosophy. This year the theme is basically "why retrospective journalism goes wrong" and I have to write their end-of-workshop assessment. I'd rather it be a real test than a vibes essay, and I'm stuck on the middle of it.

Can you write me one essay question on hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy in historical explanation, plus a model answer and a marking scheme? Closed book, they get one index card, 40 minutes, 20 marks total.

What I need from it:

The question should come with a short invented excerpt — say 120-160 words, a lede and nut graf from the kind of ten-years-on feature a magazine would defintely run, that "everyone saw it coming" register. Then it asks them to diagnose what's wrong with it. The diagnosis needs to actually be hard. If the excerpt just says the outcome was inevitable and everyone knew it, any fool spots that. I want it to be at least partly careful about the ex-ante uncertainty — so the defect sits somewhere subtler, and the interesting answers have to explain why a piece that dutifully lists all the things nobody predicted can still be doing the exact thing the narrative fallacy describes.

It can't be a question where a competent summary of the two concepts scores full marks. I care about whether they can seperate the two and say where they overlap. My own rough sense, for what it's worth: hindsight bias is a thing that happens to probability and inevitability judgments once you know the outcome, and narrative fallacy is a thing that happens to causal explanation when you compress a mess into a plot. Related, not the same, and a fellow who uses them interchangeably should lose marks.

The bit I most want forced into the answer: warning people about a bias doesn't reliably switch it off. There's that old study, Beyth and Fischhoff I think, where people assigned probabilities to various outcomes of Nixon's trips to Beijing and Moscow and then afterwards reconstructed their own numbers so they tracked whatever actually happened — Fischhoff's original label for the whole phenomenon was creeping determinism, if I've got that right. Given that, a reader-facing sidebar saying "this was less predictable than it now looks" is not debiasing, it's set dressing. So I want a must-hit point on what an actual newsroom practice would look like instead — dated contemporaneous records, a forecasting log, whatever — and crucially on why that works when self-warning doesn't. If the answer can't explain the mechanism, it shouldn't get the mark.

And the crux I want the A answers to nail and the B answers to fumble: writing that resists the narrative fallacy is usually worse writing. Coherence is what makes an explanation land with a reader, and the reward structure of my industry pays for coherence. So one part of the question should ask them to name the cost of the fix and say something non-trivial about whether that cost is a reason to abandon the fix or a reason to be smarter about it.

Deliverables, in this order: (1) the question exactly as the fellows will see it, excerpt included; (2) a model answer of about 650 words; (3) a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points, with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on the specific mistake that usually loses it; (4) three or four lines on what seperates a first from a strong second — the moves, not the adjectives.

Two housekeeping things. Please don't turn the model answer into a reading list; the fellows can't look anything up and neither can I, so one or two named studies is the ceiling, and only where they're load-bearing. And keep the excerpt off anything current or partisan — I don't want twenty answers arguing about whether the reporter was factually right, I want them arguing about the epistemics.
