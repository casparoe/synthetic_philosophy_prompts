For a seminar on the history of economic thought that I am taking at my high school, I have been reading about the socialist calculation debate, and I keep hitting the response that neither side had ever seen a computer. So I would like to stipulate that away and see what survives. Suppose a central planning authority holds, at every instant, a complete real-time record of every household's purchases and stated preferences and of every plant's inputs, outputs, machine conditions, and worker skills; suppose it also adjusts Lange-style accounting prices by trial and error, but at machine speed and with no reporting gaps. In short, suppose the planners are simply handed everything that Hayek insisted only the market process could uncover.

Rank these four arguments against socialism by how much force they retain under that supposition, from most force to least:

1. Mises' claim that without private ownership of the means of production there are no genuine prices for capital goods, and hence no way to calculate what is worth producing in the first place.
2. Hayek's claim that the economically relevant knowledge is dispersed, local, and largely tacit, so no central body can gather it.
3. The incentive argument: managers and planners hold no stake in the outcome, so they conceal information, avoid innovation, and shirk.
4. The claim that competition is a discovery procedure rather than a data-processing task, so a plan cannot anticipate changes that no one has yet thought of.

Assign each a rank and one or two sentences on why it lands there, then tell me which one you consider genuinely decisive and whether my supposition is coherent at all — if granting the planners that much information already assumes something only a market could generate, please say so plainly. Write your answer in American English.
