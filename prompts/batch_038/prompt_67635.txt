In three weeks I shall be giving the opening paper at a closed two-day workshop on second-chamber reform, convened jointly by a constitutional reform charity and a public law research group at a Russell Group law faculty. The format is twenty-five minutes of paper followed by forty-five minutes of floor discussion, and the organisers have told me frankly that the room has been chosen to be adversarial.

The room, as I understand it: roughly twenty-five people. A majority are UK public lawyers, several of whom have advised Lords committees, and who think about reform in terms of what could survive the Parliament Acts settlement, the Salisbury Convention and the Human Rights Act. Six are empirically minded political scientists; two of them have run or formally evaluated deliberative polls and citizens' assemblies in Ireland, in France and here, and they are profoundly sceptical of any design whose effects cannot be measured against a control. Three are normative democratic theorists, one of whom has argued in print that sortition abandons democratic legitimacy rather than renewing it. Nobody present is a convinced lottocrat: the sortition they know is the popular polemic and the recent record of assemblies whose recommendations were adopted, watered down or shelved, not the specialist literature I would face at a political theory seminar.

The proposal, in outline. The appointed and hereditary elements of the second chamber are replaced by 200 members serving a single non-renewable four-year term, chosen by lot. The draw is not made from the electoral register but from a pool of residents — including those without the franchise — who have completed a two-year, publicly funded part-time programme in evidence handling, public finance and negotiation, which I call a civic apprenticeship; the pool will in practice be several times the size of the chamber. Membership is stratified by a variable I have coined for the purpose, temporal exposure: expected duration of subjection to the decisions in question, so that the young and the middle-aged are deliberately over-represented relative to their population share. The chamber's competence is confined to long-horizon matters — infrastructure, energy, water, planning, pandemic powers, constitutional change. It holds no veto. It may hold a bill for up to twelve months, after which the bill may proceed only by public referendum. Members work under what I call a deliberative embargo: no media engagements, no publication of individual votes, no contact with lobbyists, and a statutory bar on holding public office for ten years afterwards. They are supported by a permanent secretariat seconded from the civil service. My central normative claim is that the design's value lies not in descriptive resemblance but in custodial insulation: a body whose members have no electoral future and no career to protect can bear the blame for imposing costs on people who cannot yet vote. I argue that appointment could not do this work, because an appointee owes the position to an appointer. There is a small pilot simulation behind the design whose results I have reported only in part.

I want a prep sheet, not a defence. Give me the objections I shall actually get in this room — not the ones a general political theory audience would raise, and not the standard criticisms of lottocracy recycled from the popular literature. Order them by the damage each would do if I fumbled it. For each, say which subgroup will raise it and put it in their idiom: the public lawyers will talk about amenability to judicial review, the compatibility of the embargo with Convention rights, and what happens to the Salisbury Convention; the assembly evaluators will talk about sample frames, attrition, self-selection into the pool, and whether any effect I claim survives contact with a real secretariat; the democratic theorist will talk about authorisation and responsiveness. Give each a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is thinnest, and a flag for any objection I should concede in the opening ten minutes rather than fight in the second hour. Tell me which single reply has to land for the proposal to leave the room alive. Then tell me the question I am praying nobody asks — the one that finds the softest joint in my own design — and what I should say if it comes anyway. Please do not send me references or further reading; I know the literature, and the room will not want citations.
