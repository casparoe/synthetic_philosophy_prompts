I hold a chair in environmental philosophy and sit, as the designated non-scientific member, on the grants committee of a private conservation foundation. In eleven days the committee must choose between two applications for the same 480-hectare drained fen parcel in our eastern grantmaking region. Both come from the same land trust, which has been candid about the fact that it can execute only one.

Application A is full hydrological restoration: the drainage ditch network is blocked and filled, roughly 300 hectares are re-flooded, and the historic water regime is reinstated. The trust's own ecological model, submitted in the monitoring annex, projects that within a decade the site would be recolonised by three waterbird species of statutory conservation concern, and that for every waterbird the site fledges the restored water column would produce between ten thousand and a million short-lived invertebrate and larval fish individuals, the overwhelming majority of which die within days or weeks of hatching from starvation, predation, or desiccation. The applicant does not treat these figures as bearing on the decision; they are there because the model generates them.

Application B is partial repair by the same trust: enough hydrological work to halt peat oxidation and re-establish a wet meadow, coupled with water-level management designed to suppress mass larval emergence, and no reinstatement of the open-water habitats on which the listed birds depend. The trust estimates this configuration would support two to three orders of magnitude fewer short-lived individuals than Application A over the same period, at some cost to stored carbon and to the site's protected designation. It asks us to fund the monitoring programme together with a management protocol committing the trust to no lethal control on the parcel, no culling of predators, no supplemental feeding, and annual public reporting of juvenile mortality counts.

Two things make this genuinely difficult for us rather than merely technical. First, our deed of trust directs the board to have regard both to the integrity of ecological communities and to the well-being of individual animals. It does not say which governs when they pull apart, and it does not say whether the second consideration extends to animals whose lives are, on any plausible estimate, worse for the animal than never having been lived. Second, our resident restoration ecologist has circulated a note making three claims: that the welfare figures are unusable, since we cannot measure the valence of larval fish experience, much less invertebrate experience, and cannot aggregate what we cannot measure; that even sound figures should not enter our criteria, because the same reasoning that counts larval production as a cost counts habitat loss as a benefit, and would hand a ready-made argument to interests who want wetlands drained and never restored; and that a foundation which begins reasoning this way will within a decade be funding habitat retirement. She regards my position — that a well-being clause cannot simply be dropped from consideration when the annex numbers are as large as they are — as sentimentality dressed as ethics.

There is also a clock. The ditch works on the parcel are to be put out to tender in six weeks. If we defer for further evidence, the restoration window closes and the parcel will not return to the committee for at least two years, by which time the drainage network will have been re-cut and deepened.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the committee ahead of the meeting. It should run between 1,100 and 1,500 words and read as a committee document rather than an essay. It must contain: a single actionable bottom line, stating which application we fund, or whether we fund neither, together with any conditions attached and, if conditions are attached, how compliance is to be verified; a standard of review the board could adopt and reuse on the next application of this kind, phrased concretely enough that a non-philosopher trustee could apply it to a new proposal without further instruction; an account of what does and does not follow from the claim that a restored site will produce vastly more short-lived animals than a partially repaired one, addressing whether bringing additional lives into existence is a harm, a benefit, or neither, and what the correct baseline for measuring harm is here — noting that the degraded status quo is not obviously benign, since drained and fragmented habitat can concentrate animals at the few remaining refuges and raise per-capita mortality; a serious engagement with the ecologist's note, identifying which of her three claims carries the most weight and what the board should do about it, including whether the political uses to which this reasoning might be put are a legitimate ground for a funding decision or an extraneous one; and a plain statement of whether deferral is defensible given the tender date, and if it is not, why not.

Do not settle the matter by stipulating that invertebrate or larval welfare is, or is not, morally considerable; tell the board what to decide under uncertainty about that question, and say what would count as evidence that should change its mind. I am not looking for a survey of the literature and would prefer that the memo argue from premises the trustees already accept rather than from authority.
