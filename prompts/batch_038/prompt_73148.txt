I teach philosophy and I'm taking a paper to a law-and-ethics workshop in a few weeks. Rather than hand you polished prose I'll give you sections 2-4 in condensed form, mostly as I have them in my head. Tell me where it breaks.

Setup. Everyone agrees an at-will worker may quit. A single resignation needs no special justification — she can walk out Tuesday, no reason given, and nobody calls that coercion. But when the same people walk out together the act seems to change type: concerted refusals to work were treated for a long time as a species of conspiracy, and even now the strike is a thing that has to be earned and fenced in — notice periods, votes, renunciation of sympathy actions, prohibitions on certain targets. So the question isn't "may workers withhold their labor." It's "why does adding coordination change the moral type of the act?"

The obvious answer and why I reject it. The obvious answer is leverage: one quitter costs the firm a replacement search, ten thousand quitters at once can stop the plant, and the harm is inflicted precisely in order to bend the other party's will. Coercion, needing justification. My objection is that the same description fits plenty of collective action nobody wants to restrict. A consumer boycott has exactly that structure — many people withholding in concert so the cost lands — and so does a run on a bank, a coordinated sell-off, a town deciding to relocate. If simultaneous will-bending withdrawal is what makes a strike presumptively wrongful, we've committed ourselves to saying that about those too. I'm not willing to bite that bullet, so I take it as a reductio of the leverage story.

What I put in its place. The disanalogy, I think, is that the striker withdraws something she is already jointly producing. The quitter takes her labor away; the striker takes away a product that exists only in combination, a product she co-authors and whose terms are nonetheless set without her. So the right to strike isn't a liberty to exit — that one's cheap and everyone already has it — it's a claim on the joint product, exercised by withholding it. Note what follows. The claim is strongest exactly where contribution is most joint and least individually attributable: the assembly line, the hospital ward, the warehouse shift, where no one's output can be seperated from anyone else's. Weakest where work genuinely is individually contracted.

The consequence I don't like, which is where I most want comments. Thats backwards from the law. As I understand it — and flag it if I'm leaning on something shaky, I'm not a lawyer — the people whose work is most individually contracted, the contractors and freelancers and app workers, are the ones pushed outside the labor statute and exposed to competition law when they act together, on the theory that what they're doing is sellers coordinating on price. So my account generates the most robust right to strike for the workers who need it least and the thinnest one for those who need it most.

What I want from you:

1. Does the boycott reductio actually land, or have I walked past a disanalogy — e.g. that a strike targets a counterparty you're already inside a relationship of subordination with, and a boycott doesn't?
2. Is the joint-product premise doing something sneaky? It makes a right depend on facts about production technology, so automation or deskilling could dissolve it. Bug I should own, or fatal?
3. Can I say "claim on the joint product" without signing up for a whole theory of property in the firm? Right now I gesture, and I expect the room to go straight for that gap.
4. On section 4, should I bite the bullet (the right to strike really is weaker for gig workers, and that's a criticism of the concept, not of my account) or restructure so jointness isn't tracked so literally?
5. Where the draft is too cute — I already suspect the closing line of section 2 is showing off — and anything I've got wrong as a matter of framing rather than argument.
