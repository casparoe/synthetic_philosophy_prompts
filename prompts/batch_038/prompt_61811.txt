Here's the question as it appeared on the exam:

> **Question 3 (25 points).** A classmate of yours offers the following compressed argument:
> (1) Soldiers on both sides of a war are, in the relevant sense, doing the same thing: they follow orders, apply trained violence, and pose a lethal threat to enemy soldiers.
> (2) Anyone who poses a lethal threat to others forfeits any complaint against being killed in order to remove that threat.
> (3) Therefore combatants on both sides have the same moral status: each may permissibly be targeted, and each acts permissibly in targeting the enemy.
> (a) Name the doctrine this argument concludes to and situate it in the broader framework it belongs to. (b) There is a gap in the argument. Locate it and explain why it's a gap. (c) Now suppose we grant premise (2) as written. Does the revisionist objection — that combatants fighting an unjust cause are liable to attack while combatants fighting a just cause are not — still bite? Explain.

And here's the student's response in full:

> (a) The conclusion is the moral equality of combatants, the symmetry thesis in the traditional just war picture — the one Walzer defends and McMahan attacks. It says combatants on either side have the same moral status regardless of whose cause is just: each may be targeted, and each acts within his rights when he targets the enemy. It's the in bello half of the traditional view, which also holds that the in bello rules apply independently of who started the war justly.
>
> (b) Premise (2) gives you liability, and liability is all the conclusion's first half needs: if you've forfeited any complaint against being killed to remove your threat, then killing you to remove your threat is permitted. The only thing left to constrain the attacker is proportionality, which is an in bello matter rather than a defect in the argument. The real gap is premise (1). It describes both sides as doing the same thing, and that's precisely what's in dispute. On the revisionist account what matters isn't whether you pose a lethal threat but whether the threat you pose is unjust, and a soldier defending against aggression poses a threat that violates nobody's rights, so he hasn't forfeited anything. Premise (1) builds symmetry into the description of what combatants do, which is what the argument was supposed to establish.
>
> (c) Granting (2) makes the revisionist objection sharper, not weaker. As written it makes liability follow from threat-possession alone, and the revisionist says that's the wrong criterion: liability tracks moral responsibility for an unjust threat. McMahan's claim is that just combatants stand in the position of someone defending himself against a culpable aggressor, and the self-defense analogy breaks down for them in both directions, which is why he ends up with a view on which hardly anyone may kill anyone. It also exposes what the traditional view is really committed to. If unjust combatants are liable to be killed, they're on the hook as murderers, and on this reading they ought to be prosecuted as war criminals — except that the law has never gone that way, and that's normally taken as evidence that the moral equality thesis survives the philosophical attack, since it keeps doing in practice the job it was designed to do. One more thing: most soldiers on an aggressor's side are conscripts, and a conscript isn't posing a threat voluntarily, so the traditional doctrine probably has to be read as applying to professional soldiers, which narrows the disagreement considerably.
