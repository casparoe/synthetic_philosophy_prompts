For forty-two years I directed a neutron scattering laboratory, and I have spent the three years since my retirement working through the philosophy of science in something like a systematic way. Our local discussion group took up, last month, Richard Dawid's proposal in *String Theory and the Scientific Method* (2013) that a framework which cannot be tested at accessible energies may nonetheless be confirmed by non-empirical means — the no-alternatives argument, the argument from unexpected explanatory success, and the meta-inductive argument. Two members remained behind afterwards and disputed the matter for the better part of an hour. Neither is a string theorist: H. spent his career in particle phenomenology, and V. is a historian and philosopher of twentieth-century physics. I sat and listened, took notes, and could not determine at any point who was ahead. The exchange as nearly as I can reproduce it is below.

(1) H.: Confirmation is nothing more than the raising of a posterior probability, and nothing in Bayes's theorem stipulates that the item doing the raising must be a detector readout. That four decades of serious searching have produced no rival framework for quantum gravity is itself evidence — provided the search was competent, and in this community it plainly was.

(2) V.: Your proviso is carrying the entire load, and the competence of the search is not an independent fact I can go and look up. The community that failed to locate alternatives is the same community that trained its students inside the framework, staffed its hiring and refereeing committees with people committed to it, and made work outside it very nearly unemployable. So the claim you need — that had there been alternatives, this search would have surfaced them — is licensed only by confidence in the framework, which is precisely what the argument is supposed to establish.

(3) H.: Then your objection sweeps up everything. Every historical instance of the same inference was equally socially mediated; nobody approached general relativity in 1915 through a sociologically neutral research culture, and the confirmation that followed was real for all that. If entanglement with the community voids the string-theory case, it voids the rest of physics with it. What is really being claimed, after all, is narrower than you make it sound: in fundamental physics the number of distinct frameworks meeting the same body of constraints is small, and the absence of a rival is evidence that the space has been exhausted.

(4) V.: Those cases did not turn on the culture, because the underdetermination was broken from outside — light deflection, the perihelion of Mercury, redshift. That is what an experiment is for: it settles which of the socially entangled camps happens to be right. Withdraw that possibility and the sociology is all that remains. And as to your narrower claim, "the same body of constraints" is not a natural kind. The constraint set is chosen, and by tightening it — quantum consistency, unification, no adjustable parameters, the correct low-energy limit — one can always arrange that no rival qualifies. What your inference measures is the narrowness of the description, not the scarcity of the rivals.

(5) H.: There has been external contact already, of a subtler sort than you allow. Nobody constructed string theory in order to reproduce the Bekenstein–Hawking entropy of a near-extremal black hole, and a microscopic count of states inside the framework landed on it. Nor was the gauge/gravity duality contrived as an instrument for estimating the shear viscosity of a strongly coupled quark–gluon plasma, and yet it supplied one, with the ratio fixed at a specific value. Unexpected explanatory reach of that kind is what has historically tracked truth.

(6) V.: What it tracks historically is mathematical fertility, and mathematical fertility has been a poor guarantor of anything. Deferents and epicycles rendered the planetary positions with real precision for centuries; the Ptolemaic system's difficulty was never computational reach. And the framework you are now crediting is the one said to admit on the order of 10^500 distinct vacua. A structure supple enough to accommodate whatever value of the cosmological constant turns up cannot be given credit for accommodating one particular value.

(7) H.: You are conflating a theory with its solution space. General relativity likewise admits infinitely many solutions, and you do not indict it for flexibility, because what constrains it is the field equation rather than the count of solutions. What constrains string theory is conformal invariance, modular invariance, and the cancellation of anomalies — requirements severe enough that a massless spin-two state is forced into the spectrum rather than chosen. The entropy calculation was not a fit to an adjustable parameter; it was a derivation whose answer was settled in advance up to a coefficient.

(8) V.: Then tell me what the framework rules out. If no observation would count against it once enough vacua and auxiliary structure are on hand, then what you are praising are demonstrations of internal coherence, and internal coherence is a virtue of a research programme, not evidence that the programme's ontology obtains. Which brings me to the meta-inductive argument, and there I think the whole edifice shows its foundation. It generalises from a handful of past episodes in which a theory lacking known alternatives was subsequently confirmed empirically, to the conclusion that a theory lacking known alternatives will be so confirmed. The sample is tiny, it is drawn entirely from the episodes where the inference worked, and its reliability is the very thing under dispute. That is not a modest extension of scientific method; it is a claim that the method could always have run without data.

(9) H.: Modest or not, the inference is unavoidable, since you deploy it whenever you trust an established theory over an unfashionable rival without commissioning a new experiment. Your closing charge is a slippery slope wearing the clothes of an argument: if the reasoning is genuinely probabilistic, a theory that lacks these indicators simply does not get its posterior raised. The risk is not that we confirm everything elegant, but that we confirm too little, too slowly.

I did not intervene. I should like your adjudication of the exchange move by move: for each of the nine numbered steps, say whether it succeeds, partially succeeds, or fails, and justify the rating. Please resist the temptation to recite the familiar general case for and against string theory, which I have read at some length; what I want is a verdict on these particular moves as these two people actually made them. Then tell me which of the two comes out ahead on the whole. Finally, and this interests me most, tell me what neither of them noticed. I have a suspicion that part of the stalemate concerns what the absence of alternatives is evidence *of*, rather than whether it qualifies as evidence at all, but I may be manufacturing that symmetry in hindsight — say whether it is there, and if it is, whether it tells in favour of either of them.
