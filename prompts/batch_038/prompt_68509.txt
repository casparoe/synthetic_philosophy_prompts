I'm drafting a piece about it for a small blog and I want a consistency check on the views before I post them, since what I say I believe doesn't obviously explain what I did.

Here's the set:

(1) Cyclical preferences are a genuine rational failure, and the pump is the proof — anyone disposed to pay to end up back where they started, minus money, has defective preferences rather than merely defective information.

(2) The usual defense — that pumps only bite myopic agents who can't see the whole sequence, and that a foresighted agent with the very same preferences just declines the first trade — is basically right. So intransitivity is survivable if you can see far enough ahead.

(3) What I ought to do at any given moment depends only on the options I'm actually facing at that moment. Not on what I decided last week, and not on what I'd prefer if I stepped back and looked at the whole sequence at once.

(4) It can be fully rational to bind yourself in advance, and to honour the binding when the moment arrives and you'd genuinely rather not.

(5) Paying a small cost now to avoid a larger foreseeable loss later is just ordinary instrumental rationality — there's nothing specially virtuous about it and nothing paradoxical either.
