I've been lurking on rationalist-adjacent forums for something like fifteen years, and one thread pattern keeps coming back: whether the no-free-lunch results actually settle anything about the problem of induction. Last week a long arguement broke out between two regulars on a board I read — call them Marcus and Nadia — and I still can't tell who was winning. Partly because they kept sliding between the search/optimization version of the theorem and the supervised-learning version, and partly because both of them are considerably smarter than me. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

1. **Marcus:** NFL is Hume with the armchair removed. Average off-training-set error over every possible target function and all learning algorithms come out identical. So it's not just that induction *seems* to need assumptions about the world — here's a proof that there's no such thing as a method that generalizes as such. People have been hand-waving about this since the 1700s and now it's in a journal with a proof attached.

2. **Nadia:** It's a bookkeeping identity, not an impossibility result. That average runs over *all* functions, and the set is closed under flipping labels — for every function in it, its negation is in it too. Any symmetry like that forces every method to tie. You haven't shown generalization is impossible, you've shown that a uniform average over a set built to be flat is flat. Weight the set non-uniformly — the only setting anyone actually cares about — and the theorem goes silent.

3. **Marcus:** That's my point, conceded. The weighting *is* the assumption, and the theorem's contribution is that you can't have it both ways: no weighting, no generalization; weighting, an assumption you now have to own. Hume's version was a vibe. This one tells you exactly which object has to be supplied and where it enters the machinery. And the symmetry isn't a cheap trick — closure under relabeling outputs is precisely what buys you a result about *all* methods at once, so you can't keep the generality and drop the symmetry.

4. **Nadia:** You can drop it without losing anything real, because the interesting worlds are a proper subset. Restrict to computable target functions — which isn't ad hoc, it's forced on us, since we can only implement computable methods and can only ever gather computable data — and the free lunch is back. A universal, Solomonoff-style predictor is within a bounded factor of the best computable predictor on any computable environment, with the gap controlled by how much prior mass the true environment has. So "no method beats any other" is just false in the only setting that could apply to us.

5. **Marcus:** Read your own bound again. The gap depends on the prior mass of the true environment, i.e. the prior is doing all the work, i.e. the theorem was right that the prior is doing all the work. And the optimality is self-certifying: you're optimal with respect to a loss defined by your own prior. Nobody compelled the universal prior except its elegance. Anti-inductive computable environments are still in your class — you've just handed them tiny weight, and that's a choice, not a discovery.

6. **Nadia:** A choice with an argument behind it, which is more than Hume's problem permits. The universal prior isn't pulled from the air; it's what you get when you demand a prior not dominated by any other computable prior, and prediction under it is compression, which you can check on held-out data. So what's left between us isn't transcendental, it's empirical: which weighting actually compresses the world we're in.

7. **Marcus:** "Check on held-out data" is the fallacy in a phrase. Held-out data is future data relative to the check, and inferring from past compression success that the prior will keep working is exactly the inference that needs a license. You've built a machine that converts a justificatory question into an engineering question and then announces that the justificatory question dissolved.

8. **Nadia:** And you've built a machine that converts an engineering question into a justificatory one so that nobody ever has to answer anything. If a scheme compresses held-out data better than every alternative anyone has managed to propose in three centuries, "but have you *justified* the prior" has no content left in it. Tell me what your justification would look like that isn't either circular or itself an empirical claim.

9. **Marcus:** Circular-or-empirical is precisely the trilemma, and it isn't my job to fill in a blank — that blank *is* the problem of induction. Small thing too: half this thread has been sliding between the no-free-lunch results about search and the ones about supervised learning. Different theorems, different setups — fitness functions versus target concepts — and you've been answering a claim about generalization with material about optimization.

10. **Nadia:** The structure is identical: a uniform average over the problem space makes all solvers tie. And the supervised-learning versions are the ones I used in moves 4 and 6, so that jab doesn't land. Meanwhile your own toy cuts against you. Three training points, one test point, so 16 possible target functions. A method that outputs the majority training label is right on 8 and wrong on 8. A coin-flipper is right half the time on all 16. Same average, wildly different profile. A theorem that's flatly silent about the profile — the only thing anyone cares about — isn't telling you generalization is impossible. It's telling you the average is the wrong summary statistic.

11. **Marcus:** That toy is mine, not yours. It shows the theorem is compatible with everything I've said: the average is flat, the profile is where the content lives, and the profile is fixed by whatever assumption you smuggled in. Majority-vote wins big on smooth worlds and loses big on anti-smooth ones. That's the localization claim from move 3, now with numbers on it.
