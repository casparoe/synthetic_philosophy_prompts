I'm putting together a little paper for our department's decision-theory reading group in about three weeks, and a mate of mine has completely demolished the framing I'd sorted out, so I'd like a second pair of eyes before I try to rebuild it.

The case we're working from is the Pasadena game. You toss a fair coin until it comes up heads; if heads first appears on the nth toss, the payoff is (−1)^(n+1) × 2^n/n pounds — so +2 for heads straight off, −2 if it's heads on the second toss, +8/3 on the third, −4 on the fourth, and so on, growing without limit in both directions. Multiply each payoff by its probability of 1/2^n and the powers of two cancel, so the expectation is just 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, the alternating harmonic series, which converges to about 69p but doesn't converge absolutely. And because it doesn't converge absolutely, you can reorder the terms to make them sum to any number you please. That's the whole puzzle: a game with a perfectly definite-looking small positive value that also seems, on a different but equally natural way of writing it down, to be worth nothing in particular at all.

Here's Dan's argument, which he says shows that expected value doesn't merely get awkward with exotic infinite gambles but stops doing any work whatsoever:

(1) Expected value is supposed to be a genuine feature of a decision problem — the number a rational agent uses to compare her options — and not an artefact of how somebody happens to write the problem down.
(2) Two ways of listing a gamble's outcomes that pair the same states of the world with the same payoffs are two descriptions of one and the same gamble.
(3) So if two such listings give different sums, the gamble simply has no expected value: not a disputed one, none.
(4) The Pasadena game admits listings that give different sums — any real number you care to name.
(5) So the Pasadena game has no expected value, and decision theory is silent about it: it cannot rank taking it above refusing it or the other way round.
(6) Where a theory of rational choice is silent, no criticism of the agent is available; you cannot be irrational in a respect the theory does not speak to.
(7) Any gamble can be paired with a Pasadena game: keep the original bet and add a side bet on the coin tosses that costs nothing. Since the expectation of the pair requires the expectation of each part, the pair has no expectation either — and by (6), nothing the agent does with the pair is criticisable, including how she handles the perfectly ordinary bet sitting inside it.
(8) And you can always tack on such a side bet, so no choice whatever escapes (7).
(9) Therefore expected value can never ground a criticism of anybody's decision, and a theory that can never criticise anything isn't a theory of rationality.

The conclusion is plainly too strong, but I've read (1) to (9) about six times and can't find the joint that gives. My instinct is to fight over (7) and (8) rather than (1) to (4), because I want to keep the order-independence requirement — an expectation that depends on your bookkeeping isn't a property of anything — but I can't say what's actually wrong with the pairing move, and I suspect Dan will just ask me why the original bet's expectation is supposed to survive being bundled with a component that has none. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable in place? Then price the alternatives for me, because I want to know what I'm signing up for either way. Specifically — is (2) even true of the reorderings (4) needs, given that a rearrangement chosen by looking at the signs and sizes of the payoffs seems to reassign payoffs to states rather than merely relist them; if I give up (4) instead, do I owe a story about why listing in order of toss number is the privileged listing, and does pointing out that any bounded utility function makes the series absolutely convergent solve the problem or just dodge it; does (8) confuse the act the agent actually faces with an act she might have been offered; and does (6) do any independent damage, since silence about the pair doesn't obviously buy silence about the part? If the honest verdict is that the argument is valid and one of (1), (3) or (6) has to go, tell me which is cheapest and what we lose — I'd rather hand in a talk that concedes something real than one that waves at a step I haven't identified.
