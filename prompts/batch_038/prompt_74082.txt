Retired climate modeller here — thirty-odd years running ensemble forecasts, never read any philosophy until I stopped working, so I've only been at this a couple of years. Next month I'm giving a talk in a university philosophy department's colloquium series, and my line is that the usual "your one flight or one steak makes no causal difference" objection to individual action on climate just fails, but that philosophers keep missing why. They argue about whether a single act changes the *expected* amount of harm. I want to say that's the wrong quantity: its the shape of the distribution that shifts, particularly out in the bad tail, and separately that "you can't detect any effect from one act" is a claim about statistical power, not a claim that the effect is zero. I'll use ordinary probability and no measure theory, and I'll say explicitly that I don't think voting fits the same template.

The room: mostly Kantians and virtue ethicists, one environmental economist who does cost-benefit work for a living, two or three grad students who have actually read the collective-harm literature, and no decision theorists or statistically minded people at all.

Build me a prep sheet for the question period. What are the six or seven objections this particular crowd will actually raise — phrased the way each of them would phrase it, so I recognise it mid-sentence — ordered by how much damage it does to the talk? For each, give me a reply I could say out loud in about two minutes without a whiteboard, plus a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which two I should just concede on the spot and how to concede them without giving away the whole thesis. And finish with the question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that would show I've wandered into a debate I only half understand.
