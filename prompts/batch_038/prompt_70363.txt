Our seminar on perceptual psychology has two people who've spent a whole term talking past each other, and I'm trying to get them to sign something. One is a hard-line encapsulationist in the Fodor/Pylyshyn lineage: early vision is informationally encapsulated, and sure, cognition can steer attention and pick which inputs get processed, but that's controlling what goes into the machinery rather than revising the computations inside it, so it isn't penetration. The other says that concession swallows the thesis — if beliefs determine where you look and what you look at, then what you see is belief-dependent, and "encapsulation" is doing no work that couldn't be done by a claim about processing speed or automaticity. The cases they keep lobbing at each other are the action-specific ones (hills look steeper when you're tired, distances look longer with a heavy backpack on, or when you're frightened) versus perceptual learning and expectation effects on ambiguous figures.

Write the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should have produced. Start with a numbered list of claims both could sign about what's actually established: which top-down effects are robust, which look like response bias or post-perceptual judgment, and what preregistered replication has and hasn't settled. Then a table of the residual disagreements, each worded so its own side would accept it as a statement of thier position. For every row, commit to a call on whether the disagreement is substantive or a dispute about what penetration and perception mean — I want a verdict with reasons, not "it might be partly verbal." End with the two cruxes where a study or argument both would accept as relevant could actually move one of them, with each side's prediction recorded beforehand. Please don't just survey the options; the point is that they should both be able to put their names to it. Cap it at 800 words, table included.
