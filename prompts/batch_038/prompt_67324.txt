next thursday i'm giving a talk at a policy school workshop — audience is about a dozen diplomatic historians, four or five IR people who work on foreign policy decision-making and analogical reasoning, and two behavioral scientists who study judgment and debiasing. nobody in the room is a philosopher and they will not be charitable about vagueness. no grad students worth mentioning, and one of the historians has spent his whole career on how British ministers actually used the past in the 1950s.

the thesis I'm defending, roughly:

the standard complaint about lessons-of-history reasoning is that analogies are loose, get stretched too far, and ignore disanalogies. I think that diagnosis is wrong, and that the fix everyone proposes — be more careful, list the disanalogies, check whether the cases really resemble each other — is aimed at the wrong stage of the process. the damage is done before anyone reasons. an analogy doesn't get evaluated and then accepted; it installs a *description* of the present case, and once the description is installed the inference to a policy conclusion is basically automatic and often perfectly valid. so "check the disanalogies" can't help, because which features count as analogies vs disanalogies is already settled by the description you were handed. the selection happens silently and then gets laundered through what looks like careful reasoning.

two illustrations I'll lean on: Suez in '56, where describing Nasser's nationalization as Munich-again made intervention look compulsory, when the same events could have been described as the two exhausted colonial powers doing what they'd done before and losing — which points the opposite way. And Vietnam, where the escalation case rested on a Munich/domino description, and George Ball's dissent was itself an analogy running the other direction: the French in Indochina, Dien Bien Phu, the terrain and the politics. note what that means — the dissent wasn't a refusal to analogize, it was a competing description. which is my whole point.

so the proposal I'll actually be selling: any policy memo that leans on a historical analogy has to carry a paired counter-analogy drawn from the same period and about equally well evidenced, described so that it points the other way, plus one sentence saying which description you're adopting and what would decide between them. not to stop decisions — to force the description step out into the open where it can be argued about.

write me the prep sheet. the eight-ish objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each does to the talk, each phrased the way that audience would phrase it — the historians will come at me with archival practice and what ministers actually say in the record, the IR people with operationalization and whether 'equally well evidenced' can be specified without begging the question, the behavioral scientists with what the debiasing literature says about whether procedures like this actually change anyone's mind. two-minute reply to each, and a note under each on where my reply is softest.

also: what should I concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, what's the single reply that has to land or the proposal dies, and what's the question I'm really hoping nobody asks. be honest about that last one, don't spare me.
