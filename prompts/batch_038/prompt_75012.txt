I retired from a national lab about five years back — solid-state chemistry, mostly X-ray diffraction, forty years of staring at patterns and arguing about what structure could produce them — and since then I've joined a philosophy reading group at the public library. Six of us: two retired lawyers, a former high school English teacher, a nurse, a guy who ran a hardware store, and me. Last month we did Lewis Carroll's tortoise-and-Achilles piece, the one where the tortoise cheerfully accepts both premises about the triangle, accepts the conditional that ties those premises to the conclusion, and still refuses to grant the conclusion, and then just keeps asking for one more conditional forever, so that Achilles ends up with an infinitely long notebook and no argument. The group all nodded at the standard moral — you can't turn a rule of inference into just another premise, the regress proves it — and we went on to something else. I couldn't. It nags at me the way a peak in a pattern nags at you when it doesn't index against the structure you've proposed: usually it means your structure is wrong, not the peak.

So here's what I'd like you to build me: a thought experiment in which Achilles actually wins. I want a realistic case — an ordinary person, no talking reptiles, no memory problems, no time pressure, no bad faith, no confusion about words — who accepts some general principle, accepts a couple of perfectly ordinary factual premises, and nonetheless sits there unable to reach a conclusion that anyone else in the room would call obvious, and where adding exactly one more conditional to what they accept does cure the block. And I want a control: a second case that matches the first in every respect I can think of, in which adding the same kind of conditional does not cure it. The pair has to differ in one feature only, and you choose the feature. My own hunch, which I may well be wrong about and which you should treat as a hypothesis to test rather than a conclusion to write toward, is that the operative difference has to do with whether the added conditional is about the situation in front of the person or about conditionals in general. Each scenario should be roughly one paragraph, and the whole thing should be followable by the two lawyers without any formal logic, but please don't sand off the interesting parts to make it smooth.

What the case is for, so you know what would count as cheating: I want to find out whether the standard moral is really forced on us by the structure of inference, or whether it leans on some quirk of Carroll's tortoise that a flesh-and-blood reasoner wouldn't have. So after the two scenarios, walk me through it the way I'd walk a colleague through an experimental design — state the block in each case, state the added conditional in your own words, show step by step why the block dissolves in one and survives in the other, and name precisely which feature is doing the work. Then be honest with me: have you beaten Carroll, or changed the subject? Is your cured tortoise actually the same animal as his, or did you quietly subtract something his has that yours lacks — and if so, would putting it back restart the regress? Finish with how someone who holds the standard line would attack your design, and what you'd say back. No references or reading lists, please — I'm not going to chase those down and the machinery is what I'm after.
