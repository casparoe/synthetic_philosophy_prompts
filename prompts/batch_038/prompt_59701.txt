I am in the fourth year of a dissertation on the absorption of the Stoic dichotomy of control into twentieth-century psychotherapy and, more recently, into what I would call the wellness register of the modern revival — the books, courses, and apps that present "attend to what is up to you and release the rest" as a technique of emotional triage. One section of the chapter advances a structural worry rather than a historical one: that a doctrine introduced as a discipline of assent can be operated instead as a standing permission not to assess one's ends at all, since anything that looks like a demand to justify one's commitments can be refiled as an external and therefore indifferent. A colleague in the English department, hearing me work this out at length, suggested Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a test case, and I have become convinced the novel is an unusually precise instrument for the worry. Stevens's own account of dignity — that the accomplished butler is the one who is never shaken out of his professional being, whatever happens around him — is so nearly a paraphrase of Stoic self-command that the novel's admirers routinely take it as one; and yet his image of the great house as a wheel, in which the butler's standing derives from proximity to a hub where great gentlemen turn the affairs of the world, looks less like self-command than like the wholesale outsourcing of the criterion of value. So my first question is whether Stevens's dignity is a corrupted version of Stoic composure or a different thing altogether that merely rhymes with it, and whether the distinction can be drawn from inside the ancient framework rather than imported into it.

Several more specific things I would like you to work through. First, the doctrine of roles: Panaetius's personae as transmitted in Cicero's On Duties, and the recent revival of "role ethics" in Epictetus scholarship, make the proper occupancy of an assigned role central to the good life, which seems to leave the doctrine poorly equipped to condemn a man whose failure consists in occupying his role flawlessly. Does the novel show that role ethics needs a prior account of which roles may be inhabited without loss, or is there a Stoic answer available — something about the citizen-role overriding the occupational one — that Stevens simply never hears? Second, the reserve clause: Stevens appears to serve without reservation, treating his employment as unconditional rather than as something undertaken subject to nothing's preventing him. Would inserting the clause have changed the two scenes the book pivots on, the conference during which his father is dying upstairs and the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids at his employer's instruction, or is the clause itself useless once an agent no longer recognizes the events that would trigger it? Third, the trichotomy now widely proposed as a correction to the popular dichotomy, adding a middle class of things only partly up to us: my suspicion is that the novel's tragedy lives in exactly that third category and that Stevens's self-description cannot admit the category exists, and I have been calling the resulting condition "vicarious prohairesis" — a coinage I am not attached to and would happily discard if you can tell me whether it names a failure mode the ancient texts can accommodate. Fourth, the affective register: is Stevens's suppression apatheia misdescribed, or its opposite, and is the closing pier scene a genuine prokopē, a turn, or the bare recognition of a wasted life for which no conversion remains available? I worry the popular register reads that ending as healthy acceptance, which strikes me as the most damaging misreading on offer.

Please also tell me where the reading strains, since I would rather cut the section than force it. The obvious objection is that the novel's target is political and social — deference, class, the abdication of judgment in the appeasement years, with the American guests twice warning Darlington that he is out of his depth — and that reframing all this as a botched control-scheme psychologizes an indictment that is about power rather than the soul. Rival diagnoses I want weighed against the Stoic one: a Kantian account of servility and the loss of self-respect in the sense developed in the well-known paper of that name, and Williams's objection that an agent who has surrendered his ground projects has nothing left from which to act at all. Which of these actually fits the textual evidence better? Finally, the claim I most want you to attack: it seems to me that the dichotomy in this novel is applicable only from the outside. The narration is so systematically unreliable that Stevens cannot do the sorting himself, and the reader is the only party positioned to distinguish what was up to him from what was not — which would make the dichotomy a second-personal instrument rather than a self-help one, and would mean the revival has the epistemology backwards. If that is right, I want to know what the novel thereby adds to the debate that argument alone could not; if it is wrong, say so plainly and say where. Assume I know the plot, so no summary and no bibliography, and please do not worry about quoting Ishiguro verbatim — I want analysis, and paraphrase is entirely sufficient for my purposes.
