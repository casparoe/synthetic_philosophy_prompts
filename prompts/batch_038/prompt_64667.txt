I'm a second-year law student in my university's Legislation and Regulation course. Our section runs a semester-long exercise set in a fictional state, Meridian, precisely so that none of the materials can be looked up; everything below comes from the course packet, and the packet stipulates that there is no case law, no agency rule, and no legislative history beyond what I reproduce. In week six we were given this provision of the fictional Meridian Beverage Container Act (enacted 1989; § 12(e) added by amendment in 2019):

§ 12(a) [Findings]. The legislature finds that beverage containers discarded in public places are a leading component of roadside and shoreline litter in this State, and that redemption at the point of purchase gives consumers a direct financial reason to return containers rather than discard them.

§ 12(b) [Definitions]. (1) "Beverage container" means a sealed glass, metal, or plastic container of not more than 120 fluid ounces holding a beverage intended for human consumption and offered for sale at retail in this State, bearing the refund marking described in § 14. (2) "Retailer" means a person engaged in the business of selling, from a place of business in this State, goods that include beverage containers. (3) "Refund value" means five cents.

§ 12(c) [Duty to accept]. A retailer that sells beverage containers shall accept for redemption, upon presentation by a person, any beverage container bearing the refund marking, whether or not the container was purchased from that retailer.

§ 12(d) A retailer may limit its obligation under this section to 200 containers per person per day, and may accept containers only during posted business hours at the location where the containers are sold.

§ 12(e) In lieu of accepting containers itself, a retailer may, by written election filed with the department, arrange for a certified redemption center located within one mile of each of the retailer's stores to accept containers on its behalf.

§ 13 A distributor shall reimburse a retailer the refund value of each container the retailer accepts under this Act.

The fact pattern: Kestrel Provisions is registered in Meridian and sells groceries exclusively online. Orders are placed through its website, packed at a single fulfillment center outside the state capital, and delivered by Kestrel's own vans, which run routes throughout Meridian and return to the fulfillment center twice a week. Kestrel sells roughly 9,000 beverage containers a day and has no storefronts. It does not take back containers, and its website directs customers to municipal recycling; it does, however, accept in-person returns of damaged or wrongly delivered goods at a service window at the fulfillment center during posted weekday hours. The department is considering a penalty under § 12(c). Meridian has about 400 grocery retailers with stores and three that, like Kestrel, sell only online; online accounts for roughly 18 percent of grocery beverage sales in the State and is growing.

Two classmates, Priya and Dev, kept arguing about it after section and posted the whole exchange in our group chat. I was asked to referee and could not settle it, so I'm passing it to you. Their moves:

1. Priya: Kestrel is a retailer that sells beverage containers, so § 12(c) applies by its terms. The operative sentence fixes coverage by reference to selling, not to having a store, and it expressly reaches containers the retailer never sold. Every reference to a place in § 12 appears in (d) or (e), and both are permissions; a permission to do less cannot cut down a duty to do more.

2. Dev: Read (d) and (e) again. (d) allows a retailer to accept containers "only during posted business hours at the location where the containers are sold" — Kestrel has no such location, since its sales are concluded on a website and its goods ship from a warehouse. (e) allows substitution of a redemption center within one mile of "each of the retailer's stores" — Kestrel has no stores and so can never elect. On Priya's reading Kestrel would be the only retailer in Meridian bearing an unlimited, unlimitable, non-delegable duty to take in containers from strangers. That is absurd, and absurdity is evidence of meaning.

3. Priya: The absurdity is on your side. If online sellers fall outside, any retailer escapes § 12 by moving its sales to a website, and the statute's coverage shrinks as retail modernizes — which (a) rules out, since the findings tie the duty to keeping containers out of the litter stream. And you're treating "stores" in (e) as a statement of who is covered when it is a distance formula: the 2019 legislature was telling grocers how far away a substitute center may be, not amending the scope of (c).

4. Dev: (a) is a findings clause, not operative text; it is preamble, and its reference to redemption at the point of purchase describes a counter, not a cursor. More importantly, your reading doesn't extend § 12, it completes it: to make the duty workable for Kestrel the department would have to invent a mail-in procedure, a shipping reimbursement scheme, and a cap, none of which appear anywhere in § 12. Courts don't supply missing machinery. When a provision cannot be applied without legislating three new administrative rules, that is a sign it was never drafted to apply.

5. Priya: That's a practical objection in a semantic costume. The performance is specified: accept, upon presentation by a person, any marked container. How the retailer accepts is left to the retailer — Kestrel can staff the door of its fulfillment center, or take returns in the vans that already drive to every customer address and back twice a week. And expressio unius: the legislature plainly knew how to write a location limit, and wrote one only inside a permission.

6. Dev: Then you've changed the subject. If the duty is satisfied by whatever arrangement Kestrel devises, § 12(c) doesn't require Kestrel to accept containers; it requires Kestrel to design a returns program, an obligation whose content the obligor chooses. And nobody "presents" a container to a moving van. Finally, the 2019 amendment is itself a reading of § 12(c): a legislature that spent a session on this statute and drafted its entire alternative-compliance route in terms of stores was telling us who (c) is about.

7. Priya: A 2019 legislature's failure to mention a business model that barely existed in 2019 is evidence of nothing. "A retailer that sells beverage containers" was true of Kestrel the day the amendment passed and is true of it today; the general term governs, not the applications its drafters expected. That is the whole difference between the meaning of a text and the expected reach of a text.
