I am drawing up criteria for evaluating cosmological theories under the Boltzmann-brain objection, and I would rather have the criteria in hand before I look at any particular proposal. Specify the desiderata: what a theory of the universe's global structure and thermodynamic history must deliver in order to count as an acceptable account of a world in which observers like us draw inferences from their own evidence.

Give the consideration behind each item, not merely a label. At a minimum, the list should take a position on the following:

- The asymmetry between the entropy cost of a fluctuation that yields one momentary brain equipped with apparently coherent records and the cost of a fluctuation that yields an entire ordered low-entropy cosmos — and what a theory owes us given that the cheaper fluctuation dominates overwhelmingly.
- Whether the theory's own probability assignments over observer states suffice to generate predictions, or whether they must be supplemented by a principle telling us to expect to be typical; and if supplementation is needed, whether that principle can be anything other than a further postulate the theory does not itself justify.
- Whether a theory that predicts our evidence exactly, while implying that the evidence is no guide to the world, is refuted by that evidence, unfalsifiable, or neither. The objection may be epistemic rather than empirical, and the list should say which, and why that matters for adequacy.
- Whether a theory that undermines the warrant for the reasoning which produced it thereby fails a requirement, or merely fails to be rationally holdable; how that distinction should be drawn, and whether either outcome disqualifies a cosmology.
- How the requirements shift depending on whether the theory posits infinite spacetime volume, an eternally recurring de Sitter phase, or a finite-dimensional state space in which recurrences cannot be avoided.
- What a theory owes us concerning the arrow of time and the low-entropy boundary condition (the kind standardly called the Past Hypothesis): in particular, whether deriving that condition from a statistical postulate over microstates is a genuine desideratum or a preference of one school, given that the derivation is precisely what makes freak observers dominant.
- Whether a theory must explain the reliability of our records, or need only be consistent with it.

Mark every requirement as non-negotiable, as negotiable but costly to surrender, or as a partisan demand that an otherwise good theory may simply refuse. Then identify any subset that cannot be jointly satisfied and say which member has to go; if the list is itself the interesting result here, that is what I most want to see. Close with the single requirement that does the most work in excluding attractive cosmologies, and with no more than a paragraph each on how three generic responses fare against the list: modifying the cosmology to suppress the fluctuations, retaining the cosmology and denying the objection its force, and rejecting the typicality principle so that freak observers never enter predictions at all.

No bibliography or references, please — I want the reasoning, not the sources. A numbered list with a sentence or two of motivation per item is fine.
