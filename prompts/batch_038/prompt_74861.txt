For several years I have been working through economics and decision theory on my own — no coursework, no degree, just books and long arguments with anyone who will have them. Last week one of those arguments ended abruptly, and I want to understand precisely why, because I suspect the sentence that ended it is capable of doing several different jobs, and that at most one of them is even a candidate for the job it was hired to do.

The argument: I claimed that a person's choice does not always reveal a settled preference, because the preference itself may have been shaped by the narrowness of the menu they were ever offered. My example was the old fable of the fox who decides the unreachable grapes were sour anyway. I said that someone who reports being entirely content with a life they have never had an alternative to may stand in exactly the fox's position, and that in such a case there is a live question about whether their choices show what they would have wanted under different circumstances — that the choice reveals a preference only relative to the history that produced the preference.

His reply was a single sentence: "de gustibus non est disputandum" — there is no disputing about tastes. He took it to settle the matter, on the ground that you cannot argue a person out of what they prefer, so I was being unfair to the person I was describing.

What I want is not a defense of my position and not a gloss on the maxim's meaning. I want the distinct, precisely statable claims that one sentence could be standing for, pulled apart from one another: a claim about what we can know of other people's preferences; a claim about whether statements of taste can be true or false at all; a claim about whether a preference is anything over and above the pattern of choices that expresses it; a claim about what an inquirer is entitled to hold fixed as a working assumption when explaining behavior; a claim about whether one person may criticize another's ends. There may be others I have not thought of.

For each candidate claim, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or simply false; whether it is consistent with the others or whether holding two of them at once is already a confusion; and — the part I most need — whether it would actually license dismissing my point, or whether it changes the subject. My hunch is that several of them are true only in a sense that renders them useless to him, and that at least one is a rule about how to conduct an inquiry rather than a fact about persons, which is a very different thing to appeal to in a quarrel about a particular person's life. Say also, plainly, which of the readings someone reaches for when they use this maxim to stop a conversation rather than to continue one.

Close with the single sentence I should have said back to him: the one that identifies which reading he was borrowing and shows why that reading does not reach the case I had raised. No sources, no reading list — I want the analysis and nothing else.
