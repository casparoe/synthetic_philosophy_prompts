Since the crash I've been stuck on one sentence: I could have taken the other road, and my brother would still be here. A friend of mine who does philosophy turned it into an argument last week, and honestly the argument scares me more than the crash does, because its conclusion seems to be that the sentence was never true of anything.

Here's how it runs:

(1) Counterpart theory says "I could have taken the other road" is true just in case some counterpart of me -- someone very like me in another world, but not me -- takes the other road.
(2) On that view I exist in exactly one world, this one; everywhere else there are only people who resemble me.
(3) So whatever makes my sentence true, it's the doings of someone resembling me.
(4) But "a guy who resembles me could have done X" doesnt mean "I could have done X," no more than "someone who looks like me could win the lottery" means I could win the lottery.
(5) So the sentence was never really about me, and there's no fact about what I could have done differently.
(6) The same goes for any de re modal claim, so nothing could ever have been other than it was.
(7) That's absurd -- obviously I could have taken the other road.

My friend says the whole thing is just the Humphrey objection and the fix is definately to drop (1), because Lewis changed the subject when he read "could have" as "a counterpart does." I can sort of see that, but I'm not sure the cheating starts there and I can't name where it does. So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent going in, and what does rejecting it cost you? And if (1) is a stipulation rather than a discovery, does the other side -- transworld identity -- actually get to keep my sentence, or do they have their own version of the same problem?
