I am preparing the opening specification for a graduate seminar on the paradox of deontology, and before my students read a single argument for or against agent-centered constraints I want them to have the specification itself: what a theory of such constraints must be able to do in order to count as adequate. Write that specification for me.

Fix the purpose as follows, and let it discipline the whole list. I am not after a theory that is merely true; I am after one that is operable — one that could be handed to a decision-maker who must act now, who cannot be certain of the numbers, and who will afterwards be held to it by people who disagree with her.

Four requirements I would already put on the list, in my own coinage. You may sharpen them, and you should tell me if any is bogus.

1. Contribution-Continuity. As the agent's causal relation to a threat moves along a spectrum — from failing to notice it, to failing to block it at negligible cost, to redirecting a threat that would otherwise pass through something she owns, to originating it herself — the stringency of any constraint must vary either smoothly or at points where the theory can identify a morally significant break. A theory may not merely assert a chasm between doing and allowing and leave the spectrum unmapped.

2. Description-Invariance. Whether a constraint is engaged may not turn on which of two equally accurate descriptions of the same event is selected ("withdrawing the water" rather than "drowning him"; "letting the surge continue" rather than "routing it").

3. Grounding-Pressure. If the theory grounds constraints in the value or the separateness of persons, it must explain why that very value does not equally generate agent-neutral reason to minimize violations of the same constraint by others — which would dissolve the constraint it was invoked to secure.

4. Selective Non-Minimization. The theory must explain why an agent may not violate a constraint in order to prevent more violations of that same constraint by others, while remaining free, and perhaps obliged, to prevent deaths that involve no violation at all. An account that treats these two situations alike has explained too much.

To these add every further requirement you judge genuine. More importantly, identify the demands that are ordinarily smuggled in as requirements but are in fact the preferences of one camp — some assumption about exceptionlessness, perhaps, or about what a theory must do with the agent's own distress, or about matching intuition in every variant of every famous case — and say why each fails to be a requirement at all.

So that the requirements can be shown to discriminate rather than merely to sound plausible, test them against a battery I have constructed. A dam operator can open a spillway that will save five workers in a downstream channel; the diverted water will drown one worker trapped in a maintenance gallery. Variants:

- I-1: The gallery worker is trapped by accident; his drowning is foreseen but forms no part of the operator's plan.
- I-2: The diversion saves the five only because the water is routed through the gallery, so his presence is part of the causal route by which the saving is achieved.
- I-3: The surge is one the operator himself released minutes earlier, in the course of a routine and permitted operation.
- I-4: The gallery worker is a saboteur who is about to open a second sluice that would drown five others elsewhere; diverting the water into the gallery is the only way to stop him.
- I-5: Identical to I-1, except that the operator's instruments give her only 0.6 confidence that there are five in the channel at all.

For each variant, state which requirement it probes and what a theory failing that requirement would predict there.

Present each requirement with a label; a one-sentence statement; the case or consideration motivating it; an explicit failure condition, saying what it would look like for a theory to violate it; and a status marking it non-negotiable, negotiable, or camp-prejudice.

Then do the part I most want. If some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, demonstrate it: set out the subset and give the argument as a short derivation rather than an assertion, then say which requirement must be dropped or weakened to restore consistency, and what the loss costs us. If you think the list is jointly satisfiable, argue that as carefully, and describe what a satisfying theory would have to look like.

Close with a scorecard, of no more than a few sentences per approach, of at most four approaches to constraints — identified by description rather than by author or title, since I want no bibliography or reading list anywhere in your answer — against the finished list.

Before the final line, state in prose which single requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories and why. Then end with one line in exactly this form, containing only the label: Verdict: \answer{label}
