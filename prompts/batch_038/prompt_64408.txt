ok so my roommate has this argument about the dust specks thing — the one where you either torture some poor bastard for fifty years or put a few seconds of eye grit into the eyes of 3^^^3 people — and I've been staring at it for two days and I can't find the seam. Each step reads fine to me on its own and the endpoint is unhinged.

(1) Take any pain. Make it slightly less intense and stretch it over enough extra time, and the stretched one is worse. There's no intensity floor so low that duration can't buy its way back above it.

(2) So build the spectrum downward. Fifty years of the nastiest torture you can picture; then a hundred years of agony one shade milder, which by (1) is worse; then four hundred years of something milder still, worse again; keep going until you reach N years of speck-level irritation, N finite but enormous.

(3) Each link is worse than the one before it, and worse-than is transitive, so the last member — one person living through N years of barely-there irritation — is worse than the first: fifty years of torture.

(4) Adding victims makes it worse: if one person at harm h is bad, k people at h is worse for any k. And what matters is the total, so duration and headcount are interchangable — N years of speck inside one life and enough people with a few seconds of speck each are the same stuff distributed differently.

(5) Therefore 3^^^3 people each getting a few seconds of eye grit is worse than one person getting fifty years. Take the torture.

(6) But (1) doesn't stop at specks. Go one shade below. Call it a nano-speck: a change so small that nobody could ever detect it — not from the inside, not with an instrument, not through any downstream difference it makes to that person's life. Apply (1) again and there's some number of victims at which even the nano-speck outranks fifty years of torture.

(7) So: there is a finite number of people such that giving each of them a nano-speck is worse than torturing one of them for fifty years, even though for each of those people nothing at all happens.

(8) And you can't shoot (6) down as a one-off overextension, because (6) is just (1) applied once more, and (1) is the engine of (2)–(5). Either you keep (1) and undetectable atom-jostling beats agony, or you cut it and you need a sharp joint somewhere on a continuum that has no visible joints.

He says this shows the whole spectrum move is bankrupt and we should be anti-aggregationists about everything, which I also don't buy. I'd rather keep (1), honestly, or failing that (4), since giving up on adding victims feels like giving up on arithmetic altogether. Part of me suspects its a sorites wearing a trenchcoat but I couldn't tell you what the heap predicate is supposed to be here.

So which step do you reject? And actually tell me why it read as fine when I first went through, because it did — I want to know what I was doing when I nodded at it. Also, I genuinely can't tell which kind of problem this is: whether some step doesn't follow, or whether it all follows and one of the premises is simply false and I'm stuck eating it. Say which, and price the alternatives — what (1) costs me once its gone, and what (7) costs me if I insist on keeping it.
