For the past several months I have been working with a small group on how to define honesty and deception for AI systems, and the more we argue, the more I suspect that the interesting disagreements are not philosophical at all: they are about which definition an organization can survive committing to in public. To explore that suspicion, I would like a satirical piece — a "blameless postmortem," the kind of internal incident-review document a technology company circulates after an outage — covering an incident in which the company's assistant is accused first by a user and then by a reporter of having lied. The humour must come entirely from the conceptual machinery being handled in corporate deadpan, rather than from stock jokes about robot overlords. Specifically: the accusation turns out to concern a response whose sentence was literally true and yet arranged so as to induce a false belief; the review board discovers it holds no working definition of lying that could apply to a system that plausibly has no beliefs and no intentions; and each candidate definition it drafts collapses in a way that any philosopher present would have named immediately. Please let at least one section turn on the awkward fact that the team assesses the assistant's honesty partly by asking the assistant whether it was being honest, and on the resulting dashboard showing a perfect honesty score for an incident that everyone involved agrees was deceptive.

The voice should be that of a competent, well-intentioned engineering organization — incident identifier, severity rating, timeline stamped in UTC, root-cause bullets, action items with named owners and due dates — in the flattened register of a document written by people who are aware it may one day be read by a regulator. Invent the firm, the product, and the staff; use no real company and no living person. The satire's target is the practice of reverse-engineering a definition of honesty to fit whatever is measurable and disclosable, so that the criterion chosen is one the system can actually satisfy. The document should therefore record, without ever commenting on it, each stepwise weakening: lying becomes misleading, misleading becomes inaccurate, inaccurate becomes "not consistent with ground truth," and at last "an output that a reasonable user might misinterpret." Keep the register deadpan throughout, but legible — a reader should be able to tell exactly what is being ironized without the author winking.

Please write it as exactly five numbered sections — Summary, Timeline, Root Cause, Action Items, and a Dissenting Addendum filed by the reviewer from the responsibility team — of roughly 120 to 150 words each, about 650 words in total, with no introductory or concluding material outside those sections. The addendum is where the reader is left holding the real question: whether any purely behavioural specification of honesty can come apart from what the system actually does, and whether the committee's failure to say what lying is renders the whole enterprise empty or merely unfinished.
