I facilitate a monthly reading group that has drifted over two years from academic philosophy into something closer to a practice circle: half the room are grad students, the other half are people using Stoic exercises to get through difficult work, among them a palliative-care nurse and a man I'll call D., who lost his company in a bankruptcy and now spends most of his discretionary income and all of his free time on a climate-litigation campaign that his own lawyers describe as a long shot. D.'s answer to every worry raised in the room is the archer. An archer governs his stance, his draw, his aim, his release; he does not govern the gust that arrives halfway downrange, the target being moved, the flaw in the shaft. So the archer does everything in his power to hit, and then treats hitting as something he merely prefers rather than as the point of the activity. D. says this single image disposes of two objections at once — that the dichotomy of control counsels passivity (it doesn't: the archer still shoots, hard, at a target he may well miss), and that indifference to outcomes is coldness (it isn't: grief over a miss is a category error, like a marksman reproaching himself for the wind). Last week he pushed it further, arguing that the archer shows his campaign to be exactly the right kind of thing to spend a life on, because once the outcome has been reclassified the years cannot be wasted; and he added that if he ever felt despair about the odds, the despair would itself be evidence that he had misclassified something.

I want an audit of that image, not a general defense or debunking of Stoicism. Begin with the correspondence made explicit — archer, bow and shaft, target, the execution of the shot, the wind, the score, the practice rounds — saying which element of D.'s situation each is meant to stand for, and then, harder, which elements of his situation have no counterpart in the archery at all: the fact that other people's fortunes are the stakes rather than his own performance, the fact that the campaign alters the people it concerns, the fact that D. chose his target instead of being assigned one by the rules of a game, and the fact that his aiming extends over years rather than a second. Then sort the disanalogies by whether they genuinely bear on D.'s two conclusions or are merely local color. I have three candidates I can't settle: that an archer's feedback is immediate, visible, and attributable, whereas in a decade-long legal campaign one often cannot tell whether one missed or the target moved and may never learn, so the self-assessment of "aiming well" that the image is supposed to secure may be unavailable to the archer himself; that archery is a repeatable game with internal stakes and cheap misses, so the analogy may be borrowing its plausibility from the low cost of failure in the source domain and quietly transferring it to a one-shot, irreversible undertaking whose cost is borne by others; and that inside the source domain hitting is precisely what archery is for — a bowman who never hits is not a good bowman — which makes it an odd place to source the claim that hitting does not matter.

For each of these, say whether the analogy survives intact, survives with repairs, or collapses, and what the repairs cost. Then tell me what the image assumes that the dichotomy of control is supposed to establish rather than presuppose: in particular, whether "aiming well" can be specified independently of outcomes at all, or whether every test we actually apply to good aiming turns out to be outcome-sensitive, which would leave D. with a criterion he can only apply by consulting results he has declared irrelevant — and if so, whether his position has become something no evidence could embarrass, and whether that is a defect here or a legitimate feature of a view about the proper object of effort. Assess also whether the Stoics' own distinction between the involuntary shock that arrives before judgment and a full-blown passion could rescue a weaker reading of his second claim — no endorsement of despair, but no requirement of equanimity either — and whether D. is entitled to that weaker reading or is explicitly refusing it. Finally, tell me whether some other image would do better work: a helmsman who navigates well without being promised the harbour, a physician treating a patient she cannot cure, a defense lawyer who does not choose the verdict, a gardener, a lighthouse keeper. For each, say what it buys that the archer cannot and what it fails to capture, and whether any of them can carry the strong claim D. wants — that the years cannot be wasted. I don't want sources or suggestions for further reading; I want the argument worked through in detail.
