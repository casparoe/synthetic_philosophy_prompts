I've been reading medieval kalām for about two years now, mostly at night, after a decade spent around prediction markets, calibration threads, and arguments about the difference between conditioning on a variable and intervening on one. Next month I'm facilitating a ninety-minute session of a reading group — eleven to fourteen people, mostly software engineers and data people, two philosophy graduate students, and a couple of practicing Muslims who are there for the argument and not for a costume drama. The session is on causation in al-Ghazālī: the seventeenth discussion of the Incoherence of the Philosophers, together with Ibn Rushd's rejoinder.

I don't want to lecture first. I want the group to run into the argument with no vocabulary in hand. Design an in-class activity that makes participants feel two things simultaneously: that observation ever delivers togetherness rather than production, and that the gap between a connection holding of necessity and a connection having held — and so far as anyone in the room can tell, continuing to hold — cannot be closed by any quantity of data collected from inside the room. At the same time the activity must leave them able to predict, intervene, and score bets competently, because al-Ghazālī is not a skeptic about practice and I emphatically do not want the group walking away with skepticism about induction as the takeaway.

Concretely, I need:

- An apparatus I can build in twenty minutes from index cards, tokens, dice or a spinner, and a whiteboard ledger. State the hidden generative structure precisely: the variables, what interventions are purchasable, what the arbiter or scheduler role does, and exactly when and by what rule the habit is broken, so that the departures are principled rather than arbitrary or theatrical.
- Rules and a betting-and-intervention scheme with payoffs that require no real money. I'll award beans and let the winning side choose next month's reading.
- A timeline for the ninety minutes, including the point at which the text itself is introduced.
- A debrief that maps named moments of play onto named components of the position: the attribution of necessity to the philosophers, the slide from togetherness to production, al-Ghazālī's weaker concession that things do have natures which are themselves given and sustained, and Ibn Rushd's objection that if this is right there is no fixed knowledge of anything at all.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I already suspect two failures: an accusation that the facilitator is cheating, which I think is the philosophically correct reaction and should be channeled rather than deflected; and a statistics-minded participant who models the departures as a mixture distribution and thereby dissolves the intended effect within ten minutes. Tell me how to run both, and give me a fallback if a third of the room simply refuses to bet.
- One honest paragraph on which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and which I will have to supply myself. My guess is the theology: I want the argument staged so that it works with a deliberately neutral hidden arbiter, and I want the debrief to raise, not resolve, the question of what al-Ghazālī actually gains by putting God in that slot.

Nobody should hear the words occasionalism, tahāfut, or al-Ghazālī until the activity is over.
