So, I run the volunteer mod team for a VR hangout world — about 600 regulars, some of whom are basically in there every night, and last month we banned a guy who then wrote a genuinely good appeal about consistency, and I couldn't answer him. That's when I realized our rulings are all vibes. We've started a shared doc trying to write down actual reasons, and it's turned into a mess, partly because when I list out my own verdicts case by case they don't hang together, and partly because my sister keeps saying I'm drafting a legal code for pixels. Here's where I actually land: A player got into someone else's account and vaporized a crafted jacket that took her two years to make — I'd call that real harm and real wrongdoing, and I'd say "theft" without scare quotes. That same jacket won fair and square by a stranger under the game's own duel-and-loot rules — no wrong, nothing to appeal, even though the original owner felt at least as bad about it, maybe worse since she was there watching it happen. Our devs pushed a balance patch deleting the entire jacket class and compensated everyone in currency at roughly market value — annoying, but not a wrong, which is strange because in total the playerbase lost more stuff than in the first case. Somebody's 400-hour build in our sandbox corner: a real artifact and a real achievement, worth protecting. But when the studio rebuilt the engine and the identical build got regenerated from the save file, I felt nothing had been lost at all, so it can't be about the particular bits. A guy verbally went after a regular in a voice room using the exact same words he'd used in a text forum a week earlier where I did nothing — in the room it felt like assault, in the forum it was rude, and I think the difference is presence and the fact you can't look away. A player in single-player content killing NPCs who beg for mercy, nobody else involved, nothing persistent — strictly speaking nobody was harmed and nothing wrong happened, and yet I'd want to know about someone who does that a lot. Compare: a player built a photorealistic avatar of a real streamer who'd banned him from her chat and used it to enact humiliating things in a private instance; she never found out, zero effect on her; my verdict is that this was wrong. Those last two fight each other and I can't explain why. Also, if someone asks me whether there's an oak tree in the plaza I say obviously yes, there's a virtual tree, but if an engineer asks how many trees are in that biome I just read off whatever the spawner reports and think of them as rows of data — both feel natural and I can't tell you which one I mean when I say the tree exists. Real money spent on a virtual lamp that the studio later removes feels like a taking; 5,000 in-game coins for a virtual coffee feels like no transfer of value at all. And a regular who talked every night for a year to an AI-driven companion NPC we eventually deprecated: I think her grief was appropriate and not a mistake, but I don't think the NPC lost anything or was harmed.

Is there a principle — or a small set of rival candidates — that actually generates these verdicts? I want it precise enough that I could hand it to another mod and get the same ruling twice, so please state each clause and tell me which of my cases motivated it. Then run it on the two I keep flip-flopping on: a player running an in-game casino that's quietly rigged, takes only in-game currency, discloses nothing, and has cleaned out three teenagers' worth of a year of grinding — under my own "coins aren't value" verdict nothing was taken, but it feels like fraud; and a player who recorded a private conversation in our world and posted it to an outside forum, where I think the wrong happened outside the game but the material was made of nothing but game. And give me a verdict on something I haven't thought of that would discriminate between the candidates. If the only way to save everything is a baroque multi-clause monster, say so, and instead tell me which one of my verdicts to give up and why — I'd rather have a clean rule plus one admitted mistake than a rule nobody can apply. And if your honest answer is that all of this just tracks local social convention inside our community, fine, say that, but then I want to know what makes it more than etiquette, because the appeal letter is still sitting in my inbox. No bibliography needed, I'm not writing a paper, I just need something to put in the doc.
