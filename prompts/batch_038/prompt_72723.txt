You are an expert in both international criminal law and the moral philosophy of atrocity categories — someone who has spent real time on the places where the legal definition of genocide strains against ordinary moral judgment. Please think carefully before answering: begin by brainstorming two or three candidate principles in rough form, then test them against my cases and settle on the one you think survives.

Some context for why I am asking. I have just started at a small advocacy organization whose reports use the term genocide, and my first assignment is to draft the determinations section for a set of situations. Working through them, I discovered that I already have firm verdicts, and I want to know whether they add up to a principle I could defend in public.

Here are my verdicts. A regime that kills well over a million of its own people, selecting victims chiefly as class enemies and political suspects, is not committing genocide on my view — but when that same regime turns on one ethnic and one religious minority within the country and kills them as such, that part is. A campaign in which almost nobody is killed — schools shuttered, a language banned in public life, children taken from their families and adopted out — is genocide, on my view, even though the group's death rate is unchanged. A famine deliberately concentrated on a region whose peasants are also a distinct national group, engineered by a state that knows exactly who will starve, is genocide; I am fairly confident but not certain. Systematic rape of one group's women by another group's men, conducted so that the children are born into the perpetrators' group, is genocide. Killing the men of military age from a single town and expelling everyone else — the group survives, in large numbers, elsewhere — is genocide, and I would not budge on this. Yet destroying a group's homeland outright, flooding it or mining it until return is impossible, so that its way of life ends while its members survive and reproduce perfectly well in diaspora: not genocide, merely something terrible. And a state whose indifference predictably kills members of one group in numbers exceeding several acknowledged genocides, with nobody aiming at the group's destruction as such: not genocide, however many dead.

The pair that worries me is the town case against the homeland case and the indifference case: in one, the group survives and I call it genocide; in the others, the group survives, or dies in great numbers, and I do not.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that a colleague could apply it to a situation neither of us has seen. For each clause, say which of my verdicts it exists to deliver. Then tell me what it yields for three situations I have not listed: forced mass conversion of adults with no killings and no child transfers; a settlement enterprise that does not target a group as such but whose expansion makes the group's continued existence impossible and is pursued anyway; and the killing of a group's leadership and intelligentsia only, with the population at large spared. Finally, be honest about the cost: which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, which one you would have me give up, and why that loss is cheaper than the alternatives. The verdict I keep going back and forth on is the engineered famine, so please say explicitly what your principle does with it and whether it forces a decision.

You do not need to cite instruments, decisions, or authors — I want the reasoning, not the apparatus.
