I am a second-year graduate student in political theory, and this week I have to deliver a twenty-minute commentary to our department's early-modern reading group on the chapter of the second part of Hobbes's Leviathan that deals with what feeds a commonwealth and with how it sends out colonies — the passage, in other words, where Hobbes turns from the founding of a commonwealth to its nutrition and its procreation. The group is an awkward mix: two members who work on Hobbes, three who work on contemporary identity and persistence and read early modern texts only under protest, and me. My assignment was to explain a paragraph that reads, in context, as a digression: Hobbes breaks off from discussing provisions and plantations to raise a puzzle about ships and rivers. What follows is my own modernization of that stretch, written out so the logical articulation is visible. I have deliberately not reproduced the seventeenth-century syntax, and I have stopped short of the rest of the paragraph, because I suspect the sentence or two that follows does further work and I would rather be told whether my reading survives either way. Here is the rendering. First: concerning the individuation of a river, or of any other movable thing, only one difficulty arises. Second: suppose that a ship, after long sailing, has had so many of its parts repaired that not a single board remains of the ones it was built from; is it the same ship, or not the same? Third: and is the ship that sailed out of the harbour the same as the ship that sailed back into it, or are they two? Fourth: if that ship is the same, and yet its form has changed, the form it had at departure not being the form it had at return, then those who find fault with so many alterations ought, even more readily, to grant that a river, which over any stretch of time is perpetually changing, remains many rivers at once; which, he says, is absurd.

What I need is help understanding what that fourth step is doing and why it sits where it sits. Specifically: (1) What is the logical form of the move? Is it a reductio against a criterion of identity stated in terms of form, a reductio against the people who find fault with the alterations, or a demonstration that those people are committed to an absurdity they would disown — and who exactly is the target, given that Hobbes is arguing against scholastic accounts of individuation elsewhere in his work? (2) In my rendering I have written the word as form. Should that be read as the configuration or arrangement of parts, or as a substantial form in a recognizably scholastic sense, and does it matter for the argument? My suspicion is that the second reading imports a framework Hobbes is in the business of dismantling, but I cannot tell whether that suspicion is exegetically responsible or simply me preferring the tidy answer. (3) Are the second and third steps one puzzle or two? The third does not obviously ask whether a ship persists through replacement; it asks whether the ship that left and the ship that returned are two. Is that a question about diachronic identity at all, or about what makes something one thing rather than two — and if the latter, what does the opening talk of individuation amount to, given that the contemporary literature tends to run these two questions together? (4) Why is this paragraph in a chapter about nutrition and procreation? I want an account of what work the digression does for Hobbes's view of political identity, including his treatment of colonies as the children of a commonwealth and of civil strife as what kills one. If the ship case is meant to constrain the case of the commonwealth, in which direction does the constraint run: does the commonwealth survive the replacement of its subjects the way the river survives the replacement of its water, or does its identity ride on the persistence of something else?

Two diagnoses were offered at last week's session, and I want you to tell me which is better supported by this text rather than which is more fashionable. One member argued that Hobbes is a conventionalist or nominalist here, that there is no single mind-independent fact about whether the repaired ship is the same ship, which is why he can be so relaxed about the case and which would explain why the river clause registers as absurd only relative to a way of counting we have adopted. Another argued that Hobbes is a proto-perdurantist: the ship that sailed out and the ship that sailed in are temporal parts of one four-dimensional whole, and the apparent puzzle dissolves once we see that he is not asking the question we assume he is. I would also like you to flag anything in my modernization that may have built in an answer — in particular, whether writing the same ship and the same river, in places where the original may not license a univocal use of that word, has quietly settled a question I mean to be asking. Please lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my questions. Aim for roughly a thousand words, since I have one slide and about fifteen minutes of whiteboard time before people start objecting. I am not after citations or a bibliography and will chase down the secondary literature myself, but I do need you to be scrupulous about separating what is genuinely settled among people who know this text from what is contested or merely plausible: if a claim about Hobbes's intentions or about how his commentators treat this passage is something you are reconstructing rather than recalling, say so plainly, and do not present a disputable reading as though it were the consensus. Finally, where you think the continuation I have omitted would decide between the readings, tell me what to look for when I go back to it.
