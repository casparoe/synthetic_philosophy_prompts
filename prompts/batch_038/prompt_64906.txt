My advisor says the argument in chapter 3 of my thesis refutes itself and I can't see how. Cleaned up, it goes:

(1) Choosing rationally is picking the option that's best, all things considered.

(2) Looking for better options costs — time, effort, and the offers that expire while you're still looking.

(3) So what's best all things considered is whatever maximizes value net of search cost, and a rational chooser maximizes that.

(4) But you can't tell which option maximizes value net of search cost without knowing what more looking would have turned up, and knowing that just is doing the looking.

(5) So applying (3) needs the very information whose cost (3) tells you to economize on.

(6) Any procedure that escapes the circle has to fix a threshold or a stopping point in advance — take the first candidate above X, or look at three and pick the best of those — and fixing it in advance means settling for whatever turns up even if one more look would have found something better.

(7) So the maximizing standard can only be followed by satisficing.

(8) And the circle isn't special to maximizing. You can't tell which decision procedure is best without trying them, and trying them costs the same way. So no procedure is rationally required and none is ruled out.

(9) Hence nothing counts as an irrational way of choosing.

I want to keep (1) and (2) — they seem obviously true and its what the whole chapter rests on. Which step should I reject, what does rejecting it cost me, and does (8) survive even if (7) goes?
