so i'm in a consciousness reading group that meets Thursday evenings in someone's kitchen, and one of the members — a postdoc in a vision lab, not a philosopher — has built this argument that he reckons demolishes the whole phenomenal/access distinction. Three weeks of him repeating it and I still can't say what's wrong with it. It starts from the global workspace picture, which most of us are happy to grant for the sake of argument, and ends up somewhere nobody in the group actually wants to live.

(1) A mental state is conscious in the phenomenal sense only if its content is globally broadcast — that is, made available to the systems that do reporting, reasoning, and voluntary control.
(2) If some content were experienced without being available in that way, then for the subject it would be indistinguishable from content that wasn't experienced at all: it could never be reported, never be used, never be noticed as missing when it went.
(3) Where two situations could not differ in any respect, there is no fact of the matter about which of them obtains.
(4) So there is no experience that outruns availability. No overflow. Phenomenal consciousness just collapses into access consciousness.
(5) Now run the same thought inside one subject at one time: at any moment what you experience is what's available to you then, and what's available then is a small fraction of what's being processed.
(6) The partial-report results say exactly this. After a brief display people can name three or four items at will, but cue one row and they do far better on it — so the whole array was sitting in some high-capacity, fast-decaying store, and only the cued bit ever got in.
(7) But there's nothing special about letters. At any given moment I'm reasoning from, and could report, maybe a handful of things: the shape of your face, the tail of what you just said, the rough layout of the room. So by (5) the hum of the fridge, my feet inside my shoes, everything out in my peripheral field — none of it is being experienced.
(8) So conscious experience at any moment is radically sparse, practically a few items floating in nothing.
(9) But that flatly contradicts what it's like to look around a room: a filled-in, detailed, continuously present scene. So either the workspace picture is false, or it's true and refutes itself, since it entails a phenomenology no one has.

He says (9) is introspective confabulation and he's perfectly happy to bite that bullet. I'm not, but I also can't point at the step that's doing the damage, which is why I'm asking. What I want: name the step or steps you'd reject, explain why each one looked so innocent going in, and then price the alternatives properly. If I let go of (2) and (3), what have I signed up for on zombies and inverted spectra, and are those two premises really as free as he presents them? If I block the move into (7), do I still have enough of a workspace theory left to be worth calling one, or have I just conceded the thing he was arguing for? And if I reject (9), what's left of phenomenology doing any explanatory work at all? I'm attached to (6), honestly — the partial-report stuff is what got me into this in the first place — and I'd rather not just wave the richness intuition away as an illusion if I can avoid it. The step I keep circling is (9), but that's only because it's the one whose conclusion I don't like, and I know that isn't a reason.

British English please, and don't send me off to a reading list — I want the reasoning laid out, not pointers to where I might find it.
