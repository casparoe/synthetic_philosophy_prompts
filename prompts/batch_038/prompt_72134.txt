I've been reading around advance directives for a few months now and I keep running into the same shape of argument without having a word for it. Someone writes down, while clear-headed, what they want to happen if they reach a certain state — advanced dementia, say, or a bad manic episode. Later they reach it and report, sincerely, that they're fine, actually pretty happy, and don't want the thing they asked for anymore. Then the people arguing about the case split into two camps: one says the current contentment shows the earlier self got the prediction wrong, they had no idea what that state would feel like from the inside. The other says no, that contentment is precisely the adaptation the directive was written to anticipate, so counting it as evidence against the directive is circular.

It's that second camp's move I can't find a name for. Not preference change in general — the specific case where the change was predicted by the person making it, in advance, and pre-emptively written off as not counting. Two more so you can see where the edges are: a recovering alcoholic who, sober, tells his friends not to lend him cash no matter how convincing he sounds later; and someone diagnosed with Huntington's who writes that her family should ignore her protests that she's "not ready yet."

Is there an established concept for this, and for the further question of whether the advance discounting actually works? What are the nearest neighbours — I keep bumping into stuff about adaptation, about tying yourself to the mast, about the earlier self versus the later self — and how exactly does each differ from this? Where does one phenomenon stop and two start? I'm not after a label, I want the carving. And please separate what's settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely still disputed; I don't want to walk away thinking one contested position is the consensus.
