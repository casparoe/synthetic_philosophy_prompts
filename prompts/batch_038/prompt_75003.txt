I am drafting a short paper for a political philosophy workshop and would like sharp critical comments on it — not a summary, and not encouragement. The draft follows; my specific questions come after it.

**The Dormant Draft: Conscription in Abeyance and the Problem of Unexercised Power**

The philosophical debate about conscription is conducted almost entirely on one metric: interference. How many people are compelled, for how long, on pain of what penalty. On that metric an all-volunteer force is the least coercive arrangement, universal conscription the most, and a selective draft sits somewhere between, its cost varying with its selectivity. My claim is that this metric is too coarse to distinguish three actually existing European arrangements, and that once it is refined the rankings shift in a way that matters.

Take Finland. Roughly four-fifths of each male age cohort serves; the service period runs from 165 to 347 days, civilian service is a year, and refusal of both draws a fixed custodial term of about six months. Interference here is maximal. Yet the claim the state holds is almost wholly non-discretionary: it attaches by rule to everyone in the cohort, on a published schedule, with an appeal to a standing board, and the state cannot plausibly aim it at particular people. A Finnish conscript knows to within a month what will be taken from him and when.

Now take Sweden between 2010 and 2017. In 2010 conscription was not repealed but paused: the statute creating the general duty to serve was left standing, and in 2017 the government reactivated it by decision, citing the changed security environment, without new primary legislation. The reactivated system surveys an age cohort of roughly 100,000 and takes on the order of 4,000 for a year of training — and the duty is not symbolic, since the first custodial sentences for draft evasion since reintroduction were handed down in 2019. Interference in any given year is small. What the citizen carries instead is a standing liability: the state holds a switch, and the switch is already wired.

Switzerland supplies a third configuration. Those who do not perform service pay an exemption tax — three per cent of taxable income, up to a capped number of years — and each day of civil protection service credits four per cent off that year's levy. Switzerland, in other words, publishes an exchange rate between days of service and money, which is exactly what the inalienability intuition denies: the idea that the duty to serve is not the kind of thing that can be discharged by paying.

Here is the category I want to isolate. Call it an obligation in abeyance: a duty that has been created, never repealed, and is not presently owed to be discharged, which the duty-holder can activate unilaterally at a time of its own choosing. It is not a mere legal power to legislate later, since no new law is needed. It is not an ordinary conditional obligation, since the condition lies wholly within the obligee's control. It is not a permission, since the obligated party has no matching power to switch it off. I think this category is doing real work in the Swedish case and is simply invisible on the interference metric.

The lens I want to use is Pettit's. Freedom as non-domination is a claim about exposure to arbitrary interference rather than about interference actually suffered, and that is precisely what a dormant draft creates: exposure without exercise. The analogy I keep reaching for is the suspended sentence, or a landlord's reserved right of entry on notice — arrangements that cost almost nothing in interference terms and are nevertheless objectionable in a way interference-talk cannot register.

But this produces a dilemma I cannot yet solve. On Pettit's own criterion, interference is arbitrary only insofar as it is not forced to track the interests and opinions of those subject to it. Sweden's activation was legally constrained, publicly reasoned, applied across a cohort by published criteria, and ratified by a political process that had switched the duty off seven years earlier. So the republican may have to say this is not domination at all, only a highly conditional duty — in which case non-domination collapses back into interference and buys me nothing. If instead the republican insists the dormant draft is domination, the theory is committed to ranking a system that takes four per cent of a cohort worse than one that takes eighty per cent, a verdict few will accept and one I am not sure I can defend. Either way the concept of abeyance seems to lose its payoff.

Comments I would like:

1. Is obligation in abeyance a genuine carving, or a redescription of a conditional duty plus a standing legal power that does no new work? If it does no work, where exactly does it fail?
2. Does the closing dilemma hold, or is there a republican reply — for instance that arbitrariness attaches to the selectivity exercised once the duty is activated rather than to its dormancy, or that the right comparison is Sweden-in-2016 against Sweden-in-2010 rather than Sweden against Finland?
3. Is the Swiss exemption tax a counterexample to the inalienability framing, or is a levy on non-service something other than a price on service — at least for those exempted as unfit, who could not perform at any price?
4. Are the institutional facts stated correctly and still current? If any is wrong, say so, because the whole comparison rests on them.
5. State the strongest objection to this paper that I have not already stated myself, and say whether the paper can absorb it or has to be restructured around it.
