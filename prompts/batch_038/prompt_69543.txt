I spent 35 years doing probabilistic seismic hazard assessment for a national survey — mostly fitting distributions to earthquake catalogs and arguing with structural engineers about whether a 1-in-10,000-year number means anything at all — and since retiring I've been reading philosophy in the evenings, badly and out of order. I've gotten deep into the subjective Bayesian stuff and the Dutch book arguments, and I've been writing down what I actually believe so I can stop fooling myself. There's a reading group at the library; one of the members (retired lawyer, sharp as a knife) said my list looked unstable but couldn't say where, and that's been bugging me. So I'd like a proper audit. Here's the list, in no particular order:

1. Credences are degrees of belief, and a rational assignment of them has to be coherent: if someone could assemble a package of bets you'd each accept on your own terms that guarantees you a loss, your beliefs aren't rational. De Finetti convinced me of this and I think its the whole foundation.
2. The money is only a measuring stick, tho. Incoherence is an epistemic failing, not a matter of prudence — a hermit who is never offered a bet, and would refuse one if he were, can still be flat-out irrational in his degrees of belief.
3. Updating should be by conditionalization, and the diachronic version of the Dutch book argument settles it for me: an agent who intends to revise by some other rule can be trapped by bets she takes now and later, and that's enough to call her irrational.
4. But credences don't have to be sharp. Where the evidence is thin — a newly mapped fault with two historical events on it — a range is the honest state of mind, and forcing a single number is false precision. I think imprecise credences can be perfectly rational.
5. On Sleeping Beauty I'm a halfer: one half. People tell me the halfer is Dutch-bookable, but I don't think a book that makes use of her forgetting shows anything about whether her degrees of belief are rational.
6. I don't accept the Reflection principle in general. If I'm confident that next winter's me, in the middle of a bad spell, will rate some project hopeless, I don't see why I have to rate it hopeless now.
7. Subjectivism doesn't leave me stranded: with enough data any coherent prior converges on the truth, so the long run recovers objectivity. Coherence plus the usual convergence results is all I need, and I don't see why I should assume countable additivity on top of it — that always looked to me like an extra axiom doing no work.
8. Coherence isn't sufficient either, obviously: someone at 0.99 in a flat earth can't be booked and is still a fool. So it's necessary, not sufficient.

Go through this and tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other and which pairs only look like they do — I'm fairly sure I've got at least one of each and I don't want everything flagged as equally sick. Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what would have to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere on the list. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most damage. I'm deliberately not signalling which of these I'd rather keep, and I'm not after references or a history of the debate — I want your reasoning, and where I think you're wrong I'll argue back. One more thing: tell me how confident you are in each verdict, roughly, and then single out the one place in your whole audit where you think you're most likely to be mistaken.
