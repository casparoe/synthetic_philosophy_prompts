I'm preparing for my qualifying exams in political philosophy, and I have spent the week trying to get clear on what responsibility-sensitivity actually adds to egalitarianism. In the process I built an argument that I find genuinely disturbing, because it seems to show that the addition is nothing at all — that the view collapses in two opposite directions at once — and I could not find the exit. My adviser told me to write it out carefully and come back with a diagnosis rather than a rebuttal, so that is what I am asking for. Let me set out a case first, then the argument.

Case. Ana and Bo work the same shift in the same warehouse and are offered the same overtime at the same rate. Ana takes it, and does so for eleven consecutive weeks; Bo declines every time. Over a decade the difference compounds: Ana puts down a deposit on a flat, Bo does not. Suppose the social-scientific story is complete and uncontroversial. The divergence is fully accounted for by differences in temporal discount rates, in sensitivity to fatigue, and in the caregiving obligations each inherited, together with a good deal of situational noise about which supervisor made the offer on which day. Nothing in the story mentions anyone's freedom, and nothing in it is in dispute.

Argument. (1) On luck egalitarianism, an inequality between two people is unjust just in case, and to the extent that, it does not reflect their own responsible choices. (2) A distribution reflects someone's responsible choice only if she could have chosen otherwise in a sense that excludes the choice's being fixed by factors outside her control. (3) By the case above, and by any relevantly similar case, the factors that fix what people choose are genetic endowment, upbringing, situational cues, and stochastic noise, none of which they selected. (4) So no choice anyone makes is a responsible choice in the sense (2) requires, and every difference in lifetime prospects between any two people is a matter of brute luck. (5) By (1), then, every inequality is unjust, and the entire content of luck egalitarianism is the demand for equality of outcome. (6) The same reasoning runs the other way at the same time: if no choice is responsible, then no holding is entitled by anyone's choice either, so the argument disposes of every entitlement- and consent-based theory that shares the premise; and it applies with equal force to the choices of the officials and citizens who would carry out the leveling, so the theory can pronounce a distribution unjust without being able to say that anyone's choices bear on repairing it. (7) Luck egalitarianism therefore faces a dilemma. Accept (2) and it collapses into plain outcome egalitarianism, losing the distinction it was built to draw and dragging every other choice-tracking theory down with it. Deny (2) and either it reduces "attributable to the agent" to "causally connected to the agent," in which case every caused choice is equally attributable and the distinction still does no work, or it reintroduces a freedom condition, in which case (3) is waiting for it again. (8) Therefore the choice/luck distinction cannot do the work luck egalitarians assign to it.

I would rather keep (1) and (3) than (2), since (3) looks like ordinary social science and (1) is only a statement of the view under discussion; what I cannot tell is whether giving up (2) leaves me with anything worth calling responsibility. So: which step or steps do you reject, and why did each of them look unobjectionable to me when I wrote it down? Please be specific about the reading on which each rejected step is false and the reading on which it is true, since I suspect at least one of my premises is doing double duty. Then price the alternatives. What does the luck egalitarian who denies (2) owe by way of an account, and what does she pay for each of the familiar replies — that the distinction is normative rather than metaphysical, that identification or endorsement rather than origination is what makes a choice one's own, and that the whole worry confuses the justice of a distribution with the blameworthiness of an agent? Finally, say explicitly whether (7)'s dilemma is exhaustive, and if it is not, which horn you are rejecting and what the third option costs.
