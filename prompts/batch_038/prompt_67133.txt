I am in the fourth week of an upper-level epistemology seminar on testimony, expertise, and the evaluation of conspiracy claims, and one maxim has done an enormous amount of work in our discussions: "ask who benefits," or, as two of my classmates prefer to put it, "cui bono." Our seminar leader deploys it to introduce cases, the readings we have been assigned seem to deploy it to close arguments, and yet in four weeks nobody has said where it comes from, what it licenses, or what would count as violating it. I have taken one course in formal epistemology, so priors and likelihood ratios are fine by me, and I have a vague sense that the maxim is either trivial (some identifiable party stands to gain from nearly any event worth discussing) or an enthymeme with at least three suppressed premises. What I cannot yet tell is whether those are two versions of the same objection or genuinely different ones.

Last Tuesday gave me a concrete case, and I want the analysis anchored to it rather than floating free of examples. We were discussing the Piltdown material: as I have it, an amateur antiquarian named Charles Dawson produced skull fragments and an apelike jaw from a Sussex gravel pit around 1912, presented the finds jointly with a senior anatomist at the British Museum, and the assemblage was accepted for roughly four decades as a candidate missing link, until tests in the 1950s showed the pieces to be of mismatched ages, the jaw to be that of an ape with its teeth filed flat, and the bone to have been artificially stained. Our leader remarked in passing that the case has generated a whole literature of competing suspects. One classmate then argued that Dawson is the obvious culprit precisely because he was the man whose reputation stood to gain from a spectacular discovery, and that this is exactly what the maxim is for. A second classmate used the same maxim in the opposite direction, arguing that the forgery survived forty years of scrutiny because British science benefited from having a homegrown candidate for the missing link, so nobody with the standing to check had an interest in checking. Both moves were accepted as reasonable and neither was examined, and I have been asked to write the seminar paper on precisely this exchange.

Could you lay out for me the distinct precise claims that "ask who benefits" might be expressing, as a menu rather than a single favoured interpretation? For each candidate reading I would like the parameters fixed explicitly: what counts as a benefit (actually realised, foreseen, merely likely, or structural in a way nobody intended); whose benefit is in question (an individual agent, an institution, a diffuse class); over what candidate set the maxim is supposed to discriminate; and what work the benefit is being made to do — a probabilistic reason to think the beneficiary caused the event, a filter that trims the hypothesis space before any evidence is weighed, a heuristic for allocating scarce investigative attention, or a stand-in for a functional explanation on which the benefit accrues to no one who brought it about. Then give me your verdict on each reading: platitude, substantive and defensible, or false; and where a substantive reading needs extra premises to be at all plausible, name the premises and say whether they are the kind of thing one could ever check. Finally, tell me which readings were actually available to each of my two classmates — whether the argument about Dawson gains anything that the ordinary physical evidence does not already supply, and whether the argument about the forty years is covertly sliding from the benefit of an agent to the disposition of a collective. Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want your analysis, and I have no way of checking references.

Format, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — five or six sentences giving the shape of your answer, including which single reading you take the maxim to carry in most ordinary use.
2. **The readings** — a numbered menu of the precise claims, each stated in one or two sentences with its parameters spelled out; roughly 700 words.
3. **Verdicts** — one short paragraph per numbered reading, classifying it as platitude, substantive and defensible, or false, and saying why; roughly 500 words.
4. **Back to Piltdown** — applying your menu to each of my classmates' arguments in turn; roughly 350 words.
5. **The maxim I can keep** — the strongest defensible version stated in one sentence I could say out loud in seminar, plus the two questions one has to answer before applying it; roughly 150 words.
