So me and two friends are putting together a little zine of philosophical fiction, and I want to do one on higher-order theories of consciousness — the family of views where a mental state is conscious not because of anything intrinsic about it but because the subject has some further state, a thought or a perception-like representation, about that very state in herself. Transitivity, roughly.

Write me a short story, somewhere around 1500 words, set in a long-term care unit that's running a trial of an implant that doesn't produce any sensations whatsoever. It only ever produces the second layer: a representation, inside the patient, that they're in such-and-such a state. Its got two modes. In attuned mode it reads genuine first-order activity and generates a matching higher-order state about it. In generative mode it can put out a higher-order state with nothing underneath it at all — a state about a state that isn't there.

I want the story to actually work through the problems with this kind of view, but as story, not as a lecture. Nobody should ever give a speech about philosophy. Specifically, build in:

- a scene where a patient in generative mode reports a vivid, awful pain while the tech watching the first-order channel sees it sitting flat and silent, and the unit has maybe twenty minutes to decide whether to treat her for it;
- a calibration fault where the implant's higher-order states start getting aimed at the wrong target — at the implant's own states, or at the mug the patient is holding — so she begins describing what it's like for the mug. I want the characters to react to this the way the classic rock objection would want them to, but in their own words, and don't name any philosopher;
- a firmware update that switches the device from actually producing those second-layer states to merely having them available, ready to go off if the situation calls for it, and the fight between a nurse who thinks the patients are exactly as conscious as before and one who thinks the update just switched off everybody's inner life;
- an ending where a human decision does get made — somebody flips a switch, or refuses to — but the theoretical question doesn't get settled.

Constraints: first person, told by the technician. Pick a tense and stick to it. No robots asking whether they're alive, no simulation reveals, no dream-within-a-dream. The patient should describe something from the inside at least once, and it should be strange and specific rather than generic. And try not to use the words qualia, sentience, or consciousness more than twice between them — let the idea show up in the machinery and the arguments instead. Give it a title.
