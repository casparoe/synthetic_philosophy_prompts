Last Thursday I sat through the same five-word slogan being deployed twice, to opposite conclusions, and I have not managed to think about much else since. In the afternoon a visiting speaker on language models and what Dennett called counterfeit people leaned on "competence without comprehension" to close off a question from the floor: yes, the system's conversational behaviour is remarkable, but that is precisely what one should expect from a process that has accumulated enormous competence without ever acquiring comprehension, so there is no residual mystery to explain and no candidate for moral standing in the offing. At the wine hour a fellow graduate student used the identical phrase in the other direction: since all competence, ours included, was assembled by processes that comprehended nothing, the demand that a system exhibit comprehension before we credit it with understanding is a residue of the dualism the slogan exists to dismantle, and the slogan therefore cuts against the person who invoked it. Both plainly took themselves to be repeating the same claim. I help convene the reading group that hosted the talk, and the convenor has asked me to circulate a short concept note before our next session so that the phrase stops doing unexamined work; I would rather that note be a menu than a verdict, because I am not yet sure I know which verdict is right.

What I need is a disambiguation. Please lay out the distinct precise theses the slogan could be expressing, keeping apart at least two axes that I strongly suspect were being run together. On one axis, what is comprehension supposed to be here: the capacity to state, and to be moved by, the reasons for one's own output; a representation of why things are as they are rather than reliable tracking of the fact that they are; a self-model, so that the process is in some sense transparent to itself; conscious experience; or nothing more demanding than robustness under counterfactual perturbation and the ability to answer novel questions about one's own performance. On the other axis, what work is "without" doing: that comprehension could not have been present at any point in the history that produced the competence; that it is not necessary for the competence; that it is empirically absent in the case under discussion; or merely that no appeal to comprehension is needed in the correct explanation of that case. I would also like readings on which the slogan is a thesis about the order of explanation rather than about any individual system, readings that concern diachronic processes such as evolution and training rather than the finished artefact, and readings on which it collapses into something definitional or unfalsifiable — for each of these, say what it would take for it to be wrong, and whether it admits of degrees.

Then tell me which readings do which work. Specifically: which of them would actually license the afternoon speaker's move from impressive behaviour to no understanding and no moral claim; which would license the wine-hour retort; which are neutral between the two; and whether there is any pairing of readings on which the two speakers genuinely contradict each other rather than talk past one another. I would also like a double-standard check on every reading under which the model fails: apply the same criterion of comprehension to ordinary human cases, such as the fluent native speaker who cannot state the rules she follows, the strong chess player who cannot articulate why a move was good, or the competent arithmetician who mis-describes what she is doing, and flag the readings where the standard is one that people routinely fail too. Where a reading is vulnerable in that way, say how its defender might repair it rather than simply scoring the point.

Please organise the note under these headings, in this order: "The slogan and the two jobs it was made to do" (about 120 words); "Readings" (numbered, each stating one thesis in a sentence or two of plain language — aim for seven to nine readings and roughly 700 words in total); "Verdicts" (for each numbered reading, classify it as platitude, substantive and defensible, contentious, or false, with one sentence of justification — about 300 words); "Which use each reading serves" (about 250 words, ending with a direct answer on whether the two speakers contradicted each other); "The double-standard test" (about 200 words); and "What I will circulate" (a single paragraph of about 150 words, drafted so that I can paste it into an email to the group without further editing). Two constraints: no bibliography and no quotations from the literature, since I cannot check them — and I am not asking you to locate where the phrase first appears, as I intend to chase that down myself. Where a distinction has a technical name, define it in your own words instead of invoking the name. British English, please.
