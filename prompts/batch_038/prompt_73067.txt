I sit on the acquisitions and collections committee of a small museum, and I have been asked to draft an internal policy on authenticity: what we may call a work without qualification, what must be qualified on a label, and what we may not acquire at all. Before I can write anything general I need to know what my own accumulated judgments amount to, because at present they exist only as reactions to particular objects. Here they are, as bluntly as I can state them.

1. A forger's pastiche in the manner of a seventeenth-century Dutch master, sold as genuine for decades, now publicly unmasked and exhibited under the forger's own name: the exhibition is legitimate and the pictures are admirable. I would pay a fraction of what the deceived purchaser paid, but I would not call them worthless.
2. Those same pictures hung as by the master: not merely mispriced. I want to say that what I was admiring is no longer there, although the paint is identical.
3. Suppose a modern violin is indistinguishable from a celebrated Cremonese original in blind listening. I think the modern maker deserves the same credit for the same result. Yet I do not think the seven-figure price of the original is a straightforward error, or that its buyers are simply confused.
4. Bronzes cast posthumously from the sculptor's own plasters, authorized by the estate, in an edition of twelve, honestly labeled as posthumous casts: these are the work. I would acquire one.
5. The same estate licensing the museum shop to sell five hundred authorized reductions, on the same kind of paperwork and with the same disclosure: merchandise, not the work. I would not acquire one and would not hang it in a gallery.
6. A painting in which roughly two-fifths of the surface has been replaced by skillful, documented infill after a fire: diminished, even though the result is easier to look at than the charred alternative.
7. A conservator's removal of discolored varnish and later overpaint, revealing what the artist actually laid down: improved, and closer to the work than the object shown to me before treatment.
8. A monument destroyed in a war and rebuilt from measured drawings and photographs, in the original materials and by traditional methods: valuable and moving, but not the thing that was destroyed.
9. A living artist who has assistants fabricate everything to specification and signs the results: fine. The same artist licensing their signature to a manufacturer producing hundreds of authorized objects in their manner: not fine, though the authorizing act is of the same kind.
10. A model trained on a living illustrator's corpus and producing new images in that manner, with the process openly described and nobody deceived: I judge this a wrong to the illustrator and the outputs not works at all. A human copyist openly selling inexpensive studies after the same illustrator: perfectly fine, and I would own one.

My tentative principle is that what matters is whether an object stands in the right causal chain to a particular person's exercise of skill, and that provenance paperwork, price, and the presence or absence of deception are all downstream of that. I suspect this is too simple, and cases 5, 8, and 10 are what make me suspect it.

What I want is the principle, or the short list of principles, that actually generates these verdicts. State each candidate precisely, clause by clause, and say which of my cases each clause exists to accommodate. Then test the candidates on four cases I have not decided: a workshop picture largely executed by an assistant but signed and sold by the master as his own during his lifetime, which we must now label; a painting the artist destroyed in his twenties and re-made from memory forty years later, better than the original; a celebrated violin so thoroughly re-graduated, re-necked, and repaired over three centuries that almost none of its original material survives, though its identity has been continuously maintained; and a photograph printed after the photographer's death from the photographer's own negative, by a master printer, following the photographer's documented instructions.

Tell me which of my verdicts the best candidate cannot save, and say plainly which of them you would have me abandon rather than complicate the principle to protect. I am especially interested in two tensions I cannot resolve: 4 against 8, since both involve later-made material produced from the maker's own record and honestly disclosed, yet one is the work and the other is not; and 3, where I seem to credit the maker exactly as the market credits the object, which may be incoherent. If either verdict is simply confused, say so and say what would show that it is not.

Because this ends up as label text, also address two consequences: if the principle makes an object's standing depend on facts invisible in the object, what does the institution owe a visitor who cannot see them; and is the principle gameable by a fabricator skilled enough to disclose everything and still produce the work?

I would rather have your own formulation than a survey of what has been written on forgery and authenticity; please do not supply references or reading recommendations. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which of my cases, read differently, would push you toward a different principle, where in this reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong, and — if you conclude that authenticity as I use it names a bundle of unrelated properties rather than a single one — what would count against that conclusion.
