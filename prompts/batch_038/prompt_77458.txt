You are a professional philosopher who works on theories of well-being — hedonism, desire-satisfaction accounts, and objective-list theories — and in particular on whether a person can be harmed by states of affairs she never learns about. Please think carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming: generate as many distinct readings of the proverb as you can, from the most innocuous to the most radical, before you evaluate or prune any of them.

Here is my situation. I belong to a monthly discussion group that mixes clinicians and philosophers. Two of our recent meetings turned on the same proverb — "what you don't know can't hurt you" — and I have become convinced that it did quite different work in each, and that the group never noticed the switch.

First meeting: we discussed an elderly woman with advanced dementia whose husband of fifty years died last month. She asks after him several times a day and is distraught on each occasion she is told the truth, but forgets within minutes. Several members defended the care team's policy of saying he is away on business and changing the subject, on the grounds that the truth is re-experienced as a fresh shock each time. One member summed the position up in a single sentence: what she doesn't know can't hurt her.

Second meeting: we took up Nozick's experience machine. The same member argued at some length that hedonism is false, because a life inside the machine lacks something a real life has, and because that lack is a genuine loss for the person living it — she is worse off, he insisted, even though from the inside she cannot tell the difference.

I am not asking you to settle either case. I want a rigorous disambiguation of the piece of language that both cases were made to rest on. Please do the following:

1. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise claims the proverb could be expressing. For each, specify what sort of "hurt" is at issue (occurrent distress? the frustration of standing desires? a set-back to interests that outlast awareness? a lowering of the value of the life as a whole?), and what the scope of "know" is (believing at the time? dispositional knowledge? the mere capacity to be informed? being in a position to be informed?). Then classify each reading as a platitude, substantive but defensible, or false — and where it is false or near-false, give the counterexample that shows it.

2. For each of the two meetings, identify which reading or readings the argument actually needed in order to go through, and say whether that is a reading you would endorse independently.

3. Address the tension I suspect directly. Does the reading that licenses the deception in the dementia case sit comfortably with the reading that makes the machine-dweller worse off without her knowledge? Is there a single theory of well-being that can hold both, or does the group owe a retraction at one of the two meetings? If the two are incompatible, say which claim the member should give up and why, rather than splitting the difference.

4. Flag the close cousins of the proverb that are not the same claim at all — for instance, the thesis that harm requires a subject capable of being harmed at the time, or that only states of affairs a person could in principle become aware of bear on her good, or that what matters is the shape of a life as judged from outside it — since I suspect part of the group's confidence comes from sliding between the proverb and one of these neighbors.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which of your verdicts in parts 1 to 3 is least secure, what argument or case would push you to a different assignment of readings, and where is your reasoning most likely to have gone wrong? I would rather have your uncertainty located precisely than have you sound uniformly confident throughout.
