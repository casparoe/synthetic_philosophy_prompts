I co-teach a small graduate seminar on decision theory, and this term the discussion of ambiguity aversion has ossified around a claim I want to put under pressure. One of my students maintains that the familiar preference for options with stated probabilities over options whose probabilities are left unstated is nothing but pessimism about the unstated ones: an agent who evaluates each option by the worst expected value it could take across the probability functions she treats as live is, on her account, thereby committed to avoiding ambiguity in every choice involving gains alone, so that a strict preference for the ambiguous option can only reflect optimism, a misdescription of the case, or a slip in the arithmetic. I want to hand the seminar a counterexample to work through rather than an assertion to argue about, and I would like you to build it.

Construct a decision problem in which one and the same agent, confronting one and the same source of ambiguity, exhibits the standard pattern in one choice and the reversed pattern in another: she strictly prefers the option with the precisely specified probability when the payoffs are moderate and the favorable outcome fairly likely, but strictly prefers the option whose probability is unsettled when the payoffs are longshots. The specifications are as follows. The setting must be ordinary and realistic — small-business procurement, clinical choice, municipal budgeting, hiring, or something of that grain — and must contain no urns, balls, colored slips, decks, coins, dice, lottery tickets, or other gambling apparatus; the ambiguity has to arise from a situation in which the probability is genuinely unsettled rather than merely unknown to the agent, and you should explain in a sentence or two why that is so rather than a matter of her ignorance. Both choices must draw their uncertainty from the same source and the same kind of source, so that the reversal cannot be attributed to two different species of uncertainty; only the payoff structure and the baseline likelihood may vary. Give every number explicitly, with payoffs at magnitudes a normal person might actually face, and state the specified probability as a definite figure. Keep the scenario itself under roughly 250 words and self-contained, so that a reader who has not seen this request can follow it, and put the analysis after it rather than inside it. Finally, the reversal may not be explained away by the agent's irrationality, her confusion, a framing effect, or an error of calculation on her part: it has to fall out of the structure of the case.

Then show that the case does what I have asked. Run both choices through three evaluators, described functionally rather than by name: an agent with a single probability function and a utility function over outcomes; an agent who scores each option by the lowest expected utility it attains across a set of probability functions she treats as live; and an agent who forms an expectation over that same set using a weighting of her own and brings a distinct attitude toward the spread itself. For each evaluator and each of the two choices, give the arithmetic rather than a summary — I want to see whether the second evaluator really is locked into the standard pattern in both halves of the case, as my student claims, and what the third must look like if it is to deliver the reversal. Afterwards, identify the single structural feature of the case that does the work, say which verdicts change when that feature is removed, and offer the strongest reply available to my student: an argument that the case is misdescribed, or that one of the evaluators has been applied outside its intended range, together with your assessment of whether the reply succeeds. If you conclude that my specifications cannot all be met at once, say so and locate the conflict precisely instead of quietly relaxing one of them. I am after the reasoning, so no references or reading list is needed.
