I run a monthly reading group — two philosophers, several political psychologists, and a handful of interested non-academics — and we are working through the moral foundations account of left–right difference, where the foundations are routinely explained by analogy to taste receptors: everyone comes equipped with the same small set of receptors, and cultures build widely varying cuisines out of them. At our last meeting two members drove the same analogy to opposite conclusions. One said that if the foundations are taste buds, then a moral profile that engages only two or three of them is a deficit in exactly the way that a congenital inability to taste bitterness is — something is missing, not merely differently seasoned. The other said that if the foundations are taste buds, no moral system can be better than another, any more than one cuisine can be better than another, and that the first speaker has smuggled in a ranking the analogy cannot supply.

Please audit the analogy for us. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — receptor, cuisine, chef, palate, the person who cannot taste bitterness, acquired taste, food poisoning — and then identify where the mapping breaks. Distinguish the breaks that matter for these two arguments from the ones that are merely picturesque, say what each side quietly imports from the source domain that the moral case lacks, and give your verdict on whether either conclusion survives. If both fail, propose a better analogy for the foundations: one that makes it possible to ask whether a broader profile is better without begging the question in either direction.
