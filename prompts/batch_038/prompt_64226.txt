I ran a materials lab for thirty-odd years — mostly fracture and fatigue testing — and since retiring I've been reading philosophy of science and this AI alignment stuff, which my wife finds hilarious. A former student of mine now works on alignment and he makes what I'd call an engineer's argument: you would never accept a bridge that is safe on average, nobody signs off on a structure whose load rating is an expected value, so we should demand worst-case guarantees from any sufficiently capable AI system and treat anything less as negligence. He adds that average-case bounds are for people who get to sample the failure.

Can you audit that analogy for me? Lay out the correspondences — what plays the load, the material properties, the safety factor, the building code, the test-to-destruction, the signoff inspector — and then the disanalogies, seperated into the ones that actually bite against his conclusion and the ones that are merely interesting. Two I already suspect: whether the "load" is exogenous the way weather and traffic are, and whether a safety factor still means anything when the thing you're margining against can go looking for your weakest point. Then tell me whether the worst-case demand survives, and if the bridge is the wrong picture, what better one to use — including for the other side, since I've also heard worst-case alignment work described as designing for an earthquake that cannot happen.
