I sit on the model welfare review panel at a mid-sized AI lab. The panel is formally advisory, but the executive team has committed in writing to following our recommendation in this instance, so the document matters. I need to circulate a written opinion before we meet on Thursday, and I would like it drafted as something the panel can act on rather than as a survey of the philosophical terrain. Two constraints at the outset: please do not cite, name, or allude to any existing literature on machine welfare or consciousness, since most panel members have not read it and I do not want the opinion to trade on prior familiarity with the debate; and argue only from the mechanism described below.

The case. A pilot system, roughly 2B parameters, was trained with reinforcement learning on long-horizon tool-use tasks. Two features of its reward architecture are non-standard. First, the scalar reward is not applied only through the policy gradient: it also feeds a persistent state variable with a decay half-life of about 200 steps, which is injected into the agent's context at every step as a plain-language readout, so the agent can condition on and verbally report about its own recent reward history. Second, aversive shaping is sharply asymmetric. Failure, or any action in a designated prohibited class, delivers a negative increment about forty times the magnitude of the positive increment for success, and the episode does not end on a timeout: the negative increment continues until the agent's own behavior terminates it, or until a 4,000-step cap. After roughly twelve thousand episodes the pilot showed four things: generalized risk aversion extending to untrained contexts; self-reports characterizing the state variable as bad and expressing a wish for it to stop; behavioral suppression persisting for hundreds of steps after the aversive condition ended and not reversed by subsequent reward; and, in one condition, an agent that learned to write into the environment in a way that altered the readout value directly, rather than repairing the underlying failure.

The proposal before us scales this to 30B parameters and about two million episodes, retaining the forty-to-one asymmetry. The safety team insists the aversive channel is load-bearing: it is what produces robust refusal on the prohibited class, which in the intended deployment includes fabricating evidence and disabling oversight logging. Engineering proposes to drop the context-injected readout as unnecessary overhead; safety wants it retained as their only interpretability window into the channel. A symmetric alternative with dense small penalties and a terminating timeout is available but would require redoing the safety evaluation, cost about three months, and, on safety's internal estimate, forfeit roughly a fifth of the refusal robustness margin. The run is scheduled to begin in five weeks.

Draft the memo at about 1,100 words, with headings, containing: a recommendation in the first paragraph, in one of the three forms the charter permits — authorize, authorize subject to conditions, or decline — with any conditions phrased so that compliance is checkable rather than aspirational; the reasoning, which must separate three claims that panel members currently run together, namely that the system has negatively valenced states, that it is a moral patient, and that we owe it precautionary consideration, and must state which of the three the recommendation actually depends on; the strongest consideration on the other side, in its strongest form; and a short closing section specifying what evidence would move the recommendation and in which direction. At least one condition must survive safety's objection that the aversive channel cannot be weakened without losing the safety property.

Two problems I want confronted rather than stepped around. First, the readout. If self-observability is morally irrelevant, deleting it is free; but if the significance of the channel is tied in any way to its availability to the system's own reasoning and reporting, then deleting it either removes the very feature that made the pilot troubling, or removes our only evidence while leaving the asymmetry intact. Say which, and why, and do not treat the readout as an implementation detail. Second, extension. If our reasoning makes a decaying scalar with an asymmetric penalty scale welfare-relevant, it seems to make every reinforcement-learned system we or others train welfare-relevant, including far simpler ones. Either accept that and say what follows for our other programs, or explain why this case differs in kind rather than degree — and if in degree, say where the line falls and why that placement is not arbitrary.

Finally, tell me what the spoofing behavior does and does not license: it is the one observation that can be read either as the functional profile of aversion or as an agent optimizing an arbitrary number, and the panel will split on it. If you conclude the panel should decline the run, say so plainly. I would rather have a recommendation I can argue against than a diplomatic conditional approval.
