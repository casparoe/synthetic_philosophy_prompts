I run settlement and reconciliation infrastructure for a card network — fifteen years of sagas, compensating transactions, idempotency keys, orphaned records, the reconiliation queue. I read a fair amount of political philosophy on the side but I've noticed I can never hold both vocabularies in my head at once, so I want to try something.

Take the reparations-for-historical-injustice debate, the whole cluster of it: whether descendants of the enslaved or of people dispossessed by colonial expropriation have a claim at all; whether the state is the right debtor given that none of the people who did it are still around; whether someone who immigrated in 1995 is on the hook; whether the point is material transfer or acknowledgment; and the two wrinkles I find hardest — "you can't put a number on it," and "a lot of present-day claimants wouldn't exist but for the injustice."

Restate that debate in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. In our shop we separate three things everyone else just calls a refund. A reversal is a forward entry that offsets the original, and both stay on the ledger forever. A void treats the original as never having committed, and it's only legal if nothing downstream consumed its effects — if something did, we call it a tainted rollback and it's forbidden outright. Compensation is a new entry whose amount is set by policy, not by the amount of the original. Use those. Also use: participant records, cohorts versus named parties, who bears liability when the originating institution no longer exists, idempotency (paying twice is not paying better), and settlement — the property that at some point a ledger closes and you stop reopening it.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Going move by move through the debate: what translates cleanly, what only translates with a distortion you should warn me about, and what has no expression in a ledger whatsoever. And tell me plainly whether the residue is a defect in my framework — ledgers are engineered to reach a terminal state, moral claims maybe aren't — or whether it suggests the philosophers are fighting about something less substantive than it looks.

One ground rule: don't smuggle. My vocabulary comes preloaded with assumptions — every obligation has a determinate amount, liability gets assigned by contract rather than by causal or moral connection, and every transaction is supposed to terminate. If a claim only "translates" because you quietly imported one of those, flag it instead of doing it.

My own hunch, which I'd like you to attack if it's bad: I think much of the debate is people demanding voids when only compensations are available, and then being baffled that the compensation feels like it doesn't reach the thing.
