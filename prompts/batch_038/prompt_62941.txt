Spent four years as a staffer on an oversight committee and now I'm trying to write something philosophical about it, but I keep hitting a wall.

Here's the pattern I saw over and over, and its not a scandal story, it's just how the machinery runs. An operation gets stood up inside a compartment — special access, need-to-know — and the compartment is drawn so that the people at the top are deliberately not read in on the operational details. Not because they're too busy; the design goal is that they can honestly say they didnt know. Then when the operation goes bad and leaks, three things happen basically at once. The person who actually ran it gets disciplined or referred for prosecution and is treated as a bad apple. The department's lawyers produce a memo — sometimes drafted after the fact — concluding the operation was within existing authority, and the agency adopts the memo as its institutional position without ever saying the actor did anything wrong. And the principal publicly says that they take responsibility for everything that happens in the building, and everyone in the room accepts that as a complete answer and moves on.

So: ignorance engineered on purpose at the top, guilt concentrated at the bottom, retroactive conversion of the act into an authorized one, and a public ritual of responsibility-taking that seems to cost the principal nothing and change nothing.

What theory of political responsibility does that whole apparatus presuppose? I know there's a literature on dirty hands and I know the rough shape of it — the idea that an officeholder sometimes does something genuinely wrong because the office demands it, and is still on the hook for it even when doing it was the right call. But if that's the picture, the machinery I just described looks like it was built so that nobody ever actually has to occupy that position: the wrongness gets relocated down the org chart and the top gets a clean record by construction. So is that a rejection of the dirty hands picture, or a way of implementing it?

Reconstruct the implicit theory. Specifically: what does the practice have to assume about the link between knowledge and blame; about whether responsibility can be subdivided and handed off, or whether it has to stay whole; and about the retroactive memo — is it functioning as a discovery about what the law already permitted, or as a performance that makes something authorized? Tell me which of those assumptions the practice strictly requires versus which are just a comfortable fit that several rival theories could also supply, and tell me whether the responsibility-taking speech is doing any moral work at all in this system or is purely expressive. Also: what rival pictures does the design quietly rule out — is there a view of holding an office under which the compartmentation would itself be the wrong rather than a mitigation, and if so, which of the four pieces you'd have to cut first to build the machinery on that view instead.

Don't give me a reading list or citations, I'm not chasing anything down and I know you'd be guessing anyway. Just the reconstruction and the reasoning.

At the very end put one line starting with Verdict: and then exactly one of COHERENT, INCOHERENT, or UNDERDETERMINED, for whether all four pieces can hang together under a single theory of responsibility.
