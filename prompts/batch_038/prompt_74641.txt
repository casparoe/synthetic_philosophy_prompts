I am in the final drafting of a novel whose third movement is an inquest, and I need a philosophical machine built to specification rather than an illustration. The setting: a frozen-food distribution depot on a tidal river, its high-bay racking served by stacker cranes whose movements are scheduled by an adaptive algorithm that has been tuning its own parameters against throughput for some eighteen months. A maintenance fitter — I have called him Raymer — is killed in an aisle that the schedule reported as clear. Nobody chose between saving him and saving someone else; nobody was asleep at a console; nothing was defective, unlicensed, or concealed. My book appears to endorse the thesis that machine autonomy and opacity hollow out the space in which blame can live, and I would rather discover now than in front of my editor that the thesis is false, or true for reasons that have nothing to do with machines. So please construct the case for me, and then prove to me that it works.

The specifications are these. Write the scenario itself in a single paragraph, in ordinary prose, set in that depot or somewhere like it. Then show how each of the following four accounts handles it, with the reasoning made explicit rather than asserted. First, the control principle, on which an agent can be blameworthy only for what was, or could have been, within her control: this must yield the verdict that no individual is blameworthy for Raymer's death. Second, the tracing account, on which responsibility for an outcome can be inherited from an earlier voluntary choice even when control was lost in between: this must yield the verdict that the operations manager, who elected eighteen months earlier to move the cranes from a conservative to an adaptive scheduling policy, is the person the inquest should be about — and here is the hard joint, because that election must be one a competent, well-informed, conscientious professional would have made. No corner-cutting, no ignored warnings, no quarterly pressure. I want you to tell me honestly whether a trace that begins in a reasonable choice can deliver blame, or only answerability and outcome-responsibility, and what the difference costs a coroner and a widow. Third, the conglomerate account of corporate agency — the view that a firm is a moral agent in virtue of its internal decision structure, its flowchart of offices and its procedural and recognition policies: this must yield the verdict that the firm is blameworthy, and it must reach that verdict without any defective policy to fasten on. If your case requires the company to have been negligent, you have not met the specification; the fault has to be generated by the structure aggregating individually impeccable contributions into a corporate end that no individual holds. Fourth, the problem of many hands: this must yield the verdict that responsibility is real and genuinely distributed, so that no gap exists, while remaining unable to name anyone whom the state could prosecute or whom Raymer's family could intelligibly resent. The net effect I am after is that three of the four accounts insist there is no responsibility gap, and yet the thing my inquest needs — a person answerable as a person for this death — is absent on all four.

Three further requirements. Run the substitution test: replace the scheduling algorithm with a human scheduler holding the same information, the same minutes, the same incentives and the same place in the organisation, and tell me whether each of the four verdicts survives unchanged. If all of them do, say plainly what feature of the case is doing the work in place of the machine's autonomy, and what happens to the case if I remove that feature. Then give me a paired variant — the same scenario with one or two sentences altered — in which exactly one of the four verdicts flips while the other three hold; identify the altered sentence and the feature it toggles. Finally, tell me which account my construction damages most, and how its defender would attack the case: which of my stipulated facts they would call question-begging, and whether they would be right. On that last point, note what counts as cheating, since I have told you what the case is for. Loaded description will not do — you may not smuggle the verdicts into adjectives, and the words unforeseeable, opaque and inexplicable may not carry any of the load. Nor may the gap be closed or opened by the usual cheap devices: no manufacturer's defect, no vendor's misrepresentation, no regulatory violation, no fatigued or distracted operator, no trolley-shaped dilemma, and nothing drawn from vehicles, weapons or medicine. What I want is a case where the verdicts are generated by the theories out of plain facts, so that when my coroner asks who is responsible for Raymer, four respectable answers arrive and none of them is a name she can write on a form.
