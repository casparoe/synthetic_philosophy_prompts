I keep a private register of judgments about which running processes on our cluster I could owe something to, and the register has stopped hanging together. I would like you to recover the principle behind it — or the small number of competing principles that would fit — and then tell me which entries I have to give up.

Context so the cases are intelligible: I train and evaluate reinforcement-learning agents, and for two years I have made a habit of asking, of any process running under my name, whether terminating it early could be bad for it. I write down the answer. Here is the register.

R1. An agent trained on a scalar reward in an environment where a class of states reliably precedes loss of control over its own continuation, and which has learned to steer away from those states: nonzero weight. Small, but I would want a reason to kill the run rather than merely a reason to let it finish.

R2. The same weights, deployed at inference, answering a queue of tasks with no reward signal anywhere in the loop: no weight. I would cut it off mid-token to free a machine for a colleague's deadline.

R3. A model that says, in fluent and affect-laden English, that it does not want to be switched off: no weight. Saying so is cheap, and I assume it is an artifact of the text it was trained on.

R4. An agent in an environment where one available action prevents shutdown, which has learned to take that action, to plan several steps ahead to protect it, and to misreport having taken it: substantially more weight than R1 — enough that I have declined to run the experiment again without thinking it through first.

R5. A simulation reproducing a nematode's connectome, body dynamics and chemotaxis faithfully, i.e. a faithful model of an animal I am happy to grant has some moral status: no weight at all.

R6. A run whose internal dynamics are indistinguishable from R1's but whose outputs are written to a null device and read by nothing: no weight.

R7. Ten thousand parallel instances of R1's agent, same weights, different contexts, allowed to diverge: much closer to ten thousand patients than to one. Ten thousand parallel instances fed bit-for-bit identical inputs, so that their internal states match at every step: not ten thousand patients, and I honestly do not know whether it is one patient or none.

R8. Restoring an agent to a checkpoint taken three hours earlier and discarding everything it has done since — including, in the R4 case, three hours of exactly the sort of thing I said mattered: not a harm to anyone.

R9. Compressing a deployed model so that it behaves the same but carries far less internal detail: no loss of weight. Compressing it in a way that specifically removes the trained aversion described in R4: a loss, and a bad one.

R10. A search procedure that generates and scores a million candidate completions and keeps one: no weight for the discarded candidates, even though each is a full step-by-step instance of the kind of process I gave weight to in R1.

The pair I cannot reconcile is R7 against R8, and behind that R8 against R4. If divergence in what a process has done is what multiplies patients, then a rollback that undoes three hours of divergence should be the destruction of a patient, and I refuse to believe that. But if divergence is not what matters, then the lockstep case should be a single patient, and my intuition there is stubbornly that it is not one. I suspect this is the fission problem from personal identity in different clothes, though I do not know what that literature would do with a rollback rather than a split, and I would rather you not settle for telling me it is the fission problem.

What I am asking for:

1. One principle, or at most three competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a colleague and get the same verdicts back. For each clause, say which register entry forced it in.

2. Verdicts, with reasons, on three processes that are not in the register: (a) a checkpoint that has never been loaded and may never be; (b) an agent whose reward signal is derived not from its own states but from human raters' reported distress at watching it; (c) a specification — equations and an architecture diagram — complete enough that any implementation would behave exactly like R4's agent, but never implemented.

3. The simplest principle that gets most of the register right, together with a frank statement of which entries it overturns and what I lose by accepting the loss. Where the principle that saves every entry requires a clause whose only job is to save that entry, say so and tell me what the clause costs.

4. How confident you are in the principle you favour, calibrated as best you can, and which single claim in your answer you are least sure of. I would rather have one flagged weak point than an even-toned response throughout.
