Retired four years ago after 35 in a materials lab, and only now getting round to the philosophy I always meant to read. I run a small reading group at the local library — six of us, ex-engineers mostly, plus one historian — and next month we're on testimony. Problem is, everyone turns up having skimmed Hume and Reid and announces they're a "reductionist" or an "anti-reductionist" purely on vibes, usually within thirty seconds and usually matching their politics. So I want to build them a sorting instrument instead.

What I'm after: a decision tree of roughly ten concrete cases, all drawn from actual scientific working life — nothing with teleporters or amnesiacs, my group would riot. Each case offers two or three response options, and every branch has to turn on what the person does or assumes in the situation. Nowhere may the words belief, knowledge or justification appear in a question, and no question may hint at which leaf it leads to.

The leaves I want it to terminate in: check-everyone-always reductionism; the local version where trust is the default but monitoring kicks in past some boundary — and here the tree should tell me *where* that boundary sits for whoever's being sorted, since that's the interesting bit; Reid-style credulity, where you only check when something looks off; and the view on which the reason to accept comes from the fact that someone addressed you with it rather than from your evidence about them. If some answer-pattern the cases permit corresponds to no position anyone defends, say so, and say whether that's because it's incoherent or merely unattractive.

Two honesty requirements. First, work in at least two cases where the witness isn't a person — a calibration certificate, an instrument's automated readout, a notebook left behind by a postdoc who's died — and then tell me whether those belong on the tree at all, and if not, what my criterion for excluding them has to be. Second, I suspect local reductionism and the telling-based view come out identical on anything my lot would answer. Flag every pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely separate, and for that pair in particular, name the case I'd have to add to pull them apart.

Finish with the scoring key: for each leaf, the pattern that lands you there, and one line on the awkward commitment they've just signed up for.
