I'm co-teaching a seminar on AI governance this term with a colleague from the law school, and next week I have to put my own package on the table so the students — and she — can attack it. Rather than let them improvise, I want to hand them a written list of what I actually hold, and I'd like it audited properly first. Think of yourself as the most annoying person in the room.

Here's roughly where I've landed on whose values a widely deployed AI system should serve:

1. Neutrality, in the justificatory sense. Anything that's going to be near-universal infrastructure has to have aims that can be justified to citizens without leaning on one particular comprehensive view of what makes a life go well. I explicitly do not mean neutral effects — I think that's incoherent and I'm not after it.

2. Which is why I'm an anti-perfectionist about content: the objectives get built out of what people actually value, aggregated, rather than out of a philosopher's list of goods. Nobody's account of flourishing gets privileged.

3. But actual preferences don't all get equal billing as inputs. Preferences formed under conditions that undermine the person's own capacity to form preferences — deprivation, dark patterns, engagement-maximizing feedback loops, a lifetime of being marketed at — get discounted, because taking them at face value just locks in the conditions that produced them.

4. The aggregation itself should be democratic in form: equal weight per person, not per dollar or per unit of willingness to pay. And I don't think that commits me to cardinal interpersonal comparisons of wellbeing, which I'm pretty sure we can't make in any reliable way.

5. Where value conflict is genuinely intractable — not merely costly, intractable — the system should abstain and hand the question back to human decision procedures rather then resolving it itself.

6. A case I'm confident about: a public-facing health assistant that steers patients toward the better-evidenced treatment option, against their stated ranking, is doing something legitimate. That's about facts, not values, so 2 doesn't reach it.

7. What I worry about most in practice is laundering — designers getting their own values in through the back door via defaults, hyperparameters, which datasets count as evidence, and the choice of aggregation rule.

So: go through this as a set. Which pairs actually pull against each other, and which ones only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by severity — I don't want a flat list of five equally-weighted complaints, I want to know which is fatal and which is cosmetic. For each real one, say what would have to give and what it costs me to give it up, including whether the obvious repair just relocates the problem somewhere worse. And don't tell me which of these I'm least attached to; I've deliberately left that out and I don't want you guessing at it.

No lit review and no reading list, please — I know the terrain, I just want the argument. One more thing: be explicit about how confident you are in each call you make, and end by naming the single point in your whole analysis you're least sure about, and why that one.
