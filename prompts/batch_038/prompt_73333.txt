I run a graduate reading group on self-locating belief, and roughly half the room has met the Sleeping Beauty problem only through summaries. I want a diagnostic instrument that sorts members by what they would actually do inside the experiment, not by which camp they think they belong to. The deliverable is a decision tree, together with the logical space it is drawn from and a scoring key.

Open with a single sentence naming the one fork on which, in your judgement, the whole dispute turns — the question whose answer determines everything else. Then give the instrument. No preamble, no restatement of this request.

**Part A. The space first, the tree second.** Choose two or three axes that generate the debate rather than summarise it — candidates include what an awakening is supposed to tell her, whether information of that kind counts as evidence at all, which updating rule is licensed once she has it, and whether her Sunday-night planning perspective must agree with her waking perspective. Enumerate the combinations of answers those axes permit. Then say, for each combination, whether it is occupied by a live position. For every empty cell, diagnose why it is empty: is it incoherent, so that the answers jointly entail a contradiction; is it merely unattractive, so that the view is consistent but nobody wants its consequences; or is it simply unexplored, so that nothing rules it out and no one has claimed it? Say which of those three it is in each case, and do not let the diagnosis be vaguer than that.

**Part B. The tree.** Between six and eight nodes. Every node must be phrased as a wager with money on the table: a stated price, a stated payoff, a stated moment at which it settles, and a choice she can accept or refuse. The words belief, credence, probability and degree of confidence may not appear in any node. Each node must be answerable by someone who has never heard of the problem, and each must be a genuine fork on which at least two of the leaves in Part D disagree — no node may be a question on which every position gives the same answer.

**Part C. Cases the tree must run on.** At least one node must be drawn from each of the following, and you may not collapse two of them into one question, since positions come apart differently on each:

1. The standard procedure, with a single bet placed before the experiment and settled once when the experiment ends.
2. The standard procedure, with an identical bet offered separately at every awakening and settled separately each time.
3. A generalised version in which the losing branch produces many awakenings rather than two — a hundred, say — and the winning branch produces one.
4. A version in which, on one awakening only, the experimenters tell her which day it is.
5. A version in which she may, on Sunday, commit her future amnesiac selves to a policy she cannot revise.

**Part D. Leaves.** Each leaf carries the standard label if one exists and if you are confident of it — thirder, halfer, the doubled version of halving, the hybrid that accepts some commitments from each camp, the view that no unique answer is licensed, the view that both answers are correct relative to different betting arrangements, and the answer that drops out if the experiment is read as a branching-universe case rather than a single-world one. Attach a proponent's name only where you are sure of the attribution; otherwise describe the view without naming anyone, and say that you are doing so. For each leaf give one sentence on the commitment its defenders find most awkward, and one sentence on what that leaf says about each of the five cases in Part C.

**Part E. What the tree cannot separate.** Flag every pair of leaves that your nodes leave indistinguishable, and for each pair say whether some further wager would separate them — and if so, state it — or whether they are indistinguishable in principle given what the experiment discloses. Do not paper over these.

**Part F. Scoring key.** Close the instrument with a compact table: for each leaf, the string of answers to your nodes that lands on it, so that a reader can be scored mechanically.

**Part G. Where you are most likely wrong.** Say which fork you think is drawn incorrectly, which argument or consideration would make you redraw it, and which leaf you would occupy if you were so persuaded. Also say where your list of empty cells is likelier to reflect a failure of imagination than a discovery, and name one combination you dismissed that a determined opponent could plausibly defend.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations. Keep the whole thing to roughly 1,600 words, with the tree as an indented outline or numbered nodes.
