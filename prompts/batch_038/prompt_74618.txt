I write a monthly ideas column and we've recently started running a short piece of fiction next to it — the editor calls the slot "concept fiction," which is exactly as pretentious as it sounds, but it pays. This month's column is on self-knowledge, specifically the luminosity arguement (Williamson's — the one where he shows that basically no interesting mental state is such that you're always in a position to know you're in it, because if one were, a sorites-style margin-for-error principle would force the state to be either universal or empty). I've also been tangling with the KK principle, and with how its not the same thing at all: KK says if you know p you know that you know p, luminosity says if you're in the state you're in a position to know you're in it. People conflate them constantly. I did, for about a week, in print-adjacent drafts.

So I want a short story, 1,200 to 1,400 words, that does that argument as narrative instead of as a lecture.

The setup I'd like you to build out: near future, one small private clinic, one couple, aggressively ordinary furniture. The clinic sells two neural products that are marketed to the public as the same thing — total self-transparency about a single chosen state — but the consent form has two checkboxes, and the two boxes are in fact the two principles. One guarantees luminosity for the state; the other guarantees KK and nothing more. Which box a patient ticks should cost them something concrete in the story.

A few things I care about:

- Pick one mental state and only one, and it has to be the kind of thing that fades by imperceptible degrees rather than switching on and off, because that's the whole engine. Something in the neighborhood of "still being in love with him," or "meaning it when you say you're fine." Your call.
- The triviality result has to land as an event in the plot, not as a speech. If the guarantee holds at every moment and the state fades by degrees, the only coherent readings are that it never went away or that it was never there — and somebody has to live inside one of those readings. Maybe the clinic's logs can't tell them which, and that's the scene.
- Under the KK product alone, she should be able to arrive at knowing that she doesn't know — a stable ignorance she can't close, no matter how hard she introspects. That's the bit readers will find genuinely new, so please don't soften it into "she was confused."
- No named philosophers in the story, no dialogue that sounds like a seminar, no evil-corporate reveal, no robot-wakes-up beat, no it-was-all-a-simulation ending.
- British English throughout, please — we run a UK edition and their copy desk will murder me otherwise. I know I'm writing to you in American English. Sorry about that.
- No bibliography, no further reading, no list of sources at the end. I've done the reading. I just need the fiction.

Format: give me the story under a title, and then exactly one section after it called "What I built in" — about 250 words — where you point to the lines that carry luminosity, KK, and the margin-for-error step respectively, and tell me which of the two trivial readings you chose and why you chose it. Just those two things, in that order.
