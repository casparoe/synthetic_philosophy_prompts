Ok so I'm a second-year PhD student and in about three weeks I'm presenting at our department's philosophy of language work-in-progress seminar. What I'm defending is a version of epistemicism, but not the familiar one. The short version: vague predicates do have sharp boundaries, but what fixes the boundary isn't usage simpliciter and it isn't a safety principle about knowledge either — it's reference-magnet style eligibility, applied to candidate cut-offs. Here's the sketch I'll put on the board:

1. For any vague predicate, our total pattern of use — dispositions to apply and to withhold, the causal-historical chain, all of it — leaves open an interval of candidate cut-offs rather than a unique one.
2. Which candidate is the semantic value is settled by a metasemantic tie-breaker: the most eligible candidate, in Lewis's sense of eligibility (roughly, the most natural, least gerrymandered), wins, and there is always a unique winner, because eligibility is a perfectly natural relation and a bounded set of candidates has a maximum under it.
3. Our ignorance then needs nothing exotic: we know the tie-breaker is running but we can't read off its output, since we have no epistemic access to perfectly natural properties, only to the coarse deliverances of use, which underdetermine between candidates by hypothesis. (Someone will want to know how this differs from Williamson's safety-based story about why we can't know the cut-off, and I've got an answer about step 1 doing work his story leaves to brute stipulation, but it's thin.)
4. So the sorites fails at a step, that step is unknowable, and unlike standard epistemicism I don't have to say ordinary speakers are blind to their own language — they track the candidate interval very well, they just can't get inside it.

The seminar is about twelve people and its not friendly territory. Two are working on supervaluationist semantics, one on higher-order vagueness and one who thinks the problem of the many is the litmus test every theory of vagueness has to pass, and both are sharp and will be perfectly polite right up until they gut the argument. There's a third-year in formal pragmatics who thinks borderline cases are a conversational-dynamics phenomenon and that the whole metaphysics of reference is beside the point. The convenor is a formal epistemologist who mainly cares whether a view generates different credences and will keep pressing for testable differences. Nobody in the room is an epistemicist so far as I can tell.
