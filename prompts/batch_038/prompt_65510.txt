I sit on our hospital's ethics consultation roster, mostly as the token philosopher, which means I get paged for the cases nobody else wants and then have to explain myself to people who have known the patient for years. Last Tuesday's consult turned into a fight between two of the regulars that still hasn't been settled, and I have to bring something to the committee on Thursday. The case: a woman in her late forties with bipolar I, extremely sharp, who wrote a psychiatric advance directive during a stable stretch. The first part was routine — she authorized a particular medication regimen, named her sister as proxy, asked that if she's manic and refusing, the directive be followed over her objections. Then there's clause 7, which is what everything turned on. Clause 7 says that any attempt she makes to revoke the directive while manic — and she predicts, correctly, from three past episodes, that she will try — should itself be treated as confirmation that the directive ought to be enforced. Her reasoning, spelled out in the document, is that a revocation issued from inside the episode isn't her revoking anything, it's the episode revoking, so she's putting that power out of reach deliberately. She also added that if she appears calm and lucid and asks to be released from clause 7, the team should wait seventy-two hours and ask her again, twice. Nobody on the committee disputes that she was competent when she wrote it or that she's competent now in a general sense; the fight is entirely about what clause 7 is allowed to do.

The argument was between Amara, one of our attending psychiatrists, and Joel, a bioethicist who's on the committee through some administrative accident. It went roughly like this. (1) Joel: clause 7 is just what a Ulysses contract *is* — Odysseus doesn't merely ask to be tied to the mast, he instructs the crew to bind him faster the harder he begs, and a self-binding the bound party can undo at the exact moment of maximum temptation isn't a binding, it's a wish. (2) Amara: that works for Odysseus, who stays the same person between the shore and the mast, but here we don't know that the woman who wrote clause 7 and the woman who'd revoke it are the same agent — the directive is six years old, written just after her second hospitalization, and her life has changed a lot since; she even invoked Dworkin's distinction between critical and experiential interests to say that critical interests are precisely the kind of thing that drift, so honoring the old ones forever isn't respecting her autonomy, it's picking the version of her we happen to prefer. (3) Joel: that cuts both ways — if the manic self doesn't get to revoke because she isn't really her, you've conceded that some of her selves count and some don't, and you owe a criterion; without one, the criterion is just the psychiatrist's read on which self is authentic, which is exactly the discretion clause 7 was drafted to take away from you. (4) Amara: I do have a criterion — take the revocation as valid unless it's shown to be episode-driven — and here's the asymmetry you're sliding past. Clause 7 doesn't just give the earlier voice extra weight, it rules the later voice inadmissible as evidence about anything at all, including about whether she's lucid. That's not entrenchment, that's a standing rule for discounting testimony in advance, and Joel's own constitutional analogy shows the problem: an amendment procedure that treats every attempt to amend as proof the amendment was needed isn't entrenched, it's unfalsifiable. (5) Joel then offered a compromise he called a cooling ratchet — a revocation counts only if she repeats it after each of three spaced, documented intervals, and every revocation that *is* honored lengthens the interval for the next one — which Amara shot down as a one-way valve: the terms can only ever get harder, so over a lifetime she's effectively signing a contract she can never renegotiate, with a counterparty she'll never meet. (6) Joel's last move was that a contract you can always renegotiate from the weaker position is worthless by construction, so Amara's objection proves too much and would condemn ordinary precommitment generally, including the seatbelt-and-designated-driver kinds we don't blink at.

Could you go through this move by move and tell me who's right where — I'd like each of the six rated on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating, and I want you to say explicitly which of Joel's two analogy moves (the mast in 1 and the amendment procedure Amara turns on him in 4) actually transfers and which doesn't. Then tell me what both of them are missing, because I'm fairly confident there's at least one live question neither raised, something about what clause 7 does to the *sister*, who has to be the one who says no while her sibling is begging her. Lead with your bottom line in two sentences — who's more right overall and what the committee should actually do Thursday — then the scorecard, then the missing piece, and please keep the whole thing under 600 words. I'm prepping a grad seminar on personal identity at the same time so I'll notice if you lean on identity talk without saying what you'd need for it to do any work here.
