One of my students came in with a writeup he did on his own time — he's clearly been deep in some hard-money podcast or other, he kept saying "the debasement problem" — and he thinks he's gotten a proof out of Aristotle that fiat currency can't underwrite just exchange. He's sharp and he'll smell handwaving instantly, so I want to go in knowing exactly where the thing breaks and what breaking it costs. Here's his argument, roughly his numbering:

(1) An exchange is just only if it exhibits proportionate reciprocity between the parties and their products — that's the whole diagonal-conjunction business. (2) Two things stand in a proportion only if they're commensurable, i.e. measurable against some one common unit. (3) So there has to be a single unit in terms of which both a house and a shoe are measurable, and Aristotle names it: money, which is "a measure of all things." (4) But Aristotle also says of money that it "does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will." (5) Whatever can be altered at will has no value of its own; its value is just whatever the person doing the altering decides it is. (6) And a measure with no value of its own doesn't measure anything — a ruler that the measurer can strech at will isn't a ruler, it's a disguise for his preferences. (7) So money does not measure or commensurate the goods. (8) Then by (2) and (3), house and shoe can't stand in a proportion at all, and by (1) no exchange priced in such money can be just — maybe legal, maybe mutually convenient, but not just in the sense Aristotle is after. (9) Therefore every currency whose value the issuer can adjust, i.e. all fiat, fails Aristotle's own test, and only money the issuer physically can't alter — specie, gold, bitcoin — can back a genuinely just exchange.

He says he'd give up anything rather than (4), since that's the textual premise and not his, and that (9) is just modus ponens on the rest. My own hunches, none of which I could turn into an argument he'd respect: (a) he slides from "can be altered and rendered useless at will" to "its value is whatever we decide," and I don't think (4) licenses (5) — the remark looks like it's about the possibility of demonetization or debasement, not about there being no determinate value; (b) I half-remember there being a line or two right after the bit he quotes that he's quietly skipping, but I couldnt pin down what it said; (c) (3) may not follow either, since Aristotle says the standard is "in reality demand" and money only represents it by convention, so money might be a stand-in for the unit rather than the unit itself; (d) his rubber-ruler image in (6) may cut against him, because in the equity discussion a few chapters later Aristotle compares the right standard for indefinite material to the lead rule the Lesbian builders use, the one that bends to the shape of the stone — and I genuinely can't tell whether that's a defense of flexible measuring or only of flexible judging. Biggest worry of all: on (5), a silver coin is alterable at will too — you clip it, you debase it, and its purchasing power floats with demand anyway — so (9) ought to condemn the hard money he wants just as thoroughly as the fiat money he hates.
