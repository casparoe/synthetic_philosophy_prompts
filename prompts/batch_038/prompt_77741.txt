I am putting together a graduate seminar on the epistemology of thought experimentation in the natural sciences, and I want the specification in hand before we read a single proposal. Your task is to write out the desiderata: an enumeration of what any account of how thought experiments can yield knowledge about the world must deliver in order to count as adequate. For each requirement, state the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a partisan preference of one camp.

The list must be built to handle, not merely name, at least the following families of case. (a) Reductio experiments that run entirely on an adversary's own commitments, such as Galileo's two falling bodies joined by a cord, which refutes the Aristotelian proportionality of weight to speed without appealing to any independent principle. (b) Experiments whose target is a formalism rather than nature: the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen completeness argument, and the Newtonian supertask constructions in which a system of gravitating point masses is arranged so that a particle arrives from spatial infinity in finite time, having been absent from every earlier moment. (c) Experiments that depend on an intervention no actual agent could perform, such as reversing the velocity of every molecule in an equilibrated gas, where the conclusion is nonetheless a modal claim about what would happen. (d) Experiments in which the intuitively obvious answer is the wrong one: Bell's thread stretched between two identically accelerating rockets, the pole and the barn, the twins. (e) Experiments subsequently carried out, where I want an explicit verdict on whether execution changes the epistemic standing of the conclusion already reached in imagination.

Two further things are required. First, tell me which of the demands routinely made in this literature are genuine desiderata and which are one camp's conclusion restated as a criterion — the demand that every experiment be eliminable in favor of an argument being the obvious suspect, though not the only one. Second, identify which subsets of your requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most to exclude candidates; I expect the binding constraint to run through the idealizations themselves, since rigid bodies, frictionless planes, and exact symmetries are what usually carry the weight of the conclusion.

Close with a short scorecard — no more than a paragraph each — of Norton's argument view, Brown's appeal to a priori intuition of natural law, and one model-based or simulation account. Keep the weight on the criteria; the scorecard is an afterthought. Please write in American English, and do not append a bibliography: describe each case you invoke rather than merely citing it, so that the entry stands on its own for students who have not read the literature.
