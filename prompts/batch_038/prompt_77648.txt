The APA's December 1973 decision to strike homosexuality from DSM-II — a unanimous board vote, upheld by a membership referendum the following spring, with the placeholder category "sexual orientation disturbance" substituted — is the stock exhibit for the claim that psychiatric taxonomy is politics wearing a lab coat. It came after protests at the 1970 and 1971 conventions, after a gay psychiatrist addressed a 1972 panel in disguise under a pseudonym, and it left behind a compromise diagnosis, ego-dystonic homosexuality, that survived in DSM-III until 1987. I want that sequence analyzed against the naturalism/normativism debate about disease rather than against the usual sociology of professions.

So take the naturalist side seriously, as its defenders would. On a Boorse-style biostatistical account, dysfunction is a statistically subnormal part-function relative to the species-typical design, with survival and reproduction as the organismic goals; "disease" then adds a value layer on top. What does that theory actually predict for homosexuality in 1973 — deletion, retention, or silence? I suspect it does not line up with the way the story is normally told, and I want that tension made explicit rather than smoothed over. Then tell me what Spitzer's stated rationale (no distress, no generalized impairment, nothing egodystonic about the orientation itself) commits him to; whether ego-dystonic homosexuality reads as a concession to normativism or as a residue a naturalist can absorb; and what the whole episode does to each position — confirmation, anomaly, or a case neither anticipated. Where a framework has nothing to say about the activism, the referendum, or the timing, say so plainly instead of conscripting it. No citations or reading lists: I know the history, and I want the argument.
