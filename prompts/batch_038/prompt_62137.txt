I've taught Kripke's dogmatism paradox in my graduate epistemology seminar for years and it generates the same non-conversation every single time, which is why I want a fresh pair of eyes on it — not a survey, a diagnosis.

Setup, in my words. If I know p, then anything that indicates not-p is misleading, and I seem able to know that much; so I appear licensed to discount future evidence against p in advance. The room then splits. One side says the inference is fine and the lesson is that knowledge really does license a principled closed-mindedness — the conclusion only sounds absurd becuse we've smuggled in open-mindedness as a virtue in itself rather than as an instrument for getting at the truth, and once you're a knowledge-holder the instrument has nothing left to do. The other side says something must break: closure under known implication, or the slide from knowing a general conditional to knowing each instance of it (the junk-knowledge style move), or the assumption that knowledge is the sort of state a subject can deploy in reasoning about her own evidence at all. Nobody has moved anybody in decades, and my students reproduce the standoff exactly.

Here's my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the stalemate isn't over any premise. It's that the two camps are answering different questions under the single heading "may I ignore this evidence" — one asking a normative question about what belief revision permits, the other a structural question about what one's total evidence supports — and the argument only looks like it forces a verdict because nobody notices the switch. An alternative I can't rule out is that it's verbal, turning on whether "misleading" is indexed to the actual world or to the subject's epistemic situation.

To keep this from being trivial, two variants I cooked up that I've never been able to get settled. Kripke's own version was pitched in terms of true belief rather than knowledge, and that's the thread I want pulled:

1. The agnostic dogmatist. Strip knowledge out and put nothing in its place: justified true belief that p, no higher-order view whatsoever about whether I know it, not even a disposition to claim I do. Everything that drove the original inference at the level of truth still drives it — p is true, so counter-indications are misleading. If the conclusion still comes through, then closure, KK, and factivity are all beside the point and half the literature is arguing over the wrong premise. If it doesn't come through, tell me precisely what's missing and whether what's missing is knowledge or just self-trust.

2. The competence swap. Replace p with a claim about my own reliability: I know I'm a competent evaluator of arguments of type T. The "evidence" is now an argument of type T that persuades me I got it wrong. Does the paradox generate, collapse, or generate something worse — e.g. does it make it structurally impossible for me to ever update on argument? And does whatever answer you give here constrain the answer to variant 1?

So: is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about "misleading" or "know", a disagreement about which states can be used as premises in reasoning (i.e. about standards, not facts), two questions wearing one hat, or an ill-posed question I haven't spotted? And what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question — or should we expect none, and why not?
