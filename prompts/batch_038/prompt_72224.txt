A mate of mine lent me one of those popular books about morality that has a chapter on Buddhism, and it sums Buddhist ethics up like this:

'The first thing to notice is that Buddhist ethics has no room for the idea of obedience: there is no lawgiver, so nothing is wrong merely because it has been forbidden. What we have instead is a consequentialism, pure and simple — an act is skilful insofar as it reduces suffering and unskilful insofar as it increases it, and that is the whole of the matter. The doctrine of karma is just this insight dressed in ancient vocabulary. Karma is a law of moral cause and effect, in which every action returns its fruit to the doer, so Buddhism arrived at consequentialism two thousand years before Bentham. The Buddha was to that extent a pragmatist: he refused to answer speculative questions about the origin and extent of the cosmos, and he told inquirers to test his teaching against their own experience rather than deferring to tradition and scripture, which is exactly the temper a consequentialist needs. And since the aim is the reduction of suffering for all sentient beings without exception, Buddhist ethics is a utilitarianism — a more demanding one than Bentham's, because it counts animals too. The precepts against killing, stealing and lying are rules of thumb, which any serious practitioner will set aside when keeping them would produce worse results; the tradition says as much in the story of the Buddha, in a former life as a ship's captain, who kills a would-be murderer in order to save the other passengers. Where Buddhism improves on Bentham is only in sophistication: it understands that the reliable way to bring about good consequences is to work on your own mind, so virtue and meditation matter enormously — but they matter as instruments, not as goods in themselves.'

I've got a suspision that some of this is gung-ho, mainly because my meditation teacher goes on about karma being about the intention behind an act rather than about outcomes, which sounds like the opposite of consequentialism to me. But I don't know enough to say where the trouble is, and the book is clearly written by someone who's read a lot. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what a scholar of Buddhist ethics would nod along with, what's a defensible simplification for a general audience, and what's actually wrong or seriously contested?

Three things especially: (a) does karma being a law of cause and effect really make the tradition consequentialist, or are two different senses of consequence being run together — because it strikes me that karma's fruits landing on the agent is if anything the opposite of the impartial, agent-neutral outlook the chapter assumes; (b) is consequentialism being used interchangeably with utilitarianism, and does it matter here; (c) does the captain story actually show what the chapter says it shows, or is something important about it being left out?

And then: once the errors are corrected, is there anything left of the chapter's conclusion that Buddhism is basically a smarter, more psychologically realistic version of Bentham? Or does fixing the summary pull the rug out from under it? Finally, I keep running into people who read Buddhist ethics as a kind of virtue ethics instead — is that a genuine substantive disagreement with the consequentialist reading, or are the two sides just arguing about whether Western categories fit the material at all?
