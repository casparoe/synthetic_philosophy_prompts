I got talked into ontic structural realism — structure is what there really is, objects are derivative at best. My reasons are the usual three: Fresnel's equations outlived the aether, so the pessimistic meta-induction tells you to bank the structure and ditch the ontology; QM won't tell you which particle is which, so individuality isn't doing any work anyway; and the category-theoretic reconstructions build the theory out of composable processes and only get states as a derivied notion.

What would count against it? Rank the defeaters, fatal down to merely awkward. A few I want you to actually engage rather than list: does the Newman worry — that a purely structural claim about the world collapses into a statement about how many relata there are, hence near-trivial — transfer to the ontic version at all, or does it only bite the epistemic one, and what does the standard repair cost me? Does OSR risk anything that plain scientific realism, or a straight instrumentalist holding the same equations, doesn't? That's the one I'd find hardest to explain away. And is there a case it can't describe — what happens to "structure all the way down" if quantum gravity dissolves spacetime, so there's no standing background of relations to be structural about? Finally: does OSR just collapse into the mathematical-universe thesis in a lab coat, and would that be a defeater or a welcome consequence?

Tell me which of these a committed defender concedes and which they wave off, and be blunt about whether the view has been qualified into something that can't lose — and whether that's a problem for a thesis claiming to say what the world is made of.
