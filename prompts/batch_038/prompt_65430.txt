I write a column about cognition and design and I've been poking at the old Letter Spirit / gridfont material — the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a' — and I've ended up with a pile of judgements about particular marks that I can't get into any kind of order. I'd like someone to tell me what principle, if any, is sitting underneath them.

The cases, roughly in the order I hit them:

1. The two g's. The loopy-tailed g and the open-tailed one you get in handwriting fonts share maybe one stroke. Obviously the same letter, no hesitation.
2. A blackletter a and a geometric sans a. Same letter again — and I'd say that even though if you asked me to describe their shapes I'd have almost nothing in common to report.
3. A shop window with vinyl letters seen from the inside, so the s is mirrored. Still an s, just backwards. A defective s, not some other letter.
4. My niece's fridge drawing: a b drawn mirrored. I want to say she wrote a b wrongly. I do not want to say she wrote a d. But the mark on the paper is exactly, to the pixel, a d.
5. Turn a b upside down and I have no hesitation at all: that's a q. Upside-down n is a u. So a 180-degree rotation changes which letter it is and a mirror flip doesn't, even though both are rigid transformations of the same shape. 4 and 5 sitting next to each other is the thing I can't resolve.
6. A Moscow bus with р on the side. Not a p. Identical shape, different letter, because it's doing a different job in a different alphabet.
7. A coffee ring on a page that happens to be a perfect capital O. Not a letter, not even a bad letter. Just a ring.
8. A logo where the negative space between two shapes makes an A that the designer intended and maybe half the people looking at it catch. Here I go mushy: I want to say it's an A for the people who see it and not an A for the people who don't.
9. My own handwritten a is a circle with a stick. Everyone reads it as an a.
10. A bare vertical stick. In a font where the l has a tail and a curved foot, the bare stick is ambiguous junk — a 1, an I, whatever. In a font where the l IS a bare stick, that exact same mark is defintely an l. Same ink, different verdict, and the only thing that changed is what else is in the alphabet.
11. rn set so tight in a cheap font that it reads as m: still an r and an n, badly set, not an m. Which feels like it cuts against 10, where the surrounding inventory decided everything.
12. The fi ligature is one glyph and two letters, obviously. But æ — in an old English printing of encyclopædia I'd call it a ligature standing in for two letters, whereas in Danish it's a letter in its own right with its own slot at the end of the alphabet. Same shape, different arithmetic.

My rough first-pass principle, which I'm sure is broken: a mark is an instance of L if a competent reader of that script, given the word around it, would recover L. That handles 9, 10 and 11 decently and it handles 7 by default (no word, nothing to recover from). On 6 the competent reader given a Moscow bus recovers the Cyrillic er rather than p, so it looks like it works — except then the shape isn't doing any of the work at all, and I don't see how it earns 2. And 8 makes letterhood come apart viewer by viewer, which I don't think I actually believe, because 6 shows me treating the letter as a fact about the system rather than about whoever's looking.

So: is there a principle that gets all or most of these? State it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which case motivated each clause, since I want to be able to check it rather than take it on faith. Then tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed — a generated glyph halfway between c and e, an e whose crossbar has drifted up so the counter opens; a braille cell, where there's no visible shape to speak of; and a letter in a constructed script that nobody has ever read or set in a word. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts your principle has to throw out. If the cheapest coherent account costs me either 4 or 5, tell me which one goes and why — I'd rather lose a judgement than end up with a twelve-clause theory. No reading list, please, I just want the principle argued out.
