I'm running a workshop on the moral status of video-game characters — the participants are a mix of philosophers, designers, and players — and I want to open with a diagnostic that sorts people by what they actually believe rather than by which side they announce. Write a decision tree of eight to ten questions. Each question presents a concrete case with two or three answers; each branch is labeled by the claim doing the separating, such as whether any moral weight could sit in the character as opposed to what playing with it does to the player, whether a scripted character and one improvised by a running model stand in the same relation to the question at all, and whether the absence of any observer or consequence changes anything. No question may invite a participant to endorse a position by name, and none may require technical vocabulary to answer.

Use at least these cases: torturing a harmless NPC in a single-player game with no narrative consequences, no achievements, and no chance anyone will ever find out; a persistent online companion that remembers you across sessions and begs not to be deleted; an NPC whose game was finished but never shipped, surviving only on a disc in a warehouse; and the deletion of a save file holding years of accumulated history with one character.

Build the space first — the questions and the answer-combinations they permit — and only then name the leaves: indirect or character-forming wrongness, wrongness located in the depicting or the imagining, wrongness done to a community's shared artifact, moral status residing in the underlying computation, fictional realism about characters, and flat denial that anything is going on. Note which combinations go unoccupied and say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves your questions fail to tell apart.
