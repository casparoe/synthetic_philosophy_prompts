You are to work in two capacities at once: first as a philosopher with a working command of the metaphysics of dispositions — stimulus conditions, manifestations, categorical bases, the conditional analysis and its known failures, including finks, masks (or antidotes), reverse finks, and multi-track dispositions — and second as a test engineer who has to turn that material into something a technician can execute without any philosophical training. Think carefully before drafting: begin by brainstorming, in a short section, which distinctions from the philosophical literature actually do work in a laboratory and which are elegant but inert, and say which of the inert ones you are deliberately dropping. Do not give me a literature review, and do not cite papers, articles, or standards documents; I want the procedure, not the sources.

Here is my situation. I chair a technical working group that writes the qualification clauses of an internal standard for a manufacturer of safety-critical electromechanical assemblies. Our clauses are full of dispositional language: an encapsulant is said to be brittle below a threshold temperature, a cell is said to be liable to vent under overcharge, an alloy is said to be creep-limited above a service temperature, a coating is said to be self-healing. Suppliers assert these properties, our laboratory tests them, a certification engineer signs off on the result, and the signed claim later appears in purchase contracts and in incident investigations. Our current test logic is crude, and I want to replace it. It amounts to this: apply the stimulus condition, look for the characteristic manifestation, and if the manifestation does not appear, record that the disposition is absent. Everyone on the group knows this inference is unreliable, but nobody can say precisely when it fails or what to do about it.

Four cases from our own files illustrate the difficulty.

Case A. We qualify a cell chemistry for the property of venting under overcharge. Every unit carries a protection circuit that interrupts charging current well before the overcharge condition is reached. The test never produces venting, and the test bench cannot easily be configured to defeat the protection without also altering the unit.

Case B. We qualify a self-healing coating for the property of cracking under a scratch of specified geometry. The coating repairs the scratch within minutes, so the crack never persists long enough to be scored. The healing agent is consumable: after roughly forty repair cycles the coating stops repairing and the cracks appear immediately.

Case C. We qualify a shape-memory actuator for the property of recovering a specified strain when heated above its transition temperature. The recovery is measured in a fixture that is rigid at temperature for reasons of measurement repeatability, so the actuator generates force but no displacement.

Case D. We qualify a polymer housing for the property of fracturing under a drop from one metre. Sample preparation in the current protocol includes a stress-relief bake and a 48-hour conditioning period at controlled humidity immediately before the drop. We have since learned that both steps suppress the fracture mode for several days after conditioning ends, after which it returns.

Your task is to write the replacement procedure. I want a decision procedure my working group can adopt as standard text, satisfying the following requirements.

1. It must be a sequence of ordered questions or steps, each with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, and it must specify the possible outputs — including an output meaning that the question cannot be settled by test on the available sample, and an output meaning that the decision passes to a named human because it turns on values rather than facts.

2. It must distinguish, and give the technician a way to tell apart where possible, at least these four situations: the disposition is genuinely absent; the disposition is present but its manifestation is blocked by something in the test set-up; the disposition was present but the test itself destroyed or consumed its basis; and the disposition was absent during the test but is reliably acquired under conditions the test does not reproduce, including conditions created by sample preparation. Where two of these cannot be separated with a finite number of destructive tests, say so plainly rather than pretending the procedure settles it.

3. It must address multi-track properties: what a pass on one test condition licenses as a general claim, and what it does not, with the required hedging written into the language of the clause itself, since these clauses are read by lawyers and by investigators years later.

4. It must state the default when evidence is inconclusive, and must flag that default as a value choice rather than a finding, since for a safety-critical part the conservative default and the commercially realistic default point in opposite directions.

5. It must be runnable within real constraints: technicians without philosophy training, small sample allocations because many tests are destructive and some units are expensive, a fixed bench, and a certification engineer who signs the report and needs to see which steps were executed and which were skipped.

Then apply the procedure, step by step, to Cases A through D, showing what it outputs in each and what the certification engineer would sign. At least one of these applications must produce a result I will not like, and you should say so explicitly rather than softening it. Case B in particular should be handled honestly: there is a reading on which the coating genuinely has the cracking disposition and the standard nevertheless certifies it as non-cracking for its service life, and I want the procedure to say whether that is a defect in the procedure or a correct answer to a different question than the one I thought I was asking.

Close with a short section titled Limits, covering the failure modes of the procedure — the kinds of case it will get wrong, the ways a supplier could satisfy it while still shipping a hazardous part, and the points at which it depends on judgment that no wording can eliminate. Finish with a one-page technician-facing summary in plain language: the steps in order, the three or four things to record at each step, and the single sentence that tells the technician when to stop and escalate.

Flag every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure. Where you are unsure whether a distinction survives translation into bench practice, say that it is unsure and explain what would settle it.
