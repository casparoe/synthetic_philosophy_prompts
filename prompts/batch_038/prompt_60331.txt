I'm prepping for my third-year seminar on measurement and the social sciences and I've got myself properly tangled up on Goodhart's law, so I did the thing my tutor suggests and wrote down my gut verdicts on a pile of cases to see what they add up to. We read the taxonomy that splits Goodhart into regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial variants, and she keeps insisting that gaming is only one species of Goodhart, not all of it, but every time I try to say where the line is I end up contradicting myself. Can you work out what principle, if any, actually generates my verdicts?

The ones I'm confident about — gaming, and bad:

1. A hospital graded on A&E waiting times, so porters leave ambulances queued in the car park and nobody is formally admitted until the four-hour clock is already blown.
2. A call centre scored on average handle time, so agents hang up on the difficult callers and phone them straight back, which logs as two short calls.
3. Warehouse pickers paid per scan, so they scan items and then put them back on the shelf.
4. A borough that plants trees and mows the verge hard along the specific route the mobile air-quality van drives.
5. A police force graded on recorded crime, so officers talk victims out of reporting or log a burglary as "lost property".

The ones where the number and the thing come apart but nobody, as far as I can tell, has done anything:

6. A firm that hires on a structured interview score. In the normal range the score tracks job performance fine, but the very top scorers turn out to be no better than the middle, because whatever is producing the extreme scores (polish, confidence, having done the prep course) isn't the productive trait. No candidate lied. It still feels like a Goodhart failure, just not a gaming one.
7. A sales team with a modest quota hits it honestly and the company does well. Same team, same people, same motives, quota doubled, and now they're parking fabricated orders. My verdict flips between the two, and I don't think anything about the agents changed.
8. That old boat-racing game the AI people love, where the agent finds a lagoon full of point pickups and drives in circles racking up score instead of finishing the race. I want to call this gaming, but there's no one in there with an intention, which makes me suspect my principle is secretly about psychology when it's supposed to be about measures.

The ones I'm probably inconsistent on:

9. Professor A organises a citation ring with three colleagues. Professor B writes genuinely excellent review articles knowing full well that reviews get cited far more than original work. Same metric, same movement in the metric, both driven by the same incentive. A is a scandal; B is just being a competent academic. I feel this strongly and I can't explain it.
10. A department head who sets herself an easy target for the year and then visibly smashes it. Nobody was deceived about the target — she chose it. Still feels like gaming to me, but nothing here breaks a correlation between measure and target, so my rule doesn't catch it.
11. A charity that reports mosquito nets distributed as a proxy for malaria prevented. Staff hit the target, they distributed exactly the nets they claim, and malaria genuinely falls. Obviously fine. But structurally this looks identical to case 1 except that the correlation happened to hold, which would make whether someone gamed a matter of luck about the world rather than anything they did.
12. The case I keep going back and forth on: me revising by drilling past papers until I can reproduce the model answers to within a phrase, versus reading around the subject. The exam is a proxy for understanding. I'd say I'm not gaming, I'm studying efficiently, and I do better. But if a lecturer did the equivalent of case 4 to my exam script, I'd be furious.

My tentative principle is roughly: you game a measure when you raise the measure by attacking the link between it and the thing it stands in for, rather than by improving the thing. Case 11 makes it depend on luck, case 6 makes Goodhart come out as non-gaming which I like but case 8 then shouldn't count, and case 10 slips through entirely.

So: give me one or two candidate principles stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forces it, and tell me whether the principle is fundamentally about agents and what they do, about measures and what happens to them under pressure, or whether it has to be two principles doing different jobs. Then test it on cases I haven't listed — invent three of your own, ideally ones that would embarrass the principle you've just proposed, and say what it delivers. And be honest about the cost: if saving every one of my verdicts needs a six-clause monster, tell me that, and give me the simpler principle plus which verdict I should give up and why. Please write the whole thing in British English.
