I run a ninety-minute training session for volunteer gallery guides, most of whom already believe that a counterfeit nobody can distinguish from the original is just as good as the original. I want to teach them one idea: that what we value in a painting is not only how it looks, but that its surface is the record of one person's sequence of decisions made under uncertainty within a particular tradition, so that a work produced by matching appearances — however technically accomplished — is a different and lesser achievement even where the two look alike, and that this is an aesthetic claim rather than a complaint about lying.

Plan the session rather than drafting its script. Give me the order in which the components have to be introduced and what each one depends on, since several of them cannot be stated before the guides accept that a work has properties no amount of looking will reveal. Tell me which single case should carry the whole thing and what that case must and must not have. I am inclined toward a reattribution — a painting long catalogued under a master's name and later assigned to a workshop assistant, where nobody deceived anyone — because it strips out the moral issue; say whether that is the right choice or whether it quietly changes the subject. Map the confusions the guides will bring, in the order they will surface, and fix the exact point in the sequence for heading each one off, including "if you can't see the difference there is no difference" and "so you're saying the forger is less talented." Give me one sentence of pushback to have ready for the visitor who insists the museum only cares about signatures. Name one thing I should openly decline to settle at this level, and end with a question that shows whether the idea has landed rather than whether they enjoyed the hour.
