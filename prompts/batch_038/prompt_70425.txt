Retired after 35 years doing uncertainty analysis for a power company — Monte Carlo, priors, sensitivity studies, the works — and now I read philosophy for fun, which is how I fell into the two-envelope business. The puzzle itself I'm comfortable with: two closed envelopes, one holds twice what the other does, you pick one, and the switching argument says the other has expected value 5x/4 against your x, so you should switch, which symmetry says you shouldn't.

What I can't get my head round is why this has been argued over for thirty-odd years with no agreement even on what kind of mistake is in play. One camp says the setup is impossible — no prior over the amounts is consistent with it — so the question is malformed. Another says the expectation just doesn't exist, it's infinite or undefined, and anyone plugging it into a decision rule is doing nonsense arithmetic. A third says it's a scope slip, that you can't treat the amount in your envelope as simultaneously fixed and unknown. A fourth says the case is simply underdescribed and there's no fact of the matter about whether to switch.

My hunch, offered only as a hunch: these people are answering different questions and the phrase "expected value" is doing double duty. I checked something myself — I coded up a version with a bounded vault, the payer can't go above a known ceiling. Average gain over many runs is dead zero (for particular amounts you open, positive and negative cancel). Delete the ceiling and the numbers start doing what the second camp says they'll do. So maybe there isn't one puzzle here but two, and the stalemate is everyone refusing to say which one they mean.

Is that right, or am I dissolving a real disagreement into an equivocation that isn't actually there? Tell me which of the four diagnoses is load-bearing, whether any of them are the same claim wearing different clothes, and what would count as progress — a theorem, a sharper statement of the case, an agreed sense for "expected value", or nothing, because there's no fact to converge on. Under 400 words please, with your diagnosis in one sentence at the top.
