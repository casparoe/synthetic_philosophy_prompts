I am in the fourth year of a comparative politics PhD, and the second chapter of my dissertation concerns how to conceptualise corruption when the norms of one's field site do not map onto the categories the literature supplies. Before I circulate a draft to my committee I would like a consistency audit of the position I have actually arrived at, which I fear I assembled piece by piece out of different arguments and never tested as a whole. Here is the position.

First, my working definition is the familiar functional one: corruption is the use of a public office for private gain. I favour it because it is operationalisable and because it avoids building any particular moral theory into the concept.

Second, I hold that the public/private distinction on which that definition depends is not itself universal. In the district where I did my fieldwork, an officeholder's obligations to his kin and to the patrons who financed his candidacy are understood as normative claims upon the office rather than as intrusions into it, and I resist describing conduct that discharges such claims as corrupt.

Third, I am nonetheless a moral universalist about the matter: I want to say that some acts are corrupt, and wrong, everywhere, and I do not want my second commitment to collapse into the view that local norms settle the question.

Fourth, I hold that consent and mutual benefit cannot legitimise a corrupt exchange, since two parties can both gain from an arrangement that still wrongs people who are not party to it.

Fifth, on method: I think the concept must be stipulated rather than recovered from ordinary usage, precisely because ordinary usage is what is in dispute across settings.

Sixth, I have a positive criterion of my own, which in the chapter I call the office-reasons test: an exchange is corrupt when the officeholder's decision is moved, even in part, by a consideration that he is authorised to act upon only insofar as it applies to everyone within the jurisdiction of the office.

Seventh, the empirical verdict that started all this. A district official systematically awarded procurement contracts to a businessman who had paid his daughter's school fees; on every occasion the businessman submitted the best bid, and an external audit found no overpricing and no departure from specification. I want to say that this is not corruption, because the decision was right on the merits and nobody was made worse off by it.

Eighth, I use a well-known cross-national corruption perceptions index as a control variable in my regressions, and I take it to be measuring something real enough to warrant that use, even though I argue elsewhere in the same chapter that such indices largely capture outsiders' perceptions.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a survey. Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? I expect that at least one pair here is a false alarm, and I would much rather be told which one than have all of them flagged as equally troubling. Order the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to be surrendered or reworded, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me elsewhere in the set. I am particularly interested in whether my definition in the first commitment and my office-reasons test in the sixth are really two different criteria or one criterion described twice, and in whether the verdict in the seventh is something my own principles can deliver or something I am simply unwilling to give up. Close by identifying the single revision that would repair the most damage, since I would prefer not to rewrite the chapter. Please write your response as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
