Question as it appeared on the paper: Chalmers argues that the standard structure of a conceivability argument moves from an epistemic premise to a modal conclusion and then to a metaphysical one, and that objections to the second step usually turn on an ambiguity in the notion of conceivability. He distinguishes prima facie from ideal conceivability, positive from negative conceivability, and primary from secondary conceivability, and treats these as three largely independent dimensions, so that there are up to eight notions in the vicinity. Explain each of the three distinctions. Then explain how the framework is meant to handle two classes of apparent counterexample to the principle that conceivability entails possibility: (a) complex mathematical hypotheses, such as Goldbach's conjecture and its negation, which seem conceivable although at most one of them can be possible; and (b) a posteriori necessities, such as the claim that water is H₂O. Conclude by saying what the framework leaves of the zombie argument against materialism.

Student's answer: Chalmers thinks that arguments like the zombie argument move from an epistemic premise about what we can conceive to a modal conclusion about what is possible, and that most objections to that move rest on failing to notice that conceivable is ambiguous along three separate dimensions.

Prima facie conceivability is what seems conceivable to a subject on first reflection; ideal conceivability is what would seem conceivable to a reasoner who had considered the matter as carefully as possible, under no cognitive limitations. This distinction is meant to handle the mathematical cases. Since Goldbach's conjecture and its negation are both prima facie conceivable, and they cannot both be possible, conceivability cannot entail possibility unless we idealize. An ideal reasoner would settle the conjecture, after which only one of the two hypotheses would be conceivable, so ideal conceivability is untouched by these counterexamples.

Negative conceivability is a matter of a statement's not being ruled out a priori: we can detect no contradiction in it. Positive conceivability is a matter of being able to form a coherent positive conception of a situation that would verify the statement, rather than merely failing to detect a contradiction in its negation. On Chalmers's account positive conceivability implies negative conceivability but not the converse, so positive conceivability is the stronger notion.

Primary and secondary conceivability track the two ways a statement can be evaluated at a world: a world can be considered as actual, in which case what its terms refer to is settled by what that world is like, or as counterfactual, in which case reference is settled by how our own world is. Primary possibility is thus roughly epistemic possibility and secondary possibility roughly metaphysical possibility, which is why the distinction lets Chalmers keep Kripke's cases without giving up any link between conceivability and possibility.

This is what does the work in the a posteriori cases. That water is not H₂O is conceivable but not possible: we can conceive of a clear, drinkable liquid filling the lakes and rivers that is not H₂O, and yet there could not have been such a liquid that was water. On the framework, the mistake is to treat this as a counterexample to the entailment from conceivability to possibility, because what is conceivable here is only negatively conceivable: the negation of the identity is not ruled out a priori, but no positive conception of a world in which water is not H₂O is available to us. Ideal negative conceivability does not entail possibility; only ideal positive conceivability does. So Kripke's cases are neutralized by the positive/negative distinction rather than by anything modal.

Applied to the zombie argument, the result is that the argument fails. The materialist may treat the claim that phenomenal states are identical to physical states as an a posteriori necessity on exactly the model of water's being H₂O. Zombies are conceivable only in the sense that nothing about a complete physical description of a world rules out the absence of experience a priori; conceivability of this merely negative kind does not license an inference to metaphysical possibility. Since ideal positive conceivability does entail possibility, the anti-materialist would have to establish that zombies are positively conceivable, and any such establishment would already presuppose the contested claim that the physical facts do not exhaust the mental facts. So the zombie argument is not after all an argument from conceivability to possibility, and the framework Chalmers builds to defend that inference is what defeats it.
