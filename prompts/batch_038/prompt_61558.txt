For the past year I have been drafting the ethics memorandum that accompanies a proposed tightening of work-search conditionality in our benefits system — specifically, harsher sanctions for claimants who decline a training placement — and six weeks ago I went through a work-capability assessment myself, after an injury that ended a research contract. That has made the exercise considerably less abstract than I would like, and considerably more urgent: I am now the person who has to say, in writing, why the proposal is defensible rather than merely administrative cruelty. The trouble is that the argument I have constructed to defend it appears to prove a great deal more than I need, and I cannot find a version of it that stops where I want it to stop.

It runs as follows. (1) The benefits of social cooperation — the surplus no individual could produce alone — are held in common, and anyone who draws on that common pool owes the scheme a return proportionate to their capacity to contribute. (2) Someone who draws on the pool while contributing nothing, though they are able to contribute, free-rides, and free-riding is unjust. (3) An able-bodied claimant who subsists on benefits while declining work that is available to them is in precisely that position. (4) Therefore the state acts justly in conditioning their benefits on work, and they have no legitimate complaint when sanctions follow. (5) But (1) fixes the size of the required return by capacity to contribute and by what the scheme values, and nothing in (1) or (2) singles out wage labour as the only form of contribution, or benefit receipt as the only form of drawing. (6) So the same premises condemn, with considerably greater force, the far larger transfers made to those who could contribute and do not: the heir who has never worked, the recipient of ground rent, the owner of unimproved land, the shareholder living on returns generated by other people's labour. And they favour, equally by the same measure, those who contribute a great deal while drawing nothing — the full-time carer of a disabled relative, on whose unpaid labour the scheme depends and for which it does not pay. (7) Therefore either reciprocity demands a programme vastly more radical than the one I am proposing — the abolition or heavy conditioning of inheritance and land rent, and something like a wage for care — or, if I refuse that programme, I owe an account of why one and the same principle selects the unemployed claimant and spares the heir.

I cannot accept either horn of (7). I do not want to sign a memorandum that commits the department to a theory of justice which, pursued consistently, would nationalise inheritance, and I certainly do not want to concede that the claimant and the rentier stand in the same relation to the cooperative surplus, since the second concession seems to me to be simply true and to destroy the proposal from the inside. I would most like to keep (1) and (2), because they are what makes conditionality a claim about fairness rather than about desert and the moral character of poor people — a distinction I think matters enormously and that most public debate collapses. I would give up (3) before I gave up (1).

So: tell me which step fails, or, if none of them fails, which of the plausible ones I should surrender at the lowest price. I want the diagnosis to be specific about the kind of failure — whether a term shifts between (1) and (3), whether the principle is being applied at a level it was never meant to govern, whether the slide is from a claim about distributions to a claim about what may be enforced against a particular person, or whether (7)'s dilemma is simply false because a principle can be weighty and still license selective application. Then price each option: for every premise you think I could give up, say what the proposal loses, what it retains, and who bears the cost. Finally, tell me what would change your verdict — which consideration, if I pressed it, would move you to a different diagnosis — and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong, since I intend to argue with you rather than simply accept the answer.
