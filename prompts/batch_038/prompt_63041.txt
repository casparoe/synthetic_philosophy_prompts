So I've been going around in circles on the old Meno puzzle — Socrates' point that a true belief about the road to Larissa gets you there just as well as knowledge does, so what exactly is the extra value supposed to be — and I decided to stop reading and just write down my gut verdicts on a pile of cases to see if they add up to anything. Here they are.

1. Two drivers, same route, both arrive safely. One has driven it 200 times, one memorized a printout someone else made, and the printout happens to be right. I think the experienced driver is in the better state. But if all I need is a ride, I'm totally indifferent between them.

2. My GP says the drug works, I believe her, it works. Compare me to a patient who read the trial herself and can explain why it works. Same true belief, same outcome. Hers is better, obviously, and not just marginally.

3. Firefighter A gets a hunch, "everybody out now," and they all make it before the floor goes. Firefighter B genuinely understands the physics of flashover but loses two seconds to it. In that moment A's state was worth more, and I can't shake that.

4. I know the proof — I've reconstructed it a dozen times — but under exam panic nothing surfaces. My classmate has a rote true belief lifted from a worked example that comes out on demand. For the exam hers is worth more. And unlike 3 I don't think that's just about the situation, I think it says something about whose state is better full stop.

5. A neighbour of mine has believed since the eighties, on the strength of a horoscope, that her son would emigrate to Canada. He did. She has never doubted it, nothing could shake her, she acts on it constantly and correctly. Perfectly stable true belief. I say it's worse than knowledge, not just no better.

4 and 5 are the pair I cannot hold onto at the same time. 4 says availability when you actually need it is what makes a state worth having. 5 says it can't be, because stability and successful use without the right hookup to reasons doesn't earn you anything. Both can't be right unless the principle ends up with a lot of moving parts, which makes me suspicious.

6. My navigation app has gotten me there every single time for years and clearly has some model of the traffic in it. I don't think it's better off than me when I know the way. But I also don't think it's worse than my neighbour in 5. So reliability and stability are both failing to do the work here.

7. Knowing the exact number of grains of sand in a bucket I'll never see again, versus having the same number as a lucky true guess. I feel nothing. The knowledge doesn't register as better at all, whereas in 2 it registers enormously. That suggests the extra value tracks how much the subject matter bears on what you do, which pushes me toward the thought that it's all just usefulness — and that once you've already got the truth, whatever credit you'd give the source that produced it gets used up by the truth itself. I know people press something like that against the reliabilist answers. But 5 is what resists it for me: her source has never once let her down, and I still want to say she's worse off.

8. A homing pigeon. It gets home. Does it know? Is its state worth more or less than my app's? I don't know what I think, but whatever principle I land on has to give an answer here and I'm nervous about which one it gives.

Also worth noting: Socrates' own answer, as I remember it, is that true beliefs are like unsecured things that wander off unless you tether them by working out the reason why. That's no help to me, precisely because of 5 — hers is tethered by nothing and has never wandered.

My own tentative principle, which I suspect is wrong: knowledge beats true belief exactly when the true belief could easily have come apart in the situations where it actually gets used. Test that against all of the above and tell me where it breaks.

What I want back: one principle, or a small set of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without guessing — with each clause tied to the case that forced it in. Then tell me which of 1–8 it gives up on, and don't try to save everything; I'd rather have a clean principle that overturns two of my verdicts than a baroque one that saves all eight. Then give me its verdict on a case I haven't listed, ideally one you think I've been avoiding, and on the one I keep flip-flopping about: my grandmother's settled, comforting, never-tested true belief that my grandfather is still watching over her, versus my knowledge that he isn't. Which of us is in the better state, and is that even the same question Socrates was asking?
