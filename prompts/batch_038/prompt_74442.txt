I served as a non-voting observer and minute-taker for a county flood-risk review panel last autumn, and two of its members — I'll call them Adaeze and Marcus — have been arguing ever since about the panel's own report. The panel had eleven members. A proposed levee redesign was presented by the engineers; the first two speakers after the presentation endorsed the safety margins; the remaining nine endorsed them in turn, one after another; the report went out unanimous. Since the levee underperformed in a storm in March, it has emerged that at least four of those nine had privately thought the margins were thin and said nothing.

Marcus's opening position: nobody in the nine did anything wrong, and the unanimity is not evidence of anything. Each later speaker was responding rationally to the information the earlier endorsements carried; the sequence is one judgment amplified nine times rather than nine independent judgments, so it was worth nothing epistemically, and no individual can be faulted for failing to extract information from it. To blame any one of them, he says, is to require them to disregard evidence they genuinely had.

Adaeze's four replies:
(1) The endorsements carried no evidence in the relevant sense, because everyone in the room could see that the later speakers were reacting to the same engineer's packet and to each other; an agent who discounts that correctly ends up with nothing to go on and should therefore have voiced her own doubts.
(2) Marcus's standard locates the entire failure with the first two speakers, who had no predecessors to follow — yet those two had exactly the same evidence everyone else had, so his account either makes the originators uniquely culpable on no principled basis or dissolves culpability altogether.
(3) Even if each silence was individually rational, the report was an assertion by the panel, and it misrepresented the panel's members' actual views; a group can be at fault for what it asserts even when no member is at fault for their part in asserting it.
(4) Whatever the decision theory, these people were seated precisely so that private doubts would surface, and a role obligation of that kind is not discharged by observing that everyone else was equally failing to meet it.

Marcus's four counters: on (1), if everyone discounts the cascade then the silent equilibrium is stable, so her argument shows the trap rather than an exit from it; on (2), an absurd distribution of blame is a reason to reject the blame, not to redistribute it; on (3), he denies the report asserted anything beyond "no member objected"; on (4), a duty with no designated bearer and no procedure for discharging it is a wish, not an obligation.

Please adjudicate their exchange in the following sections, in this order:

**The crux** — about 100 words: what the disagreement ultimately turns on, if it is one thing.

**Move by move** — the bulk of the answer: for Marcus's opening position, for each of Adaeze's four claims, and for each of Marcus's four counters, give a verdict of *sound*, *partly sound*, or *unsound*, with two to four sentences of justification each. Where a reply defeats only part of a claim, say which part.

**What neither raised** — about 150 words: the strongest consideration bearing on the case that both of them left out.

**Overall** — about 150 words: who is more right, and about which specific question.
