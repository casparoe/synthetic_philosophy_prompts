My aunt is retiring after forty-odd years as a welfare economist and I've been volunteered to give the toast at her dinner. Half the room is her old coauthors and former students — people who have actually read the papers on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — and the other half is family who describe her job as "math about fairness." My uncle and my cousins are going to be at the same table as three people who've written responses to her.

Here's the problem I need you to solve. She made her name by refusing to let anyone call that theorem a proof of utilitarianism. Her line was always that the theorem only gets you this far: social utility is a weighted sum of individual utilities. And becuase each person's utility is pinned down only up to a positive affine transformation, those weights are a free parameter until somebody normalizes them — so the equal weights aren't in the theorem at all, they come from the extra impartial-observer / extended-sympathy assumption, which she insisted was a moral premise wearing a lab coat. She also could not stand people citing it as an argument for equality, since once the weights are fixed it's totally indifferent to distribution: two worlds with the same total score the same no matter how they're split.

So write the toast, about five minutes spoken. Two hard constraints. It can't say anything she'd consider false about her own work — no "the mathematics showed we all count the same," no smoothing over the fact that the equal-weights step is doing all the ethical lifting and isn't earned by the Pareto assumptions. But it also can't turn into a seminar: the family half has to follow every sentence, and no jargon survives unless it's cashed out in the same breath. And I want one genuinely good joke that lands on both halves of the room — either built on the impartial observer idea, or on the fact that the theorem lets someone who wants a thing more intensely count for more, since my cousins have a long story about who got the last piece of pie in that house and about how their mother made them say whether they liked a deal before finding out how it would turn out. It should end on affection, not on a lesson.
