I work in compliance at a broker-dealer, not in philosophy, so forgive the framing, but something in our own rulebook has been bugging me for months and I cant tell whether it's a real tension or whether I'm just missing the point.

Here's the setup as I see it. Two commitments sit side by side in the same regime. First: if you have material non-public information about a company, you either broadcast it to everybody at once or you stand aside from trading — and if you tip a friend, your friend has to stand aside too. A company that leaks a number to a couple of analysts has to put it out to the whole market immediately. Second: the same regulators, the same firms and the same economists talk about the market price as an information machine. Prices are supposed to discover and aggregate what's scattered across millions of heads, which is why you trust the price more than any analyst, why takeovers get priced off the market, and why people keep proposing prediction markets for everything from elections to pandemic dates.

And then the third thing, which is what really gets me: nobody thinks it's the slightest bit improper to trade on a hunch. On a wild private theory, on a chart pattern, on a gut feeling, on a coin flip. Enormous positions get built by people who freely admit they have no idea. Knowing something is a crime. Guessing is protected, and in fact it's the fuel.

So what theory of knowledge does this combination actually presuppose? Walk me through the epistemology it only makes sense under — what it takes prices to be doing, what it takes traders to be doing, and what it takes information to be worth. Is there a coherent view on which the ban and the faith in prices hold together, or is the regime quietly committed to the price being informative while nobody is allowed to knowingly make it so?

I've heard there's a theorem to the effect that two rational people who started from the same prior can't agree to disagree, and a related result that they would never trade at all except for reasons that have nothing to do with information — hedging, cash needs, different appetites for risk. If that's right, what does the sheer volume of everyday trading imply about who is actually on the other side of my customers' orders, and does that change what the law is protecting? Because it looks to me like the statute forbids the informative trades and blesses the noise.

Then tell me which rival epistemologies the current setup rules out, wether the pieces are jointly consistent, and what you'd have to redesign — the disclosure rules, the insider trading statute, the places we lean on prices as evidence — if you swapped in a view where a price is just an aggregation device with no claim to being right. Please separate what the regime strictly presupposes from what merely sits comfortably with it; I suspect several different accounts could license the same rules and I want to know which ones and why. No citations or reading lists, I can't look anything up — I want the reasoning itself.
