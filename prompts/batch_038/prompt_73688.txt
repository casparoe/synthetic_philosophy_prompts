I am preparing a paper for a workshop on perception and ideology, and my test case is China Miéville's *The City & the City* (2009). I want a critical reading of the novel through the philosophical literature on the cognitive penetrability of perception — one that treats the novel as a genuine candidate case for the debate rather than as an illustration of conclusions already reached.

Let me sketch the apparatus so that we are working from the same map. The question is whether what a perceiver believes, desires, or fears can alter perceptual experience itself, as opposed to altering only post-perceptual judgment. Fodor's encapsulation and Pylyshyn's thesis of the cognitive impenetrability of early vision supply the conservative default. The candidate counterexamples range from the contested mid-century value-and-need studies — children judging coins to be larger than matching discs — through desire and stereotype effects on judged distance, slope, and lightness, to colour-constancy cases and the effects of labels and expertise. Fiona Macpherson's work is especially useful here because it sorts putative penetration into direct and indirect mechanisms, the indirect ones reaching perception by way of attention, imagination, or memory rather than through the content of a belief. Susanna Siegel's argument supplies the stakes: if experience is penetrated by states the subject has no reason to trust, its justificatory force is downgraded — and the standard conservative reply, that the subject is merely attending differently or has acquired perceptual expertise, is what keeps the impenetrability thesis intact.

The novel's premise is that two city-states, Besźel and Ul Qoma, occupy overlapping territory and are held apart not by walls but by trained perception. Citizens learn from childhood to "unsee" the other city — its buildings, vehicles, and inhabitants, even in crosshatch areas where the two populations interleave inches apart — while remaining exquisitely sensitive to the cues of dress, gait, and architecture that identify what is to be unseen. Only Copula Hall permits legal passage between them, and passing through it leaves the traveller at the same coordinates in the other city: the border is not spatial. Noticing, even inadvertently, is "breach," treated as worse than murder and policed by a shadow institution also called Breach, rumoured to inhabit the same interstices as Orciny, the possibly fictitious third city. Inspector Tyador Borlú investigates the murder of Mahalia Geary, a visiting student whose research had drifted toward precisely that question, and ends inside Breach's own epistemic situation — interrogated about a city whose existence he cannot check, because every instrument of checking is itself trained.

My hypothesis, which I would like you to attack rather than endorse, is that unseeing names something the standard taxonomy has no slot for: manufactured encapsulation. The paradigm penetrating states are beliefs and desires, and penetration is supposed to track them — revise the belief, revise the percept. Borlú's case runs backwards. He comes to doubt the order he was trained into, discovers that Breach is fallible, overworked, and ignorant, and even entertains that Orciny is a myth projected out of fear of Breach; none of this restores the other city to his vision. The encapsulation is real, it is cognitive in origin, it was installed by command rather than by conviction, and it survives the withdrawal of the command's justification. If that is right, impenetrability — the very criterion the conservatives read as evidence of a clean, informationally sealed module — is compatible with a perceptual system saturated in politics, and the debate's test for whether perception is ideologically loaded is looking in the wrong place.

Please work through the following, in this order:

1. Classification. Where does unseeing actually belong: genuine penetration; attentional set and inattentional blindness; perceptual learning and expertise, the citizen functioning as a radiologist of dress-cues; aspect-seeing in the duck-rabbit sense; or something in the family of withheld acknowledgment rather than perception at all? Say which fits best, and say what would distinguish these diagnoses from one another, either textually or empirically.

2. My claim. Does the failure of belief revision really separate manufactured encapsulation from perceptual learning — given that learned expectations are also resistant to belief revision and are already classified as an indirect mechanism? Is the notion doing work, or is it a redescription? If it collapses, say into what.

3. Siegel's downgrade, applied. What is the epistemic standing of a witness trained to unsee, and does the novel's own legal machinery depend on that downgrade, given that breach is adjudicated by what someone saw and how they responded to it? Note also the asymmetry between insider and visitor: the visitor sees one city, the citizen two and a half. Is that asymmetry an argument for penetration, or merely evidence of a trained attentional set?

4. Resistance. Press as hard as you can the objection that Miéville describes unseeing chiefly as comportment — averted gaze, withheld eye contact, careful gait — rather than as any change in visual phenomenology, and that the honest verdict may be that the novel concerns the ethics and jurisdiction of attention, not its metaphysics. Include the possibility that my whole framing is a category error.

5. Contribution. Consider the collective dimension: penetration is studied one subject at a time, whereas the novel depicts a perceptual order maintained by mutual expectation, in which each citizen's unseeing depends on everyone else's and no individual's evidence can revise it; and the bootstrapping problem posed by Orciny. Say whether these are real additions to the debate or restatements of it in fictional terms.

A few constraints. Assume I know the plot well; keep summary to the minimum the argument requires. No verbatim quotation from the novel is necessary, and I would rather you not attempt it. Where you invoke empirical studies, describe them and flag their contested status candidly instead of supplying confident specifics; if you are unsure whether a given distinction or result belongs to a given author, say so rather than guess. Around 1,500 to 2,000 words. I would prefer a reading that ends by telling me my hypothesis is a redescription to one that flatters it.
