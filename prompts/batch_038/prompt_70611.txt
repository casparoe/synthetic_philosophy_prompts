I was at a workshop on liability for autonomous systems last month and the rapporteur's write-up of one talk has been rattling around in my head. The proposal was called a residual duty-sink. Here's roughly how the summary put it, as best I can reconstruct it:

"Start from the standard fourfold distinction — causal, role, moral, and legal responsibility — and note that the so-called responsibility gap arises when an autonomous system produces a harm that satisfies none of them. The gap is not a discovery but a theorem: it has been demonstrated that certain kinds of machine action admit of no responsible party, and the only live question is how the law should paper over the result. The proposal allocates responsibility in three tiers. Tier one: causal responsibility attaches to the person whose act or omission was a but-for cause of the harm. Tier two: role-responsibility attaches to whoever occupied the relevant organisational role at the time, whether or not they were at fault and whether or not they contributed causally. Tier three: whatever survives both tiers is absorbed by the duty-sink — a single natural person, licensed by the regulator and registered before deployment, who bears strict and exclusive liability for it. Exclusive means the victim has no claim against anyone else once the sink is liable. Because the sink is a person, she is causally responsible for the residual harm in the ordinary sense; the law simply decides who that person will be. The result is that every harm has an answerable human attached to it, so the responsibility gap is closed by construction rather than by finding someone who caused the outcome. Two further points. First, once strict liability is in place the distinction between legal accountability and moral blame collapses, so the philosophers who kept the two apart were chasing a pseudo-problem. Second, objections that this is scapegoating mistake the ethics: the bearer does not do harm, she allows harm she could have prevented by refusing the licence, and allowing is less bad than doing. Critics reply that a bearer facing unlimited liability will stop monitoring altogether, but the empirical literature on strict liability has shown this is false — precaution does not fall under strict liability — so that objection is not available."

A few things I want from you. Go through it roughly sentence by sentence and sort what a philosopher working on this stuff would accept, what is a fair simplification, and what is actually wrong or loaded. Then tell me whether the last few moves survive the correction — specifically the pseudo-problem claim, and the claim that the gap is closed rather than just relocated. And separately: is the duty-sink proposal any good? I'm inclined to think it's just scapegoating with a licensing regime wrapped around it, but I can't tell whether that intuition is doing real work or whether the doing/allowing response genuinely defuses it.
