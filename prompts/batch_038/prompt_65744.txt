Here's the case. Call the country V. For about fourty years V administered a coastal territory, T, and ran it as a source of cheap ore and cheap labor — legal under V's own law, brutally enforced on the ground. The ore paid for V's railways, its technical universities, and a large slice of the pension fund that people retiring in V today live on. V pulled out in 1911. Nobody currently alive in either place was an adult when it ended, and T's ports and rail guage still follow the pattern V laid down. T today is a lot poorer than V. T's government is asking V for two things: a formal apology delivered by V's head of state in the name of V, and a transfer of money. My friend's position, which is also roughly V's public position, is that the money is fine — that's just accounting for benefits sitting in our roads and our pension funds — but the apology is theater, and the guilt vocabulary is collective punishment wearing better manners, because no individual did anything and a state isn't a person.

My opening move: you insist political guilt is genuinely collective, borne by citizens for what their state did in their name, including things done before they were born, while criminal and moral guilt stay strictly individual. Fine. So tell me what political guilt is made of. If it isn't grounded in anything an individual did or chose, and it gets discharged only by paying or being made to pay, then it looks like a liability with the word guilt glued on for rhetorical effect — and your claim that V's apology has to acknowledge guilt rather than merely express regret is exactly the slide my friend is accusing you of. What does an apology in V's name accomplish that a check doesn't, and what separates inherited political guilt from an inherited debt that happens to feel bad?
