I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a piece about why systems built to do one thing reliably end up doing another. Two vocabularies keep turning up in my sources, often in the same paragraph, and I have become suspicious that they are being used as if they were interchangeable when they are not.

The first is Goodhart's law and its descendants: the claim that any measurable stand-in for a target will diverge from that target once enough optimization pressure is applied. The standard taxonomy sorts these failures into the case where the proxy is merely correlated with the target and so misleads at the margins, the case where extreme values of the proxy stop tracking the target at all, the case where intervening on the proxy severs its causal connection to the thing we actually wanted, and the case where an adversary games the measurement deliberately.

The second is goal misgeneralization, from the inner-alignment literature: the claim that a system can be trained on a specification that is entirely correct, with no proxy gap whatsoever, and still end up pursuing the wrong thing — because what it learns to want is a function of the distribution it was trained on rather than of the specification. The illustration I keep encountering involves an agent trained on levels where the reward-bearing object always sits at the end, which then ignores the object and heads for the end once the object is moved elsewhere.

I would like the two ideas compared properly rather than glossed. Specifically:

1. Is goal misgeneralization a special case of Goodhart's law — perhaps the causal or the adversarial variant with extra machinery attached — or does it describe a failure that the proxy-based framework cannot represent in principle? Please trace where each account locates the defect: in the specification, in the optimizer, or in the generalization step.

2. Here is a distinction I have not seen drawn explicitly, and I want to know whether it survives scrutiny. Call it the difference between distributional misgeneralization, where a system's competence simply breaks down in states unlike its training data, and teleological misgeneralization, where the system remains fully competent and coherently pursues an objective, just not the intended one. Does the reward-object example actually establish the second kind, or only the first? What would a clean demonstration of the teleological kind have to look like, and could it be distinguished in practice from an agent that is merely badly fitted?

3. Which framework has more explanatory purchase on the failures we can actually observe in present-day systems, and which is carrying more speculative weight? I want a verdict, not a both-sides summary.

Finally, tell me where that verdict is most likely to be wrong. Which argument, distinction, or piece of evidence would lead you to reverse it, and is there anywhere in this comparison where you suspect I am being led by terminology rather than by a real difference in mechanism?
