You are a political philosopher with a background in planning theory, and you have been asked to help a facilitation team preparing a citizens' assembly on housing in a mid-sized English city. Think carefully, and begin with an explicit brainstorming stage before producing the instrument: sketch the full space of positions people in the room are likely to hold, list candidate axes of disagreement, and then prune to the axes that actually separate those positions. Only then design the questions.

**The deliverable.** A diagnostic sorting instrument for assembly participants, to be completed before any deliberation. The aim is to place each participant at a position in the debate on the basis of what they say about concrete cases, so that the tables can be balanced and so that participants can be shown, afterwards, which commitments they were in fact making. It must not function as a self-identification exercise.

**Structure required.**

1. Seven questions, each built around a concrete case in this setting, with two or three answer options. The cases should feel local and specific — for instance, forty homes proposed on a former bus depot; a Victorian terrace house converted into three flats; a taller building on the edge of a conservation area; a council estate redevelopment in which existing tenants are offered equivalent floorspace; a compulsory purchase order for land needed for a new station approach; a developer offering a playground and a share of units below market rent in exchange for permission; an owner refusing to sell land except at a price reflecting speculative permission. Design the rest yourself.
2. A decision tree in which each branch is labelled by the claim that separates it from its siblings, and each leaf is a named position.
3. A scoring key that also copes with mixed or inconsistent answer patterns, since real respondents will not follow a single clean path. State your tie-breaking rule.

**Constraints on the questions.** No question may use the vocabulary of the positions, no question may ask anyone what they are or what they support in the abstract, and no question may be answerable by guessing where it leads. At least two questions must be ones on which the intuitively pro-housing answer does *not* point towards the supply-side leaf.

**Axes you must cover.** The instrument should turn on at least the following distinctions: whose interest is the relevant one — owners, current residents, or prospective incomers; whether value created in land by public investment and by the presence of other people entitles the community to capture it; whether an objection is to this particular scheme, to more housing as such, or to market provision of housing; whether compensation in money or in kind could in principle resolve a legitimate complaint; whether the decision belongs properly to the immediate neighbourhood or to a wider polity; and whether local character is a genuine interest or a proxy for exclusion.

**Leaves.** At least eight, and they must include: supply-side market provision; land-value-taxation or Georgist positions; decommodification through social housing and community land trusts; large-scale state-led housebuilding as distinct from decommodifying the existing stock; defensive localism by incumbent owners; conservation and heritage positions; anti-displacement defence of existing tenants; and a proceduralist position on which the substantive question is who decides rather than what gets built. Name each leaf with the label used in public debate where one exists, plus a descriptive name of your own.

**Two further sections.** First, identify the logically available combinations of answers that no leaf occupies — for example, someone who denies that any neighbourhood has a legitimate interest yet wants housing withdrawn from the market, or someone who holds that land value belongs entirely to the community but supports restricting supply — and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot in practice separate, say why, and give the question that would separate them even if it will not fit.

**Style and limits.** Write the whole thing in British English. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; I will add references myself. Do not invent studies, statistics, or attributed quotations. You need no technical command of English planning law beyond what an informed layperson would have, and the cases may be schematic. Close with one short paragraph on where this instrument is most likely to misclassify a real participant, particularly given the social pressure not to be seen as an objector.
