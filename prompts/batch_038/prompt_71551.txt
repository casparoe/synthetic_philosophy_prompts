I retired five years ago after thirty-three years in analytical chemistry, most of which was spent persuading other people that a mass spectrum meant what I said it meant. I have taken up philosophy of science in earnest since, and I am now at the stage of writing a short paper for a local philosophy society on the question of whether evidence can ever settle a dispute between rival theories. Before I commit anything to print, I should like my settled views audited. Here they are, stated as plainly as I can manage.

(1) I am a realist about unobservable entities, and the ground of my realism is that the predictive success of our theories would be inexplicable otherwise. The electron is not a convenient fiction that happens to sort mass spectra correctly; its doing so is evidence that there are such things.

(2) I accept that no single hypothesis is ever tested in isolation. What an experiment confronts is a bundle: the hypothesis of interest, the theory of the instrument, the calibration assumptions, the sample-handling assumptions, and a good deal more. In my own working life this was a daily practical fact rather than a philosophical thesis.

(3) I nonetheless hold that underdetermination is global and standing: for any theory, whatever the evidence, an incompatible rival could in principle be constructed that accounts for all of it. I take this to be a conceptual feature of the relation between theory and evidence, not a finding about particular pairs of theories that someone has actually written down.

(4) I also hold — and this is the part I regard as my own contribution, modest as it is — that empirical equivalence is never a determinate two-place relation between theories. Two theories are equivalent only relative to a specification of what counts as an observation, and different specifications render different pairs equivalent. Since no one has ever supplied an absolute observation-scheme, I conclude that the global thesis asserted in (3) has never actually been exhibited but only assumed, and that much of this debate is a pseudo-problem, rather in the way I suspect the old question of whether space is really Euclidean was.

(5) I hold that simplicity, unifying scope, and conservatism are genuine tie-breakers among theories, and that a scientist who disregards them commits an epistemic error rather than merely revealing a taste.

(6) I want to maintain all of this without conceding anything to instrumentalism. My theories are literally true or false, and the virtues listed in (5) track something about the world rather than something about our comfort.

(7) Finally, I hold that there is a principled difference between legitimate auxiliary adjustment and ad hoc rescue — the difference between an auxiliary that purchases a novel and independently checkable prediction and one that merely insulates the theory from an anomaly — and that this difference can be stated without appealing to hindsight about how the episode eventually turned out.

Please audit the set. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely cannot be held together, which only appear to conflict and dissolve on inspection, and, for each real conflict, what would have to be surrendered and what I would lose by surrendering it. Rank the conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as equally damaging, and finish by naming the single revision that would buy me the most coherence at the least cost. I would much rather be shown where I have cheated myself than be reassured.
