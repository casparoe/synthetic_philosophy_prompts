Group meeting blowup, need help pulling a slogan apart.

I'm a second-year PhD student in an ML group. I pitched the idea that if you generate a family of tasks procedurally from a fixed set of constraints — say grid-world puzzles where the constraint set stays put but the layout varies — then an architecture whose bias matches those constraints should transfer to held-out members of the family, and that this isn't just benchmark overfitting, since the held-out tasks were never in the training suite. Our senior researcher killed it in one sentence: "There's no free lunch. Averaged over all possible problems every learner is exactly as good as random guessing, so any advantage you show on your suite is paid for by a loss somewhere else. Your benchmark is just hiding where."

Half the room nodded and I didn't have an answer, but I'm fairly sure he stacked at least three different claims into that sentence and some of them are way weaker than he thinks. Can you break it into the seperate precise theses it could be standing for? For each one I want: what it says exactly (which set of target functions, which distribution over them, which error measure, in-sample or off-training-set, static deterministic targets or not), whether it's an actual theorem / trivially true / substantive but defensible / flat false, and above all whether it would license his dismissal of my proposal.

Some specific places I'm stuck — fold them in wherever they fit:

- Does the real theorem need the uniform average over *all* functions from inputs to outputs, and does anything survive if you average over a non-uniform distribution instead? - Is "your win is paid for by a loss elsewhere" a restatement of the theorem or an extra claim on top of it? And if it's a restatement, does it say anything about *which* problems the loss lands on, or about problems anybody would ever actually run?
