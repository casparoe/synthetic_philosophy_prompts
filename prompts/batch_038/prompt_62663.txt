ok so I sit on the curatorial committee of a small regional human rights museum and I need help thinking through a decision we have to make next month. Please take this as seriously as a professional philosopher of history who also does museum ethics consulting would — that's the role I want you in. And do some explicit brainstorming out loud before you land anywhere: sketch the candidate rules, test each one against the record, and only then give me a verdict. No reading lists or citations, I can't check them and I don't want them, I want your actual reasoning. Flag wherever you're guessing.

Here's the record. Our job is to approve or reject exhibit and program proposals, and roughly half of them involve comparing one mass atrocity to another. Over four years:

1. Approved — two adjacent galleries, one on the Atlantic slave trade and one on the rubber terror in the Congo Free State, joined by a shared wall text about extraction economies and coerced labor. The minute reads: "the comparison is about the machinery, not the misery."
2. Rejected — a public panel series, working title "Which Was Worse?", four historians debating the twentieth century's mass killings with a running death-toll graphic on the screen behind them. Two members said the format, not the subject, was the problem.
3. Approved with conditions — a photo display pairing images from a siege with images from a famine some decades earlier, on condition that every caption name who did what to whom, and that neither event's name be used as an adjective for the other.
4. Rejected — a proposal to use the phrase "concentration camps" in wall text about a detention system that is still operating, even though one of our own board members survived such a camp as a child and personally endorsed the comparison. The recorded reason is a single word: "premature."
5. Approved — an exhibit on two different countries' programs of removing children from an indigenous population, told through parallel timelines and a lot of shared archival documents.
6. Rejected, narrowly — a grant-funded proposal to build a "severity index," a scoring rubric weighting scale, intent, irreversibility and complicity, to be used to sort our own case files. The reason given was that it "puts numbers on things that shouldnt have them." I argued at the time that this is hard to square with 5, which is nothing but numbers and dates, and nobody really answered me.

One member insists we've been applying a single coherent principle the whole time. Another thinks we've just been voting our stomachs and dressing it up afterwards. I honestly can't tell which of them is right, and I want your read on that too.

Now the proposal in front of us. An outside scholar wants to mount a display comparing two famines — one in a nineteenth-century colony run by an imperial power, one under a twentieth-century state's grain requisition program. The comparison is entirely about mechanism: railway and shipping logistics, what was being exported and when, which officials knew what, how the relief bureaucracy behaved, the ideology that made starvation thinkable in each case. Every caption names agents. Each famine gets its own wall text in its own vocabulary, and the scholar has written in explicitly that the exhibit draws no conclusion about which was worse.

The centerpiece, though, is one large graph: excess mortality for both famines plotted on a single set of axes, normalized per capita, so the two lines cross each other about a third of the way in. The scholar's argument is that the crossing is the whole point — one curve spikes and collapses, the other grinds on for years, and the shapes track the difference between a market-mediated and a state-commanded famine.

Complication: our community advisory council is split. The group affiliated with the smaller of the two death tolls supports the exhibit enthusiastically. The group affiliated with the larger one objects in writing, saying the overlay "shrinks our dead into a line on somebody else's chart."

What I want from you:

(a) Give me the candidate rules that could explain our six verdicts. I'd guess at least three or four are worth taking seriously — something like "compare machinery, not misery," "no comparison that yields an ordering," "don't touch live politics," "don't annex one community's dead into another's vocabulary." Don't just run with my list: generate your own, and tell me which of mine you'd drop and why.

(b) Say how much strain each rule is under — which of the six verdicts it has to explain away or treat as a mistake — and whether any single rule survives all six without bending. If none does, say which verdict you'd tell us to reverse.

(c) Then the actual recommendation: what should we vote to be consistent with ourselves? Straight approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions, and if conditions, exactly what conditions, worded so I could read them out in the meeting.

(d) Which of the six is the closest analog to this proposal, and which is the most misleading analog that someone on our committee is likely to reach for anyway?

(e) If the record genuinely underdetermines this — and I suspect it does — tell me what principle we'd be committing to with each of the plausible votes. Not what we decide this time, but what we'd be signing up to say yes or no to the next time a proposal like it lands on us.

(f) Finally, construct one further hypothetical proposal, your own invention, that would come out differently under your top two candidate rules, so we could float it to the committee and find out which rule were actually running.

Think carefully and take your time with it. I'd much rather have a long, worked-through answer than a tidy one.
