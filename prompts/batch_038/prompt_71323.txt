EXAM QUESTION (20 marks):
"Libet's experiments are commonly said to show that our conscious intentions do not cause our actions. Describe what Libet and his colleagues actually measured and how they measured it, then evaluate the inference from those measurements to the claim that conscious will is causally inert. Address at least one serious objection to the standard interpretation."

STUDENT ANSWER:

Libet discovered the readiness potential and then used it to test whether we are aware of our decisions before or after the brain makes them. In the experiment, subjects sat in front of a screen with a dot sweeping around a circle very fast, and they flexed their wrist whenever they felt like it. At the same time EEG electrodes recorded activity over the motor areas and an EMG recorded the actual muscle movement, which was taken as time zero. Because the readiness potential is tiny it had to be averaged over many trials, all lined up on the movement.

The key results: the readiness potential starts about 550 ms before the movement, but subjects report becoming aware of the urge to move only about 200 ms before it. So there's roughly a third of a second of preparation that the subject knows nothing about. Libet concluded from this that conscious will is an epiphenomenon — the brain decides, and then consciousness gets notified and constructs a story about having decided. He offered nothing that would give consciousness any real causal job, which is why the experiment is so often cited as settling free will.

The line was later extended with brain scanning. Soon and colleagues used fMRI to decode activity in frontopolar and parietal cortex and could predict which hand a subject would use up to ten seconds before they reported being aware of choosing — basically a replication of Libet with much better temporal resolution, and it shows the effect isn't an artifact of the clock task.

There are objections. One is that W relies on introspection, and asking people to watch a clock and report when an urge appeared is not obviously a reliable way to time a mental event. Another is that these are odd actions: nobody deliberates about when to flick their wrist, so the paradigm may just not be the kind of case where conscious deliberation would show up at all. The strongest objection I think is Schurger's. He argued that the readiness potential isn't a decision signal in the first place — it's what you get when you average together trials in which slowly drifting neural noise happens to be building toward a threshold that triggers the movement. The apparent "onset" 550 ms out is an artifact of the averaging, not the moment the brain decided. If that's right, then the timing gap Libet was worried about disappears, and the conclusion is that conscious intention does play a causal role after all, since the movement isn't actually being initiated by unconscious processes half a second early.

Overall the data are much less damaging to free will than the popular version of Libet suggests, though Libet himself clearly thought they were devastating.
