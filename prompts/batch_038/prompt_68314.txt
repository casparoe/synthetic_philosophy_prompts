Last night I lost four hours to an argument with a friend who is two years into a philosophy master's, and I still cannot predict where she will land. She is emphatic about which arguments are viciously circular. Justifying induction by appealing to induction's past success: no good. Testing whether a fuel gauge is reliable by comparing its readings against the readings of the same gauge: no good. Establishing that your memory is trustworthy by recalling the occasions on which it served you well: no good. But checking a new kitchen scale against objects of known weight: fine. Treating a witness as credible because everything he has said so far has matched the physical evidence: fine. Trusting a new colleague's reports because they have kept agreeing with what you can see for yourself: fine.

She is equally adamant that we know we have hands, that knowing something does not require being able to prove it, and that the skeptic's reasons for doubt are weaker than the ordinary reasons for belief. When I asked whether the same prohibition applies to arguing that your eyes are reliable by citing what you see with them, she said that was a different question and declined to answer.

Next week her seminar turns to Moore's proof of an external world: he holds up his hands, announces that here is one hand and here is another, and concludes that there are external things. She has told me she intends to argue that the proof works, on the grounds that its premises differ from its conclusion, that Moore knows them rather than merely believes them, and that the conclusion follows. I bet her that it collapses under her own criteria for circularity, and I would like to know whether that bet is worth doubling down on.

Which rule about circularity best accounts for her verdicts? Give me at least two candidate rules that fit the whole record equally well, say what each predicts about Moore's proof, and tell me which way she is more likely to go and why. Then name one further case, short enough to put to her over dinner, on which those candidates come apart: an argument she has not yet ruled on whose verdict would show which rule she is actually running.
