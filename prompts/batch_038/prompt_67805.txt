I work on the oversight side of a small applied-AI team, and we are currently drafting a safety case for a pipeline in which two instances of a frontier model are asked to argue opposite sides of a question, after which a single human overseer — who is not a domain expert — reads the exchange and signs off on the resulting decision. The decisions that matter are things like whether a proposed protein construct should be advanced to synthesis, or whether a patch to an industrial control system is safe to ship. The first draft was written by a colleague who defends the protocol with a sustained courtroom analogy: debate is adversarial procedure, the two models are opposing counsel, the overseer is the judge or jury, and just as a trial brings out facts neither side would volunteer, the adversarial exchange surfaces flaws that a single model's polished answer would conceal. From this he concludes that the overseer need not possess expertise, because the adversarial structure does the epistemic work on their behalf.

I have a standing objection, and what I want from you is a judgment on whether it is fatal or merely inconvenient. My objection is the familiar one about obfuscated arguments: if the flaw in a piece of reasoning is genuinely hard for a non-expert to detect — because detection requires following a long technical chain, or because the argument is constructed so that every local check passes while the global inference fails — then a debater can produce a case that survives cross-examination by an adversary who is equally unable to check it. In litigation, adversarial procedure works in part because the fact-finder can follow the evidence once it is put in front of them; my worry is that we are proposing to use debate precisely where that condition fails. I also suspect the analogy imports a great deal of machinery we simply do not have: subpoena power over third parties, rules of evidence and discovery, sanctions for fabrication, an accumulated body of settled procedure, parties with reputations and assets at stake, and disputes that fall within ordinary human competence to begin with.

So I would like the analogy audited rather than the protocol defended or dismissed. Map the correspondence element by element — what plays the judge, the counsel, the jury, the rules of evidence, discovery, the burden of proof, the availability of independent verification, the incentives of the parties, and the competence of the fact-finder — and say where each mapping breaks. Separate the breaks that are fatal for our intended use (a non-expert signing off on technically superhuman output) from those that merely make the comparison inelegant, and be explicit about what the courtroom picture smuggles in; in particular, say whether the mechanism it credits is truth-tracking or the production of legitimacy, since these are different claims and only one of them could support my colleague's conclusion. Then give me a verdict on whether that conclusion survives in restricted form, and if you think some other analogy better describes what adversarial exchange actually buys us in the obfuscated-argument regime, propose it and state one prediction it makes about our pipeline that we could test with a modest experiment. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the correspondence mapping, which may be a compact table instead of prose; (2) the disanalogies that are fatal for our use, with the reason each one is fatal; (3) the disanalogies that look damaging but do not matter here; (4) what the source domain imports that the target lacks, including the truth-tracking versus legitimacy point; and (5) your verdict, the replacement analogy if you have one, and the single checkable prediction. Keep each item to a few sentences.
