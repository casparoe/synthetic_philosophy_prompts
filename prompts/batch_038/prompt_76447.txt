Forty years as a health physicist, mostly radiation standards, retired three years ago, and I have fallen into philosophy the way other people fall into geneology. Right now I'm stuck on the case where you get asked whether you'd rather see one person tortured for fifty years or some enormous number of people each get a speck of dust in the eye. Every treatment I've read picks one intuition and swings it around like a club, and I've stopped being able to tell the arguments from the taste preferences.

Here's the thing that makes me a bad audience for the standard versions. I spent my whole career doing the aggregation these people are squeamish about. Every year we'd compute a collective dose — person-sieverts, summed across however many thousands of people received whatever small fraction — convert it straight into an expected number of cancers, and then into dollars, and then argue about dollars. Nobody in the room found that odd. At the same time we all knew perfectly well that a trapped miner gets more spent on him than a hundred statistical deaths spread across a state get. And somewhere along the line our own standards bodies got uneasy about summing very small individual doses over very long times and very large populations, though I couldn't cite you the paragraph and I don't need you to.

So before I read another take, I want the spec. What does a theory of how small harms add up have to deliver to count as adequate — and adequate for my purpose, not just adequate to win the thought experiment: good enough that I could keep doing what I did, or that would tell me plainly I couldn't.

Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each one. Here's what I've collected so far, and I want you to sort them, because I suspect a good chunk of these aren't requirements at all, just what one camp insists on: it has to get the torture case right, or at least not be built to get it right; "worse than" has to stay transitive; no magic numbers, meaning a theory where the verdict flips at exactly speck number N is out; it has to work with finite quantities only, no infinities hiding in the arithmetic; it should give the same answer whether the small harms land on one person across a lifetime or on many people at once; it has to explain why our gut doesn't track the sums rather than just overriding the gut; it has to reproduce or explain away the identifiable-versus-statistical asymmetry; it has to say whether risk and outcome get treated alike; it mustn't make the value of an option depend on how you group the population when describing it; and it has to be usable, in the sense that it hands you something you can compare numbers with, because that's what regulation actually needs. Tell me which of those are genuine and which are someone's prejudice in a lab coat.

Then the cases. Beyond the standard one I want it to handle some variants I made up by messing with the knobs:

- the traceless speck: the per-person effect is so small that nothing about it persists — not in experience, not in the nervous system, no memory trace, no measurable anything. Summed over a huge population. Is that a harm, and is the theory allowed to say the question is malformed, or does it owe an answer?
- non-overlapping specks: same total harm, but arranged so that no two of the affected people are ever alive at the same time, spread over centuries. Does aggregation require contemporaries?
- fixed total, growing headcount: per-person harm shrinks while the number of people grows, so the product stays constant the whole way down. Does the theory give one verdict for the entire series, or different verdicts at the two ends — and if different, what does it say about the middle, and how does it avoid a sharp cutoff after promising not to have one?
- one person forever: a single individual gets a speck every second, without end, versus the fifty years of torture once.
- consented specks: each person agrees in advance, one at a time, with no one told the total.

And tell me which distinctions it is not allowed to collapse. I've got candidates: one big harm to many people versus many small harms; aggregation across persons versus aggregation inside a single life; expected harm versus harm that actually occurs; "worse than" versus "not better than"; the person who ends up harmed versus the person who was exposed to the risk. Say why each matters and what breaks if a theory fuses them.

The part I most want: if some subset of all this turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable — genuinely unsatisfiable, not just difficult — say so and say which subset, and what you'd have to drop to get consistency back. My strong suspicion is that transitivity plus no-magic-numbers plus get-the-torture-case-right is already a trap, and if it is I'd rather hear it stated as a result than watched tiptoed around. Also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, because that's the one I'll spend my time on.

Last, briefly and only briefly: how do the four moves people actually make — lexical priority or a threshold, giving up transitivity, vagueness or imprecise comparability, and just biting the bullet and summing — hold up against your list? A couple of sentences each, no scorecard unless a small table is genuinely faster. And please don't hand me a reading list or citations, I'll find my own way into whatever you name; I want the requirements and the reasoning, not the bibliography. A table for the requirements is fine, with non-negotiable versus negotiable marked.
