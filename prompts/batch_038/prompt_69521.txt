So, background: I write literary fiction, three novels out, none of them about philosophers, and I have zero formal training in any of this. I've been stuck on a scene in a draft and it sent me down a rabbit hole into what people call the dogmatism paradox. As I remember it, roughly: if I know p, then I also know that any evidence I might later run into against p would have to be misleading evidence, since I already know p is true. And it seems rational to disregard evidence you know is misleading. So it's rational for me to disregard any future evidence against p. Which would mean that genuinely knowing something licenses you in shutting your ears, and that's obviously wrong, but I cant see which step to drop.

Here's what I want from you. Translate that whole thing into the vocabulary I actually work in: scenes, point of view, what a character is entitled to know at a given point in the book, dramatic irony, the reveal, plants and payoffs, unreliable narration, motivation, stakes. Go through it piece by piece — the knowledge claim, the step that says any counterevidence would be misleading, the permission to disregard evidence you've already diagnosed as misleading, the generalisation out to all possible future evidence, and the conclusion — and for each piece tell me what it turns into in my terms, what you had to bend or fatten to make it sit there, and what has no expression at all in how I think about a book.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. My hunch going in is that the paradox is a category error, because in a novel a character who refuses to look at further evidence isn't being irrational, she's being characterised — denial, fear, loyality — and what the philosophers are calling knowledge is doing the same job that "written as knowing" does in mine. Tell me whether that hunch survives an honest translation, or whether I'm just smuggling my own framework's assumptions across the border and announcing them as a refutation.
