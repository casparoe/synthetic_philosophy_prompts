And here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

> Probabilism is the thesis that an agent's degrees of belief ought to form a probability function: credence 1 in tautologies, credence 0 in contradictions, and additivity over mutually exclusive disjunctions. Two arguments are standardly given for it.
>
> The older is the Dutch book argument, going back to de Finetti. It begins from the thought that a credence is manifested in a betting disposition: if you assign 0.7 to P, you should be disposed to accept a bet that costs you 70 cents and pays a dollar if P is true. Given a bridge principle linking credence to betting behaviour, one can prove that if your credences violate the probability axioms there is a package of bets, each of which you would accept, that loses you money in every possible circumstance. Non-probabilistic credence is therefore pragmatically self-defeating.
>
> The trouble is that this result is hostage to facts about the agent that have nothing to do with belief as such. It assumes she actually has the relevant betting dispositions, that money enters her utility linearly, and that she has no independent aversion to gambling. A monk who has renounced property, or anyone who simply will not take bets, may hold flagrantly incoherent credences while remaining invulnerable to any book whatsoever. What the Dutch book argument establishes, when it establishes anything, is a fact about the agent's practical situation.
>
> The accuracy-dominance argument, due to Joyce, avoids this by changing the currency. One fixes a measure of inaccuracy that assigns a penalty to an entire credence function at each possible world, according to how far that function is from the truth there; the Brier score, which uses squared error, is the familiar example. A credence function is accuracy-dominated when some alternative is strictly more accurate than it at every possible world. Joyce's result is that any non-probabilistic credence function is accuracy-dominated by some probabilistic one, while probabilistic credence functions are not dominated at all. The structure is that of an ordinary decision-theoretic dominance argument, but what dominates in is closeness to the truth rather than money, so the argument reaches the monk and the non-gambler exactly as it reaches the bettor. That is the normative gain: it shows incoherent credence to be defective in a respect internal to believing, since believing is aiming at the truth.
>
> Nor does the argument inherit the Dutch book's dependence on contested premises. The measure of inaccuracy is a mathematical device for tracking distance from the truth, so the argument carries no evaluative assumptions; it is simply a theorem about which credence functions are dominated. And because that theorem picks out the probabilistic credence functions as precisely the undominated ones, accuracy-first epistemology can go further and rank the probabilistic functions themselves by how accurate they are. This explains why some coherent agents are epistemically better off than others, and why conditionalizing on new evidence makes a credence function more accurate than it was. On both counts the accuracy argument is strictly stronger than the Dutch book argument, which can only tell you not to lose money.
