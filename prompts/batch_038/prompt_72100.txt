I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a British current-affairs magazine, and for the next instalment I want to run a self-contained piece of satire rather than another essay. The column has been working through the way quantitative scoring has colonised the evaluation of research — league tables, impact multipliers, ratings agencies — and I would like a fictional document that carries that argument without stating it.

The conceit: an invented ratings agency, of the kind that grades sovereign debt or structured credit, has opened a new line of business issuing truthlikeness ratings on scientific theories. Not verdicts on whether a theory is true, but gradings of how close to the truth it is, complete with letter grades, outlooks, watch listings and an investable index. Please name the agency yourself, in the register of a City firm or a standards body.

Write the agency's published methodology handbook — the document a client would download before relying on the ratings. Around 800 words, plus a short table defining the rating grades. Numbered sections, deadpan corporate-regulatory prose, British English, and no winking: the reader should be able to work out what is being ironised without the author's help.

The humour has to come from the technical content being right, so please make sure the handbook addresses all of the following:

1. Its founding scandal. The original scoring method — a theory graded by subtracting the content of its false consequences from the content of its true ones — was proved unusable, with the consequence that no false theory can be ranked above any other false theory. The handbook must explain how the agency survived this, in the tone of a firm describing a legacy methodology it has not entirely abandoned.
2. The consequence for the scale: since no theory is known to be true, the top grades have never been awarded. The handbook should account for why the upper rungs of its own flagship scale are permanently unpopulated, and advise clients how to price an instrument whose best possible rating is theoretical.
3. Its treatment of degenerate cases. A self-contradictory theory entails everything and is therefore, on a content-based measure, maximally informative; a tautology says nothing at all and cannot be wrong. Both must be rated somewhere. Please make the resulting grade assignment visibly absurd and yet internally principled.
4. The successor method, which grades a theory by the overall similarity to the actual world of the worlds in which it would be true. This requires the ratings committee to choose a similarity metric — that is, to weight respects of similarity against one another. Stage a paragraph of committee reasoning about how much weight to give, say, getting the number of planets right against getting their orbits right, and let the reader notice that fixing the weighting seems to require already knowing the answer.
5. The alternative workaround, which grades only the relevant consequences of a theory rather than all of them, and the agency's procedure for deciding what counts as relevant — including who is permitted to submit a relevance determination and whose it accepts.
6. The commercial conflicts: theories are rated at the request and expense of their sponsors; agencies compete for mandates; include a rating action that was timed badly, a client who went rating-shopping, and the tracker fund built on the agency's index.
7. The verification problem, which should be the quiet centrepiece. To check whether a rating is correct, one needs the truth, which the agency does not have and cannot obtain. Handle this in a limitations-and-liability section and an appeals procedure, so that the handbook's own fine print concedes that no rating it issues can ever be audited — without the agency appearing to notice.

Include at least three worked case files, invented rather than historical, showing a theory's rating history over time: an upgrade, a downgrade following an unexpected observation, and one theory left on negative watch indefinitely.

Two constraints. First, do not name any real philosopher, real theory, real paper or real institution; the satire is aimed at the practice of treating closeness to the truth as a measurable, auditable, investable quantity, and at the audit culture that demands it — not at any person. Second, no footnotes, no bibliography and no references of any kind: the piece must stand entirely on its own invented apparatus.
