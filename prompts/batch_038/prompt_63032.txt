Retired after 38 years running a lab — mostly spectroscopy, so an enormous amount of my life was spent arguing about error bars. Since I stopped working I read philosophy for fun and I've gotten properly stuck on Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, the one in Knowledge and Its Limits. My daughter-in-law is doing a philosophy degree and we go at it over coffee.

The setup as I understand it: someone wakes up feeling cold, and over the course of the day gradually stops feeling cold, case by case, with no sharp cutoff anywhere in the series. If feeling cold were "luminous" — if whenever you feel cold you're in a position to know you feel cold — then that plus some margin-for-error premise gives you the absurd result that they never know they feel cold at all. And because knowing is itself a non-trivial condition, the same machinery is supposed to show the KK principle fails: you can know p without being in a position to know that you know p.

Here's my problem. That smells exactly like the instrument-resolution argument I used to give grad students: a thermometer rated at ±0.2 reading 21.4 does not license "it is exactly 21.4", and if you push on it hard enough you slide all the way to "you don't know the temperature at all". Which makes me suspicous that the conclusion is true but boring — finite discrimination wearing a new costume, not a deep discovery about knowledge.

So I want your actual credence on this proposition, precisely: KK fails for human beings in ordinary circumstances — there are real cases where someone knows p but is not in a position to know that they know p. A number, and the decomposition behind it, not a survey of the literature.

Sub-questions you might score separately, add or drop whatever you think matters:
- how secure is the margin-for-error premise when applied to knowledge rather than to measurement?
- how much depends on there being no sharp cutoffs in the feeling-cold series, versus epistemicism, which would give you cutoffs nobody can locate?
- how much rides on "know" being context-sensitive, so the argument slides between a strict and a loose reading?
- how much depends on treating knowledge as a mental state in its own right rather than belief-plus-justification-plus-X?
- and what's the probability the conclusion is right but philosophically empty?

I'd say 0.75. She says 0.3, mostly on grounds that its all equivocation. Tell me where our disagreement most likely lives, which single term your number is most sensitive to, and what would actually move it — a better thought experiment, a result about vagueness, empirical psychology of introspection, or something else entirely.
