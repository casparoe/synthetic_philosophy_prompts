I work in treasury operations at a UK commercial bank — note logistics, vault positions, settlement accounts — and last year I took an evening course in the history of economic thought, mostly to have something to read on the commute. One week we looked at Berkeley's monetary writings, the dialogue where he argues against the minute philosopher about whether words have to stand for clear ideas, and the pamphlet on Ireland cast as a long list of numbered questions. What struck me was his insistence that money is not itself wealth but a token or counter entitling the holder to other people's labour, that its whole value lies in stirring up industry, and that it works on the imagination and on expectations rather than by naming any real thing. He also wanted a national bank issuing paper. Ever since, I have not been able to look at the institution I effectively work downstream of in the same way, because the modern arrangement seems to me to be either a realisation of his view or a bad imitation of it, and I cannot tell which.

Here is what puzzles me, and it is three things that are supposed to sit together. First: the note still carries a printed undertaking, signed by the Chief Cashier, reading "I promise to pay the bearer on demand", and the issuer states that this promise holds for all time and that any note may be exchanged for its face value at any time — yet since the abandonment of the gold standard in the 1930s the only thing a bearer can obtain is another claim of exactly the same kind, so the promise can be discharged only in a fresh instance of itself. Second: those notes are carried on the issuer's balance sheet as liabilities, matched against holdings of government securities, which implies they are debts owed to somebody, even though no holder can point to anything the issuer owes them beyond more notes. Third: the same institution's statutory job is to keep consumer price inflation at two per cent, which is to say it is committed to engineering a steady, publicly announced decline in what a note will buy — while describing itself, in the same breath, as the guardian of the currency's value and of the public's trust in money as a store of wealth.

What I would like is a reconstruction of the theory of money this bundle presupposes, not a list of labels. Which account of money makes all three features intelligible at once: one on which the note's worth is the worth of some commodity standing behind it, one on which it is an IOU generated by private lending and settled by offsetting debts, or one on which it is a creature of sovereign authority whose value is fixed by the state's demand for it and its power to manage expectations? Tell me what the practice strictly cannot do without, as against what merely sits comfortably with several rival theories, and where it is philosophically underdetermined. Then tell me whether Berkeley's own position — money as counter rather than thing, valuable only insofar as it sets people to work, meaningful through its effect on the imagination — actually explains the bundle, or whether it explains the second and third features while leaving the first as a stranded artefact; and if it is an artefact, why an institution would keep printing it. Finally, take the tension head on: can an issuer simultaneously undertake to pay the bearer on demand and promise to make what it pays worth two per cent less every year, or does the promise have to be read as something other than a promise — and what would have to change in the practice, in the accounting, and in the mandate if we insisted on reading it literally?
