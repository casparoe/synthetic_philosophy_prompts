Bit of an odd request. I'm a couple of drafts into the absence-causation chapter of my book and before I send it off I'd like someone to kick the tyres on the package as a package, not on the individual arguments for it. Here's what I'm committed to:

(1) causation is counterfactual dependence between distinct actual events, and it's a natural, non-normative relation — nothing evaluative gets smuggled into the analysis;
(2) omissions, absences and nothings aren't events, so they're not in the domain of the relata at all;
(3) hence no causation by omission — strictly speaking the plant didn't die because nobody watered it, and the Queen's not watering it is no more a cause of its death than my not watering it;
(4) but absences still do real explanatory work, and I'm perfectly happy with contrastive claims like: the plant died rather than survived because it wasn't watered rather than watered;
(5) in the responsibility cases I want some discrimination — the nurse who was under a duty and didn't turn the patient caused his death in a sense strong enough to ground blame, while the visitor under no duty caused nothing by not turning him, even though the counterfactuals run the same way in both cases;
(6) and I want the metaphysics, not merely the norms, to carry the doing/allowing distinction.

There's a soft spot in there and I cant see where. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for each real tension, what has to give and what it costs me to give it up? Rank them by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and finish with the single revision that would buy me the most.
