I am a second-year graduate student in a seminar on the logical foundations of agency, and our present unit concerns self-trust: what it takes for a reasoner to rely on its own conclusions. The readings have left me dissatisfied, because each author treats a different failure mode as the central one and none of them states up front what a successful account would have to accomplish. For my term project I want to write that specification before I evaluate any proposals, and I would like your help drafting it.

To fix the target, I have in mind an agent with roughly the following architecture. Before acting, it searches for derivations within a formal theory that it also uses for planning. It treats a course of action as licensed when it can derive in that theory that the action yields an acceptable outcome. It is expected to revise both its theory and its decision procedure over time, and to consult subroutines — some stronger than itself, some weaker — whose outputs it may incorporate. It must act under a hard time bound. What I want is the list of requirements any account of this agent's self-trust must meet to count as adequate, where adequacy means: good enough to serve as a design brief for building such an agent, not good enough to settle the metaphysics of mathematical truth.

Please give the desiderata as a list, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. Here are cases I have assembled myself, several of them my own constructions, and I want each of them treated as data the account must accommodate rather than as illustrations of a favorite puzzle:

1. Trust in a lemma. The agent manages to derive, inside its own theory, that if its theory ever derives a certain claim, then that claim holds. State what follows and what the agent may permissibly conclude, and say why an account that permits the intuitive conclusion here is in trouble.

2. The validator regress. Every derivation the agent produces is checked by a validator whose correctness is itself something the agent could demand a derivation of. Say what an adequate account must do about the regress — block it, absorb it, or declare it illegitimate — and why.

3. Deferred action. The agent's policy is to perform some beneficial act only once it has derived that it will perform that act at some later time. Say what goes wrong, and whether the diagnosis is unsoundness, mis-specification of the policy, or something else. Then consider the variant in which derivations are required to be shorter than a bound that shrinks as time passes, so that no contradiction arises at all; say whether that variant is a solution or a different disease, and what an account owes us here.

4. Deference to a stronger advisor. The agent is told a claim by a reasoner it believes to be strictly stronger than itself, and it cannot reproduce the argument. Say what trusting that report amounts to, and why an account of self-trust that cannot also handle this case is incomplete.

5. Self-modification. The agent must decide now whether to adopt a successor decision procedure about whose behavior it can presently derive nothing. Say what the account must permit here, given that verifying the successor's reliability would require exactly the kind of self-verification the classical results appear to forbid.

6. Opacity. The agent's own decision procedure is not fully representable in its theory — because the theory is smaller than the procedure, or because the procedure consults a bounded search whose results it cannot characterize. Say what an account must say about an agent that cannot state what it is.

Alongside the cases, state the distinctions the account may not collapse (consistency versus soundness; trusting a proposition versus trusting a process that issues propositions; deductive versus defeasible trust; being right versus being able to establish that one is right; trust in one's own outputs versus trust in one's own reliability), the formal results it must remain consistent with (Löb's theorem, the diagonal lemma, the second incompleteness theorem), and the practical constraints it must respect (no unbounded regress, no unbounded computation, and above all no trivial satisfaction by weakening the theory until nothing interesting is derivable).

Then do the following with the list, which is where most of my interest lies. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are local prejudices of a camp. In particular, I suspect that the demand that self-trust be expressible as an internal theorem of a single fixed, recursively axiomatized theory that also contains the agent's own derivability predicate is a prejudice of the classical camp rather than a genuine requirement, and I want to know whether you agree. Identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied: if the list is itself an impossibility result in disguise, that is the part I most want to see, with the argument sketched. And tell me which single requirement excludes the largest number of candidate accounts.

Finally, briefly — a paragraph each, no more — how four styles of proposal fare against the list: assuming the soundness of the agent's own reasoning as a defeasible default premise; avoiding self-reference by having the agent reason about duplicates or successors of itself rather than about itself; replacing derivation with a sequence of estimates that converges without ever becoming certain; and simply forbidding the agent to reason about its own future reasoning.

I want a committed answer rather than a survey. Where you think one of my requirements is bogus, say so and say why. Where you think a subset is unsatisfiable, state the claim and give the reasoning that supports it. And if you believe my stated purpose — design brief rather than metaphysics — already smuggles in a contested assumption, flag it and tell me what it costs to keep it. Do not lay out the options and leave me to choose among them. I am not asking for sources, references, or a reading list; I want the specification and your reasons for it.
