I ran a state environmental lab for thirty-one years — mostly trace metals in drinking water and river sediment, a lot of lead and arsenic, a lot of paperwork — and I'm three years into retirement and about two years into philosophy, which I read badly and out of order in the recliner with the good lamp. Right now I'm chewing on this idea that a theory can be false and still be closer to the truth than another false theory, and I keep thinking the whole thing is something I lived inside of for decades without ever naming it. So let me describe a practice I know from the floor, and ask you to dig out what it assumes.

Every quarter we shipped blind samples off to an interlaboratory proficiency-testing scheme, along with every other accredited lab in the region. A few weeks later came a report telling us our bias, our spread, and a z-score, which is basically your distance from the reference value measured in units of how much disagreement the scheme will tolerate. Stay inside the limits and you keep your accreditation; fall outside a couple of rounds running and you're getting a visit. Here's the thing that bugs me now that I have the vocabulary for it: that reference value is, as often as not, just a consensus — the average of what all the participating labs reported, sometimes robustified to throw out the wildest outliers. We were being graded against the pooled opinion of the very same community whose pooled opinion we were part of. And yet the official story of the whole discipline is flatly realist: there is a true value of the lead concentration in that particular bottle, every result comes with an uncertainty budget that's supposed to describe an interval around that true value, our instruments get calibrated against standards that trace back up a chain to a national lab, and the international guidance everyone works from says the true value is an idealization you can approach and bracket but never actually obtain. Even the certified reference materials we bought to anchor our curves sometimes turn out to have gotten their certified number from a round of labs measuring the same vial, so the anchor is itself a consensus wearing a traceability certificate. I used to think nothing of it. Now it looks to me like the same institution is saying two different things about what truth is, and I can't tell whether that's a contradiction or just a division of labor I never bothered to notice.

So: reconstruct the implicit philosophy here as carefully as you can. What account of truth, and of closeness to it, does this apparatus strictly need in order to run at all — what breaks on the lab floor if you take it away? I want you to be picky about the difference between what the practice genuinely presupposes and what merely fits it comfortably; my hunch is that a lot of the metaphysics is optional and the practice would run just the same under several rival stories, and I'd like to know which ones and why. Consider the obvious candidates and say whether each can license the machinery: a plain correspondence picture with real magnitudes out there; truth as what the community would settle on at the ideal limit of inquiry; truth as whatever survives the best-designed comparisons; something deflationary where the reference value is the whole show and "the true value" is just a manner of speaking. Then tell me where the two halves of the practice really do pull against each other, if they do — grading people against a consensus mean while insisting in the paperwork that the consensus is only a fallible estimate of something independent — and whether the tension is philosophically fatal or just bookkeeping. And one more thing I'd especially like you to take seriously, because this is the part that got me into the verisimilitude literature: does the practice's notion of nearness-to-truth actually generalize? It works because the target is a single scalar, so "distance" has an obvious meaning and the estimate can be held fixed while the methods vary. What would have to be true of a whole theory — not a number but a bundle of claims about, say, how arsenic behaves in an estuary — before you could say the same thing about it, and is there anything in this lab practice that gives a philosopher reason to be optimistic or pessimistic about that move? Please answer in American English; the last few things I asked about came back full of "analyse" and "behaviour" and my wife found it unbearable. And don't bother giving me a reading list — I can't get at most of those books out here and I'm after the reasoning, not the references.
