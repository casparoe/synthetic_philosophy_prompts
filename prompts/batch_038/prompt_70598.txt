Last month my city council voted 6–3 to issue a formal apology for how the highway through the East End got built in the late fifties — the neighborhood got declared blighted, a few hundred families were pushed out, and almost none of them could buy back in anywhere else in the city. My uncle was watching the livestream with me and he kept repeating one sentence: "you can't apologize for something you didn't do." He used it at least four times and each time it seemed to be doing something slightly different. First it was that the apology is impossible, since nobody on that council was born when the houses came down. Then it was that it's insincere, since you can't feel bad about something you had no part in. Then it was aimed at the two million dollar housing fund in the same resolution, which he said was just taking money from people who did nothing wrong. And finally when I pushed back he said it was really about guilt: nobody's saying the city didn't do wrong, he said, he's saying I personally didn't do it and neither did he, and an apology is a confession.

I'm pretty sure at least one of those is a different claim than the others, and I suspect some of them are true but boring while exactly one is doing all the heavy lifting. But I dont have the vocabulary to pull them apart, and every time I tried at the dinner table I ended up conceding the point.

So treat that one sentence as an object of study rather than a position to take — you're a political philosopher who works on collective responsibility and you're prepping me for the next council meeting, not writing an op-ed. Start by brainstorming before you commit to anything: just generate as many genuinely distinct propositions as you can that the sentence could be expressing, including ones about who the speaker of an apology is, what apologizing does as a speech act versus what it merely signals, what "did" is doing (caused? participated in? benefited from? authorized? belongs to?), and whether a city in 2024 is even the same moral agent as the city in 1958 or just shares a name and some buildings with it. Then organize them into a numbered menu I could bring to a meeting. For each reading give me (a) the precise claim it amounts to, (b) whether it's a platitude everyone already accepts, a substantive thesis that needs an argument, or just false, and (c) which of my uncle's four uses it would actually support if any. I especially want to know whether ANY reading makes the apology literally impossible as opposed to pointless, insufficient, or misdirected, and whether it's coherent to hold that a continuing institution can apologize while denying that any living person is guilty of the original act — because I think that's what I actually believe and I couldn't defend it for two seconds. Don't give me citations or a reading list, I can't look anything up and I don't want guesses. Think it through carefully before writing, and if you're unsure whether two readings are really distinct, flag that instead of quietly merging them.
