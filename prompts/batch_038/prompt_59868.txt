Okay, so I TA a senior political theory seminar and for the last three weeks each pair of students has to stage a live debate in section. I'm supposed to hand them one worked example so they can see what I mean by "actually respond to each other instead of delivering two parallel monologues," and the week I got stuck modelling is Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry — which is definately the week where the readings go over everyone's head, so the example has to do real work. Write me a dialogue between two invented characters: a Deweyan, and a critic who thinks Dewey has just described a well-run technocracy and stapled the word "public" onto it. The critic should be in the spirit of Lippmann without being Lippmann — don't name him, don't quote anything, and neither character should cite a philosopher as an authority, because leaning on "well, as Dewey says" is exactly the move I'm trying to get my students to stop making.

Here's the situation to build it around. A mid-sized city's school board is weighing whether to close two underenrolled middle schools, and it proposes a two-year pilot: half the district's kids get lottery-assigned into the consolidation plan, half don't, and an independent evaluation runs at the end. A citizens' assembly would be convened to decide what the evaluation counts as success. The Deweyan is the board's consultant and wants the assembly's criteria to be binding; the critic is a policy-school professor who says the whole design is incoherent. The thing I care about most is that neither one gets to cheat. The critic needs to land the point that an experiment requires its criterion fixed in advance, so "we ran it and looked at what happened" isn't a verdict unless somebody already said what counted — and the point that a citizens' assembly has no feedback loop of the kind that rival expert offices impose on each other, so calling its output "inquiry" just launders whatever narrative was most vivid in the room as knowledge. The Deweyan needs to land the point that the district's evaluation office reports to the same superintendent who proposed the closures, which makes the expert criterion endogenous to the power that framed the question in the first place — and that the people who eat the indirect consequences (bus times, who walks the younger siblings home, whether the neighborhood loses the only building with a gym) are the ones actually positioned to say what counts as a cost.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1200 words of alternating turns, with nothing but a short speaker tag between them. I want at least two spots where one of them concedes something that genuinely costs them, and I want them to end up disagreeing rather than hugging it out — the students need to see that a real crux stays unresolved. Then, on the last line, give me the single proposition such that if the critic granted it the Deweyan would basically have won the argument; one sentence, starting with "Verdict:". I'll read that line out loud to section beforehand so they know what to listen for.
