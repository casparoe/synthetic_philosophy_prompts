I have been reading and arguing about the difference between lying and misleading for the better part of two decades, mostly in rationalist-adjacent corners of the internet, and I have come to suspect that the usual method of trading intuitions about individual cases never really puts the distinction under load. I would like you to write a short story that does.

I know the machinery well enough: what is said versus what is implicated, the hearer's license to infer more than was asserted, the two familiar positions that the liar does worse because he makes himself the author of a belief he is answerable for, and that the misleader does worse because he conscripts the hearer's own cooperative inference into the deception. I want the story to exploit that machinery without ever naming it, and to test whether the distinction survives a situation in which people know about it and are trying to live inside it.

Here is the setting I have in mind. Please keep the structural features, since the philosophical pressure comes from them; adjust the surface details as suits you.

A closed community — an order, an intentional settlement, a company town, your choice — lives under exactly one enforceable speech rule: no member may assert anything they believe to be false. Two further provisions matter. First, a member who is directly questioned by another member, or by an adjudicator, may not decline to answer; silence, a shrug, an explicit refusal, and "I would rather not say" are all punishable, so the ordinary exit from the whole problem is closed off. Second, the community keeps a Relevance Ledger: when an utterance is contested, the speaker must afterwards state on the record why what they said bore on the question asked, and an adjudicator may find that a perfectly true statement was offered in bad faith if the speaker cannot show that it bore on the question at all.

After a generation under these rules, a craft has grown up in the gaps: producing false beliefs out of nothing but true assertions. Selecting which truths to volunteer. Ordering them. Answering an adjacent question so fluently that no one notices the one that went unanswered. Letting the hearer's own assumptions complete the sentence. The practitioners are not merely tolerated but respected, and some are sought out for instruction. Invent the community's own vocabulary for this craft and for its offices — do not import academic terminology, do not name any philosopher, and do not let any character deliver a lecture about what they are doing. A term should sound like something these people would actually say over a meal.

The story should turn on one hard case: an adjudicator must decide whether a particular exercise of the craft violated the rule, was a legitimate use of it, or belongs to a category the rule was never drafted to reach. Construct the case so that the strongest argument for each of those three verdicts is genuinely available from the facts as presented, with no hidden tell that quietly settles it. Make the adjudicator someone who is himself an accomplished practitioner of the craft, and make that fact do work in the story rather than serve as decoration. I would also like at least one Ledger entry to appear as an actual document in the text — the speaker's stated reason for relevance, written in their own hand, and visibly strained.

Please aim for roughly 2,000 to 2,500 words. Prose should be concrete and restrained, with the philosophical content living in institutional detail, in what characters choose to say and in what order, and in what they are obliged to write down afterward. I would rather the story end without the verdict being announced, or with a verdict whose stated rationale the reader is left to distrust, than have the question resolved by authorial fiat. This should be an original piece, not a recognisable reskin of a familiar tale with the names changed.

After the story, in a separate note of no more than a few hundred words, tell me which of my two structural provisions did the most work in generating the difficulty, and what the story suggests about whether the line between asserting something false and merely inducing a false belief can remain stable once it has been made the explicit object of a rule that people are simultaneously trying to comply with and to defeat. Do not recommend further reading or point me toward sources; I am interested in your assessment, not in a syllabus.
