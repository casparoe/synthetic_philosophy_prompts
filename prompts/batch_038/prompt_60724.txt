I write literary fiction and I'm about 60% into a novel whose narrator is a ghostwriter — a very good one, two prize-shortlisted memoirs to her name, never credited, genuinely unbothered by it. In the second half she's on a festival panel and gets cornered by a novelist who's just come through a scandal about an AI-drafted chapter. I want her to win that exchange on the merits, because he's a sympathetic character and I don't want to take the scene by making him slow. So I've been sharpening her arguement for weeks and now I've hit a wall.

Her case, numbered because my editor made me lay it out that way and it turned out to help:

1. The only properties a text has that can bear on its value are properties a reader could encounter in reading it.
2. Who actually produced the words isn't one of those properties. From inside the reading you can't tell whether a sentence came from its nominal author, from a ghost, from a model, or out of a dead poet's notebook.
3. So facts about production don't bear on a text's value.
4. So two texts that are word-for-word the same, and read the same, can't differ in value, whatever their provenance.
5. Which means an undetected plagiarism isn't a worse piece of writing than its source; my uncredited chapters aren't lesser chapters for being uncredited; and a passage a model wrote that nobody can distinguish from my own is exactly as good as my own.
6. So the authenticity industry — the forgery scandals, the disclosure rules, the panic — isn't making a claim about value at all. It's tracking something else and mislabeling it.

Here's my problem. I don't believe (5) or (6), and my narrator only half-believes them, which is supposed to be the interesting part. But I can't find the break. Every step looked like something I'd have signed when I read it, and the movement from step to step looks like its just unpacking the words already in the premises.

My instinct is that (1) is the load-bearing wall, but if I knock it out I'm left with value that nobody could ever encounter, which for a working novelist is a worse place to stand than the one I started in — it makes the whole job mystical. I'd much rather lose (4). Except (4) looks like (3) plus arithmetic.

So: which step do you cut, why did it look fine while it was standing, and what does my narrator have to hand over once you've cut it? If there's more than one repair available, price them against each other — I need to know which version of her is most interesting to be wrong in, and whether she can keep (1) at all without losing the rest of the argument's spine.

Separately: I know you'll reach for that Borges story about the French symbolist who rewrote Don Quixote verbatim, she'd have the poster on her wall, but I've never been able to tell whether Menard's Quixote is supposed to come out better, worse, or as a different kind of thing altogether, and my step (4) has a confident answer about it that I don't trust for a second. Tell me which of those the case actually supports and whether it cuts for her or against her.
