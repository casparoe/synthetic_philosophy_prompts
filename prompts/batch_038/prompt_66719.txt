I am preparing a draft of an essay for a workshop on the explanation of mass atrocity, and I would be grateful for candid comments. The essay is unfinished and deliberately argumentative; I would rather be told where it fails than reassured. The draft follows, and my specific questions come after it.

---

**The Price of Refusal: situational and cultural accounts of Reserve Police Battalion 101**

**1. A shared evidentiary base, divergent readings.** The standard way of framing the debate between Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* (1996) is as a contest between situation and culture. But the framing conceals something odd: to a large extent the two accounts rest on the same documentary base, namely the postwar judicial testimony of the men of Reserve Police Battalion 101, a Hamburg order-police reserve unit of middle-aged, largely working-class men who had been found ineligible for regular military service and who were deployed in occupied Poland in 1942 for roundups, deportations, shootings, and the pursuit of Jews who had gone into hiding. Both authors describe the same unit, the same actions, and much of the same testimony, and they reach opposite conclusions about what it shows. That is itself the most interesting datum the debate produced, and neither book treats it as such.

**2. Separating two quantities the debate conflates.** Let P be the underlying disposition — the fraction of the men who would have killed in the absence of social pressure. Let c be the price of refusal — what it cost an individual man, in sanction, assignment, standing, and self-conception, to decline publicly. Observed compliance is a joint function of the two, and the dispute is only genuine if c can be estimated independently of P. Browning's material is best read as evidence about c; Goldhagen's as a claim about P. Neither says so.

The point becomes sharp when we look at the single datum most often cited by both sides: the battalion's first major shooting action at Józefów in July 1942, where the commander assembled his men, explained the task, expressed his own visible reluctance about it, and invited anyone who did not feel up to it to step out. Fewer than a dozen of roughly five hundred did. That figure is usually presented as a measure of P — either as proof that these men needed no ideological fuel, or as proof that the fuel was already there. I want to argue the opposite: the Józefów episode is close to a natural experiment in c, and it is therefore almost uninformative about P. It is the one occasion in the unit's history in which refusal was authorized from above, individuated, and given a respectable vocabulary. A price that has been officially lowered to near zero should produce a certain volume of exit, and it produced very little; but the size of the residual is bounded by how many men could, in that moment, conceive of themselves as the sort of person who steps out. That is a claim about the availability of a self-description, not about antisemitic belief.

**3. The curve, not the point.** What the two books both underuse is the shape of compliance over time. Early refusal could be framed as personal incapacity — nerves, an inability to shoot children — rather than as dissent, and the commander's offer made refusal a sanctioned category. As the unit's routine hardened across 1942 and 1943, refusal lost its authorized vocabulary, and each act of participation raised the cost of the next refusal by converting the man from bystander into accomplice in his own eyes and his comrades'. On this reading the escalation is a fact about the dynamics of c, and it yields a testable signature: evasion should be more frequent in actions where refusal could be individualized and where non-participation was not visible to peers, and less frequent in killings carried out face to face in a cordon under mutual observation. I offer this as a conjecture about the battalion's record, not as a finding.

**4. An equivocation on ordinary.** Browning's "ordinary men" is a demographic claim: these were not SS careerists but reservists with trades and families. Goldhagen's "ordinary Germans" is a cultural claim: they held what their society held. Both claims can be true simultaneously, and much of the public controversy consisted of asserting one against the other. Once the equivocation is removed, the surviving disagreement is considerably narrower than the publicity implied, and it concerns the content, distribution, and causal weight of German antisemitism rather than the composition of the battalion.

**5. Testimony under penal interest.** One asymmetry in the shared evidentiary base deserves more attention than it has received. The testimony comes from men who were suspects or witnesses in homicide proceedings, and therefore had an interest in how their motives were described. Admissions against penal interest are worth more than exculpatory claims. Situational explanations are exculpatory: the man who says he was swept along by orders and comrades is offering a defense. Reports of ideological conviction are, on their face, against interest. That gives the cultural reading an epistemic advantage on a narrow class of statements — those in which a man acknowledges having wanted to kill, or having approved of the killing — while leaving the situational reading with the advantage on everything else.

**6. Vulnerabilities.** Three objections I have not resolved. First, a price schedule explains compliance but not enthusiasm; where the record suggests gratuitous cruelty, c is silent, and the excess must be assigned to P or to something neither author names — group bonding, or dehumanization as an acquired practice rather than an inherited belief. Second, the P/c split risks unfalsifiability, since any act of compliance can be assigned to c after the fact; the discipline must be that c is measured only by features independent of the behavior to be explained (whether a sanction was actually offered, whether refusal was authorized by a superior, whether non-participation was visible to peers). Third, character may be a third variable that is dispositional without being ideological: the men who stepped out may have differed chiefly in possessing an identity — trade, family, church, age — that made their self-conception less dependent on the group.

My questions, in order of importance:

1. Does the P/c decomposition do any explanatory work, or is it a redescription of the situational account with new labels? In particular, does section 3 predict anything that a straightforward peer-pressure story would not already predict?
2. Please flag every factual claim in the draft that is wrong, imprecise, or overstated. I am least confident about: the number of men who stepped out at Józefów and the size of the battalion; whether the commander's offer preceded the men's arrival at the killing site; how far Goldhagen drew on the same testimony as Browning rather than on other archival material; and my description of the unit's later activities.
3. Section 5 is the part of the draft I most want attacked. Does the asymmetry survive the following counter-consideration: a diffuse attribution of antisemitism to the whole society is itself exculpatory, since it distributes responsibility, and so statements of the form "everyone thought that way" are not admissions against penal interest at all? If it does not survive, is there a narrower version of the point that does?
4. State the single strongest objection to the essay as a whole, and say whether anything in section 6 answers it or merely concedes it.
5. Is any of this interesting to a reader who already knows the debate, or is the draft mostly recapitulation with terminology added? Please rank sections 1 through 6 by how much they need cutting versus expanding, and say in one sentence why each lands where it does.
6. Suggest one comparative case — another killing unit, another occupation regime, another genocide, or a non-genocidal institution such as a military or police force — that would most sharply test the price-of-refusal account, and say what result would count against it.

I am not looking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list, and you should not feel obliged to supply them. I want your own judgment: where you think a claim of mine is false, say that it is false and say what you take the fact to be, and where you are unsure, say that too rather than hedging into agreement.
