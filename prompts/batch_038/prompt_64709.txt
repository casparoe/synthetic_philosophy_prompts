So for like six months my local group has been running a homebrew Nomic thing — you know, Suber's game where changing the rules is a move, the one Hofstadter wrote up. Ours has a mutable amendment clause (two-thirds to pass anything), plus we kept a few of Suber's structural ideas: an immutable/mutable split, unanimity required to transmute something immutable, a bar on retroactive effect, and a rule saying nothing is impermissible just because it's self-referential or amends its own authority.

Problem: we've accumulated a pile of disputed votes, and my gut verdicts on them are not obviously consistent. Here they are, roughly in the order they came up.

1. A 2/3 vote added a new immutable rule whose whole content is "this rule may not be transmuted, amended, or repealed." I called it valid. But our transmutation rule lets a unanimous vote transmute anything immutable, so the new rule clashes with an existing one — and 2/3 isn't unanimity. I still think the amendment was valid, just inert wherever it collides. Weird, but that's my verdict.

2. Later the group passed a 2/3 amendment repealing the amendment clause itself, with no replacement. Verdict: valid, and the game should have ended right there. Everyone hated this and I stood by it.

3. A 2/3 amendment setting the threshold for all future amendments to unanimity: valid. Three sessions later, a 2/3 amendment restoring the threshold to two-thirds: also valid. I know that's bad — if the group can undo its own entrenchment by the same majority it entrenched with, entrenchment means nothing. But I can't bring myself to call the second one void.

4. An amendment saying "any amendment adopted before today is hereby repealed": void, because of the no-retroactivity rule, which I think is doing real work and isn't just procedural decoration.

5. A vote was held while one player was in the bathroom, so it arguably lacked the required participation from every voter. Everyone played under the new rule for five sessions without objecting; then it started costing someone points and they tried to declare it void from the start. Verdict: too late, acquiescence cured the defect.

6. Someone amended the win condition to "the player who most recently amended the win condition wins," and won on the spot. Verdict: valid but degenerate, and I'd have wanted to block it — though I'm not sure the "clearly destructive of play" clause in Suber's rules is a real principle or just taste with a rule-shaped shell.

7. A proposal to enact "no rule change of any kind may ever be proposed again," in a system that also requires there to always be at least one mutable rule. Verdict: void, head-on collision with the at-least-one-mutable requirement. I can see this is nearly the same move as case 2, which I called valid, and I am not okay with that, but I can't fix it.

My tentative principle, which I keep poking at: an amendment is valid iff the rule system *after* the change still contains a rule that licenses the change — validity as a fixed point, the post-state has to retroactively bless the act that produced it. It handles 3 nicely, it handles the 1 collision okay, and it flatly contradicts my verdict in 2 (post-state licenses nothing, so the repeal should have been void).

What I want:

- Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets most of these right? State each clause precisely and say which case motivates it — I'd rather have three clauses that each earn their place than a principle with a bespoke exception per case.
- Which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? Give me the ones I lose, not just the ones I keep.
- What does it predict for cases I haven't hit yet: (a) an amendment changing who's eligible to vote, passed by a vote that the newly-disenfranchised player didn't get to cast in; (b) an amendment reading "the rules in force are whatever the person reading this rule aloud says they are"; (c) two amendments in the same vote that each void the other.
- How much of this is tracking something real about legal systems versus being an artifact of us writing the immutable/mutable split into the rules in the first place? Suber's own note claims no rule is absolutely immune to change, since transmutation is always available at unanimity — is that a solution or a dodge? And would the "continuing" view (the current group always retains full power, no binding of successors) have given cleaner verdicts across all seven than the "self-embracing" view I seem to have drifted into without noticing?

Also: which single case do you think the whole dispute actually turns on, if I only get to re-litigate one of them?
