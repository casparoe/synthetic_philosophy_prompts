I am assembling a small anthology of very short fiction about live problems in practical ethics, and I would like you to contribute a piece built around the doctrine of double effect — specifically around its condition that a bad effect may be foreseen but must not be intended, and must not be the means by which the good effect is brought about.

What interests me is not the usual question of whether the doctrine delivers the right verdict in a familiar case, but a problem about descriptions. Whether a death was intended as a means or merely foreseen as a side effect seems to turn on how finely or coarsely the agent describes her own act: pitched at the level of her purpose, the death is incidental; pitched at the level of what she did to the water, the death is precisely how the purpose was achieved; and nothing in her psychology appears to settle which of these is the true description of what she did.

Please build the story from the following situation. You may rename people and adjust details, but keep the structure intact:

- A woman is the night operator of a flood-control sluice on a river above a small town. During a storm she opens a secondary channel that is known to run past an occupied caretaker's cottage; the town of several thousand is spared and the man in the cottage drowns.
- A compensation board must afterwards classify her act, because the classification decides the widow's award and whether a charge follows: a death brought about as a means is treated one way, a death merely foreseen another.
- The board asks her to state, in her own words and at whatever level of detail she chooses, what she was trying to do. Each wording she offers is examined, and each comes out differently depending on the granularity she happens to have picked.
- Consider wordings pitched at several distinct levels: what she wanted for the town, what her hands did at the wheel, where she sent the water, what she knew about the cottage, what she would have done had the cottage been empty.
- The piece should end on her recognizing that the wording is now being chosen for reasons that have nothing to do with the truth of what she was aiming at — and that the identical test, applied to the sluice's automatic controller, which opened the same channel on its own during an earlier storm, produced a clean classification without the slightest difficulty.

Format: give me the story as a numbered list of exactly five scenes, each one or two sentences long, in narrative order, with no title, no introduction, and no closing note. The five scenes should track five successive attempts at the wording, so that the problem surfaces from the sequence itself. No character may name the doctrine, lecture about it, or step back to comment on the vocabulary; the board's forms and questions may use whatever terms they like. Keep the prose concrete and understated.
