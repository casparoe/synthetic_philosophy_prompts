I am in my first year of a physics PhD, and this term our department's student reading group ran a four-week unit on the thermodynamics of computation, finishing with Maxwell's demon. The session in question was led by a postdoc who works on stochastic thermodynamics, and he laid out what I understand to be the standard modern account: start with Szilard's one-molecule engine, note that the demon acquires one bit when it determines which half of the box the molecule occupies, that this lets it extract an average of kT ln 2 of work per cycle from a single heat bath, and that the books balance only once you notice that the demon's memory has to be returned to a standard blank state before the cycle can repeat — an erasure which, on Landauer's bound, dissipates at least kT ln 2 into the reservoir at temperature T. A friend of mine who is in computer science rather than physics objected that nothing in that argument forces the demon to have a *small* memory: equip it with a store far larger than the number of cycles it intends to run, let it write down each measurement result and never overwrite anything, and it appears to keep converting heat into work without ever paying the erasure cost, with the second law rescued only by an unstated finiteness or cyclicity assumption. The postdoc said that 'information is physical' and moved on to a slide about reversible computing, and the room accepted this and we went for beer.

My difficulty is that I have now heard that same sentence used to mean at least half a dozen different things, and I cannot tell which was being invoked, or whether any of them actually answers my friend. So I would like a menu rather than an explanation of one preferred reading. Among the candidates I can think of are: the ontological claim that a bit is never anything other than the state of some material system, so information has no existence independent of physics; the claim that *acquiring* information about a system carries an unavoidable energetic price of at least kT ln 2 per bit, which I had thought was undercut by the result that measurement can in principle be carried out reversibly and the cost relocated to erasure; the narrower claim that only logically irreversible operations have a strict dissipation floor, and that resetting a record to a standard state is the paradigm case; the claim that Shannon entropy and thermodynamic entropy are literally one and the same quantity up to a factor of k ln 2, which is what licenses adding the demon's information to the gas's entropy in a single ledger; the claim that entropy is observer-relative, so that a system does not simply *have* an entropy but has one relative to a state of knowledge, which would explain why the demon and we disagree about the books; the claim that there is no computation apart from its physical implementation, so thermodynamic limits are limits on computation rather than on cleverness; and the much stronger claim that the second law is not really a law about gases at all but a theorem about what any physically embodied information-processing agent can achieve in expectation.

For each candidate, please state it precisely enough that I could recognize whether a given argument is using it; say whether it is a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive but defensible claim, or an overreach that is simply false; say whether it on its own would answer my friend's infinite-memory objection or whether something else is needed alongside it; and say which of them the postdoc most plausibly meant. Please also point out where two of these readings are routinely conflated in ordinary usage, because I suspect the sentence's rhetorical force depends on sliding between a version that is trivially true and a version that is doing real argumentative work. Finally, a request about how you calibrate the answer: I know this is not a closed topic. My impression is that most working physicists treat the erasure bound as settled, while a minority of philosophers of physics have argued that the usual derivations are circular or admit counterexamples, and I have no way of weighing that minority view. Please be explicit about which of your verdicts reflect a genuine consensus among people who work on this and which are contested, and do not smuggle a contested position in as though it were agreed upon — nor, in the other direction, represent an established result as though it were still up for grabs. Where you are unsure which reading is the standard one, say so. I am not looking for references or a reading list, just the distinctions and your assessment of them.
