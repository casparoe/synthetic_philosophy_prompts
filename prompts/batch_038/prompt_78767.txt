We run a marketplace for local errand work. Customers post a job, couriers accept or decline it. Sam, our data science lead (ex-econ PhD), built a model that forcasts accept rates. The core is deliberately dumb: a courier accepts if and only if the implied dollars-per-minute on the job clears a reservation threshold, and that threshold is roughly stable across couriers and across weeks. That's the whole engine. It does not represent that a big chunk of couriers refuse any multi-stop job on principle, that some won't go downtown after 5pm, that long restaurant wait times make people bail mid-shift, that accept rates depend on who else is online. Out of sample it lands within ~2 points of actual accept rate, which is better than anything we had.

Marcus is a staff engineer who spent five years in ops before he could code. Their argument, roughly in order:

1. Marcus: the model is junk and the fit is luck. The central assumption is flatly false — couriers do not compute dollars-per-minute, and if they did, the threshold isn't stable. A model whose engine is a known falsehood that happens to match last year's data isn't a model, it's a coincidence with a good backtest.

2. Sam: the truth of the assumptions is not a test of a model. The only test is predictive accuracy. He said this is Milton Friedman's thing from the 50s about economic methodology and that it has a nickname, the F-twist, and that Marcus is doing armchair philosophy instead of looking at the error bars.

3. Marcus: that's self-sealing. If assumptions can never be criticized on their own, then literally nothing can count against a model except its predictions, and predictions can always be patched with another fudge factor. So the position collapses into "no model is ever wrong."

4. Sam: yes, and that's correct, predictions are the only thing that can make a model wrong. He added: two models built on flat-out contradictory assumptions can fit the same data, so a good fit can't tell you which of your falsehoods you're leaning on — which is precisely why you pick based on held-out error and stop there. Then he gave the billiard-player example, that expert players don't compute the geometry but play as if they do, so the math doesn't have to be inside the courier's head. And he said something like: the more significant a theory, the more unrealistic its assumptions, because significance means wide scope, and wide scope means abstracting away from detail.

5. Marcus: "unrealistic" is doing two seperate jobs there and Sam is trading between them. Leaving restaurant wait times out is abstraction — you omitted something. Asserting that couriers weigh dollars-per-minute when they actually weigh hassle is distortion — you put in something false, pointing the wrong direction. The first is normal science. The second is what our model does, and point 4 only licenses the first.

6. Marcus: and anyway we're not buying this model to forcast. We're buying it to decide whether to add a bonus on multi-stop jobs. Under a change like that the historical fit is worth nothing, because the model contains no mechanism the bonus would act on — it'll just report that accept rates go up by whatever the dollars-per-minute arithmetic implies.

7. Sam: Marcus has no positive alternative, only discomfort. The rival model — the one built partly from courier survey answers about why they decline — was fit on the same historical window, has worse held-out error, carries forty-odd fitted parameters, and encodes its own untestable assumption, namely that the reasons people state are the reasons they act. He also said the F-twist never claimed assumptions are irrelevant, only that their truth isn't the acceptance test; he's happy to inspect assumptions as a debugging heuristic, which is exactly what a sane engineer does.

8. Marcus: then you've already abandoned it. If assumption-realism guides which model you revise, which one you trust under a change, and which one you ship, then assumption-realism is doing real work in model choice — which is the exact thing the strong version of point 2 denies.
