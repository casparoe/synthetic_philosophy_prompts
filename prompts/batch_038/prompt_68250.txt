THE QUESTION AS SET (the case is invented for the exam; students were told ~600 words and that no citations were needed)

Question 2. The Alderman Island bark beetle.

Males of this beetle carry a large flattened horn on the pronotum; females carry a much reduced one. Three stipulated facts about its history and its present situation:

(i) Phylogenetic and fossil evidence indicates the horn arose roughly 2.4 million years ago and was maintained throughout that period by male–male combat over breeding tunnels: males with bigger horns won tunnels, and winning tunnels meant mating.

(ii) About sixty years ago a predatory wasp was accidentally introduced to the island. Tunnel sites have since collapsed in density and combat in the wild has all but disappeared. In its place, both sexes now jam themselves into bark crevices when wasps approach, and the horn acts as the wedge; beetles that get wedged are almost never taken. Laboratory measurement confirms the horn contributes the large majority of holding force, and staged encounters confirm it would still confer a decisive advantage in a fight.

(iii) The exception to (ii): about 4% of males still fight, and still win tunnels with the horn.

Answer:

(a) What does the etiological (selected-effect) account say the horn's function is? What does a causal-role account say? Be explicit about what kind of fact each is appealing to, and about why the two genuinely come apart in this case rather than merely describing one fact in two vocabularies.

(b) Take one wild beetle whose horn is warped, so that it can neither wedge effectively nor fight. Is this a case of malfunction on each account? And is the availability of malfunction an advantage, a liability, or neutral for that account — i.e., what does it cost or buy the view?

(c) Does the 4% change your answer to (a)? Say what a defender of each account should make of that minority, including whether it matters that the horn still delivers combat success on the rare occasions combat happens.

(d) Does this case show that one account is better than the other, or does it show that the two are not really in competition? Take a position and justify it.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(a) On the etiological account — roughly the view Millikan developed under the name proper function — a trait's function is what it was selected for, so the horn's function is fighting. It's there because ancestors with bigger horns won tunnels and thereby out-reproduced their rivals, and that history is what fixes what the horn is for. On the causal-role account — roughly the view Cummins developed — function is what a trait currently does by way of contributing to some capacity of the system containing it, so here the function is wedging, since that is the contribution the horn actually makes and the reason the beetles are still alive. They come apart in a real way, not just in words, because they give different answers: fighting and wedging are different effects, and you cannot derive one from the other.

There is a difficulty with how the etiological account is usually described, though. It's normally glossed as saying that the function of a trait is the effect that explains why the trait goes on being retained, which makes it forward-looking: the function is fixed by the effects the trait will continue to have. That can't be right, because it would make function change with the future, and it's absurd that what a horn is for should depend on what happens next. So the account really has to be read backwards, as I've read it above. But read backwards it has the consequence that the horn's function is still fighting even though fighting has stopped, which is what (b) turns on.

One thing the etiological account gets right, and I think it's the most important thing in the whole case: on that account essentially every normal beetle on the island has a malfunctioning horn at this moment, because none of them do what the horn was selected to do. Malfunction is a notion the causal-role account has no use for. All it does is describe the causal contributions a trait in fact makes, so for the warped beetle it simply says that this horn contributes little holding force, and there's no further fact about what the horn was supposed to do which it is failing to live up to. Function talk on that view is description, not norm. So the warped beetle isn't malfunctioning, it's just different, and that seems to me a serious cost: biology plainly wants to say something has gone wrong here, and wants to say it more strongly than for a beetle with a slightly odd but serviceable horn.

(c) The 4% doesn't change anything. If a handful of males still fight, the horn still has combat as its selected effect in those individuals, and the causal-role story is unaffected too, because wedging is still what nearly all the beetles do. Both accounts carry on exactly as before, so the minority is a curiosity rather than a complication.

(d) The case shows the etiological account is the better one, because it's the only account here that can ground malfunction, and malfunction is a real biological phenomenon any theory of function has to make sense of. A view that cannot distinguish a horn that is broken from a horn that is merely unusual is not a theory of function but a theory of causal contribution. So we should keep the selected-effect account and swallow the awkward consequence that most of the island's beetles are walking around with dysfunctional horns.
