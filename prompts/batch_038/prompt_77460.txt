The question, worth 25 marks, was this: *In the chapter of* Ecce Homo *titled "Why I Am So Clever," Nietzsche devotes several pages to nutrition, climate, and recreation. Explain what philosophical work these apparently trivial matters are doing, and say whether Nietzsche should be read as proposing a programme for health. You should draw on at least one other text from 1886–1889.* Because it was closed book, paraphrase rather than quotation is expected and imprecise wording should not be penalized; the answer should be marked on what the student understands, not on what the student can recall verbatim. Here is the script, which I have numbered by paragraph so that comments can be keyed to it:

[1] Nietzsche's remarks on nutrition, climate, and recreation come in the first chapter of *Ecce Homo*, "Why I Am So Clever," and at first sight they look like digression: an invalid listing his complaints and his remedies. In fact they are the clearest statement of a method. The traditional questions of philosophy — God, the soul, the moral law — are too large to be answered at all; the questions that actually decide what a human being is are the small ones, what one eats, where one lives, how one spends one's leisure. A culture's values grow out of its metabolism rather than the other way round, so anyone who wants to understand morality has to begin where morality is manufactured.

[2] The chapter is also Nietzsche's most explicit statement of his perspectivism about health. He refuses to prescribe a diet that would suit everyone, insisting that what nourishes one type may poison another, and that each of us has to experiment until we find the regimen under which our own kind of thinking flourishes. His own conclusions — small meals, nothing heavy, no alcohol, coffee given up — are offered as findings about himself rather than as rules for his readers.

[3] This connects to the *Genealogy*. In the third essay the ascetic ideal is what happens when a declining physiology is given a moral interpretation: the priest teaches the sick that their condition is their own fault, a punishment for the body and its appetites, and so turns suffering against life itself. Nietzsche's alternative is not a new set of prohibitions but the absence of prohibition. His own practice is a permission to eat, drink, and rest as his work requires. Discipline in the ascetic sense is what he is attacking, and the physiological passages show what replaces it: an experimental relation to one's own body in which nothing is forbidden in principle.

[4] What makes this a programme for health rather than mere hygiene is the idea of "great health" in the preface to *The Gay Science*. Great health is not the absence of illness but a robust vitality that the higher type can maintain through every kind of strain; the philosopher of the future is healthy in the way a strong constitution is healthy. Nietzsche's dietary advice is the practical side of that concept — how one builds and keeps such a constitution, so that one can carry the thought of the eternal recurrence without being crushed by it.

[5] So the answer to the second half of the question is yes, with one qualification. Nietzsche does propose a programme for health, but it is a programme for a few. It is frankly elitist, it is addressed to those whose existence matters to culture, and it is therapeutic in aim: the point of getting one's nutrition right is that a well-ordered body is the precondition of any great achievement, and that most philosophical error is the symptom of a badly managed one.

[6] The qualification is that the programme is not a philosophical argument and should not be read as one. Much of the specific advice is simply wrong by modern standards — his hostility to alcohol and coffee is stricter than any nutritionist would now defend, and his confidence that a change of diet improves one's thinking is asserted rather than argued. What survives is an interesting but unsupported claim that values have a bodily origin, and since the only evidence offered is Nietzsche's own case, the chapter is best read as autobiography; the fact that the book was completed shortly before his collapse in Turin gives one further pause about taking its medical claims seriously. Like the chapter titles themselves, this one is largely a joke at his own expense, and it is a mistake to treat it as doctrine.
