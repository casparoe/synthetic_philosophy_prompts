A friend of mine who works in behavioral economics and I spent most of last night arguing about whether Lewis's account of convention survives contact with the evolutionary game-theoretic program, and we got nowhere. Her position is that reinforcement, imitation, and drift in a population of learners does all the explanatory work, and that Lewis's apparatus of conditional preferences and mutual expectations is a description of where such processes happen to settle rather than an account of anything the agents themselves do. My position is that she is right about the mechanism and wrong about what it licenses, since the dynamical story cannot tell us why a convention gives anyone a reason to conform. She said my package was unstable; when I asked her to name the pair of claims that actually clashed, she said something about my wanting it both ways and we went in circles until two in the morning. I would rather have the thing audited than re-litigated with her.

Here is what I hold, as precisely as I can state it:

1. A convention is a regularity in behavior, in some recurrent situation, that solves a coordination problem, and it persists because each member of the population prefers to conform conditional on expecting the others to conform. I also accept Lewis's insistence that no agreement, deliberation, or design is required: conventions can arise by drift.

2. But I accept the Skyrmsian lesson that the agents need not represent any of this. Learning dynamics in a population of boundedly competent agents will produce and stabilize such regularities on their own, and the equilibrium structure described in claim 1 is at best a description of where those processes come to rest, not a mechanism the participants implement.

3. I reject the common-knowledge requirement. Actual participants hold partial, vague, and frequently mistaken beliefs about what others expect, and that is plainly enough for a convention to obtain.

4. A case verdict I am confident of: at three in the morning on a stretch of road I can see is empty in both directions, I still have a reason to keep to the right — not a prudential calculation about this instance, and not a reason available only to someone who has worked out that keeping right is an equilibrium.

5. Conventions are arbitrary in the strict sense: there is always some alternative regularity that would have served the population just as well, and nothing but the history of how we got here settles which alternative we in fact have.

6. The norms that matter most — property, promising, gendered divisions of labor — are conventions in this sense, rather than truths discovered by reason or fixed by the natural order.

7. A consequence I draw from claim 5: a convention cannot be unjust as such. Because the choice among alternatives is settled by history alone, the only legitimate ground for criticizing a convention is that it fails, or fails badly, to solve the coordination problem it addresses. The criticism is about efficiency, not fairness.

I want an audit rather than a survey. For each genuine tension, tell me which commitment has to give and what the revision would cost me — not merely that the two claims sit uneasily together — and rank the tensions by severity. Also identify the apparent tensions that dissolve on inspection, and say why they dissolve, because I expect at least one pair here to look opposed without being so. Two further questions: if I were permitted exactly one revision, which single change would repair the most damage; and is there any version of this package in which the Lewisian core of claim 1 survives claim 2 intact, or does adopting the dynamical account force me to give up the idea that conventions are about preferences and expectations altogether?
