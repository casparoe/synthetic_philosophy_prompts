Ok so this is a weird thing to ask but it's been eating at me for about two weeks. I teach mindfulness-based stress reduction — small group classes, mostly people dealing with chronic pain — and basically my whole job is: notice what's actually happening in your experience, then find words for it. My brother is a systems neuroscientist and over dinner he told me that on his view the feeling doesn't do anything, that my clients' reports get produced entirely by their brains while the experience just tags along, and that when I ask someone to describe what they're feeling I'm asking a question whose answer was already settled before the feeling could matter. He called it the paradox of phenomenal judgment. I couldn't answer him and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it, partly because if he's right then what I do for a living is... idk, a kind of comforting fraud.

I want to figure out where I actually stand, and I don't want to end up with whatever position sounds most reassuring to me. So: play the role of a philosopher of mind who has genuinely worked through this debate rather than someone summarizing a textbook, and build me a decision tree that starts from commitments I already have and ends at a named position on the relation between experience and the judgments/reports we make about experience.

Specifics:

1. Before the tree, spend ~150 words brainstorming candidate branch questions — the claims that could possibly drive these views apart — then say which ones you're cutting and why (redundant, or fails to discriminate).

2. The tree: 8 to 10 nodes. Every node has to be phrased as a question I can answer without knowing where it leads, so no node may contain a position's name or jargon. Concrete little cases strongly preferred over abstract "do you believe X" questions, and at least half the cases should be about reporting and judgment specifically, not the usual zombie/Mary/bat furniture. The kind of probes I mean: a chip generates all of my verbal reports while the experience stays intact; a setup where the experience and the report share a common neural cause but neither causes the other; somebody trained for years to report experiences they don't have who never notices anything wrong. Invent your own versions and make them sharp.

3. Leaves must include at minimum: bite-the-bullet epiphenomenalism (accept that judgments about experience aren't appropriately hooked up to it); interactionist dualism; the move where phenomenal concepts or direct acquaintance is supposed to secure aboutness without any causal role for the experience; illusionism / deflation of the whole problem; the collapse into identity or plain physicalism; and the Russellian-monism flavored option where experience is the intrinsic nature of the physical stuff. Add whatever else genuinely belongs in the space. Each leaf gets its name — or "unnamed, nobody defends this" if that's the truth — plus one sentence stating its single most awkward commitment, as bluntly as you can manage.

4. Mark the pairs of leaves your tree can't really seperate, and say what further question would tell them apart if one exists.

5. Then give me the map of the whole logical space: which combinations of answers are coherent but unoccupied, and for each whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's merely unattractive, or because nobody has bothered. My hunch is there's an underdeveloped cell where experience is fully causally inert AND aboutness doesn't require causation at all — if you think that's just the acquaintance leaf in disguise, or incoherent, say so.

No reading list, no citations, no philosophers' names unless the name literally is the label on a leaf. I can't look anything up and I don't want to be handed claims I'd have to take on faith.

Audience is me and maybe two friends: smart, zero philosophy training, so plain language with a one-line gloss on any term you can't avoid.

Last thing: tell me how confident you are in the tree as a whole, and single out the one node or leaf you're least sure about — the joint where a real specialist would say I've cut in the wrong place. Don't hedge everything evenly, just point at the weakest part.
