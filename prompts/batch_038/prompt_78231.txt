I have been recording the verdicts my dissertation supervisor issues on the fixed set of vignettes she uses in her seminar on the badness of pain. She presents them as verdicts on a single question — how bad a state is for the subject who is in it — and not on wrongness, permission, or the value of a life taken as a whole, though I have my doubts about two of the items below and would like you to test that framing rather than assume it.

Her verdicts so far:

1. Classic pain asymbolia. A patient with an insular lesion localizes and grades a noxious stimulus accurately, calls it a pain, and says plainly that it does not bother him. He does not withdraw, guards nothing, sits through a second and third application, and sometimes asks for another. Verdict: the pain is genuinely felt, but it is not bad for him, and nothing objectionable is done by eliciting it.

2. A second asymbolic patient who says the same thing — feels it, unbothered — but who flinches, guards the limb, abandons the task, and refuses the repeat. Verdict: here something is bad for him, and his own denial does not settle the question.

3. A child with congenital insensitivity to pain and anhidrosis: no pain sensation whatever; she chews through her tongue, burns her hands, destroys her weight-bearing joints, and will not live long. Verdict: her condition is bad for her, and gravely so; the absent pain is no gift.

4. A patient years after anterior cingulotomy for intractable pain. The pain is still there, she says, still burning, still in the same place — but it no longer matters. She has returned to work, sleeps through the night, and refuses opioids. Verdict: the operation removed the badness and left the sensation intact; that is a real success, not a self-deception.

5. A prisoner under torture who reports the pain as excruciating and wants it to stop above everything, but is trained and disciplined: no expression, no pleading, no compliance. Verdict: obviously and gravely bad for him; the composure subtracts nothing.

6. A subject under light anaesthesia who has no memory of the procedure and denies on waking that anything happened to him, though perioperative records show a sustained stress response and guarding throughout. Verdict: bad for him at the time, notwithstanding that he cannot recall it and would now deny it.

7. A masochist in a consensual scene who describes nociceptive input of the same intensity and quality as something he wants and enjoys. Verdict: not bad for him while it lasts — and she is emphatic that this is not simply because he consented, but because it feels good to him.

8. A patient who, months after a cingulotomy, is distressed that nothing bothers him any longer: he watches himself fail to protect a scalded hand and finds his own indifference frightening. Verdict: the pain is still not bad for him; the distress about the indifference is.

The new vignette, which I wrote and which she has not yet seen, runs in the opposite direction from asymbolia. A patient with intact nociception reports her pain vividly and as awful — it is horrible, she wants it to stop — and this self-report has been stable over eighteen months of testing. Yet she has never once sought relief, declines the analgesia offered her, shows no guarding, no avoidance, and no stress response; she interrupts nothing and postpones nothing; and when offered payment to submit to a further round she accepts cheerfully, saying that she wants it to stop but that she would take the money. She is not stoical, not deceptive, and not confused about what she is being asked. Is the pain bad for her, and how bad?

I would like four things.

(a) Your prediction of her verdict, with reasons, and a note on how confident you are.

(b) The rule she appears to be running, stated precisely enough that it generates all eight verdicts; then at least two further rules that generate all eight equally well, where by "further" I mean genuinely different accounts of what makes a state bad for its subject — badness as a matter of felt valence; as the state's representing bodily damage as bad; as the subject's being moved to stop it; as the frustration of a desire, actual or informed or ideal; as some two-component combination; as an objective-list entry for bodily integrity; or something you can construct that I have not listed. For each candidate rule, say what it delivers on my new vignette, and flag any rule that turns out to be internally ambiguous on that case — in particular, any view on which the answer depends on whether the relevant mental state is to be identified by what the subject says or by what the subject does.

(c) Which single item among the eight is doing the most discriminating work: which one, if struck from the record, would leave the candidate rules most underdetermined. And how the leading candidates handle items 3 and 6, which look to me like the two most likely to be smuggling in a different question — badness at a time, badness of a life, or the wrongness of causing the state. If you think any verdict in the list only coheres with the others once it is re-described that way, say which and show the re-description.

(d) I also drafted a mirror case to put to her: a patient who reports no felt unpleasantness at all and sincerely denies being bothered, yet cannot keep her hand on the plate, withdraws it repeatedly, loses the thread of her work, and cannot say why she does so. Note the difference from item 2 — in my version the hand has fully healed and the stimulus is harmless, so there is no tissue damage and nothing for bodily integrity to get hold of. Would this second case discriminate among the rules you list, or would they converge on it? Where they converge, tell me what further case would separate them, and what she would have to say about it in order to remain consistent with items 1, 2, and 8 taken together.

Keep this analytic and self-contained. I want the reasoning, not the references: no bibliography, no reading list, and name a position in the literature only where you are confident you have characterized it correctly.
