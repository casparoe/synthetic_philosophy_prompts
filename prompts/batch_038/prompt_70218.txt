I am a junior at a public high school of roughly 1,900 students, and I serve as one of the forty elected students on our Student-Faculty Senate, where six faculty members also sit without a vote. The Senate controls a student activities fund of about eleven thousand dollars a year, raised through ticket sales and one spring fundraiser, and it writes its own procedural rules; its decisions bind every student in the school, including students who never voted for anyone and who may not have known the question was on the agenda. In November the Senate considered a motion to spend $2,400 from that fund to cover registration and bus fare so that twelve students could attend a regional ethics invitational in another state. Debate ran close to two hours. Several senators argued about cost per participant, about whether a smaller number of larger grants would serve more clubs, and about what the school's stated purpose of "broadening student horizons" was supposed to commit us to. The motion failed, 20–19, and the vote that defeated it belonged to a senator who said plainly that her objection was religious: she explained that her tradition obliges her to weigh how a community treats the stranger within its own gates before it spends money sending some of its members elsewhere, that she did not think the invitational served that obligation, and that she could not vote for the motion on any other basis. She was not being evasive or dramatic. She said exactly what she believed and then voted.

Within a week, eight senators introduced what they called the Public Justification Standard, which would add a clause to our rules requiring that each senator, before casting a vote on any motion that binds the student body, be prepared to state grounds for that vote that other senators could assess without first accepting the senator's own religious or comprehensive view of what makes a life worthwhile. The Senate has been deadlocked over the clause since October, and the two sides have been arguing past each other in a way that I recognize, uncomfortably, from the independent study I have been doing with my government teacher on the idea of public reason and on the objections raised against it. I want to stress that the fight in our Senate is not a tidy version of anything I have read; it is messier, and some of the details matter. So that you are working from our record rather than from a textbook, here is what each side has actually said.

**Camp A, the eight sponsors of the clause, hold the following.** Decisions that bind people who disagree require reasons that could in principle be shared with those people, and not merely explanations of why the decider feels as she does; a senator who offers only a premise her colleagues cannot examine has, on her own account, stopped giving reasons. The clause is meant as a norm of conduct for senators and not as a rule of admissibility: it would not authorize the chair to strike remarks, to bar any statement of conviction, or to invalidate any vote, and Camp A insists that religious reasons may be voiced freely in debate provided the senator can also state grounds that do not depend on them. Accessible grounds, as Camp A uses the term, are things like evidence about costs and effects on students, the school's publicly stated purposes, and claims of fair treatment between clubs — claims a senator could weigh, dispute, or accept without joining anyone's religion. Camp A also stresses two limits on its own view: the clause would apply equally to non-religious worldviews, including the act-utilitarian reasoning a senator from my class announces he runs everything through, and Camp A denies that its standard is itself a comprehensive doctrine, describing it instead as the terms on which any of us, whatever else we believe, can address one another when we are about to exercise power over each other.

**Camp B, which opposes the clause, holds the following.** Making a shareable reason a condition of a member's full participation effectively tells students whose convictions are religious that those convictions are second-class, and the distinction between accessible and inaccessible reasons is not a neutral instrument for drawing that line but is already the disputed question, since it assumes that only some kinds of grounds count as public. Camp B argues that the supposedly neutral currency is not neutral: an aggregative utilitarian calculus, or an appeal to individual rights with no stated source, is no more inspectable from inside another worldview than an appeal to scripture, and Camp B points out that the school's stated purpose of broadening horizons is itself contested, so Camp A's supply of accessible grounds may quietly consist of whatever the majority already finds comfortable. Camp B worries most about insincerity — that a norm demanding translation will train students to manufacture presentable grounds that are not actually why they voted, which makes the Senate less informed about its own membership rather than more — and about enforcement, since a norm with no official sanction is enforced socially, and Camp B predicts that censure will fall hardest on whichever students are already fewest in number. Camp B also argues from history, in our building and elsewhere: deep reasons that are excluded from the floor do not disappear, they move into caucuses and group chats where nobody is in a position to answer them. Finally, Camp B holds that the Senate is not the whole of student life and that the clause's logic would spill over into clubs and voluntary associations, which should be free to argue however they please.

The faculty adviser, who is facilitating, has proposed an adversarial collaboration between the two camps, with a jointly signed statement due to the Senate before the spring meeting on March 14, and both sides have agreed to it, though neither has read the other's writing charitably in months. I would like you to draft that statement, in American English, as the document both camps could put their names to. Please structure it as follows. First, a numbered list of claims both camps can sign — I expect there are more of these than either side admits, including at least one claim about what senators owe to students who are bound by decisions they did not vote on, and at least one about what the Senate is not entitled to do. Second, a table of the surviving disagreements: each row should carry a label, Camp A's formulation of its side written so that Camp A would accept it verbatim, Camp B's formulation written so that Camp B would accept it verbatim, and a final column naming what would have to be settled to close the row. Third, a section of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one of them would actually move at least one camp — and for each crux, a concrete test drawn from our own situation or a specified one, the evidence both camps would agree counts, and each camp's prediction recorded in advance. Here are the cases I want you to run through, since several of them split the camps differently than their slogans suggest: an identical funding motion carried by a senator who offers only a calculation of aggregate student welfare and openly discounts a rival club's disappointment as less intense; a resolution on dress at school events in which a senator invokes an account of modesty drawn from her tradition and also offers an argument about costs borne by students who cannot afford the clothing in question, believing both sincerely and regarding the second as sufficient on its own; a senator who states a reason he knows will be received as reasonable but that is not in fact his reason; a Senate three years after adoption in which our most religiously observant students hold no leadership offices and report in an anonymous survey that the Senate is a place where they must leave their convictions at the door; and a motion supported only by premises drawn from a moral framework that half the Senate rejects as question-begging but that is not religious. Say also what the clause would even mean as applied to the drafting tool our Senate has started using, which writes motion preambbles and debate summaries that no senator fully endorses. If you conclude that part of this dispute is verbal, identify which part and explain why, paying particular attention to the two camps' different readings of what makes a reason accessible and of whether the clause governs what a senator may say or what she must be able to say.

Camp A has asked that the statement concede, in terms, that the clause imposes costs on some members that it does not impose on others; Camp B has asked that it concede that a body which settles questions by unanswerable authority rather than by reasons treats its members as subordinates. End with a joint recommendation to the Senate — adopt the clause, adopt it as amended, or do not adopt it — together with one procedural step for the March 14 meeting, such as a trial period, a pilot applied only to spending motions, a review after a set number of votes, or a standing report on how the clause is working. If the two camps cannot in fact agree on the recommendation, write instead a short joint explanation of why not, and state for each camp what evidence or argument would move it. Please do not invent quotations, do not attribute to any real person or book a sentence you cannot stand behind, and keep any references to actual philosophers to bare names at most; I want the statement to argue from the record I have given you. Aim for between 1,100 and 1,500 words, and write it in American English.
