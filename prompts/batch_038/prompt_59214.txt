I work on the estimation of long-run existential risk, and before I adopt any account of anthropic or self-locating reasoning I want to know what would entitle me to adopt one. So please write the specification rather than an argument: the desiderata that any account of self-locating evidence must meet to count as adequate. By such an account I mean a principled rule for how an agent should reason from the fact that she is *this* observer, at *this* position in a sequence of observers, rather than another, to conclusions about the process that generated the sequence. The Doomsday Argument is what made me want the list, but the list must address the general problem, and it must be pitched at a level at which I could defend it in a report whose conclusions other people will act on: usable by someone who has to commit to a number and explain why that number is not an artefact of her own modelling choices.

For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it. Below are the phenomena I expect to see handled; include them if you agree they matter, drop them with a reason if you do not, and add whatever I have overlooked.

1. The canonical case. An agent who knows her approximate birth rank among all humans who will ever live, together with a prior over the total. Whether that rank licenses a shift in her posterior over the total, by how much, and what is doing the evidential work if it does. The account must explain why the shift is dramatic under some priors and negligible under others, rather than leaving the magnitude to be read off whatever prior the writer happened to choose.

2. The reference class. Which observers count as the others I might have been, why the answer cannot be read off intuitions about similarity, and what makes a choice of class principled as opposed to opportunistic — where "opportunistic" means: adjusted until the desired conclusion falls out.

3. Conservativeness. In every case carrying no indexical content, the account must reproduce ordinary conditionalisation; and when the agent already knows the total with certainty, the self-locating machinery must fall silent rather than manufacture residual doubt.

4. Predictive feedback, which I do not believe the standard treatments cover. An agency creates observers in batches under a published protocol: whenever an observer states a figure for the eventual total the programme will produce, the agency arranges for the total to equal that figure, creating further observers or halting as required. The agent therefore knows that the quantity she is estimating is a function of her estimate. The rank-based inference would suggest a total of the same order as her rank — but if she says so, it becomes true, and if she says something else, that becomes true instead. Say what an account owes here: whether the agent's evidence includes the fact that her own conclusion will be causally efficacious; whether the sampling assumption underlying the inference survives at all once the sample is fixed by the inference; and whether the account can avoid prescribing a credence that the protocol then renders false.

5. A self-selected reference class. Suppose the only thing the agent knows about her position is that she is among the earliest observers ever to have entertained this argument. Observers who run the inference are not a uniform sample from observers who exist, and by construction they cluster near the beginning of the sequence. What must an account say about when self-selection of this kind cancels the inference outright and when it merely rescales it?

6. Infinite or ill-defined totals. A cosmology on which infinitely many observers exist, so that ranks remain finite while totals do not, and the priors that drive the argument cannot be normalised. The requirement is not that the account solve the measure problem, but that it be explicit about what it does when it has no purchase on a case, rather than quietly assuming finitude in the background.

7. Presumption, and a distinction that must not be collapsed. Inflating one's prior weight on hypotheses that posit more observers must not by itself purchase arbitrarily strong evidence for such hypotheses. More generally, the account must distinguish the likelihood a hypothesis assigns to my observations from the typicality of my observations under that hypothesis: the two come apart, and only one of them is evidence.

Then do the sorting, which matters to me more than the list itself. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable — where failure is incoherence, or contradiction with evidence no party to the dispute questions — and which are the signature commitments of one camp dressed up as neutral standards. Where a subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, identify the minimal unsatisfiable subset and say which member of it is most defensible to abandon; if the list turns out to be a no-go result in disguise, that is the finding I most want to see stated plainly. Finally, name the single requirement that eliminates the greatest number of otherwise respectable accounts, and explain why it carries that much leverage.

Close with no more than a paragraph each on how the two broad families of position — the one that treats a larger observer count as raising prior probability, and the one that treats an agent's rank as a random sample from her reference class — fare against your list. I am not asking for references, a reading list, or a survey of the literature; I want the criteria and the reasoning behind them. Please write in British English.
