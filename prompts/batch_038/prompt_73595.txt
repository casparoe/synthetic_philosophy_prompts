A friend of mine holds that Molinism is not merely false but incoherent, and he gave me the following argument. I cannot find where it breaks.

(1) Every true contingent proposition has something that makes it true.
(2) Molinism is committed to the truth of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — for instance, that had Curley been offered the bribe, he would freely have taken it — and to God's knowing them.
(3) This knowledge is prevolitional: God knows such conditionals prior to any decree to create, since otherwise they would be items of free knowledge rather than middle knowledge.
(4) Prior to any decree, no creatures exist, no free act has been performed, and God has willed nothing concerning the world.
(5) So nothing available at that point could make such conditionals true.
(6) So by (1) they are not true, and middle knowledge has no content.

When I pressed him, I pointed out that (1) and (5) appear to sweep well past Molinism. If a conditional requires a truthmaker among things that actually exist or occur, then "had the match been struck, it would have lit" is in exactly the same boat, since the striking never happened — and my friend uses conditionals of that sort himself, as when he says God would have brought about a better world had he so chosen. He insists that conditionals about free creatures are different, but would not say how. That leaves me with a dilemma I cannot resolve: either something slips between (3) and (6), or the argument is valid and the Molinist must simply abandon (1) and explain what sort of brute truth a counterfactual of freedom is instead — which looks like a price Molinists cannot pay, since on their own account the conditional is supposed to be true in virtue of what Curley would do, and not the other way round.

Which of the two is it? Pick out the precise step that fails, or the premise worth surrendering, and say why it looked acceptable in the first place. Then state what the Molinist owes once that step is repaired: specifically, what makes a prevolitional conditional about a free act true rather than merely ungrounded, and what room is left for the distinction between the worlds God could create and the worlds he could actually bring about.
