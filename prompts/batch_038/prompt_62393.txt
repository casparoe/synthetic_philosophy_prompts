I supervise a small unit in a state government whose work is unglamorous: we go through the statute book looking for provisions that can be repealed safely. Most of our cases look exactly like Chesterton's fence as it is usually told — a rule nobody can explain, whose defenders are nevertheless confident it must be doing something, where the responsible move is to leave it standing and keep digging. But a minority of our cases look nothing like that, and they have led me to suspect that the fence and the broader Burkean case for prescription are doing two very different kinds of work, and that the habit of citing the first as a convenient summary of the second is actively misleading people who do this for a living. I would like a rigorous argument for a fairly specific thesis. Please take the role of a philosopher who works on the epistemology of institutions and is equally at home in Burke and in contemporary analytic work on conservatism, precaution, and the evidential value of inherited practices. Think carefully before you start writing, and don't pad the answer with a survey of the literature or a list of further reading — I want the argument, not the bibliography.

Here is the kind of case I mean; let's call it a legible fence. A practice persists, and its founding purpose is recoverable in full: the enabling document survives, the authors are identified, the mechanism is understood, and everyone concedes that the mechanism no longer operates. Concretely — our office reviewed a requirement dating from the 1930s that certain municipal bond issuances carry the countersignature of a state officer whose specific job had been to detect a particular forgery technique; the technique was defeated by changes in printing and recordkeeping decades ago, the function has no modern counterpart, and the countersignature now survives purely as a routing step that adds eleven days to an issuance. Nothing is hidden. The origin is not merely known but known to be spent. My claim is that in such a case the Chestertonian inference has no evidential purchase whatsoever: the fence's force comes entirely from the reformer's ignorance of why the thing was put there, and once that ignorance is gone there is nothing left for the fence to say — whereas the Burkean case against repeal can remain fully intact, because Burke's argument runs through the practice's survival history, its embeddedness in a scheme too intricate for any single mind to survey, and the reformer's overconfidence in his private stock of reason, none of which is disturbed by learning what the founders intended. The two come apart precisely where they are customarily treated as one and the same view.

So argue for this: that Chesterton's fence is not a Burkean argument but a rationalist argument with a conservative first step — its terminal instruction, on the ordinary reading, being that once you have seen the use you may clear the fence away — and that collapsing the two has a real cost for people in my line of work, because it directs inquiry toward origins when the evidence that actually bears on repeal concerns present function. Begin by reconstructing each argument as an explicit inference: what its premises are, what its conclusion licenses, and at which premise the two chains part company. Then make the case, and afterward press the strongest objection you can construct against it — the most obvious being that seeing the use just is seeing everything the practice now does, latent and acquired functions included, so a fully documented origin cannot exhaust the inquiry and the fence still has work to do even in a legible case; and the less obvious being that Burke himself sometimes argues from what institutions were originally established to do, which would blur the very boundary the thesis depends on. Engage those objections rather than merely acknowledging them, and tell me whether the thesis survives them intact, or whether it must be narrowed, and if so to what exact form. I would much rather have a careful argument with its weak points marked than a tidy one that quietly assumes the reading of Chesterton it needs in order to succeed.
