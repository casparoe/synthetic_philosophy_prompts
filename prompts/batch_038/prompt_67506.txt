I run a residential program, 30 beds, for people in their late teens and early twenties coming out of the justice system, and every week my staff and I have to decide what to do about someone who's broken the house rules or done something to another resident. Right now we do it by vibes and precedent and its inconsistent. Honestly I also think some of what we do is punishment wearing a different outfit — we call them "consequences" and "repair tasks," but a three-day loss of phone privileges is not a mystery to anybody who gets one. The stakes cut both ways: go too hard and someone walks out of the program and back onto the street, go too soft and the other residents stop feeling safe in the house and stop trusting us. Everything we decide needs a one-paragraph written rationale the resident can read, and it all gets reviewed quarterly by our funder's board.

I want a written decision procedure we can run on a case in about twenty-five minutes, with three people, one of whom has no training at all and will be reading straight off the page. Ground it in the actual disagreement between people who think backward-looking desert is legitimate — the Dennett-ish line that holding people responsible is a practice that does work and earns its keep — and people who think desert in that sense is a myth, and that the only thing you're ever entitled to do is restrict someone as much, and only as much, as it takes to protect others and help them change, roughly the quarantine-style view Caruso and Pereboom defend. Don't pick a side and hide the choice from me. I want the procedure to mark where the two views tell us the same thing so we can stop relitigating it every week, and where they genuinely split — and at each split step, say which one we're following and what that costs us.

It should have: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, a hard cap on how restrictive we can get and what the cap is keyed to, which steps look like rules but are really judgment calls in disguise, and the points where it has to stop and hand the decision to a person because it's about values and not facts. Also tell me what to do when we can't establish what happened — the resident denies it, nobody saw it, there's no proof either way.

Then run it on three cases: a resident who's taken other residents' property four times in six weeks; someone who hurt a staff member badly eighteen months ago and hasn't done a thing since, but our risk instrument still rates him moderate and he wants his work-release back; and a resident who hasn't broken any rule at all but whom two other residents are afraid of, and staff want him moved to the more supervised unit before something happens. I expect that third one comes out in a way I won't like. Fine — but say so plainly up front instead of burying it in step six.

Last thing: what is this procedure bad at, and which kinds of case should we refuse to put through it at all?
