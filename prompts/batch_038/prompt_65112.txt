I write novels; my third came out last spring and has now been translated into four languages, which has left me unexpectedly deep in arguments about what translation is. One of these arguments is with a friend who works on machine translation, and it has gone wrong in a way I cannot repair on my own. I would like a diagnosis.

His opening claim was the familiar one, delivered in his professional rather than his polemical register: a system trained on parallel corpora resolves an ambiguous pronoun by statistics, not by knowledge. His probe was the standard minimal pair — a trophy that will not go into a suitcase because it is too big, and the same sentence with small in place of big, where the pronoun flips to the other noun. A system that has never been anywhere near a trophy or a suitcase can get both right, he said, purely because the pattern is in the data; that is what he means by shallow. He added what he took to be a deeper, metaphysical point: nothing in the training data fixes which content the system has attached to the source sentences, since indefinitely many assignments of meaning are compatible with the same distribution over outputs.

I tried to turn that second point back on him and produced the following, which now strikes me as proving far too much.

(1) A system understands a text only if, for each of its sentences, what settles the system's interpretation is the sentence's content — and not some pattern that merely coincides with that content across everything the system has so far encountered.
(2) In a statistical translation system, no content does the settling. Given the corpus, indefinitely many different assignments of meaning to the source strings are compatible with exactly the same output distribution, and the system cannot distinguish among them; so there is no fact of the matter about which meaning it has.
(3) Therefore such a system understands nothing.
(4) But my own case is no better. What my interpretation of a sentence consists in is, at best, my disposition to interpret further sentences: to answer questions about them, to carry the story on, to notice when it has gone wrong. Those dispositions, however generously I idealize them, are finite, and indefinitely many assignments of meaning are compatible with all of them — assignments that agree on everything I have ever read and diverge on some sentence I have not.
(5) So, by (1) and (2) applied to me, there is no fact of the matter about which content settles my readings either, and I understand nothing: not my friend's argument, not the novel I have just finished, not my own name.
(6) But I do understand those things — or at any rate the argument cannot be permitted to show that I do not, since if it does, nothing it says about machines is worth having.
(7) Therefore (1) or (2) is false; and whichever goes, the shallowness thesis has not been established by this route.

If I am forced to choose, I would rather keep (6) than (1). I am also attached to (2), though only because I suspect it is doing the real work and I should like to know what work that is.

So: tell me which step to reject. Say whether you take the argument to be invalid — some step claiming more than the preceding steps have earned — or valid with an unaffordable premise; say why the offending step looked perfectly respectable when I wrote it down; and price the loss, including whether anything of my friend's original point survives it. If the argument can be repaired without the reflexivity at (5), show me the repair. Where you are speculating rather than reporting, mark it as speculation.

Two housekeeping requests, which matter because I intend to write about this and do not want to be caught out. First, be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are settled and which remain live disagreements among people who work in the area — and note that philosophers of language and translation researchers may not mean the same thing by understand, so if that word is doing different work in (1) and in (3), say so plainly. Do not present a contested position as the consensus of either community. Second, begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning: no preamble, no restatement of my question, no throat-clearing about the difficulty of the issues.
