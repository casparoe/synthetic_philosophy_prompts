I am working through the second of Nietzsche's Untimely Meditations, the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I have stalled in the sections where he introduces monumental history. Rather than mangle the wording from memory, here is the stretch of argument as closely as I can restate it: he presents monumental history as the history of the great moments of humanity, bound into something like a continuity across the ages, and says that what the man of action draws from it is the lesson that a great thing which once existed was at any rate once possible, and therefore may well be possible again; he then adds that this kind of history proceeds by analogy, treating the past in terms of effects rather than causes and glossing over the conditions that made the single case what it was, so that anyone expecting rigorous causal knowledge from it would be disappointed and would find something closer to fiction than to science.

Three questions about this stretch, in order.

First, what is the logical force of the inference he attributes to monumental history? That a great thing existed seems to entail trivially that it was possible, so the substantive step must be the move to renewed possibility — and that step looks to require exactly the suppression of differences and causes he goes on to criticize. Is he claiming that monumental history depends on an unsound inference that action nonetheless needs, or that it rests on something true but dangerously incomplete? Say which reading the point about analogy favors, and why.

Second, when he says this history deals in effects rather than causes, is that a description of what monumental history hands its consumer — a great deed presented as though severed from its conditions — or a claim about what monumental history thinks it is explaining? The two yield quite different diagnoses of the epigone problem, where the less gifted invoke the monuments to discourage new greatness, and I cannot tell which he is running.

Third, later in the essay he insists that we are the continuation of earlier generations, heirs of their errors and passions as much as of their achievements, and that the critical historian who tries to root himself free of that inheritance typically ends up with an origin he has fabricated. If that is right, monumental history's isolation of the great individual from lineage and conditions looks like something the essay's own account rules out. Is this a genuine inconsistency between the sections, or is monumental history permitted to isolate the deed precisely because it is addressed to the agent rather than to the knower?

Please reason from the passage as I have stated it and from your own sense of the essay's structure; I am not after references or a survey of the debate about it, just the analysis.
