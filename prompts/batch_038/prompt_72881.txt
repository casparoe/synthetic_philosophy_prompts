I live in a cohousing community outside Asheville, about 40 households, and we have this woman — Barb, she's maybe 72, retired court reporter, never took a mediation class in her life — who has quietly become the person everybody goes to when two people want incompatible things and both feel strongly about it. She doesn't use any fancy words for it, but basically she brokers swaps: you give ground on the thing you care about, I give ground on the thing I care about, and we both come out able to live here. I've started keeping notes on which deals she'll help put together and which ones she flat refuses, partly because I read something once about "moral trade," the idea that people who disagree about what's right can still make fair exchanges with each other, and partly because she genuinely baffles me. Here's the track record so far, all real:

- A member who thinks gambling is a sin had blocked our annual raffle for three years running. The raffle pays for the accessible van. Barb got them to a deal: proceeds still go to the van, the raffle moves off the common lawn to the fairgrounds, and he gets a paragraph in the newsletter explaining why he thinks it's wrong. She called that one "the easy kind."
- A different member offered $15,000 to the buildings fund if we'd unbook the drag queen story hour we had on the fair schedule. She wouldn't even carry the offer to a vote.
- Two households, one wants the sunny south plot for a big vegetable garden, the other can't sleep past 6. Deal: gardener gets the south plot, no power tools before nine, and he does her walk and steps all winter. Approved, no fuss.
- One member offered another member $500 cash to flip his vote against the bylaw requiring a ramp into the common house. The guy taking the money said he honestly didn't care either way. Refused. All she'd say afterwards was "he was selling something that wasn't his to sell."
- Our beekeeper and the neighbor with a genuinely bad phobia. Hives move off the property line, gentler stock, and the neighbor drops her demand for a total ban and pays half the moving cost. Approved.
- An outside solar outfit offered us $40,000 a year for the upper field. The glare and the access road would mostly hit two households and the sledding hill, and nobody had asked either of them. Refused.
- Two guys who'd been feuding since 2019 over a property line. One apologizes in front of everybody at the fall potluck, the other drops his complaint to the board. Approved. She said afterward, "that one's the opposite of a purchase."
- A member offered to cover another member's property tax bill if he'd just stay home instead of testifying at the county hearing about his unpermitted addition. Refused.

Here's the one she has to answer by the April meeting, and it's the reason I'm writing. Dale is 84, one of the original founders, and before his wife died he promised her the orchard would never be cut down. It's written into our founding documents in a vague way that everyone has always treated as binding. We're short on parking and the parking crowd wants the east half of the orchard. Their offer: the community pays for full-time in-home care for Dale for the rest of his life, which is real money, way more than that land is worth, and in exchange he releases the promise and lets them fell the east half. On top of that they want him to stand up at the meeting and say out loud that the promise was a private sentimental thing between him and his wife and was never meant to bind the community, so the bylaw can be amended cleanly. Everyone who stands to lose is at the table and willing — Dale wants the care, his daughter is the one who brought it to Barb, the board is on board, the households who'd get the parking are thrilled. Nobody outside the community gets charged for anything and nobody who isn't a member is affected. Barb has three weeks to say whether she'll help draft it.

So: which way does she go? What I actually want is for you to work out what rule she's running, because I can't. Lay out the candidate rules that fit all eight of those verdicts equally well — I suspect there's more than one, and I suspect at least one obvious guess about money is already ruled out by the list — then say what each of those candidates predicts about Dale, then give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it. And if her record genuinely doesn't settle Dale, say so plainly and tell me what principle she'd be committing herself to either way. Finally, describe one more case I could put to her, one she hasn't seen, that would tell the candidate rules apart, and what each rule would say about it — I'd rather test her than argue with her. Please don't send me to a bibliography or a reading list, I have no idea what the literature here even is and I couldn't get at it anyway.
