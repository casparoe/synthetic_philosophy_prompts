Construct a decision problem that drives a wedge between two things Kelly betting is usually credited with securing at once: the highest long-run growth rate of wealth, and the best chance of getting where you actually need to be by the time you need to be there.

Set-up constraints. The only gamble on offer is a repeated fair-coin bet: each round the agent stakes a fraction *f* of her current wealth, and heads multiplies her wealth by (1 + 2*f*), tails by (1 − *f*). Rounds are independent; she may not borrow or lever. She is choosing between two fixed fractions — full Kelly, one quarter, and a deliberately timid one twentieth. Embed the choice in a realistic finite-life situation involving a payment she cannot miss, not a casino, in a single paragraph. You select the number of rounds and the wealth target so that both of the following hold: the timid bettor has the strictly higher probability of finishing at or above the target, and the Kelly bettor has the strictly higher expected log growth per round. Do not invoke ergodicity, or time-averages versus ensemble-averages, as a premise anywhere in the construction; the divergence must fall out of arithmetic a reader can check by hand.

Deliverables:

1. The scenario.
2. The verification: the Kelly fraction for these odds, each strategy's per-round growth rate, and the exact probability that each clears the target, with the binomial counts displayed rather than merely asserted.
3. The boundaries: over what range of targets and horizons your ranking survives, and where it flips — including explicitly whether the timid bettor still wins when the horizon is tripled, and whether she still wins if the horizon is cut to three rounds.
4. The diagnosis: which single feature of the case makes the two criteria come apart, and what becomes of the case if that feature is deleted.
5. The defense: how a partisan of geometric rationality argues this is no counterexample to her view — what she says about the target, the deadline, or the objective itself — and whether that reply concedes that the growth-rate criterion answers a question this agent never asked.
