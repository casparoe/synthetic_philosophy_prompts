I am a novelist, and I have been working for six years on a book whose action lies almost entirely in Cambridge between 1882 and 1900. One of my two principals is a moral philosopher who gave up a fellowship at his college in 1869 because he could no longer in good conscience declare himself a member of the Church of England, and who then spent the remainder of his life presiding over a learned society devoted to establishing, by statistical means, whether the dead survive — and who found nothing. He is modelled closely on Henry Sidgwick, first president of the Society for Psychical Research, and I want to use a document of that society's own devising as the epigraph to the book's third part. I would like you to write that document as a forgery good enough that I could set it in the front matter without an apology: a printed committee report, with running heads, numbered rules, a footnote apparatus, and an appended Schedule of Questions, running to something between 900 and 1,100 words. It should look like an extract from the society's Proceedings in the mid-1890s — that is, after the publication of *Phantasms of the Living* in 1886 (Gurney, Myers and Podmore, two volumes of individual cases of apparitions coinciding with a death) and at the moment when the society abandoned case-collection in favour of the census that asked some seventeen thousand people whether they had ever, while believing themselves fully awake, had a vivid impression of seeing or being touched by a human being who was not in fact present, and received 1,684 affirmative replies. Myers's word *telepathy* should appear as a term of art; the society's own founding profession that it would approach these questions "without prejudice or prepossession of any kind" should be quoted, by the chairman, at least twice, and the second time should be doing something different from the first.

The engine of the piece is that the instrument is honest and the instrument cannot be surprised. I want the report to open with a visible piece of arithmetic — set it out as the committee would, in the idiom of the doctrine of chances rather than in any twentieth-century statistical vocabulary — comparing the rate of such impressions against the ordinary death rate in order to test whether they cluster about a death more often than chance permits; the arithmetic must actually work. Then I want the Schedule of Questions to escalate, item by item, through safeguards every one of which a competent investigator of the 1890s could genuinely have defended: a second witness who was not in the house at the time; the exclusion of any percipient who had read a book on the subject, or who was in mourning, or fatigued, or feverish, or had previously known the agent; the exclusion of any impression whose agent did not die within a stated interval; the exclusion of the impression of any percipient who subsequently became convinced by it. The rules must be individually reasonable and collectively annihilating, so that the census is a total methodological triumph whose eligible sample is nobody. Since the society also did the opposite work — sending its own investigators into séance rooms and demonstrating how the effects were produced, as in the 1887 experiment in which trained witnesses mis-described a conjuror's performance — include a minute recording that the committee has, for the sake of rigour, reproduced every phenomenon submitted to it, and has therefore been unable to count any of them. Somewhere in the footnotes the chairman must disclose that he has himself been the percipient in a single case, that he has entered it in the register, and that it is excluded under one of the rules he drafted. Finally, I want a Swiftian proposal delivered in exactly the same unimpassioned register: the committee observes that what the enquiry requires is not marvels but coincidences, that coincidences may be manufactured, and that the society should accordingly raise a subscription to secure a supply of decessus at stated hours among persons previously unacquainted with the percipients, distributed across the parishes so as to lift the coincidence rate to significance. It must consider, and set aside, the objection that a death may be brought forward by the withdrawal of a nurse — and the ground on which it sets that objection aside must be administrative, never moral. That displacement is the point; do not explain it.

Two constraints of tone, which matter more to me than the jokes. First, this must be satire from inside the position, and internally consistent throughout: the chairman believes what he says, the method is sound, the null result follows honestly from the rules, and nothing in the document is fraudulent or incompetent. The target is not Sidgwick, and not Victorian credulity or Victorian scepticism; the target is evidential asceticism as a machine for never being startled, and the reader should end by being unable to decide whether the committee is heroic or absurd. No winking, no modern idiom, no raised eyebrow in the prose. Second, please do not invent additional statistics and present them as historical, do not put words in quotation marks as though quoted from Sidgwick, Myers, Gurney or anyone else, and do not have the document prophesy its own aftermath — the sixth edition of *The Methods of Ethics*, issued after his death in 1900 with emendations written in the last months, is mine to allude to in the novel, not yours here. What I want a reader with some history of the subject to recognise is that the author understands exactly what the census was for and exactly what it cost the man who commissioned it.
