An independent-study group at my school has been reading Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre, and I cannot pin down what kind of objection it actually is. It is plainly not the familiar one — that plays are dangerous because they set vice before us and we imitate what we watch — since Rousseau's view seems closer to the claim that theater mirrors and intensifies the morals a city already has than to the claim that it reshapes them. If he is right about that, the harm has to be located somewhere else, and my suspicion is that it lies in what theater displaces: hours spent in a darkened hall being moved by imaginary misfortunes one does nothing about, in place of the assemblies and open-air festivals where citizens act, and are seen acting, alongside one another. Could you set out the conceptual structure of that kind of objection? Specifically, how does an argument from displacement — that an institution is bad because of what it crowds out — differ from an argument from corruption of character, and why is the displacement version supposed to be untouched by the standard reply that audiences are not in fact made worse by what they watch? I would also like to know where his complaint about Molière's Misanthrope belongs: that Alceste is made ridiculous while the worldly Philinte is made agreeable. Is that a corruption argument, a displacement argument, or a third thing entirely? Please work through the distinction itself rather than summarizing the letter.
