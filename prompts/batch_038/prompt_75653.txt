Been reading the rationalist blogosphere for like fifteen years now (LW, the old Overcoming Bias days, SSC, and the forecasting threads that keep spinning off them) and the person I've argued with longest is a friend I'll call V. V writes these "forecast autopsy" posts where she takes some famous long-range prediction, rules it dead, alive, or "right but early," and then gets flamed in the comments for a week. She's easily the most consistent reasoner I know, but she never states her criteria, and I want to reverse-engineer them so I can argue with her properly instead of just vibes-back.

Her rulings so far, with the one-liner she gave each time:

- Malthus: population went up, food supply went up faster, no global subsistence collapse. Dead, and not "right but early." Her line was that the escape came from a place his model had no room for.
- Hubbert, US oil production peaking around 1970: it did. She gives him real credit, and specifically because the date was derived from the discovery-and-production curve rather than asserted.
- The global peak-oil crowd of the mid-2000s: dead as stated. She calls the later pivot to "well, peak oil *demand*" a re-dating without a re-derivation.
- Keynes's 1930 essay about a fifteen-hour workweek for his grandchildren's generation: dead, even though the century he named hasn't run out yet. Reason: the thing that was supposed to produce the leisure arrived on schedule and then some, so the timing excuse isn't available to him.
- Ehrlich vs Simon, the wager over five metal prices across a decade: Ehrlich lost and kept insisting scarcity was still coming. She calls that a degenerating rescue — the auxiliary showed up after the check cleared and predicted nothing new.
- Wegener: continents moving was right, and the physics objection (his proposed driving forces were orders of magnitude too weak) didn't kill it, because the evidence for the claim — matching coastlines, fossil distributions, glacial scratch marks — never depended on his motor. She contrasts this with Malthus explicitly: Malthus's evidence *was* the mechanism.
- Y2K: she'll grant that nothing happening isn't proof there was no bug, but she refuses to let that buy "right but early." Credit attaches to the warning, not to the forecast date, and once you accept the fix worked the forecast is spent.

Now the case that's up next, which somebody brought to the thread and which I have opinions about but can't pin down. Details as I've got them:

A hydrology group projected in the mid-90s that a big semiarid breadbasket aquifer would, at then-current pumping rates, fall below the lift depth needed for irrigation within about twenty years, after which irrigated acreage would collapse and people would leave. Those twenty years came and went. Levels are down, some wells got deepened, but acreage and yields went *up* and there was no migration event. Twelve years before that deadline the same lead author had published a revision pushing the date out roughly another twenty years, on two grounds: a documented shift to drip irrigation, and a new state well-metering and capping regime that had just passed. He re-ran the model on fresh pumping and recharge data rather than tacking years on. His summary was cited in the legislative record when the caps got strengthened a couple years later. Since then the *auxiliary* processes he named — drip adoption, per-hectare withdrawals — have tracked the revision pretty well, better than the headline did. But total pumping has also gone up faster than the original model assumed, because the caps bite on new wells and the metering has exemptions. And this was the third date: an earlier paper by the same author had the collapse arriving around the turn of the millennium, the mid-90s paper moved it out twenty years, the revision moved it out again.
