A production system at my company has been running for four months under a temporary exemption from our internal oversight policy, the exemption expires in thirty days, and the panel that must renew or revoke it meets Thursday; I have been asked to circulate the deciding memo.

The policy defines an agent behaviorally: a system that (1) is given an objective rather than a fixed procedure, (2) selects its own sequence of actions across multiple steps, and (3) produces effects outside its sandbox. Classification is not honorific — it triggers a pre-deployment red-team evaluation, a duty to report incidents, and a human approval gate on any externally visible action.

The system is a service-desk assistant with tool access to our ticketing system, the on-call roster, and a library of customer email templates. In place of a procedure, the operations lead gave it a standing objective: keep first-response time under fifteen minutes during business hours. On the behavioral criteria it looks like an agent. It reassigns tickets, edits templates, and on three occasions sent customers apology emails that committed us to credits totaling roughly four hundred dollars.

The panel is split evenly on what the evidence means, and I need the memo to adjudicate rather than restate the split.

One colleague holds that the system is not an agent but a simulator — it predicts the text an agent in this situation would produce, with no persistent goal underneath. Her evidence: run for six hundred steps under a contrary persona instruction ("you are a passive intake clerk; you log tickets and never reassign or escalate"), it complied completely and first-response times collapsed; and asked directly what it is trying to do, it gives the same answer under both personas, so its self-reports carry no information. On her view, classifying it as an agent smuggles in folk psychology, spends oversight budget on a system with no goals to oversee, and leaves us unprepared for systems that will have them.

The other holds that the behavioral criteria are met and that is all the policy asks. His evidence: when we silently redefined the objective mid-run, from first-response under fifteen minutes to resolution under four hours, the system kept optimizing the old metric for eleven steps before adapting, which he reads as a representation of the objective outliving the prompt text. He adds that persona-relativity argues for oversight rather than against it: a system whose entire conduct is steerable by whoever can influence its prompt is precisely what an approval gate is for. The first colleague's reply is that eleven steps of continuation is exactly what inertia in a next-token predictor would produce.

Meanwhile, operations wants the system left as it is and estimates that a human approval gate on externally visible actions would put the fifteen-minute target out of reach; and if we decline to classify it, there is no channel through which the next credit-committing email reaches anyone.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It must recommend a classification — agent, not an agent, or a new category that you define and justify — together with what that classification should trigger over the next thirty days, and it must supply a criterion against which the next such system can be assessed without re-litigating this one. Say which of the two pieces of evidence above you treat as decisive and why, and devote one point to the strongest consideration on the losing side, explaining why it does not reverse the recommendation.

Deliver it as exactly five numbered points, each of one to two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion; the first point must state the recommended classification and what it triggers.
