The institute I work for funds a strange category of project, and for two years I have co-chaired the panel that reviews the proposals in it. The projects use computational models — agent-based simulations, coupled climate-and-crop models, epidemic models — to explain particular past events: why one palace-administered economy in the eastern Mediterranean stopped functioning within a few decades, why a harvest-price spike in one province produced mass unrest while a larger spike in the neighbouring province produced none, why an epidemic that had advanced for two seasons stalled in one valley and not in the next. Our review form currently asks a single question, whether the project explains its target event, and the scores are useless because the panel splits predictably down disciplinary lines. The historians hold that models explain types and never tokens, and that a simulation which reproduces an outcome has said nothing about why that outcome happened here. The modelers reply that narrative history is unfalsifiable storytelling, unable to say why any alternative did not occur. We have to write a statement of what an explanation of a singular past event must deliver before it earns the name, and I would rather see the specification worked out independently before we draft our own.

So: what does any candidate explanation of one dated, non-repeatable event have to be able to do? Take each requirement in turn and give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a matter of taste. I want the phenomena kept in view, including what makes something an explanandum at all rather than a mere description, and what is demanded of an account that answers "why this rather than that," "why then," and "why there." I want the distinctions an adequate theory may not collapse: token against type explanation; a factor that made a difference against a condition that was merely present; a narrative consistent with the sources against a narrative the sources support; making an event intelligible against locating what turned on it. And I want the results any candidate has to live with, among them that most generalizations available to historians hold only ceteris paribus with open-ended provisos, that the evidence is one-shot, sparse, non-experimental, and produced by interested parties, and that a single run of a tuned model is not obviously better evidence than a single well-sourced chronicle.

Be concrete about the hard cases rather than describing them abstractly: an event that was overdetermined, with several independently sufficient factors converging, so that no single difference-maker can be isolated; an event whose best generalization applies only by holding fixed precisely the conditions that changed; an event that turns on actors who acted on beliefs that were false; an event that was not yet the event it later became, so that the description under which it is explanandum depends on what followed it; and a model that reproduces the outcome only after its parameters have been fitted to that outcome.

Then say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the standing preferences of one camp — subsumption under general laws, mechanism, narrative unity, contrastive structure, modal support, checkability against the record — and which single one does the most to exclude candidates. If you think some subset cannot be jointly satisfied, argue for it; that is the part I most want, because a specification no one can meet is itself a finding about the field. Close with a short verdict on how a covering-law account, an account that explains action by the agent's reasons, and a narrative account fare against your list — short, because the weight belongs on the criteria, not the scorecard.

Two constraints on the answer. Do not give me a bibliography or citations; if you are unsure of an attribution, describe the view instead of naming its author. And distinguish as you go between what is settled among people who work on historical explanation and what is genuinely contested — the standing of mechanism-talk against law-talk, for instance, is not something to announce as decided — and flag plainly wherever you are advancing your own position rather than reporting a shared one. Write the whole thing as continuous prose in paragraphs, the way you would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold, no tables.
