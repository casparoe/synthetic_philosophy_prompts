**Art without a witness**

Most arguments about whether AI images are art turn on the making. Critics say the model is an elaborate collage engine that has never done anything but recombine what it was fed, so nothing has been created. Defenders say human artists are also trained on a tradition, also recombine, and the objection proves too much. I think both sides are wasting their time, and for a simple reason: the recombination premise is true of practically everyone, so it cant bear the weight either side is putting on it. The shape of the dispute is also familiar — every time a new medium arrives the objection takes the form that this isn't making, it's just chemistry (photography) or just mechanics (film). So I want to set the creativity question aside entirely and ask a different one.

Here's the notion I want to work with. When a person makes something that surprises me, the surprise has an addressee. I can hold the maker to it, ask what they thought they were doing, and — importantly — they can turn out to have been wrong about their own work. They can have overreached. I'll call this property addressability. Artworks function partly as testimony: they are reports from a mind, and part of what we do when we look at one is assess the nerve and reliability of the reporter. A generated image can be striking, can even be genuinely novel to me, but the surprise is unaddressable. There is no one to whom the surprise is owed.

The consequence is that the loss isn't aesthetic. Nothing about the pixels is worse for it. What goes offline is a whole category of criticism — the categories that assess a maker's commitments rather than a surface: overreach, cowardice, sentimentality, bad faith, selling out. These are not judgements about how an object looks; they are judgements about risk, and they presuppose that somebody stood to lose something by making this rather than that. A model can produce a sentimental image, but it cannot be sentimental, because sentimentality is a way of evading your own awareness and there is no awareness there to evade. This is the sharpest version of the point I'm after: AI art can't fail in the interesting way.

A natural defence is the instrument reply — the prompt is a score, the model is an instrument, so we get a maker back after all. But the analogy leans on a determinacy claim that isn't available. In an allographic art like music (Goodman's distinction, roughly: arts where a notation fixes the work and performances are instances of it), the score determines the identity of the piece; the performer's contribution is executory and there's a fact of the matter about which notes belong to the work. A prompt doesn't do anything like that. It fixes a region and the model picks a point in it; different runs yield different works, none of which is a performance of the same thing. Call that a score and you've quietly upgraded the prompt from an instruction into a specification — and the entity doing the specifying work is the model, which is exactly what the defence was trying to route around.

The second defence is selection: the artist generates four hundred images and picks one, so the creativity lives in the choice. Fine, but notice what that concedes. Selection-based creativity is real — a great deal of photography is basically this — but it changes the character of the art, because the aesthetic properties now attach to the choosing rather than the making, and the interesting question becomes what the choice is a choice *about*. If the honest answer is that it's about my taste, we have decoration with an author attached. If it's about something I noticed, then we're back to testimony, and the question is whether the model can notice anything — which is the creativity question I promised to set aside, arriving by the back door.

Photography is worth dwelling on, because defenders constantly invoke it and I think it cuts against them. Photography faced the same objection and survived, but not by becoming painting. It survived partly because it was indexical: the light really came off the thing. The photograph's witness was the world. A generated image has no witness of either kind — no person behind it and no scene in front of it.

So my conclusion is that the classification question is stale and the live question is whether we're seeing the emergence of a large standing category of art without a witness: objects with all the surface properties of testimony and none of the epistemic ones. What worries me is a thing I'd call decorative drift — as unaddressable images get cheaper they crowd out addressable ones not by being better but by being frictionless, and a culture's habit of treating images as reports simply atrophies. That last bit is an empirical claim I can't support in this piece and I'm nervous about it.
