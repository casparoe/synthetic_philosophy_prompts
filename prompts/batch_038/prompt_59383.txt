I'm on the ethics consult service at a community hospital, maybe six years in now, and I'd say half my consults end up being about a demented patient who left instructions a long time ago and a family that can't agree about what to do with them. After enough of these I've noticed my own gut calls don't line up into anything I could defend out loud, so instead of agonizing some more I want to write down my verdicts and have someone tell me what principle, if any, they add up to. Case 1: woman signed a directive at 55, very specific, says no feeding tube if she ever reaches late-stage Alzheimer's. She's 84 now, doesn't know her daughter's name, but she's pleasant, eats with real enjoyment, laughs at cartoons, hums along to the radio. Family is split down the middle. My gut: don't act on the directive, and if she stops eating on her own someday that's a separate conversation, but I'm not pointing at a piece of paper from 1998 to justify starving her. Case 2: same woman, same paper, gets a plain pneumonia that two days of antibiotics clears up, and the directive also says no antibiotics in late dementia. My gut flips — I lean toward treating — and I genuinely can't say why treating is different from feeding when the document says the identical thing about both. Case 3: retired cellist who wrote that if he could no longer follow a piece of music he wanted nothing done to prolong him. He can't tell you what a cello is now, but he lights up and beats time on the tray table when somebody puts Bach on. My gut: the trigger never fired, so the directive doesn't apply, even though the disability it described absolutely arrived. Case 4: thirty-year-old, motorcycle, no brainstem function at all, unambiguous directive, parents objecting on religious grounds. My gut: honor it, obviously, honestly no consult needed. Case 5: man with advanced ALS, completely present, jokes with the night staff, by every measure we have he has adapted and is not miserable, and he has a standing request that we help him die, which we won't do. My gut: I'd do it if I were allowed. So 1 and 5 are the pair I can't reconcile — both are people whose present selves seem okay and whose earlier selves asked to be let go, and I come out opposite ways. The difference I reach for is "he's still in there and she isn't," but that seems to cut the wrong direction, because if he's still in there, isn't his current contentment exactly the thing that should outrank the paper?

I've half-formed a rule I keep testing against these and it keeps failing. Roughly: an instruction binds only when the person who wrote it is still around to be wronged by our ignoring it. Case 4 destroys that immediately, since he is precisely not around. So maybe a weaker version: the earlier self gets a veto over what we do to the later one, but no power to make us do anything — refusals bind, requests don't. But then case 5 is a request and I want to grant it, and case 1 is a refusal and I want to deny it, so the rule fails at both ends and I'm not sure it's doing any work at all. I assume philosophers and bioethicists have chewed on all of this and I'm not asking for a reading list, I just want to know whether my verdicts correspond to some recognizable position or whether I'm in honestly incoherent territory.

So: what's the principle that gets the most of these five, stated precisely enough that I can see which clause is doing the work in each case, and what does it say about the one I keep flip flopping on — a woman who wrote her directive during a bad depressive episode, then recovered and was herself for twenty years, never updated the document, and is now demented: does that directive date from the depressed version of her or the recovered one, and does it matter that she never got around to retracting it? Tell me straight which of my five verdicts your best principle has to overturn, because I'd rather drop one than have all five saved by something so baroque I couldn't apply it at 3am. And one practical thing: our risk manager wants a policy where patients re-sign their directive every two years so the most recent one always wins, and I think that's deranged for people who won't remember having signed the last one — does whatever principle you land on side with him or with me?
