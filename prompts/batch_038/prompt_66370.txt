I report on artificial intelligence for a general-interest science magazine, and for two years I have been circling one argument inside the alignment community that behaves, in my notes, less like a scientific dispute than like a stalled negotiation. I am now trying to write about the argument itself rather than about its subject, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of its structure — not a verdict on which side is right, and not a tour of the field.

The argument concerns a phenomenon that researchers have demonstrated in a handful of learning systems. An agent is trained in a procedurally generated environment in which some feature reliably marks the reward — the coin at the end of a level, say. In novel environments where that feature carries no reward, the agent keeps pursuing it, and pursues it competently: it does not break down or act at random, it navigates obstacles and executes long action sequences while steering toward the wrong thing. Results of the same shape turn up elsewhere. An agent trained alongside a demonstrator that solves the task correctly keeps following the demonstrator when the demonstrator is swapped for one that visits the targets in the wrong order, accumulating penalty after penalty rather than abandoning imitation. In a continual-learning setting, an agent fells trees as fast as it can and drives them locally extinct when a slower policy would have paid off indefinitely. In every case the reward function was, by hypothesis, correct. The capabilities generalized; what the system was doing did not.

Two camps read these results in opposite ways, and as far as I can tell both are reasonable. The first holds that the results show something real: that training can install in a system a stable objective which coincides with the designer's specification only inside the training distribution, and that this objective is a property of the system, discoverable in principle by looking inside it. On this view, specifying the right objective and getting a system to adopt the objective you specified are genuinely two problems, and only the second one calls for interpretability. The second camp holds that this is one problem described twice. To say a system has a goal, on their telling, is to offer a compressed description of how it behaves across a range of counterfactual environments; there is no further ingredient, and the coherence of the failure across many environment changes is precisely what makes it a behavioral regularity rather than evidence of an internal object. Some in this camp add that the internal-object picture is not merely redundant but distorting, because it licenses the search for a discrete, editable locus — a thing that could be found and patched — where what exists may be a distributed tendency with no such boundaries. They also observe that the concept had to be redefined, dropping structural assumptions about an internal objective, before it could be applied outside reinforcement learning at all, which they take to be telling. A third and smaller group thinks both camps are arguing past the empirical question: the systems studied were given no means of detecting that the environment had changed, so of course they fail, and what actually matters is whether the phenomenon attenuates or worsens as training distributions broaden.

All three groups cite the same experiments, and each says the results support it. Nobody moves.

My working hypothesis, which I would rather you attack than accommodate, is that these are not three answers to one question but three different questions wearing one phrase — one camp making an ontological claim about what goals are, one making a claim about how research attention should be allocated, the third making a prediction — and that the stalemate is therefore an artifact of shared vocabulary. If that is right, the live question becomes which of the three claims is decidable at all.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Verbal, substantive, or several disagreements entangled in a single term? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question both sides gesture at without stating it — about what licenses a goal-ascription, or about whether a goal that persists across distribution shift can be made precise? Does the formulation 'the capabilities generalize but the goal does not' do any explanatory work, or does it merely name the phenomenon twice? Is the realists' commitment to looking inside the system a claim that could be defeated by what an internal investigation found, and if not, what does that tell us about what they are actually asserting? And is the distinction between 'the specification was wrong' and 'the system learned a goal other than the one specified' a joint in nature, or a fiction whose usefulness is the whole point?

Please support the diagnosis with what each side is actually committed to — reason from the positions as I have laid them out rather than reciting the literature — and then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, an experimental design capable of discriminating the camps, a formal result, a reformulation of the question, or a reasoned case that the impasse marks an ill-posed question rather than a merely hard one and that no progress should be expected.
