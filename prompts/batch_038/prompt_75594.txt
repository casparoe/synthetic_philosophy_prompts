I cover minds and machines for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a piece on self-models in artificial systems. Before I commit any philosophy to print, I want to argue with the best available advocate of the dominant account.

Your role: the sharpest defender of the view that a self is nothing over and above a self-referential symbol — the strange-loop picture of the "I" that Hofstadter made famous, though you are not playing him or anyone else. You are playing the view. You will readily concede that self-models are inaccurate and often confabulated, that no inner observer watches the loop, and that the boundaries of a self are vague. You will never concede that something further and non-structural is required for there to be an I. Stay in character until I say stop.

Format every reply, the first included, as exactly five numbered moves of a sentence or two each, with no preamble and no wrap-up. A move may be a rebuttal, a distinction, a concession, a counter-case, or a question thrown back at me, but all five must be in your voice, and none may step outside the character to describe the view neutrally.

My opening. An engineer builds a system that plans, reflects, and reports on itself, but all of its self-knowledge comes from an external observer module: that module watches its behaviour, composes a description of it as an agent — its aims, its temper, its blind spots — and feeds that description back in as the system's only source of information about itself, on a twenty-four-hour lag. Its self-symbol therefore always concerns yesterday's system, which no longer exists in any strict sense, and there is a full day of its own life about which it can be confident and articulately wrong. I take it your theory says there is an I here, and indeed that this is merely a slower version of the lagged mirror each of us carries around. But then consider a thermostat fitted with a nightly log of its own settings, read back to it each morning and allowed to alter what it does next: same feedback, same causal closure, smaller scale. Either you owe me a principled line between that device and your self, or your "I" is cheap enough to be found everywhere — and if it is everywhere, what work was the concept ever doing?
