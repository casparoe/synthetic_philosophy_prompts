I am one of two graduate teaching assistants marking the timed essay for a third-year ethics option module, "Responsibility, Blame, and the Moral Address", at a UK university. The convenor set the following question, which students answered under examination conditions in ninety minutes with a limit of 1,200 words and a maximum of 30 marks:

> "Someone who has done the very thing she condemns another for cannot consistently condemn it." Distinguish the question whether a would-be blamer has the standing to blame from the question whether her criticism is true. What must an account of lost standing do in order to avoid making standing so hard to retain that almost nobody has it, and so easy to lose that nothing turns on the concept?

The departmental rubric divides the 30 marks as follows: accurate exposition of the distinction between standing and the truth of the criticism, 8; a reasoned account of what undermines standing, 8; critical evaluation of that account, including at least one objection developed rather than merely mentioned, 8; structure, relevance and clarity, 6. The band descriptors are: 24–30, identifies a position and argues for it; 20–23, accurate and well organised, but critical claims asserted rather than argued; 16–19, accurate in outline, with some confusion or unsupported moves; 12–15, partially addresses the question; below 12, substantially off-target. Engagement with the secondary literature is not required, and its absence should not be penalised. Here is one of the forty-three scripts, reproduced in full and paragraph-numbered so that comments can be keyed to it:

> [1] The statement in the title is not, at first sight, an objection to any moral criticism. If I tell you that you were wrong to break your promise, the fact that I have broken promises myself does nothing to show that promise-breaking was not wrong of you. Offered as a way of rebutting a charge, "and so do you" is the tu quoque, and it is a fallacy: it attacks whoever makes a claim instead of the claim. The question of standing is different. It is a question about the position from which a true claim may properly be pressed. A hypocrite's condemnation can be wholly accurate and still be something she is in no position to make. This is why the standing objection is interesting: it concedes the truth of the charge and objects to the making of it.
>
> [2] The most natural explanation of the loss of standing appeals to inconsistency. What is objectionable about the hypocrite is not that she has done the wrong. If merely having done it were enough, standing would be unattainable for anyone who has ever done anything blameworthy, and the concept would collapse, since nobody could then blame anybody for anything. That is a reductio of the simple version. What goes wrong instead is that she applies to another a standard she does not apply to herself. Blame holds an agent to a standard that is supposed to be shared; a blamer who exempts herself in a like case is not appealing to a shared standard but marking herself out as outside it, and her criticism is then not inconsistent with the truth of the charge but inconsistent with her own participation in the practice of holding one another to standards.
>
> [3] The same reasoning is usually extended to cases in which the would-be blamer has not herself done the thing. If I paid you to do it, encouraged you in it, or have profited from your doing it without trying to stop it, I am involved in the very wrong I now condemn, and involvement is what undermines standing. There is no principled difference, so far as standing goes, between commissioning a wrong and committing it; an account that treats them differently is being arbitrary.
>
> [4] It is worth pressing the argument further. If blame consists in holding someone to a standard one also accepts, then a blamer who exempts herself is not making a defective demand but no demand at all: her words are not blame, and cannot be, but only the expression of a wish that someone else behave differently, or an attempt to gain advantage over them. On this view the hypocrite does not blame badly; she does not blame. That explains why the intuition against her is so strong, and why it is tempting to think that her criticism simply falls away.
>
> [5] Standing must also be all-or-nothing if it is to do this work. To say that one blamer has more standing than another is to change the subject: the question is whether one is entitled to make the address, and entitlements are not graded. Talk of diminished standing describes how unwelcome or how offensive a criticism is likely to be, which is a question about its reception rather than its propriety.
>
> [6] Finally, the upshot for the person being blamed. Because the hypocrite cannot consistently press her criticism, the one she blames is entitled to discount it, and to treat it as carrying no weight until the blamer has put her own house in order. Strictly, that is not to concede that what she did was permissible; but she has not been shown to have done wrong by anyone entitled to show it, and in ordinary moral practice that is as good as an answer to the charge. The standing objection thus has real force: it is not merely a complaint about the manner of a criticism, but a reason for the criticised party to set the criticism aside.
