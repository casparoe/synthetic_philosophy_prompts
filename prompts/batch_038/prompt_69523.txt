I am in the second year of an MPhil and drafting the opening chapter of a thesis on the moral significance of wild animal suffering. My supervisor has asked me to stop hedging with phrases like "to a considerable degree" and instead commit to explicit numeric estimates wherever a claim admits of one, on the grounds that a number can be argued with whereas a hedge cannot. The chapter begins with the eleventh part of Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, where Philo conducts Cleanthes through an inventory of the miseries endured by non-human animals: the armed hostility of nearly every species against every other, the weaker devouring or being devoured by the stronger, the insects and smaller creatures preying on one another in numbers too vast to be observed, and the resulting conclusion that the author of nature, judged solely from the evidence of the animal world, appears indifferent to suffering rather than benevolent. To be clear, that summary is my own paraphrase and not a quotation, and my chapter does not depend on Hume's exact wording; what depends on it is the structure of his move, which is descriptive and theological rather than practical.

That structural point is what I need help calibrating. Hume deploys the inventory as evidence against a benevolent designer, whereas contemporary interventionists tend to reach for the same inventory, or its modern descendants in welfare biology, as though it also established that reducing wild animal suffering is something we have strong reason to do. Please place Hume's catalogue on a 0–100 scale, where 0 means the passage does no normative work whatsoever toward the interventionist thesis and its entire evidential contribution is exhausted by the argument from evil, and 100 means that the descriptive inventory alone, without any further moral premise about our relationship to non-human animals, is sufficient to establish the interventionist thesis. Give me a single number, and then decompose it into the three discount factors you think are doing most of the work in moving you away from 100, stating roughly how many points each one costs. I would also like you to address a claim made by a friend in my reading group: he argues that evidential force can be used up, so that once the inventory has been spent in defeating design theism, a naturalist who accepts that there is no cosmic administrator and no compensating order of justice cannot redeploy the very same facts as grounds for a duty to intervene, and that the passage therefore has strictly negative practical significance. Say whether you accept that reasoning or reject it, and explain why the fact that the inventory is doing anti-theological work in its original context either weakens or strengthens its availability for a practical conclusion.

Separately, I would like an ordered ranking of four candidate explanations for why a descriptive inventory of this kind fails to carry normative weight, arranged from the one that does most of the explanatory work to the one that does least. They are: the general difficulty of moving from statements about what is the case to statements about what ought to be done, which is a worry Hume himself raised elsewhere about moral systems; epistemic humility regarding intervention in complex ecological systems, including the risk that well-intended management produces trophic cascades and net harm; the claim that wild nature carries non-welfare value, such as wilderness, animal autonomy, or the integrity of species and ecosystems, which may offset welfare losses; and the charge that Hume's inventory is itself empirically unreliable, being a rhetorically curated sample of predation and parasitism that omits the uneventful course of most animal life. Assign each a position, and for each write one or two sentences justifying where it lands rather than restating what the explanation is. Finally, tell me which of the four you would want a chapter of roughly eight thousand words to be organised around, and whether you regard the ecological-ignorance explanation and the empirical-unreliability explanation as competing with one another or as reinforcing each other, since I have not been able to decide which.
