I've been working through Hofstadter's careenium — the sealed cabinet full of bouncing steel balls, flippers and chutes, in which clusters of "simmballs" come, through the machine's history of interaction with its surroundings, to stand for things: one cluster for *hungry*, another for *food-near*, eventually one for the careenium itself. The reason the toy grabs me is that the machine's behavior is vastly more predictable at the level of what its clusters stand for than at the level of individual collisions, and yet I can't decide whether that fact is about the world or about me. So I've been collecting verdicts on cases, going by gut, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I know the standard labels in this territory — the exclusion problem, the suggestion that a higher-level cause can be more *proportional* to its effect than the micro-cause that realizes it — but I'd rather work from the cases than from the vocabulary, so please don't organize your answer around who said what.

Here are the cases and where I land. (1) The careenium's *hungry* cluster fires and the machine rolls toward the fuel chute: I want to say the meaning is inert, that all the work is done by collisions and the symbolic story is a convenience. (2) A chess engine resigns because it is down a rook and sees no drawing line: here I have no temptation at all to say the description is inert — being down a rook seems to be exactly why it resigned, and it's silicon too. (3) A rumor that a bank is insolvent causes a run: obviously the content does the work, since a rumor that the CEO was retiring would have caused nothing of the kind. (4) A thermostat: I say nothing there means anything, the set-point isn't a symbol, though my reading group disagrees and says it's just case (3) with the complexity dialed down. (5) A rock whose cracks happen to spell out a warning: nothing means anything, no counterfactual sensitivity anywhere. (6) A road sign that diverts a whole morning's traffic: yes, the meaning does the work. (7) Two careenia with token-for-token identical internal dynamics and identical behavior, one raised among water and one among a watery look-alike, so that — if content is fixed partly from outside — one cluster means *water* and the other doesn't: the content differs, nothing that happens differs, so this kind of content can't be causally relevant; and yet I don't want to say that about the rumor in (3), which was surely about the bank. (8) Pain: I want to say the hurting is what makes me jerk my hand back, and that the neural account leaves something out — which sits badly with what I said in (1). (9) The self-simmball, the cluster that stands for this very cluster: here I feel the symbol genuinely pushes the substrate around, because once it exists it reroutes which collisions happen at all, in a way the *hungry* cluster doesn't — but I can't see how that is anything over and above the same physics. (10) The low-pressure center of a hurricane takes the roof off my neighbor's house: a higher-level description doing real causal work, with no meaning involved, so higher-level causation as such isn't what I'm worried about. (11) A careenium whose symbolic description reliably predicts its behavior while a full physical simulation of it is beyond anyone's computing power: the symbolic description is plainly the better explanation, but I want to insist that's a fact about us, not about the machine.

My tentative principle is that a high-level property does genuine causal work just when the effect counterfactually depends on that property across changes of realization — hold the meaning fixed, vary the physics, and the behavior stays; vary the meaning, hold the physics fixed, and it goes. Please test it, break it, and repair it. I specifically want to know whether it can distinguish (1) from (2), whether it has to say something different about content that is fixed from outside the machine, as in (7), versus content fixed by a community's conventions, as in (6), and whether (9) comes out as a special case of the principle or as evidence against it. If no single principle can carry everything, give me the smallest set of candidates and say plainly which of my verdicts each one buys at the price of which others. Then tell me what the resulting account says about three cases I haven't listed: a trained network whose internal activations a linear probe reliably decodes as *cat-detector* although nothing in its design mentioned cats; a printed word that the careenium's camera reads but which, through a wiring accident, has come to trigger exactly the same cluster as a nonsense syllable, so that the community's meaning and the machine's disposition have come apart; and a self-simmball that still stands for itself but has been disconnected from the flippers, so it can no longer alter behavior. Please structure the answer in these sections, in this order: **Bottom line** (about 150 words — the principle or principles stated precisely, and the one verdict I have to surrender); **The principle, clause by clause** (about 600 words — each clause paired with the case from my list that motivates it); **The three hard pairs** (about 500 words — (1) against (2), (7) against (3), (9) against (1); for each, whether it can be reconciled and what complexity the reconciliation costs); **Causal versus explanatory** (about 300 words — whether the principle must keep these apart, using (11) and (10) as the test); **Cases I didn't list** (about 400 words — reasoned verdicts on my three, plus one further case the principle was built to handle that I haven't thought of); and **What I have to give up** (about 250 words — which of my eleven verdicts survive, which don't, and what the simplest principle that saves the most of them costs me). I'd rather give up (4) or (11) than (7), but say so if I'm wrong about that.
