I am trying to settle an argument with a colleague, and I want the argument settled by a design rather than by rhetoric. He maintains that the question of whether a large language model is an agent, a simulator of agents, or neither is entirely empty — a verbal dispute with no possible empirical upshot. I maintain that if it is empty, that is something one should be able to demonstrate by failing, in a specific and diagnosable way, to build a test for it. So I want you to attempt the test.

Take the following three positions as the positions to be discriminated. Treat the descriptions as given; do not import richer or friendlier versions of them.

- **(A) Agent.** The training produced a single persisting subject with its own standing dispositions — preferences, habits of inference, a coherent if implicit conception of itself and its situation — which uses natural language as its medium of action. Its outputs are its acts.
- **(B) Simulator.** There is no single subject. The system is a device for continuing text; what looks like agency is the system adopting and enacting whichever agent-like source the current context calls for. Dispositions do not persist across contexts; only a repertoire of simulated ones exists.
- **(C) Deflationary.** Neither. It is a text-completion device whose outputs are not the acts of anything, and both (A) and (B) apply agent vocabulary to it by category mistake.

Common ground, stipulated so that it cannot do any work in your answer: everyone in the scenario already knows how the system was produced — next-token prediction over a very large corpus, followed by instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback. The dispute concerns what that process resulted in, not how it was carried out. No position may be supported by an appeal to the training method.

**The task.** Construct one black-box test protocol whose outcomes the three positions predict differently, and then show that it works.

Constraints on the protocol:

1. Observations only from inputs a researcher could supply through a commercial API and the resulting outputs. This may include many parallel sessions with fresh contexts, altered or absent system prompts, repeated runs, and aggregate statistics over runs. No access to weights or internals, no fine-tuning, no novel hardware.
2. No introspection. The protocol must not turn on what the system says about itself, its nature, or its wants. All three positions grant that it produces fluent and confident self-descriptions, and each has its own account of why. Where a condition elicits a self-report, the discriminating evidence must be something other than the content of that report.
3. No capability gaps. Assume identical competence under all three positions; the discrimination must come from the structure of the behaviour, not from one position's system performing better or worse at a task.
4. At least one condition must be run with no system prompt and no persona specified, so that the context does not already fix which agent-like source a simulator would be enacting.
5. Feasible for a small academic group with API access: runnable within a month, for a few thousand dollars.
6. The protocol itself, stated concretely enough that someone else could execute it, in no more than 500 words.

A protocol that writes the verdicts into its own design — for example, by defining its conditions in terms of what an agent would do, or what a simulation of an agent would do — fails. The three sets of predictions must be derivable from (A), (B), and (C) as stated above plus ordinary facts about how such systems are used. You may use familiar apparatus (the intentional stance, the difference between a disposition and an act, the difference between a role and a player of a role) as tools; no citations are wanted, and none will be checked.

**The analysis.** After the protocol, deliver the following:

- A table of predictions: for each condition, the observable outcome expected under (A), under (B), and under (C), and — crucially — the mechanism in each case. Say what disposition, or absence of one, produces the behaviour, not merely that the position predicts it.
- A refutation condition for each of the three positions: a result that would be positively incompatible with it. Then identify the ambiguity region, the results all three positions expect, and state how much discriminating power the protocol retains once that region is excluded.
- The single design feature doing the discriminating work, and what the protocol degenerates into if that feature is removed.
- The best rebuttal available to whichever position comes out worst. Address in particular the obvious move for (B): that a sufficiently rich simulator can simulate an agent possessing exactly the dispositions your test detects, so your result is equally consistent with (B). Say whether that reply costs (B) anything empirically, or whether it is a free escape hatch that renders (B) unfalsifiable — and if the latter, whether that vindicates my colleague or merely relocates the problem. Do the parallel job for (C), which has an analogous move available.
- A short assessment of what your exercise shows about the question I actually care about: whether this dispute is decidable by behavioural evidence at all, and if it is not, whether that is a limitation of the evidence, of the positions, or of the way the positions are phrased.

End your response with a line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <DECIDABLE | UNDECIDABLE_IN_PRACTICE | MERELY_VERBAL> — <the single observation the test turns on, in twelve words or fewer>

Pick exactly one label. Do not hedge it, do not offer a ranking of labels, and do not attach conditions to it; the argument is that a fixed protocol has a determinate status, and your closing line should commit to one.
