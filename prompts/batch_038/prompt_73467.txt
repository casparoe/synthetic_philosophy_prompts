I am preparing, for a graduate reading group on Avicenna's philosophy of mind, a reconstruction of the flying man argument that I have come to distrust: run carefully, it proves far more than Avicenna can use, and the excess is not harmless. The reconstruction is as follows.

(1) Suppose a person were brought into existence all at once, fully formed and with all faculties in working order, suspended in a void, sight blocked, and limbs arranged so that no part of his body touches another: he receives no sensory input, from outside or from his own body.

(2) Even so, that person would be aware of himself; he would affirm that he exists.

(3) He would not, however, affirm the existence of any bodily part — neither his limbs nor his internal organs nor his body as a whole.

(4) Since he is aware of himself without being aware of anything bodily, what he is aware of is not the body, nor anything composed of body: the self is not identical with the body or with any bodily faculty.

(5) Step (4) requires a general principle. For any F: if, in that situation, one would be aware of the self without being aware of F, then F is not the self, is no part of the self, and is not essential to it. Call this the manifestness principle.

(6) But in that situation the flying man would equally be unaware of his memories, his acquired concepts, his temperament and dispositions, his personal history — indeed of whether he had existed a moment earlier or ever at all, since the scenario stipulates that he has no history.

(7) By (5), none of these is part of the self or essential to it. The self therefore involves no psychological content whatsoever.

(8) He would likewise be aware of nothing that marks him off from any other self: no body, no history, no experiences, no perceived location, nothing beyond the bare fact of his own existence, which has the same content in every such case.

(9) If selves are not material (by 4), they cannot be multiplied by matter or by place; if they contain no distinguishing psychological content (by 7 and 8), they cannot be multiplied by that either. Two flying men would thus be indiscernible and hence numerically one: there is no principle of individuation for selves.

(10) By the same reasoning there is nothing in virtue of which the self I am now aware of is the self I was aware of yesterday: no persisting matter, and no persisting memories or character, these having been excluded by (7).

(11) Hence, if sound, the flying man shows that selves are bare, featureless, unindividuated, and without diachronic identity — one and the same in every case and at every moment.

(12) That is not simply more than Avicenna wants; it is incompatible with what he needs, since the immateriality of the soul is supposed to underwrite the survival of the individual soul, and individual survival presupposes individuals.

So the reconstruction is either invalid or contains a false premise, and I cannot say which, or where. My suspicion falls on (5) and (8), but I cannot justify the suspicion, and I am not certain the two are independent faults.

What I would like is a diagnosis, and a price list.

- Name the step or steps at which the argument fails, and say why each looked acceptable in the first place. In particular: does (5) license only a claim of non-identity, or also a claim of non-essentiality, and is any single reading available that supports both (4) and (7)? If not, which reading does the conclusion Avicenna wants require?
- Say whether the move from (8) to (9) turns on a suppressed assumption that whatever is true of a self must be manifest to that self's awareness of itself; and whether that assumption is independently plausible, or is doing covert work only because it was never stated.
- Assess (9) on its own terms: is the claim that immaterial substances can be multiplied only by matter or by place something Avicenna is committed to, something he would resist, or something needing an argument of its own? Say what such an argument would have to establish.
- Consider whether (6) is even true of the scenario as specified. A person created all at once with his faculties in working order may possess what belongs to the soul essentially while exercising none of it. If a distinction between what the soul is and what it currently apprehends blocks (6), say whether the resulting argument still delivers (7), or only something weaker, and how much weaker.
- For each candidate repair — rejecting (5) outright, restricting (5) to non-identity, denying the passage to (9), refusing (10) by grounding persistence in the soul's own continuing self-awareness, or resisting (6) — state what the flying man proves afterward, and whether the residue still supports the rest of the programme: the soul as an immaterial substance, and the individuality of its survival.
- Rank the repairs from cheapest to most damaging, and say which, if any, leave the argument dialectically useful against an opponent who holds that the soul is nothing over and above the body or an arrangement of its parts.

If I had to choose, I would rather keep (2) through (4) and (12) than anything else; tell me plainly what keeping them costs. Argue from the structure of the reconstruction rather than from textual scholarship: where a repair depends on a claim about Avicenna's own doctrines — his account of what individuates one soul from another, his reasons for rejecting the migration of a soul into a second body, his treatment of self-awareness as belonging to the soul essentially rather than accidentally — flag it as such a claim and say what it would have to amount to for the repair to succeed.
