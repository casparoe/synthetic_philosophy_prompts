I am drafting a chapter on the epistemology of testimony for a collected volume, and before I engage with any particular account I want the specification of what an adequate one would have to deliver in the hardest case available: reports of apparently paranormal events, and above all reports advanced as evidence for the survival of consciousness after death. Please write that specification.

I want the requirements anchored to Henry Sidgwick's predicament, because it makes the difficulty unusually sharp. He was the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research in 1882 and at the same time the author of The Methods of Ethics, where he argues that the conflict between rational egoism and universal benevolence — his dualism of practical reason — leaves morality without a fully rational foundation unless one may assume the universe to be so ordered that duty and happiness are ultimately reconcilable. He therefore treated empirical evidence for survival as a matter of the first practical importance for ethics, while presiding over an investigative program whose standards were such that no such evidence emerged. He also chaired the committee behind the Census of Hallucinations, published in 1894 and drawn up largely by his wife Eleanor Mildred Sidgwick, which canvassed roughly 17,000 people, recorded 1,684 reports of apparition experiences, and whose central statistical move was to compare the observed frequency with which such experiences fell within a day of a corresponding death against the frequency chance coincidence would predict, given the base rate of the experiences and general mortality figures. Any specification I adopt must make sense of someone able to do all three of these things at once without evident inconsistency.

The deliverable is a numbered list of the requirements an adequate account of evidential standards in this domain must meet, each with the case or consideration motivating it. Please cover at least the following, and add any requirement I have omitted that you judge belongs, saying why:

- the distinction it may not collapse between the reliability of a witness's report of an experience and the evidential weight of the causal story attached to it: a witness may be impeccable about having seen a recognizable figure at a given hour without that supporting survival in the slightest;
- deliberate fraud, and separately fraud of which the witness is unaware — confabulation, elaboration under questioning, and the sincere reporting of ordinary information, forgotten by everyone present, that arrives as though novel;
- the standing worry that information apparently from the dead could instead be produced by an unrestricted telepathic faculty among the living, which would leave survival empirically indistinguishable from boundless psi; and whether the cross-correspondence strategy later developed within the Society — complementary fragments, each too specific for any single sitter, gathered from automatic writers working apart — answers that worry or merely relocates it;
- an explanation, without special pleading, of why the standard applied here differs from that applied to ordinary testimony, since a differential treatment justified only by the falsity of the target claim is circular;
- a bar that some finite body of testimony could in principle clear, on pain of begging the question, together with a description of what such a body of testimony would look like;
- the non-independence of witnesses who share a cultural script, given that séance conventions shaped what was experienced, how it was described, and what counted as striking;
- an explicit attitude to repeatability and to experimental control, and to the demand for a mechanism, with no silent importation of either from laboratory science by an account that continues to accept non-repeatable singular evidence elsewhere, as in history, cosmology, or the early reception of meteorites;
- a view on whether practical stakes bear on the threshold: if the consequences of error about survival are as Sidgwick supposed, does that raise the evidential bar, lower it, or leave it unmoved — and can the same account say what is lost by suspending judgment indefinitely;
- a statement of what would count against the hypothesis, not only of what would count for it.

Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are the local preferences of a particular camp — the laboratory, the apologist, the debunker — and say what makes them camp-relative rather than genuine.

The part I most want is this. Identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. My own suspicion is that a standard demanding a mass of independent, uncontaminated, non-coincidental testimony cannot be met by a phenomenon that is, on the most favorable description its partisans offer, sporadic, private, and uncontrolled, so that the attainability requirement pulls against the independence and repeatability requirements. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, whether the impossibility is a feature of the phenomenon or an artifact of standards imported from the wrong kind of inquiry, and, if a requirement must be dropped or reformulated to restore consistency, which one and what it becomes. Then name the single requirement that does the most work in excluding candidate accounts, and justify that choice.

Close with a short assessment — half a page at most — of how three kinds of account fare against the list: a Humean treatment on which uniform experience defeats any amount of testimony; the Bayesian rejoinder that a sufficiently large body of independent credible reports can in principle outweigh any finite prior; and a credulity-based treatment on which sincere testimony is presumptively credible absent specific defeaters. Say also where Sidgwick's own Census practice sits: does it satisfy the specification he implicitly endorsed, and if not, which requirement does it fail?

Please give no bibliography, no citations, and no reading list; I want the requirements and the reasoning behind them. A table is acceptable for marking non-negotiable against camp-relative, but the impossibility argument should be in prose.
