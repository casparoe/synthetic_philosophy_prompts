Before retiring I spent thirty years in electrical metrology — primary standards, traceability chains, round-robin intercomparisons, uncertainty budgets, and the recurring question of whether a stubborn instrument is out of tolerance or simply not checkable. I have lately been reading philosophy of mind, and the dispute over introspection — the sceptics about its reliability, the transparency theorists, and those who hold that knowing one's own mind is no species of observation or detection — is the one place where I find I cannot think except by translating. Please do that translation for me, camp by camp.

Model introspective access as an instrument reading an internal quantity, and state what each position is thereby committed to about that instrument: its accuracy and resolution, its drift, whether its readings can be verified against an independent standard, or whether they partly constitute the quantity they purport to read — the difference between a working standard that is checked against something higher and a definitional standard that fixes the unit and therefore cannot itself be in error.

Then the residue report. Which claims survive translation intact; which survive only by being quietly restyled as claims about instrument error; and which — first-person authority, rational commitment, self-deception without mistake — have no metrological expression at all. My real question is whether that residue shows the measurement model to be the wrong apparatus for the job, or shows the philosophers' disagreement to have been under-specified all along: that once one asks which quantity is being read and against what standard, part of the dispute evaporates. Do not import metrological presuppositions and present them as translations; where you have to weaken or strengthen a philosopher's claim to make it fit the vocabulary, mark the exact place you did it.
