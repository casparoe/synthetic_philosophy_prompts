I'm a third-year philosophy student, and this term I'm in a seminar on necessity, possibility, and essence. I mention the seminar only because I half-expected it to help me with something that has become rather urgent in my own life, and it hasn't, at least not so far. The background is this: I was conceived using donated sperm, which I learned at seventeen when a dermatologist's office asked me for a family medical history and my mother, after a long pause, told me the truth rather than the version she and my father had intended to keep. She handled it well and has never been defensive about it, but she closed the conversation with something that has stuck in my head ever since. Roughly: genes don't make a person; if the clinic had used a different donor that day I would still be me, only with somebody else's nose and somebody else's height; and therefore there is nothing for me to go looking for. I loved her for saying it and I also felt oddly deflated, as though the whole question of my origins had just been declared a non-question, and I have never been able to tell whether that deflation is philosophical confusion on my part or a legitimate response to a confusion on hers.

The practical situation changed last spring. I wrote to the clinic that treated my parents asking for the donor's identifying information, which in this state they are permitted to release to me on request. What came back was not an identification but an apology: my mother's file records a donor code, but the laboratory log for that week is ambiguous, because two donors' samples were thawed on the same morning and the entry against my mother's name lists only a batch range that both of them fall inside. The records officer wrote that the paperwork probably cannot resolve it, that this sort of thing was not unusual in those years, and that a commercial DNA test compared against the clinic's donor registry would settle it in about a fortnight. In the meantime I have found an online group of some forty people conceived from one or other of these two donors, and I have been reading it far more closely than I have been reading my seminar assignments. Two things from it matter here. One is medical: I have a mild heart murmur that a cardiologist has called insignificant but wants monitored, and one of the two donors is reportedly a former distance runner in his sixties while the other, according to a post by a group member, lost a brother suddenly in his forties. The other is social: several members treat the shared donor as a real genealogical fact about themselves, they use the language of siblings, and at least two of them have made plans to travel to meet him.

Three people around me are now saying incompatible things, and I can't tell which of them is muddled, or whether the muddle makes any difference to what I should do. My mother and my roommate, who is a statistics major, are on one side: he insists I shouldn't bother with the test, on the grounds that I am who I am either way, that my memories and upbringing don't shift according to a vial number, and that the only thing a test could tell me is a piece of trivia I would then have to carry around. On the other side, a woman in the group, who is my age and who resembles my grandmother's old photographs to a degree I find unnerving, holds that the shared origin is precisely what makes the forty of them siblings, and that it gives her a standing to seek the man out that a stranger would not have. And a third member, a sceptical one, has written that all this is sentimentality: had the clinic reached for a different straw that morning, most of them would still be walking around, only with different faces and different surnames. That last remark is the one I keep returning to, because it seems to make my mother's consolation true in a way that also makes the second woman's whole framework empty. What I would like advice about is concrete and twofold: whether I should order the test, and whether, if it names one of the two men, I should try to contact him. And behind that, whether the answer depends on settling the argument between the three of them — because I have a suspicion, which I cannot articulate, that they are sliding between two different senses of what would or wouldn't have been me, and that the sense my mother has in mind is not the one that determines whether the search is pointless. My seminar reading has not helped, partly because everything we discuss concerns origins that are simply impossible, whereas mine is unknown but perfectly determinate, and I don't know whether that distinction is doing any real work.
