Retired three years ago after 38 in a physical chemistry lab (reaction kinetics, mostly, if you must know), and I've been reading philosophy for pleasure ever since, badly and out of order. Right now its Tolstoy's *What Is Art?* — my wife's old book group let me tag along once and I never left.

Last week I said something to the effect of "fine, but the slow movement of the late Beethoven quartets gets me every time, whatever Tolstoy thinks of the Ninth," and the group's ringleader — a retired English teacher, very sharp, very fast — waved me off with 'art is infection'. As if that settled it. As if my failing to catch the infection were a fact about me and not about the music, or maybe a fact about neither. Then she used the same three words three more times inside ten minutes: once to explain why Tolstoy calls heaps of famous stuff bad, once to say sincerity is what really counts, once to explain why his own peasant stories are supposed to be great. Same phrase, four jobs. I'm a scientist — I want to know which claim is being asserted each time, because I suspect at least two of them are not the same claim at all.

So: act as an expert on Tolstoy's aesthetics and on philosophy of art generally. You obviously know the book, so don't waste my time summarizing its plot or its history, and please no bibliography or "further reading" tail — I can't chase references and don't want to.

Think carefully, and before the real answer spend a handful of lines brainstorming out loud about what work 'art is infection' could be doing in an argument — reporting a causal mechanism, defining a category, grading quality, passing moral judgment — then give me the menu.

For each candidate reading I want:

- the precise claim, stated the way you'd state a hypothesis in a paper: what's the subject, what's the predicate, is it a necessary condition, a sufficient one, a biconditional, a claim about degrees, or just a claim about what happens in people's heads?
- whether it's a platitude nobody sane denies, a substantive but defensible thesis, or something you think is flatly false — and if false, give me the counterexample you'd use;
- whether Tolstoy is actually committed to it, given the rest of what he does: the bit about art being one of the ways people get through to one another, like speech; his insistence that the maker has to have really felt the thing and then deliberately re-arouse it in himself and push it out through external signs; the three conditions he hangs infectiousness on, with sincerity doing the heavy lifting; and his splitting art into good and bad by *which* feelings get transmitted rather than by how skillfully they're transmitted;
- and above all, whether that reading earns her the dismissal. Does it show my quartets aren't art, or does it only show that on one particular occasion I wasn't moved, or does it show I was moved by the wrong feeling? Those are very different verdicts and she blurred them.

Finally, tell me which two readings you think she was sliding between without noticing, and whether there's a clean equivocation in there I could point out next Tuesday — politely, please, I still want to be invited.
