I write a fortnightly column on the behavioural sciences for a general-interest magazine, and in three weeks I am to lead a ninety-minute evening session for about thirty of our subscribers: intelligent, curious, no economics and no philosophy, and several of them have read my column on procrastination and consider the question closed. The subject is a pairing that is usually kept apart — akrasia, and in particular the ancient argument that acting against one's own better judgment is strictly impossible, on the one hand; hyperbolic discounting and the resulting reversal of preferences over time, on the other. I am not asking you to explain either to me. I want a plan for how to explain them together, in a room with a whiteboard and no slides.

Give me the order the components must go in, and for each one, what a listener would be unable to follow if it arrived later. My working hunch, which you should challenge if you think it wrong, is that the formal result has to be established first, so that the audience meets the Socratic denial already holding a case in which one and the same agent's ranking flips with no new information; the reverse order, I suspect, lets them hear the denial as a verbal trick and dismiss it. Tell me whether that is right, and what has to be true of the audience's understanding before the philosophical question can even be posed to them.

Second, the single example that should carry the session. I have been toying with the standard shape: a smaller amount available immediately against a larger amount available in a month, and then the identical pair pushed a year into the future, where the same respondents reverse. Tell me whether that will do, what an example of this kind must have and must lack for a room of non-specialists, and how to state the reversal in one sentence that forecloses the dismissals I expect — inflation, doubt that the larger payment will arrive, an implicit interest-rate calculation, or the claim that they were simply being impatient. And tell me why not Odysseus and the mast, which is where I would otherwise have begun.

Third, the misconception map: what they will arrive believing, what they will start believing partway through, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected afterwards. The one I most fear is that the binding problem — that the agent who makes the resolution and the agent who breaks it may not be the same agent — will strike them as an excuse for the second rather than as a puzzle about the first, and that the whole thing will land as self-help about willpower.

Fourth, what I should refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud: I assume the quasi-hyperbolic parameterisation, the taxonomy of naive and sophisticated agents, and anything about competing neural systems. Tell me if something else must be explicitly set aside. Finally, one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that would show whether the pairing has landed rather than merely amused them.
