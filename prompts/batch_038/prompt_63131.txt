So I'm on the faculty conduct appeals panel at a mid-size public university, and a case that looked routine has quietly turned into a legal-philosophy problem, which is not exactly what I signed up for when I let the dean's office talk me into two years of service. Short version of the facts: about two years ago, during a stretch when the president's office was being run by an interim team, the provost emailed department chairs something labeled an 'emergency directive' telling them to report any faculty member who had signed a public letter criticizing the state legislature's funding bill. It never made it into the faculty handbook, it went only to chairs, it reached back to signatures people had already put their names to weeks earlier, and it cuts directly against a standing academic-freedom policy that the handbook does contain. Our chair complied, sort of — she sat on it for three weeks and then reported one of the four signers, leaving out the other three, one of whom is me. I'm recusing myself from the vote but I'm still the person who has to draft the memo the panel circulates before we meet, so I need help thinking it through.

Two of the omitted signers have filed a complaint asking that the chair be censured for having reported anyone at all. Her position is that the directive was a rule of the institution, it was in force at the time, and following a rule in force is not misconduct — and separately, that she reported the bare minimum she could to keep the provost's office from circling the department for a year. The wrinkle that's giving me a headache: our own panel charter says we decide cases "in accordance with the policies in force at the time of the conduct in question," which sounds like it forces us to treat the directive as in force back then, which seems to swallow the whole dispute before we've even started. My partner, who is not an academic, keeps telling me I'm overthinking it and the panel should just say the directive was garbage and move on, and honestly half the panel will want to do exactly that. We meet the second Tuesday over Zoom and at least two members don't read the pre-circulated material.

Can you draft the decision memo I'd circulate — call it 700 to 900 words, American English, written for five smart people only two of whom have any philosophy training? I want it to land somewhere specific: censure, no censure, or censure on different grounds than the complainants asked for, plus a line on what, if anything, the panel should say about the directive itself rather than just about her. And I want the reasoning to actually engage the two questions separately — whether a defect like being unpublished, retroactive, contradictory with another standing policy, or impossible to follow alongside it goes to whether the thing was a rule at all versus only to whether it should have been obeyed; and whether someone who half-complies with a rule that gives her no discretion has followed the rule in any meaningful sense. Please give the chair's side its strongest version rather than a strawman, including the point that the person who wrote the directive isn't the one in front of us. Don't bother with citations or a lit review and don't try to quote anybody — I know the usual positivist/natural-law moves and the "so unjust it isn't law" formula well enough, I just need them applied to these facts. Oh, and there's a typo somewhere in the second paragraph, I'm not going back to find it.
