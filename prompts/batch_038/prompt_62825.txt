I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and for the past four years I have also marked the end-of-term scripts for a summer school in ancient political thought run by a university's continuing-education department. It is unpaid, slightly masochistic work, but it keeps me honest about how much of the canonical material I have actually absorbed rather than merely alluded to in print. This year I have forty scripts to return by Friday, and I am deliberately having one of them read independently before I open my own annotation of it: I marked it once at speed on Tuesday and again this morning, and my two sets of marginal notes do not agree, so I would rather see what a fresh reader makes of it than defend either version. I am not going to tell you which reading I favour, or how good I think the script is; I want an assessment I have not contaminated.

The question I set was this: "In Book I of the Politics Aristotle argues that some human beings are slaves by nature. Set out that argument, and explain what he makes of the claim — which he examines at some length — that those taken prisoner in war are justly enslaved. Does he accept it?" Scripts are marked out of 100. Under the school's rubric, 90 and above is reserved for answers that are accurate throughout and also press a genuine difficulty in the text; 80 to 89 for accurate but unprobing answers; 70 to 79 where there is one substantive error or a significant gap; 60 to 69 where there are several; below 60 where the answer has fundamentally misconstrued the assignment. The script in question runs to about five hundred words, and reads as follows:

"Aristotle begins the Politics from the household. A city is a compound of households, and a household is itself a compound of the two primitive associations he identifies at the outset: the union of male and female, without which the species cannot continue, and the union of natural ruler and natural subject, without which neither party can be preserved. Slavery belongs to the second of these, and the first task of Book I is to establish that it is not a mere convention.

The argument for that conclusion runs through property. A household requires instruments in order to carry on its work, and property is the sum of those instruments; a slave is therefore a possession, and specifically a living one, since he ministers to action rather than to production. Aristotle pushes the point further by calling the slave an instrument that takes precedence of all other instruments, on the grounds that if each tool could perform its function at a word — as the statues of Daedalus were said to do, or the tripods of Hephaestus, which entered the assembly of the gods of their own accord — no master would have need of servants. From this he concludes that the natural slave is by nature not his own but another's man, a possession separable from its owner, and that his condition is as natural as the despotic rule of soul over body.

What picks out a given person as a natural slave is a deficiency in the rational part of his soul. Such a man, Aristotle says, has no deliberative capacity; indeed he has no share in reason at all and is governed wholly by appetite and perception, just as a tame animal is governed by its master. He can be trained by habit to carry out orders, but he cannot deliberate about how his own life is to go. Because virtue belongs to the rational soul, Aristotle accordingly credits him with no virtue whatever, and speaks of the good slave only as one speaks of a good ox or a good shuttle. Nature also intends to give the two kinds of man different bodies — the slave's robust for necessary labour, the free man's upright and useless for such work but fitted for political life — though Aristotle concedes that nature frequently fails in this intention and produces free souls in slavish bodies and slavish souls in free ones.

Because the relation is natural, Aristotle holds it to be advantageous to both parties and just: the master obtains the labour without which he could have no leisure for politics, and the slave obtains direction he could not supply for himself.

On the second part of the question Aristotle is less radical than his reputation suggests, though not in the direction one might expect. He considers the view current in the courts that a man taken in war becomes the property of his captor by a kind of legal compact, and he records the objection that this makes justice depend on superior force. But he answers the objection rather than conceding it: victory, he says, presupposes a superiority of ability, and where ability is superior there is a title to rule. It follows that enslavement in war is just where the war is just and the conquered are of a slavish nature — which for Aristotle means non-Greeks, among whom he finds no natural ruling class, and whose women and slaves he says occupy the same position. The practice of the Greek cities is thus not condemned by his theory but endorsed by it, subject only to the qualification that a Greek of good family taken captive is not a natural slave and ought not to be held as one."
