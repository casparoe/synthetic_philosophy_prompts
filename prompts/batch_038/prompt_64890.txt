The question, as set:

> In seminar we distinguished three ways in which a speaker may bring it about that a hearer holds a false belief: (a) by asserting a content the speaker believes to be false; (b) by asserting a content the speaker believes to be true, in circumstances in which the speaker expects the hearer to infer from it a further content that the speaker believes to be false; and (c) by declining to assert anything at all. We labelled these assurance, cueing, and withholding.
>
> Consider the Week 5 case. A tribunal investigating an assault asks a witness, W, whether she saw the defendant strike the complainant. W did see the strike. She may answer in any of three ways: (a) "No, I saw nothing"; (b) "I saw the two of them arguing in the corridor beforehand"; (c) she says nothing, and, when pressed, says that she does not recall. Stipulate that in each of the three cases the tribunal ends up believing that W did not see the strike.
>
> Explain why it might be thought that (a) is wrong in a way that (b) is not. Then set out the strongest objection to that thought. Finally, state and defend your own view on whether the distinction between (a) and (b) is morally significant, keeping separate, so far as you can, four questions: whether the two differ in wrongness at all; whether they differ in the degree of wrongness each involves; whether they differ in the blameworthiness of the agent; and whether they differ in the hearer's standing to complain.

The submitted answer:

> The distinction between (a) and (b) is standardly defended by appeal to the thought that assertion carries a warrant. When W answers "No, I saw nothing," she puts her word behind a content: she invites the tribunal to take that content from her and makes herself answerable for it. When she answers "I saw them arguing in the corridor," she puts her word behind the argument and behind nothing else; the further content — that she saw no strike — is built by the tribunal out of material she supplied but did not vouch for. On this view the two answers differ not in what they achieve, since the tribunal is equally misled in both, but in what the speaker does. In (a) she undertakes something she cannot honour; in (b) she undertakes nothing false. So understood, the case for the asymmetry has nothing to do with consequences, and that is its great strength: it survives intact the stipulation that the two answers are matched for effect.
>
> One preliminary about the criterion. Whether W has lied depends on the truth of what she says rather than on what she believes. Had she not in fact seen the argument in the corridor, her answer in (b) would have been a lie in just the way her answer in (a) is; and had she been mistaken in thinking she saw the strike, her answer in (a) would have been no lie at all, but an honest error that happens to mislead the tribunal.
>
> The objection begins by asking why assurance should matter. It matters because a hearer is entitled to rely on the word of someone who controls the information he needs. But in (b) W controls the information just as completely as in (a). She knows what the tribunal will infer, she has chosen her words in order to produce that inference, and she could have added the single clause that would have prevented it. To say that she did not undertake the inference is to identify a technical feature of her sentence and then treat it as a moral alibi. What makes lying wrong is that it deceives; the aim, the foresight and the effect are the same in (a) and (b), so if deception is where the wrong lies, the wrong is the same, and the residue — a technicality about what is said rather than what is meant — cannot bear the weight placed on it. The same point can be made from inside the framework the seminar used: W's answer in (b) is true, relevant and unambiguous, and the implicature arises not from any defect in her utterance but from the tribunal's own presumption that she is being as informative as the question requires. On that account she has done nothing uncooperative, and the offending belief is generated by the tribunal's assumption rather than by any breach of hers.
>
> I find this objection decisive, and would add an argument of my own. A general permission to cue would be self-undermining. A tribunal cannot distinguish cueing from honest incompleteness, because the cueing witness's words are chosen precisely so as to be indistinguishable from those of a truthful witness who happens to have nothing to add. If cueing were generally permitted, testimony would be discounted across the board, and the practice of assurance that makes testimony worth having would be destroyed. Cueing is therefore wrong, and wrong for a reason that concerns the value of assertion rather than anything opposed to it.
>
> It follows that the dispute over the moral significance of the distinction is a dispute about degree. Both sides agree that W does wrong in (a), and once the self-undermining argument is on the table both sides must agree that she does wrong in (b). What remains is the question how much wrong she does, and questions of degree are not properly moral questions at all but administrative ones. The same goes for blameworthiness: W forms the same intention in either case, so she is equally blameworthy in both. As for standing, the tribunal's complaint is of the same kind in both cases but weaker in (b), since there the tribunal has supplied the final step of the inference itself, and a hearer who supplies that step has less ground to object to the resulting belief than one who was handed it outright. Because standing is weaker in (b), the wrongness is correspondingly less; the two questions come apart only in appearance.
>
> On withholding there is little to add. In (c) W says nothing, and the tribunal is again left to construct the belief from what it already has, so that the inference is wholly its own. Withholding is thus the limiting case of cueing: if cueing is wrong, withholding is wrong in the same way and for the same reason.
