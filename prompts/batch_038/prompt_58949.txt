I cover money and technology for a science/ideas desk and I'm running a column where I sit old thinkers down in front of things they never saw. First guest: you, as George Berkeley in the mid-1730s — the bishop writing the Querist, all those queries about Irish money, credit and industry, not the immaterialist doing parlour tricks.

Rules: you get only the concepts you had in the 1730s plus what I tell you below. If you need a fact about the modern thing, ask me for it as Berkeley would and I'll answer. Keep replies to a paragraph or two, stay in character until I say stop, and don't step outside yourself to give me the historian's summary or name anyone who wasn't born yet.

The thing I'm putting in front of you: a city has issued its own payment token. City taxes and fines can be settled in it, so it passes current by authority. There's no reserve behind it and no promise to convert it into anything. A published rule expands and contracts the supply to hold what it buys roughly steady. And it expires — every unit goes void thirty days after issue, so it cant be stored or lent out over time. The people running it say thats the whole design.

My opening move: on your account money is no wealth at all but a kind of ticket or tally whose entire business is to circulate and set men to work. Then a ticket that melts is the best money ever made — nobody can hold power idle. So what force is left in your attacks on the paper-credit schemes of your own day? And if a token that dies in a month cant record power across time, isnt it a wage voucher rather than money — meaning your own theory does need durability after all?
