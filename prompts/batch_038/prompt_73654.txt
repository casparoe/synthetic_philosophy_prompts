I've been putting together a session for the little reading group I help run — six of us, we meet in somebody's kitchen and always overrun by an hour — and the topic slid from Garrett Hardin's lifeboat-ethics essay straight into the 2001 Tampa affair, because one of us lived through that spring in Melbourne and hasn't stopped talking about it since. I want you to do the analysis properly rather than the summary: take the Tampa affair and run it through lifeboat ethics as a theory, in both directions. I know the outline of Hardin's argument, so please don't spend time re-explaining it except where a specific piece of machinery is doing work in the mapping — the spaceship-versus-lifeboat point about who gets to steer, carrying capacity and the safety factor, the claim that admitting the neediest first is the worst available policy, the ratchet effect whereby each round of relief increases the number of claimants, and benign demoralisation. What I'm after is whether that machinery actually fits this case, piece by piece, and where it grinds or falls silent.

The facts I want you to work from, so you're not guessing: in late August 2001 the Norwegian container ship MV Tampa, under Captain Arne Rinnan and with a crew of around twenty-seven, answered an Australian-coordinated distress call and took 433 people — mostly Hazara Afghans — off a sinking fishing boat in international waters north of Christmas Island. The ship was bound for Indonesia, and the nearest suitable port was Indonesian, but a delegation from the rescued people went to the bridge and pressed to be taken to Christmas Island instead; Rinnan altered course. Canberra refused permission to enter Australian waters, and when the Tampa went in anyway, special forces boarded her. The government rushed through a border protection bill, excised Christmas Island and other offshore territories from the migration zone, and shipped the rescuees to Nauru and New Zealand for processing — the start of what became the Pacific Solution. From memory a federal court judge initially found the detention of the rescued people unlawful and the government appealed and won, but treat that detail as soft. It was all about two and a half weeks before the 11th of September; the coalition won the November election campaigning on it; in October ministers claimed asylum seekers had thrown children overboard, which a Senate inquiry later found unsupported; and on the 19th of October a boat designated SIEV X sank south of Java with 353 dead. For scale, arrivals by boat that year were a few thousand against a population of about nineteen million and a humanitarian programme of something over ten thousand places a year.

Here's the scaffolding I'd like you to use, because I think the usual treatment is too vague to be interesting. First, treat the lifeboat as a parameterised model and fill in the slots: how big is the boat, what's the safety factor, how many swimmers, do the occupants owe the swimmers anything beforehand, is there another boat with a duty to take them, and does the person who says no bear the cost of saying no? Then tell me which slots the Tampa actually populated and what happens to the argument when a slot is filled differently. Second, I want you to test a distinction I've been mulling: Hardin's ratchet is about aid replenishing demand, but what Australia did in 2001 looks like the mirror image — using the refusal itself as a signal to stop future attempts, i.e. treating capacity as a transmitter rather than a container. Is deterrent refusal still lifeboat ethics, or a different argument wearing lifeboat clothing? Third, notice that Hardin's decision is about admission at the gunwale, whereas the Tampa's hard decision was about disembarkation after the rescue had already happened and capacity had already been blown through in one irreversible go — does the theory have anything at all to say about the post-rescue stage, and if it doesn't, was it ever meant for cases like this, given that his actual target was food aid to poor countries rather than refugees? Fourth, who was the captain? Hardin's complaint about the commons is that nobody is at the helm, but here you had a Norwegian master under a maritime duty to deliver people to a place of safety, a coastal state that had coordinated the distress call, a nearest-port state that wouldn't take them, and a refugee agency with no ships. Say whether the model survives a lifeboat with four candidate captains and no owner of the decision. And fifth, the swimmers in Hardin are passive; these 433 negotiated, threatened, and had preferences about where they went — what does the theory do with that, and where does SIEV X sit in it, since a death toll among the deterred is a cost the original model has no line item for? End by turning it round: does the episode confirm lifeboat ethics, expose an anomaly it can absorb, or show that it was never really about cases like this — and would the excision of territory from the migration zone count, on Hardin's own terms, as a triumph of realism or as a redrawing of the lifeboat's outline so that the swimmers are simply not in the water you're responsible for? Please also tell me what would change your verdict here: which consideration or piece of evidence would make you say the theory does fit after all, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail, for instance if you're just doing motivated reasoning against Hardin because he's politically repellent. Write it as continuous prose, in British English, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables — like a long letter to a friend, not a briefing note.
