I'm in a seminar this term called something like "Markets and Their Critics" — half philosophy grad students, half econ — and we just spent four weeks on the socialist calculation debate. Honestly the thing that struck me is how little the room moved. Everyone had read the same material: Mises's 1920 argument that if the state owns the means of production there are no genuine exchange prices for capital goods, so there's nothing to calculate with at all; Lange's reply in the 30s that a planning board can run trial-and-error on parametric prices, tell managers to follow marginal-cost rules, and reproduce the Walrasian outcome while fixing distribution as a bonus; then Hayek's reframing, where the issue isn't solvability but dispersed and largely tacit knowledge, with the market as a discovery process rather than an allocation device. Plus the modern round: Cockshott and Cottrell's labor-time / linear-programming proposal, and the "but Walmart and Amazon are giant internally planned economies" move. Both camps in our section think the debate was settled decades ago in their favor, and neither can point to a single moment where the other side conceded anything.

My working hypothesis — tell me if it's right or just too clever by half — is that "the calculation problem" is really three different questions wearing one name, and people keep answering one while being accused of dodging another. Roughly: (1) given the data, is an efficient allocation formally computable; (2) could the data — preferences, production possibilities, local conditions of time and place — ever actually be assembled by anyone; (3) could the system track changes nobody anticipated, i.e. adapt over time rather than solve once. Lange and the computational people live on (1) and wave at (2), the Hayekians insist (2) and (3) are the only real questions and treat (1) as a red herring, and Mises's original claim sounds like (1) but carries the rhetorical force of (3). You can watch it happen in class: whenever someone brings up the Cockshott thing, the Hayekians say it assumes you already have the technical coefficients, which is precisely the difficulty, and the planners say fine, but then you've quietly abandoned Mises's impossibility claim for something vaguer that never touches Lange. Each side accuses the other of moving the goalposts, and the argument collapses into a fight over who gets to define what the original question was. If that's the structure, then neither side is ever denying the same proposition, which would explain why nobody budges.

So: what kind of disagreement is this actually? Is it verbal — a fight over "rational," "calculation," "planning," "market" — or substantive but about standards, where one side counts a formal model as a refutation and the other counts only institutional and historical process? Is there a hidden empirical question underneath that could in principle be settled (how much of an economy's relevant knowledge is codifiable, how well computation can substitute for price signals, what the Soviet record or a firm of Walmart's scale actually shows)? Or is the deeper split that each side has a different picture of what an economy *is* — a system to be solved versus a process to be participated in — so that no shared result could ever be decisive? I want the diagnosis argued for from what each side actually says, not a rerun of the history we've already read, and then an honest verdict on what would count as progress: a distinction that seperates the questions cleanly, a formal result, a real experiment at scale, a change of question, or a case that this is a permanent impasse and why. And please also tell me where my three-question reconstruction is itself tendentious — I suspect I've smuggled in something that quietly favors one side. No reading list, I've got the syllabus; I just want the structure of the dispute.
