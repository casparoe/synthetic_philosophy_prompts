I'm writing the final for a PhD seminar on multi-agent coordination. Two weeks of it were on safe Pareto improvements — the idea that two agents headed for a bad non-cooperative outcome can each hand their move over to a program that conditions on what it can predict about the other, so they end up better off, with the extra requirement (that's the "safe" part) that no agent can be left worse off than it would have been in the default, whatever the other one does and whatever the predictors get wrong.

I want ONE question, 60 minutes, 25 marks, that nobody gets anywhere on by reciting that definition back at me. Build it on this setup, and please use these exact numbers:

Two agents, each picks Share or Withhold. Payoffs (row, column): Share/Share (5,5); Share/Withhold (1,7); Withhold/Share (7,1); Withhold/Withhold (3,3). Withhold dominates, so the default is (3,3). Either agent may instead delegate to a program P. P receives a binary signal about whether the *other* agent delegated, correct with probability 0.9, errors independant across the two of them; P plays Share if the signal says the other delegated and Withhold otherwise. Take as given that when both delegate, each ends up playing Share with probability 0.9.

The question has to make them do three things. First, compute what a delegator gets when the other delegates and when the other doesn't, and say which requirement of the definition each of those two numbers offends. Second, get them to prove something stronger — there's a clean impossibility sitting in this prediction technology and I want them to find it rather than be handed it — about whether any program that ever plays Share can be safe here, and then say what that tells you about what the prediction has to be a prediction *of*. Third, once they've reached for the obvious repair (something like a joint contract both sides are bound by, so participation stops being a thing you have to guess at), make them explain why letting each side's program hold out for a bigger slice of the surplus drops you straight back into a race to commit first, and what property a splitting rule would need to avoid that.

Give me four sections, in this order:

- "The question" — as it would actually appear on the paper, parts labelled with their mark values, roughly 250 words
- "Model answer" — around 900 words, with the arithmetic shown rather than asserted
- "Marking scheme" — a table of named moves and the marks for each, adding up to 25; I'd like the last two or three marks reserved for a generalisation, e.g. what happens to the argument as the signal gets sharper, or what you can buy if you relax safety to permit a bounded loss
- "Where the A/B line is" — about 200 words on the mistakes that cap a competent-but-unspectacular answer. One of them had better be treating a positive expected surplus as if that were enough.
