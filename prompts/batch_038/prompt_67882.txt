Our trading desk and my graduate reading group have spent three weeks arguing about the same criterion and, as far as I can tell, they are using it in two incompatible ways. I want the tension laid out and resolved.

First the practice. Essentially nobody who sizes stakes by the Kelly rule stakes the full Kelly amount. Among sports bettors, card-counting teams, and the quant funds that discuss position sizing openly, the convention is to stake half the indicated amount, sometimes a quarter, and to describe full Kelly as an ideal one may approach only if the edge is known with a precision no working bettor has. Concretely: for an even-money bet you judge wins with probability p, the Kelly stake is the fraction 2p − 1 of bankroll; house style is to stake half that. By my colleague's reckoning this buys roughly three-quarters of the asymptotic growth rate for roughly a quarter of the variance in log wealth. The defense practitioners give is that p is estimated from limited data, that the stake computed from a noisy estimate is biased high, and that drawdowns have consequences growth rates don't capture: margin calls, investors who withdraw, a career that ends before the long run arrives.

Second, the argument. A school of economists insists the Kelly criterion is not expected-utility theory with a logarithm smuggled into the utility slot. Their claim is that no taste parameter is involved anywhere. Wealth evolves multiplicatively; multiplicative dynamics are non-ergodic; so the average across an imagined ensemble of parallel runs of a gamble comes apart from the average along the one run a given agent actually lives. Their standard illustration: a coin flip that multiplies your bankroll by 1.5 or by 0.6 has an expected multiplier of 1.05, which expected-value reasoning tells you to accept, yet repeated it shrinks the typical path by roughly 5% per round and drives it to zero. Only the time average describes what happens to you. On this view, the critic who says "Kelly simply assumes log utility" has misread the result, and an agent who knowingly stakes below the growth-optimal fraction has not adopted a more cautious taste — she has chosen a path that almost surely ends worse. One member of my group adds that the criterion was originally derived as a statement about the rate at which a bettor using a noisy channel can transmit information, and that nothing in that derivation looks like a claim about what anyone ought to stake; I don't know what to do with that remark either.

Here is my problem. If the growth argument is correct as an objective claim about single agents on single paths, then the desk's practice is not prudence but arithmetic error, and it is being committed by exactly the people who survive. If instead the practice is defensible, something in the argument's claimed objectivity has to give — and yet the practitioners' own main defense, that the estimate of p is noisy, sounds like a growth-based defense rather than a rival to it.

So: reconstruct the theory of rational stake-setting that the practice of fractional staking actually presupposes. Tell me which rival accounts it rules out — maximizing expected wealth, expected utility with a utility more concave than logarithmic, keeping the probability of ruin below a threshold, maximizing the chance of hitting a target by a deadline — and which of those it is merely compatible with while not requiring. Say where the practice and the growth argument can be reconciled and where they cannot, and whether the desk's appeal to estimation error does the reconciling work or just relocates the problem. Then tell me what would have to change at the desk, in its mandate or in how it defines its own bankroll, if the growth argument were simply right.

I want a verdict rather than a map. Take a definite position on both of these: whether the time-average growth rate is an objective constraint on rational staking independent of the agent's tastes or a redescription of a taste parameter, and whether fractional staking is a coherent application of the growth criterion or a quiet abandonment of it. Give me your reasons and say what your position costs you.
