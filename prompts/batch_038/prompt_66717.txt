You're going to play the part of a literary SF writer who also has a genuine grip on statistical mechanics and the foundations of cosmology — not a physicist dabbiling in fiction, someone who cares about the rhythm of a sentence as much as about whether the argument actually holds up.

I edit a small speculative-fiction zine and I want a short story, somewhere between 1,200 and 1,800 words, built out of the Boltzmann brain problem. But not the version everyone writes. The usual version is: character wakes up in the void, discovers their whole life was a thermal fluctuation, cosmic horror, cut to black. I've read that one a hundred times.

The angle I want is the one almost nobody uses, which is the *normal* Boltzmann brain. If a brain can pop into a heat-dead vacuum fully formed with a set of implanted false memories, then it can equally well be assembled by a long sequence of smaller fluctuations that trace its own decay path backwards — which means that one ages, lays down memories in real time, and every single one of those memories is an accurate record of something that genuinely happened. From the inside there is no distinguishing that creature from an ordinary evolved person. So the reductio doesn't land as a horrifying discovery about one unlucky individual; it lands as the flat observation that "you might be a fluctuation" carries no usable information at all, which I think is worse and much harder to dramatize. That difficulty is the story.

Requirements:

- First person, present tense.
- The narrator has run into the argument and taken it seriously, and is now trying to get through an ordinary week while holding it. Give them a concrete mundane problem — a form to file, a sibling's funeral to arrange, a broken appliance, whatever — and let the metaphysics leak into how they handle that problem rather than replace it.
- Register should be dry, slightly bureaucratic, faintly funny. No awe, no stargazing, no passages about the vastness of eternity.
- The phrase "Boltzmann brain" must not appear anywhere in the story. No named physicists, no quoted papers, no equations.
- One perfectly ordinary object should recur at least three times and mean something different each time.
- I'd like the history present as texture, not as a lecture: this isn't really a multiverse-era puzzle, it grows out of the recurrence objection and Boltzmann's reply to it, the "we happen to be inside a fluctuation" move is usually credited to his assistant Schütz rather than to Boltzmann himself, and it only becomes a story about single brains once someone points out in the 1930s that small fluctuations are exponentially more common than large ones. Let a character be irritated about how all this gets misattributed, or find some other way to work it in.
- End with the narrator doing something completely ordinary, and don't resolve the argument.

Think this through carefully before you start writing: work out privately which pieces of the physics the story can lean on and which it has to leave alone, what the narrator wants in each scene, and what's blocking them. Don't show me any of that — no outline, no notes, no brainstorming in the open. Begin the response with the title and go straight into the story, with no preface and no restating of what I asked for. Nothing after the final line either: no author's note, no afterwords explaining the physics or your choices. Just the piece.
