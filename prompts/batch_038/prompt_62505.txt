So me and two friends have been going around in circles for about a month now on when it's okay to break the law, and I've finally admitted that I don't have a position, just a pile of knee-jerk reactions. Here they are, in the order I wrote them down. Running a red light at 3am on an empty country road: completely fine, honestly not even a moral question, I'd find it bizarre if someone agonized over it. Underreporting a few hundred dollars of cash tips each year: not fine, and it feels specifically like free-riding on everyone who does pay. A group chaining themselves to the gate of a port to block gas exports, announcing the action three weeks in advance, being scrupulously nonviolent, and staying put to be arrested: fine, maybe admirable. The same cause and the same people, but a night action instead — masked, glued to the road, a digger's hydraulics drained, everyone gone before the police arrive: not fine. A nurse who backdates a form so an uninsured patient gets a scan: fine. A county clerk who refuses to issue marriage licenses to same-sex couples, does it loudly and publicly, and goes to jail cheerfully: not fine — and I genuinely cannot explain why, since on the formal features it looks like the port case. A nineteen-year-old who leaves the country rather than be drafted into a war he's convinced is aggressive, and never comes back: fine. A contractor who leaks documents showing war crimes and then stays abroad to dodge prosecution: fine. But note the clerk was willing to rot in jail and I said no, and the leaker refuses to face any of it and I said yes, so whatever my "you have to accept the punishment" idea is doing, it isn't what I thought. A juror who acquits a guy he believes did it because he thinks the statute is monstrous, and never tells anyone why: fine, though I'm uneasy in a way I'm not about the nurse. And squatting an empty tower owned by an investment fund and moving eleven homeless families into it: fine, but somehow only for a while — I have no idea where it stops.

My draft principle, which I already suspect is bad, is roughly: it's okay when the rule you're breaking is itself the thing doing the injustice, and when you're not making uninvolved people pay for your conscience. It seems to break immediately on the red light and the tips, which aren't about injustice at all, and I can't tell whether it gets the clerk right for the right reason or just by accident. I've also read the standard account somewhere — civil disobedience has to be public, nonviolent, political, aimed at the majority's sense of justice, and done with a willingness to take the legal consequences — and I'd like to know how much weight that checklist should carry given what I just told you, because my clerk verdict seems to run right through it. So: what principle, or small set of them, actually delivers my verdicts, and state it precisely enough that I could apply it to something new. Tell me what it says about a case I didn't list — an engineer who's ordered to ship a feature she's sure will hurt people, who quietly disables it before release, says nothing at the all-hands, and lets the schedule slip take the blame. And tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate.

Don't rescue everything. I know it's tempting to write some baroque three-clause account that saves all ten of my reactions, and if you genuinely think that's the right answer then say so and defend the complexity — but if two or three of my verdicts are just wrong, name them and say why. Then give me the flip side: what would change your mind here? Which of my cases, described slightly differently, would make you reverse your own verdict, and where do you think your reasoning is most likely to be failing — is it in how you're weighing the publicity and penalty-acceptance conditions, or in assuming my "free-riding" intuition about taxes generalizes to a duty to obey the law at all?
