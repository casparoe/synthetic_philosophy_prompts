I run a small reading group on classical Indian epistemology at my university -- six or seven people, mostly undergrads, a couple of grad students, nobody reads Sanskrit including me. Next week we're on the pramāṇa-counting debate, and I can already tell how it's going to go: half the room will announce that they're "basically a Cārvāka" or "basically a Mīmāṃsaka" because they half-remember the labels from a wikipedia skim, and then we'll spend an hour arguing about names instead of about cases. So I want to build a "which theory of the pramāṇas do you actually hold?" quiz that sorts people by what they say about situations, not by what they call themselves. Seven questions, each one a concrete little vignette with two or three answer options, all in plain English. Hard rule: no Sanskrit technical terms anywhere in the question stems or the options (they go only in the scoring key), and no question is allowed to just ask something like "do you think testimony is a source of knowledge?" or "do you believe in knowledge by comparison?" -- that's the whole point of the exercise, the reader shouldn't be able to see which option leads where.

Take the stock cases and re-skin them so a 20-year-old recognizes them rather than smelling a textbook: the roommate who supposedly never eats at home yet keeps gaining weight; the animal you've only ever had described to you as cow-like and then actually meet in a forest; the keys you're certain aren't on the table because you looked and the table was bare; the stranger on a train who tells you where the hostel is. Some specific things I need in there: one question probing whether perception comes already conceptually dressed (does someone who's never learned the word see the thing *as* that kind of thing, or just see it?); one about whether a belief you got from an apparently competent speaker is warranted by default until something undermines it, versus having to earn its warrant somehow -- I think the Mīmāṃsakas say default and the Naiyāyikas say earned, but tell me if I've got that backwards, and build the question so it doesn't depend on my having it right; one about whether an absence or a not-being-there is the sort of thing you can come to know in its own right; and one that quietly probes the counting principle itself -- whether two ways of knowing count as distinct because they're different kinds of cognitive episode, or because they latch onto different kinds of object -- without ever telling the reader there are two competing principles at work.

For the deliverable, do the logical space before the names. Give me the scoring key as a mapping from answer patterns onto positions: the one-source materialist, the Buddhist two-pramāṇa view, Nyāya's four, the five-source Prabhākara line, the six-source Kumārila/Advaita line, a reductionist leaf for someone who thinks the extras are all just inference wearing a costume, and -- if you can make it work -- a Jain leaf, since that tradition sorts kinds of knowing by the capacities of the knower (including some that involve no sense organ at all) rather than by the situation; if it doesn't fit the shape of my tree, say so rather than forcing it. Then tell me about the empty regions: seven questions at two or three options each is hundreds or thousands of possible patterns, and almost none of them correspond to any historical position, so pick out the most interesting unoccupied cells and diagnose each one -- is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because it's genuinely unexplored and maybe worth exploring? Also flag honestly any pair of leaves my instrument can't actually seperate (I suspect a Naiyāyika and a Kumārila Mīmāṃsaka will answer most of my cases identically), and say what extra question would pull them apart, or admit that it can't be done at the level of cases. Finally, rank the seven questions by how much sorting work each one does, call out any that are near-degenerate where everyone will pick the same option, and each leaf should end with one plain-English "you are here" sentence about its most awkward commitment. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations or page numbers -- position names in the key are all I want. For calibration, my own provisional answers would be: testimony definitely counts, postulation cases are really just inference in disguise, and I don't believe a bare absence is its own way of knowing. Tell me which leaf that lands me on and whether it's even a coherent pattern.
