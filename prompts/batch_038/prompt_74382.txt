I teach a required sixty-minute bioethics session to roughly forty second-year medical students, seated at eight tables of five. None of them has had philosophy, and one or two will have watched a grandparent decline through dementia; I want the session to work for those students without ever asking anyone to disclose anything. Please design a tabletop activity, with a debrief, that makes them feel the problem of an advance directive's authority over a later self: the situation in which the person who wrote the directive and the person it would govern have diverged enough that honoring it begins to look like one person sacrificing another.

The activity must not be introduced as being about advance directives, autonomy, or dementia. Those words, and the distinction between what a patient once wanted and what is good for her now, should surface only in the debrief.

Five design constraints:

1. The change in the later self must be real rather than performed. Do not ask students to role-play cognitive impairment, and do not hand out cards instructing someone to want something different. Build in a manipulation that produces a genuine reversal in what participants care about during the hour — an acquired taste, an endowment that comes to be valued, an attachment to their table — so that preferences written down in the first ten minutes conflict with preferences actually held in the last twenty. Tell me exactly what the manipulation is, why it should fire inside an hour, and what the facilitator does at the tables where it doesn't.

2. Psychological continuity should be a game variable. Give each player a continuity score that moves across rounds, and have students set, before they see any case, a threshold below which a directive stops binding. Then arrange rounds that force them to discover that the threshold they set does no real work at the margin.

3. Someone must decide whom the points belong to. Design the scoring so that honoring a directive and overriding it each impose costs on identifiable parties, with stakes that are real in the room but cost me nothing — control over the rest of the session, information about later rounds, exemption from a task. Then state which of two architectures you chose and defend the choice: a single continuing account belonging to the person, or separate accounts for the earlier and later self. That decision quietly presupposes an answer to the identity question, so give me the alternative as a variant and a sentence on what each presupposes.

4. Include a surrogate. In at least one round the decision must be made by a third party at the table who can see the directive and the later self's behavior but not her stated reasons, and who is not told whether she counts as capable of deciding for herself.

5. Include an amendment round in which later selves may revise their directives for a round still to come, and force the class to settle which directive governs when the two conflict: the one written with better information, or the most recent one.

Deliver: the opening script students actually hear; roles, printable materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline for sixty minutes; the scoring rules; a tabulation step at the end, saying what pattern you expect the class's own decisions to show and what it would mean if it doesn't; and a debrief that maps each mechanic onto the conceptual distinction it embodies — including why the earlier self is supposed to have authority at all, what happens to that rationale as continuity falls, and the contrast between the interest a person takes in the shape of her life as a whole and the interest she takes in how her days feel from the inside. Name the technical vocabulary only at that point.

Close with two honest notes. First, what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run: how a competitive table turns it into a game to be won rather than a case to be felt, where the rules will be exploited, and how to handle the student for whom the topic is a memory rather than a puzzle. Second, this design leaves the later self fully capable of deciding for herself, and stakes snacks and minutes rather than ventilation. Say plainly what that substitution buys, which feature of the real problem it cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply by hand, and how you would bridge to the clinical case in the final five minutes without lecturing the whole thing back in.
