I teach a single seventy-five-minute session in a first-year interdisciplinary seminar at a small college — a required course with no prerequisites, built around case studies in prediction, with a different faculty member taking each week. My students are mostly intending majors in business, public policy, and environmental science; three of them have had a semester of high-school philosophy and none has had any philosophy of science. They come in having read about forty pages of Malthus's 1798 essay on population, including the doubling-every-twenty-five-years argument and the contrast between population's geometric increase and food's arithmetic one, plus a modern chapter arguing that he was simply refuted by events: the famines did not arrive on schedule, yields multiplied several-fold, and the birth rates of rich countries fell as incomes rose rather than climbing with them. So they arrive already settled. In my colleague's version of this week the verdict was Malthus was wrong, and what the students have taken from it is the general lesson that long-horizon catastrophe forecasts are worthless and that anyone who issues one is either a crank or selling something. I have one whiteboard, no slides, and I have to dislodge that lesson without, in the same breath, rehabilitating Malthus.

My assigned concept is not Malthus himself but a piece of methodology: whether a theory can be right but early, and what would have to be true for postponing a failed long-run prediction to count as legitimate revision rather than as rescue. Plan the session for me; I do not want the concept explained to me, I want the teaching plan. Specifically: lay out the order in which the components have to be introduced and why that order and not another, saying explicitly what depends on what — my own guess is that they cannot understand what a rescue is until they can see the difference between a dated prediction and a standing tendency claim, and that they cannot see that difference until they have been made to notice that Malthus's own claim was conditional on the checks that hold population down, checks he revised between the first and later editions. Then tell me which single case should carry the whole thing, and what that case has to have and has to lack. I am doubtful about opening with the two-ratios arithmetic, because it invites them to grade Malthus's numbers rather than examine the structure of his claim; if you think it belongs anyway, tell me where and what job it is doing. I also want the case to let them see that right but early may be the wrong label altogether — that there are at least three distinct things that get collapsed into it: the mechanism was right and the parameters changed; the mechanism was right and the offsetting cause was real but unanticipated; and the prediction was never dated in the first place, so nothing could refute it.

Give me the misconception map with placement, not just a list. My predictions about what they will say: that Malthus was wrong because technology happened, which treats a background assumption as a failed forecast; that wrong once means wrong forever, so any surviving use of his mechanism is nostalgia; that right but early is an all-purpose shield, which is half-right and needs to be sharpened rather than dismissed; and, from the business majors especially, that a conditional theory is unfalsifiable and therefore empty, which slides from a fair complaint about vagueness into throwing out everything with an auxiliary assumption attached to it. For each, tell me the point in the sequence where it must be headed off, and what to say at that point, because a couple of them will be fatal if they surface after I have already built on the opposite assumption. There is also a further difficulty I want your judgment on: I suspect the phrase right but early smuggles in an unnoticed asymmetry — a theory whose catastrophe is undated cannot be refuted by the catastrophe's non-arrival, but it cannot be confirmed by its eventual arrival either, because by then enough auxiliary assumptions will have been adjusted that no credit can be assigned to the original claim. Decide whether that belongs in a first-year session at all, and if so, exactly where. Relatedly, a demographer colleague on the course committee insists I bring in Boserup's counter-thesis from the 1960s, that population pressure is itself what drives agricultural intensification rather than being its victim, so that the causal arrow runs the other way; tell me whether that is a genuine asset here or a second theory they will fail to keep separate from the first.

Finish with the parts people usually forget. Tell me what I should leave out at this level and say so out loud to the class — I am thinking of the full apparatus of research programmes and their progressive versus degenerating variants, the demography of the fertility transition, and the general point that a single theory is never tested in isolation, all of which I can gesture at but not teach in seventy-five minutes. And give me a closing check for understanding that is a transfer rather than a recap: one short non-population scenario, with no obvious right answer, in which they have to classify a deferral as revision or as rescue and defend the classification, plus what the different wrong answers to it would reveal about which piece of the sequence failed to land. Please do not recommend readings, sources, or further material for me or for them; I need the plan itself, timed to the whiteboard, with the reasoning for the ordering visible enough that I can adjust it if the room goes a different way.
