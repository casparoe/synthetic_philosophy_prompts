I am a junior, and with two classmates I run a student-led history seminar at my school. This term the group is eighteen sophomores and juniors who have completed a year of modern world history, and we meet once a week for fifty minutes. Next week I want to run a session on the dispute between explanations of history that credit particular individuals and explanations that credit underlying conditions, using the question of whether there would have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler. I do not want to open with any of that framing. I want the students to run into the difficulty themselves and be told only afterwards what it is they have hit.

Please design the activity. Constraints: nothing may require more than a school copier and a whiteboard; all eighteen students must be making recorded commitments throughout rather than spectating; and the mechanism must be arranged so that their own answers shift under pressure they did not notice being applied. My instinct is that the pressure should come from two directions — quietly changing what is being held fixed in the counterfactual, and quietly changing how the outcome itself is described — and that each student should be handed back a written record of their earlier verdicts at the moment their view changes, so that the drift is theirs to see rather than mine to point out. Work the mechanism out yourself, though; treat my suggestion as one option among several.

Give me a minute-by-minute timeline; the rules and the exact materials, including what each student is holding and what goes on the board; any scoring, if you use it; and a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto the two explanatory stances and shows each stance where it has to give ground. Then tell me two or three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with teenagers who enjoy arguing, and what to do about each; how to keep the session from turning into a game about the Holocaust, given that the outcome in question leads there; how to stop the students from landing on "the question is meaningless," which is not where I want them; and, honestly, which feature of the debate an activity like this cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself.

Keep the historical content to what a well-read high school student would already know — the activity must not depend on facts we would have to look up. Please write in American English.
