I'm one of seven on my state's sentence-review panel — the new law lets us reopen sentences after twenty years — and next Thursday we're hearing a case that has us split 3-3 with me in the middle, so whatever I circulate Monday is basically going to decide it.

The inmate was 19 when he and a cousin robbed a pharmacy and the clerk ended up paralyzed from the chest down. He's 44 now. Every thing you'd want is in the file: degree finished inside, a peer hospice program he built from nothing, a written account of the robbery that our chaplain calls the most honest document she's read in that job. But there's also an incident in year six — he broke another inmate's jaw over something small — and then nothing after, for eighteen years. So it isn't a clean arc of transformation, and both sides of my panel just point at whichever half of the record they like.

I've sat in a Theravada vipassana tradition for twenty-some years, and the way I actually think about this case runs through anatta — no fixed self, persons as processes rather than substances. My hunch is that this gets me somewhere the usual he's-a-changed-man plea doesn't: if identity is a causal stream that comes in degrees, the question isn't whether he's the same person (nobody is) but how much of what the sentence was *for* still has a target — who exactly is left to incapacitate, who's left to deter, and whether retribution can attach to a process whose early and late stretches are barely hooked up to each other. The simile my teacher always used, from that old dialogue between the monk and the Greek king, is the lamp burning all night: the flame at dawn isn't the flame at dusk, and yet it's one lamp, and it's the lamp you'd say burned.

But I don't want the memo to be a metaphysics lecture, and I don't want it to sound religious. Two colleagues — a former ADA and a forensic psychologist — will read anything doctrinal as special pleading, and the attorney generals office has already hinted that a release recommendation resting on a contested theory of personal identity invites a challenge. So draft the memo I'll circulate. It needs to (1) come down somewhere — release, release with conditions, defer, or deny — with dates and specifics the panel can vote on; (2) convert the no-self material into criteria the whole panel could apply without signing up for the metaphysics; and (3) take the losing side's best point seriously instead of strawmanning it.

That best point is the one I'm worst at answering, so don't soften it for me. The victim's sister filed a statement arguing that if our logic is that there was never really a self who did this, then there was never really a person it was done to either — you can't dissolve the offender without dissolving the harm, and she says we'd be releasing him on an argument that also erases her family's claim. And then there's the inmate himself: he wrote to me. He took up a practice inside, does a repentance liturgy every morning, and he says he doesn't want out on the grounds I'm considering using, because the practice only works if he's the one who did it and stays answerable for it. Being told you aren't a self, when you're trying to make amends, he says, sounds like being let off. If I recommend release on connectedness grounds I might be doing it *to* him rather than for him.

Also flag where the degrees move backfires, because I can hear the ADA already: if responsibility tracks connectedness, we've quietly invented a sliding scale of punishment that no statute authorizes, and every future petitioner will go shopping for whichever metaphysics gets them out. Tell me what to concede there and what to hold. And the memo has to deal with the year-six assault head on — a framework that only recognizes one-directional change can't explain why that shouldn't be the whole story, and I'd rather we say so ourselves than have it said at us.

Two to three pages, plain prose, no footnotes and no name-dropping philosophers. I want the reasoning, not a bibliography.
