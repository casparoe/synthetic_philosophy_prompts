So I volunteer on a claims-review panel at a small science-communication charity — journalists send us reports of apparently miraculous stuff that's getting traction in the press and we publish a short verdict. I'm trying to write up how we actually decide, because we've done about a dozen of these now and I don't think we're consistent, and I'd like to predict where we land on the next one before we meet rather than after.

Our record so far:

- A woman who reportedly came back after being declared dead for 40-odd minutes, with a dozen nurses saying they watched it happen. Not established. Our write-up leaned on the notes being unclear about whether she had really died, and on the line that you'd only accept it if all those witnesses being wrong or lying would itself be stranger than the event.
- A statue weeping an oily fluid, attested by hundreds, including a priest who'd trained as a chemist. Not established. We said the numbers and the apparent honesty don't help much when nobody thought to check for seepage and condensation, and that the earliest reports came from people already expecting a sign.
- A single witness, no motive anyone could find, statement given under oath and never varied. Not established — one person is never enough for a breach of the ordinary course of nature, however good they seem.
- Thirty independent witnesses, no obvious motive, and no natural explanation anyone on the panel could name. Split vote, majority not established, on the grounds that the reports only surfaced years afterwards and in a community where that sort of story was already currency.
- Two rival shrines each claiming a healing which, if real, would vindicate their own doctrine and embarrass the other's. We said both fail: they cancel each other out.
- A faith healing that turned out to be partial and slow, tracking roughly what remission looks like anyway. Not established, uncontroversially.
- A lottery winner and a "prophetic" dream that matched a later accident, both written up in a local paper. We said those are fine — wierd, improbable, but nothing in them breaks a law, so accept and move on.

The one landing next week: a patient with a condition the literature treats as irreversible makes a complete and lasting recovery. Imaging pins the turnaround to a matter of hours. It was documented contemporaneously by the hospital team, including two doctors on record as hostile to any supernatural reading — one of them published an attempt at a natural explanation and publicly withdrew it a month later. The patient isn't religious, gets nothing out of it, and there's no pilgrimage, no offering, no book deal. It was published within days, not years. And, annoyingly, a rival shrine is claiming an exactly parallel case from the same month which they're using to argue the opposite doctrine, so our they-cancel precedent is squarely in play.

One of us insists we're just running Hume on miracles and we should own it; another says Hume's first argument is about what testimony could in principle ever do and his second is about how testimony actually goes in practice, and that our verdicts only ever invoke the second. I honestly can't tell which we've been doing.

Could you (a) work out which rule or rules are jointly consistent with all seven precedents — including ones that look identical on the old cases but are really different theories; (b) say what each of them implies for the new case, and give me your best guess at our verdict along with the reasoning the majority would most plausibly write; (c) tell me which single precedent in our record does the most work in seperating the candidate rules, and whether it really does separate them or just looks like it does; and (d) propose one further hypothetical case we could put to the panel that would force the rules apart, so we can finally find out what we believe. Also flag anywhere our track record is flatly inconsistent with any Humean reading, since if we aren't Humeans we should stop implying we are.

British English, please. And no references or reading list — I want the reasoning, not the citations.
