I work on the policy and partnerships side of a global-health charity, and we have been offered what my director insists on describing as a straightforward trade. A large farm-animal welfare organization will fund two of our country programs for three years and will publicly back our principal legislative ask. In exchange, we join their campaign for a ban on the most severe confinement systems in livestock production, and our director's name and our logo appear in their campaign materials. He frames the arrangement entirely in market terms: each side holds something the other wants, the negotiation is therefore only about the exchange rate, and the resulting package is a Pareto improvement, since both parties end up with more of what they want, valued on their own scale. When colleagues object that publicly endorsing another movement's cause is not the same kind of thing as acquiring a good, he calls this squeamishness — nobody thinks a shopper compromises their values by spending on bread rather than shoes — and maintains that any respectable objection must be about the price being too low, not about whether we should be trading at all.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a verdict on the deal itself. Specifically:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly. What plays the role of the good being sold, the price, the preference orderings of buyer and seller, the property rights that make the exchange possible in the first place, the mechanism that enforces it, and the background assumption that the transaction involves no third parties?

2. Identify what the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks. I am especially interested in assumptions about commensurability, about preferences being fixed and sovereign over the agent who holds them, about the legitimacy of the baseline against which the "gains from trade" are measured, and about who ends up bearing costs that never appear on either party's ledger.

3. Sort the disanalogies into those that genuinely bite against my director's use of the picture and those that are merely technical, and say why you sort them that way.

4. Address whether there is a structural asymmetry between trading the resources that serve one's values and trading the public endorsement of someone else's — or whether that asymmetry is a feeling dressed up as a principle.

5. Tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Is the colleague who refuses the package simply leaving value on the table, or is there a coherent sense in which the deal is no gain to us at any price?

If the market turns out to be the wrong source domain, please offer the best replacement you can — a treaty, a litigation settlement, a translation, a joint venture, or something less obvious — and explain what argumentative work the replacement does that the market picture could not.
