been rereading the sphexishness material again (Metamagical Themas era, plus the LW-era posts that ran with it) and I want something to read at my local meetup's end-of-year night, where we each do a couple minutes. So: a pantoum, six to eight quatrains, about the digger wasp. In case you need the form — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the closing stanza traditionally takes lines 1 and 3 of the opening as its second and fourth lines, so the poem ends on its own first line. I want that form becuase the form is the argument: the poem keeps dragging the same two lines back to the threshold and going inside to check, and then seals itself shut whether or not anything happened in between.

Get the wasp right, not the cartoon version. She paralyzes the prey — cricket, katydid — and leaves it alive, hauls it by the antennae to the burrow, drops it at the mouth, goes in to inspect, comes out and drags it down. The old experiment: nudge the prey a few inches while she's inside, and when she emerges she finds it, pulls it back to the entrance, and now has to inspect again, because the routine restarted from the step that says prey-is-at-the-threshold. Nudge it again. Some accounts say she'll do this endlessly.

Here's the part I want the poem to actually wrestle with, since the standard tellings skip it. The loop only becomes visible under perturbation — an unbothered wasp runs the whole routine flawlessly and looks, from outside, exactly like a competent agent doing competent wasp things. And the story is shakier then it's usually presented: the version that did the philosophical work came to us through a 1963 popular book about the brain rather than Fabre's own reports, and at least one philosopher has argued the wasps do adapt once you've repeated the displacement enough times, and that re-inspecting a nest you just found disturbed is... arguably sensible. Which cuts both ways, and I want both edges in there: either the wasp is less mechanical than the myth, or the myth survived because it's useful to us rhetorically — which is a much less comfortable thing to notice about why we like the story.

And the human layer, without naming it: I run on precommitments, checklists, implementation intentions, calibration drills, if-I-notice-X-then-do-Y. I built the wasp on purpose, on the grounds that deciding everything from scratch is strictly worse. So the poem should hold open the possibility that anti-sphexishness is itself just a program with more nested loops, and it should not cash out into but we're free, we can always choose to change. That's the cheap ending and I'll know if you use it.

Hard constraints: the words sphexishness, free will, robot, and algorithm don't appear anywhere. Exactly one returning line is allowed to mutate, by one word, at the point where the wasp would have to break the loop — and then the traditional closing seals anyway, so you can feel the mutation fail to be an escape. No apostrophizing the insect, no Victorian naturalist register, no rhyme for rhyme's sake (pantoums don't need it). Plain diction, readable aloud by someone who isn't a performer. If it works, a reader who knows the material should feel slightly accused, and a reader who's never heard of any of it should just think it's a good poem about a wasp.
