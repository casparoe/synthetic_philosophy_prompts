Write a short story of roughly two thousand words that dramatises Thomas Szasz's thesis that mental illness is a myth — not as a slogan, but as an argument with a structure that a reader who has never heard of Szasz could reconstruct from the story alone.

To keep you off well-worn ground, the following premise is given to you and is not negotiable. A public psychiatric service has agreed, under political pressure, to submit each of its diagnostic categories to a "lesion audit". The rule is one Szasz would recognise: a disease is a bodily deviation demonstrable by the methods of pathology, so any category for which such a deviation has been found graduates out of psychiatry and becomes the property of neurology or internal medicine, while every category for which none has been found is to be reclassified as a problem in living — passed to a new, non-medical service that has no power to prescribe, no power to detain, and no power to compel. One category has come back positive. The rest have not. The story begins on the day the reclassifications must be signed.

Beyond the premise, the story must satisfy these requirements.

1. Its spine is the audit itself: the decisions, the paperwork, the arguments about wording, the people waiting to be told what they now are.

2. Both sides must be argued by people with something to lose. Include at least one patient for whom the withdrawal of the medical label is a real loss — protection from blame, entitlement to treatment, a diagnosis that made a life endurable, or immunity from a criminal charge — and at least one for whom it is a deliverance: the end of an imposed treatment, the restoration of a right to refuse, the return of a life to the person who has to live it. Neither case may be disposed of in a paragraph.

3. The Szaszian position must appear in its strongest form and in someone's mouth, carrying the actual argument rather than its reputation: that illness can only literally befall a body; that what psychiatry calls symptoms are largely communications and conduct; that finding a lesion does not vindicate the category but abolishes it; and that the medical framing is precisely what supplies the state with a warrant for doing to a person, without trial and against their will, what it could not otherwise do at all. Someone on the other side must answer in equally strong terms — that suffering is no less real for having no lesion, that a refusal to treat is also a decision and has victims, and that "problem in living" quietly relocates the problem onto whoever has to live it.

4. The historical genealogy must surface somewhere: the sense in which the institution under audit is the successor to an older authority that once handled heresy, sin and offences against God, and now handles what used to be called offences against the self. Convey this through an object, an archive, a building, a surviving job title or a piece of inherited procedure — not through a lecture.

5. No verdict. The story should be readable by a convinced Szaszian and by a convinced opponent without either feeling they have been made a fool of. It should end with a decision made and a cost incurred, not with a moral.

Craft constraints:

- The same intake form, or the same classification schedule, should appear near the beginning and near the end, and the difference between the two appearances should carry at least part of the meaning.
- No character may state the theme of the story; no dialogue may exist solely to inform the reader.
- Avoid the familiar machinery: the sardonic patient wiser than the clinicians, the doctor who turns out to be the mad one, the satire of a pill-happy ward, the courtroom climax, the closing image of an empty bed.
- Keep the legal apparatus invented rather than named. Refer generically to the detention power, the tribunal, the statute, so that nothing depends on getting a real jurisdiction right. Leave the one positive lesion finding unnamed as a category: you may describe the sort of thing it is, but do not attach it to a real disorder.
- Point of view is yours to choose, but the narrator must be someone whose signature is required.

After the story, in no more than one hundred and fifty words of plain prose, say which single Szaszian claim you think the story makes hardest to accept, and what in the story does the resisting.
