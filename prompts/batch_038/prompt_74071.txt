I cover AI and the philosophy of science for a magazine, and a researcher I've interviewed before sent me this argument insisting it's airtight and that I should build a story around it. Here it is, more or less in his words:

(1) The no-free-lunch results — Wolpert's, and the optimization version he did with Macready — show that averaged over all possible target functions, any two learning algorithms have exactly the same expected error on data outside the training set.

(2) So no algorithm is better than any other, period; only better relative to a choice of which target functions to expect.

(3) The data can't tell you which target functions to expect. That's Hume's point, and no-free-lunch is just its formal version.

(4) So any assumption about which target functions you're facing is either read off the data, which (3) rules out, or stipulated, which makes it arbitrary.

(5) Therefore every inductive method — curve fitting, a Gaussian process, a trillion-parameter net — rests on an arbitrary stipulation.

(6) Which means a random guesser is exactly as well founded as a state-of-the-art model. The only difference is that one of them works, and "it works" is itself known only by induction, so it can't break the tie without begging the question.

(7) And you can't escape (6) by appealing to the theorem, since accepting a theorem means trusting a method — proof — that you've only ever seen work so far.

He says (6) is the punchline and that anyone who rejects it hasn't understood the theorem. Honestly I find (7) the most convincing step and (6) the least acceptable, which is presumably why I'm the wrong person to diagnose this, and I dont want to run something that gets torn apart by people who actually know the literature.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look fine on first pass, and what does rejecting it cost — including what you'd have to say about Hume, since (3) is the premise I'd most hate to give up. And then tell me where your own diagnosis is shakiest: what argument or consideration would I have to bring back from him to make you change your verdict?
