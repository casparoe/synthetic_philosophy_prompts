I retired three years ago after forty in physical chemistry, and have since been reading formal epistemology with a layman's persistence. A book I recently finished glosses the Dutch book argument as follows:

"Your degree of belief in a claim just is the price at which you would buy or sell a bet on it. Ramsey and de Finetti showed that if those prices violate the axioms of probability — if you rate a conjunction more probable than one of its conjuncts, say, or fail to add the probabilities of mutually exclusive outcomes — then a clever bookmaker can assemble a package of bets you will each accept, and that loses you money however the world turns out. The converse matters just as much: if your prices do obey the axioms, no package of bets can ever cost you a penny. Nothing further is assumed — no psychology, no theory of evidence, nothing contingent about the world. It is pure arithmetic, which is why incoherent belief is not merely imprudent but strictly irrational: it amounts to a contradiction, like holding a conjunction while denying one of its parts. The same argument settles the rest. Intransitive preferences, failure to update by conditionalization, and vague or imprecise credences are all shown to be exploitable in exactly the same way, and the practical lesson is that the market need not catch you out: the arithmetic already has."

I find the arithmetic persuasive — the probability calculus has never seemed optional to me — but I suspect this paragraph asks the theorem to carry more weight than it can bear, and I cannot locate where. Please take it sentence by sentence and tell me which claims a specialist would accept, which are harmless simplifications, and which are wrong. In particular: does the opening identification of belief with a betting price function here as a premise that the passage later presents as a result? What assumptions beyond arithmetic are required before a sure-loss package is guaranteed to exist, and in which settings do they fail? Does the converse claim license the reassurance the passage draws from it? And is the extension to intransitive preferences, conditionalization, and imprecise credences a repetition of one argument or three further arguments with three further sets of premises? Close by telling me which of the passage's conclusions survives intact once the errors are removed, and how the corrected version should be stated.
