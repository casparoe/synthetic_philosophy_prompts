so I'm on my school's honor council and there's a big fight about whether we should stop using a 9-person panel and instead let the whole junior class vote (like 300 kids) on whether someone broke the honor code. The kids pushing for it keep bringing up the Condorcet jury theorem — the thing about how if each voter is more likely to be right than wrong, a big enough majority is basically guaranteed to get it right — and they act like that ends the arguement.

I want to push back, but I can't use the obvious move, because they already promised secret ballots turned in at the same time, no group chat, no campaigning, nobody knowing how anyone else voted. So "people just copy each other" is off the table and I need something better.

Can you build me a thought experiment that does the job? Here's what it has to pull off:

- realistic school situation, and the stakes have to be ones a high school actually has (an accusation of cheating on a final with a suspension riding on it, not a murder trial)
- the thing being voted on is a yes/no question with an actual fact of the matter
- every voter is genuinely more likely to get it right than wrong, all with the same odds, and nobody is a crank — give me a real number
- the votes have to be independant in the exact sense the theorem needs: no rumor chain, no single source everyone's reading off of, nobody's mistake piggybacking on someone else's
- they decide by simple majority
- the big group has to be MORE likely to be right than the 9-person panel and more likely than any single kid, so I can't win by making the crowd dumb
- and yet going with the big group's verdict has to be clearly worse for the school than just not holding the vote at all and leaving things as they are — and "worse" can't just mean less accurate, it has to mean more damage actually done

The scenario itself should be one or two paragraphs, no sci-fi, no lie detectors or anything.

Then do the checking part for me: state the number each voter has, do the actual majority math for 9 people and for 300 (rough is fine but show how you got it), and explain why the more accurate verdict ends up costing the school more. After that be honest with me — which single feature of the whole case is doing the work, what happens to the case if you delete that feature, and how a fan of the jury theorem would attack my example (would they say I rigged it, and where exactly?).

I'm gonna use this in a 5 minute speech to the student senate, so it needs to be explainable out loud without a whiteboard.

End with one last line that starts with "Verdict:" and names the feature doing the work in six words or less.
