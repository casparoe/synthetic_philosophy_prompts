"Popper's difficulty was that he assumed a single failed prediction could kill a theory, but Lakatos saw that no experiment ever does that on its own, since you can always deflect the blame onto your instruments or onto some auxiliary assumption. So Lakatos swapped refutation for something more sociologically realistic. On his account every research program has a hard core of central claims that the people working inside it agree, by decision, to treat as irrefutable, surrounded by a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that takes the hits and gets revised instead. What separates good science from bad is not whether a program ever gets patched — they all do — but the direction it's moving in: a progressive program keeps generating novel predictions that then actually come true, while a degenerating one only ever rearranges its belt after the fact to explain away anomalies. Lakatos called these after-the-fact rescue moves ad hoc and ruled them out, and that is how he thought he could preserve scientific rationality without Popper's oversimplified falsificationism. The catch, and Lakatos was frank about it, is that you can never know at the time which of the two you're in: a program that has looked degenerate for thirty years might produce a spectacular prediction next year, so on his view it remains rational to keep working inside a degenerating program indefinitely, provided you're honest that this is what you're doing. That is essentially why Feyerabend concluded that Lakatos's methodology collapses into his own anarchism — with no rule telling you when to abandon a program, method does no real work and anything goes. Lakatos's lasting contribution, then, is sociological rather than logical: what makes a program scientific is not the content of its claims but the confidence and productivity of the community around them."
