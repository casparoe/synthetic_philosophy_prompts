A general-interest science magazine recently ran a short piece on pain asymbolia whose conclusion I found convincing, though a friend thinks it overreaches. The argument runs roughly as follows:

"People with pain asymbolia — typically after an injury that disconnects pain-processing regions from the limbic system — feel a pinprick with full sensory clarity. They can say where it is, how intense it is, and that it is a pain; they often smile, and report that they simply do not mind. This shows that pain is not intrinsically bad. The badness lives in a separate affective layer that can be stripped away while the sensation itself survives intact, so what remains is neutral in the way a color or a tone is neutral. The upshot is practical: an analgesic that preserved the warning signal and deleted only the suffering would leave the patient with nothing bad at all, and would thereby demonstrate that pain as such carries no negative value."

I am inclined to accept the conclusion, and I don't know enough to say where the reasoning strains. Could you audit the passage claim by claim — which parts a philosopher working on the nature of pain would accept, which are simplifications that survive scrutiny, and which are simply false given what has actually been reported about these patients? I'd especially like to know whether the move from reported indifference to the neutrality of the pain sensation is legitimate, whether the piece is using 'intrinsically' the way philosophers do, and whether the final sentence follows from anything true in the rest of it.
