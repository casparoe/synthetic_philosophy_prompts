Six weeks into a secondment advising our national statistics office, I find myself publicly defending a view I would like properly stress-tested first: that cross-national rankings of self-reported life evaluation — Cantril-ladder style measures, which reliably place the Nordic countries at the top — track something real about how well a society's members are doing, and that a government does well to treat climbing such a ranking as a policy aim. My own gloss on it is that the ladder is best read as a measure of a society's confidence in its prospects, which would make the ranking closer to a trust index than a happiness index; I want to know whether that gloss strengthens the position or leaves it more exposed.

What would count against the whole thing? Please sort the defeaters by decisiveness: measurement artifacts that could generate the ordering on their own (differential scale use, reference-group shift, translation and anchoring problems, adaptation to circumstances), substantive findings that sit badly with it (the divergence between evaluative and affective measures, high life evaluation coexisting with high suicide), and the objection that targeting the rank converts the instrument into a Goodhart trap. Tell me which of these a committed defender of the ladder would herself concede as damaging and which she would dismiss as a rival conception of wellbeing rather than a failure of measurement. Then say plainly whether the position still risks anything, or has been qualified into something no future ranking result could falsify — and whether the near-identical ordering across survey vendors and decades is evidence for my reading or against it.
