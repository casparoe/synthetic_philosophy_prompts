I look after a machine-readable rulebase for the compliance function at a mid-size bank: regulations, internal policies, and the regional interpretations of both, all turned into structured rules that downstream automation actually fires on. It leaks contradictions constantly. Two authorities say incompatible things about the same activity, a policy gets amended in one place and not another, a regional reading flatly contradics head office. Triage right now is just whoever grabbed the ticket, and I want to replace that with a procedure a junior analyst with no logic background can run in about twenty minutes, with one clearly marked class of case that gets kicked up to me or to counsel instead of being decided at all.

Here's the naive thing I was going to do: when two rules conflict, store both, tag the pair, and let the query surface both so a human sees the ambiguity. A colleague told me that would "explode," and I honestly don't know whether that was metaphor or a technical claim about my system. Some of what I've read on paraconsistent logic suggests contradictions can be tolerated locally without everything becoming derivable, and some of it — the dialetheist stuff — suggests certain contradictions are simply true, which I'd love to be able to write on a ticket to close it, and I'm worried that's exactly the wrong instinct.

So: write the procedure. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in "suppress one rule," where it terminates in "quarantine both and stop the automation," and which step hands the thing to a person because it isn't a factual question at all. Tell me which distinctions from the logic have to survive the simplification for someone who will never read the underlying stuff, and which ones I can safely drop. Then run it on three of mine:

1. Head office policy says transfers under a certain threshold need no extra approval; our regional regulator's guidance says that class of transfer always needs a named local officer's sign-off. The regional text is newer and more specific, head office is the higher authority.

2. A retention rule requires us to keep a log of every rule suppression for seven years. A minimisation rule prohibits retaining anything no longer needed for the purpose it was collected. Someone on my team argues the suppression log is exactly such a record, so we're obligated both to keep it and to delete it, and wants it recorded as a true contradiction and moved on.

3. A pair that's been inconsistent for about two years, and the engine has been silently resolving it by evaluation order — whichever rule it hits first wins. Pulling the check sends roughly forty thousand transactions a month to humans, which was the entire point of automating it.

One of these should come out as bad news for me; I'd rather you say which than soften it. And be straight about where the procedure will misfire, what it can't settle, and how analysts will abuse it under ticket pressure.
