I audit the monthly teaching sessions of the clinical ethics service at the hospital where I work as a research coordinator, largely to keep my own notes straight for a part-time bioethics certificate. This term's unit covered what makes a patient's consent valid — deception, coercion, and competence — and one case produced a genuine argument between two of the regulars: Marta, a clinical ethicist who does the consultations herself, and Elias, a legal philosopher who attends for interest. I want to reconstruct their exchange for a study-group handout I'm writing on why offers made against terrible backgrounds are hard to classify, and I could not tell, at the time or since, who was winning.

The case, as Marta set it out: a dialysis patient is offered a place in an experimental transplant protocol. He is told the truth about the protocol's risks in plain language and understands them; nobody threatens him with anything. He is also told that if he declines he returns to the standard waiting list and that, on his current trajectory, he would likely not survive two years. He signs, undergoes the procedure, and does badly. Afterwards a junior colleague suggested the consent had been coerced, and Marta and Elias disagreed in a way that quickly turned theoretical. (1) Marta's first move was that the junior colleague had confused a hard choice with no choice: every medical consent is given against a background of unwelcome alternatives, so if a merely bad fallback voids consent, consent in medicine hardly ever exists — which she took to be a reductio of the view rather than a finding about this patient. (2) Elias replied that the reductio is cheap, because the question is not whether the fallback is bad but whether the menu was wrongfully constituted: the same institution controlled both the experimental slot and the waiting list and could have made the fallback less terrible, and when the party offering the attractive option also controls how bad the unattractive one is, that offer does normative work a structurally identical offer from an uninvolved third party would not. (3) Marta countered that this makes validity depend on a fact the patient cannot see and did not care about — his psychology, options, and preferences were exactly what they were regardless of who had power to improve the fallback — so that two patients facing the same options with the same preferences could differ in the validity of their consent purely in virtue of an institution's history, which means the concept has stopped tracking the patient's agency. (4) Elias answered that validity was never exhausted by the consenter's psychology, since deception and honest dealing can also look identical from inside the patient's head and nobody concludes that fraud is irrelevant; the invisibility objection therefore proves too much. (5) Marta's final move was that he had quietly changed the subject: the question was whether this patient authorized this procedure, and Elias was now answering a question about whether the hospital behaved well in setting the menu; on his account, she said, every wrong anywhere in the background of an agreement voids the agreement, and a clinical team could never know whether a procedure performed last year had been authorized at all, whereas the whole point of treating consent as valid is to make the touching something other than a battery. The room split roughly down the middle and the session moved on.

Could you adjudicate this move by move rather than simply declaring a winner: take the five moves in order and say for each whether it lands, partially lands, or misses, and why, attending in particular to whether each move is actually responsive to the one it answers or slides past it. Then tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — is there a distinction, or a way of splitting the underlying question into two, that would dissolve part of the exchange, and if so which part of the dispute survives the split intact and why it survives? Two constraints on your reply. First, don't send me to the literature; I will add references to the handout myself and want only the argument. Second, be explicit about epistemic status throughout: separate the points on which people who work on consent would broadly agree from those that are genuinely contested among them, and don't dress up a contested position as the settled view just because it yields a tidier verdict. Where you think one of them is straightforwardly wrong, say so plainly, and say what would have to be true for you to be wrong about that.
