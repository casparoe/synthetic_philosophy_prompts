I run a small after-school tutoring co-op out of a library room — about 14 kids, ages 12 to 16, four days a week, 90 minutes each. Once every couple weeks I like to give a short unannounced quiz, mostly because it keeps them from cramming only the night before I tell them. Last month I made the mistake of explaining to one kid why the quizzes "can't actually be a surprise if you announce them ahead of time," and he went home and did the whole backward-induction thing: no quiz on the last day, because then we'd know; so it'd have to be the day before; so that's out too; all the way back to Monday, so the announcement is empty. Now the whole group calls it "the Friday thing" and they've gotten cocky. They've also escalated past the actual paradox, which is the part that's really bugging me: one of them said that even if I pick the day randomly, it's not a surprise, because they walked in knowing there was a 25 percent chance, and knowing there's a 25 percent chance is not the same as not knowing. I have no idea how to answer that and I don't have any training in this stuff — I've just been thinking about it on my own in the evenings.

Can you write me an actual procedure I can run, maybe ten minutes on a Sunday, to decide what to announce and when to quiz, so that the quizzes stay genuinely surprising for as long as possible? Not an essay about the paradox — a set of steps in order, with what counts as a good enough answer to each step, where the procedure just tells me "don't announce that at all," and where it stops and hands the decision back to me because it's about how I want to run my room rather than about facts. I'd also want it to say what to do when I can't work out what the kids actually believe.

A few things I want you to be straight with me about:

- Which assumption in the induction does your procedure have to break? Is it the part where they treat my announcement as something they can rely on, or the last-day step, or the idea that they have to know that they know? Say which, because I want to know what my policy is actually depending on.
- One fix I keep reading about online is basically "the students only win because they assume the teacher never breaks her word, so be a teacher who sometimes does." If your procedure leans on that, tell me, and tell me whether that's just lying with extra steps and what it costs me with a room full of kids who already think they've solved it.
- The 25-percent kid. Does a probability argument defeat surprise, or is there some line where being unsure is different from being surprised, and if there's a line, is it a real line or am I just picking a number I like? Be honest if the procedure can't settle this and it comes down to what I want the word to mean in my room.

Then run it on three situations for me: (1) an ordinary week where I want exactly one quiz and I don't care which day; (2) a nine-week term where I already told them there'd be at least three and that none would be visible coming — including a case where the procedure says I can't have what I want and I should walk the promise back, because I'd rather hear that now; (3) the group that's already done the induction, walks in every day assuming a 25 percent chance, and is currently winning.

Constraints so it's usable: I can't print anything in the room, the kids have phones and a group chat I'm not in, I have to give at least one quiz every two weeks or it stops meaning anything, and one of them will absolutely publish my policy if I say it out loud — so assume anything I announce gets reverse-engineered by Wednesday. Mark every place where I'm being asked to make a judgment call that looks like it's coming out of the procedure.
