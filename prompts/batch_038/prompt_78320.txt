Here is the context. My seminar is on the justification of deduction, and we spent two sessions on Carroll's tortoise. As I understand the most popular contemporary reply, modus ponens is not the sort of thing that stands in need of justification by argument at all: it is part of what fixes the meaning of the conditional, so the tortoise has not described a coherent stance. He has shown only that he fails to grasp 'if...then...'. Achilles' complaint — "Then Logic would take you by the throat, and force you to do it!" — is on this view a category mistake, since the force in question is not a further thing the tortoise could be given; it is built into his understanding the sentences he accepts. Call this the meaning-constitutive reply.

I tried to build an argument showing that this reply cannot be had for free. It has ended up proving far more than I can swallow, and after a week of staring at it I cannot locate the slip. I will state it as carefully as I can.

(1) Suppose the meaning-constitutive reply is correct about modus ponens: the rule partly constitutes the meaning of the conditional, and so anyone who grasps that meaning, accepts p, and accepts that if p then q, is rationally required to accept q.

(2) That requirement cannot consist in the reasoner's possessing, or needing to acquire, one more proposition. This is precisely the tortoise's lesson: any candidate proposition — if A and B are true, Z must be true, and then the same again with that proposition included among the antecedents — can be entered in the notebook, and the move still has to be made; and the tortoise's retort, "And why must I?", can always be repeated of the enlarged set. Adding representations of the rule never supplies the rule's exercise.

(3) So if the requirement in (1) is to be explained at all, it must attach to the grasp of meaning itself rather than to anything the reasoner accepts: understanding the conditional must be, at least in part, being disposed to make the transition.

(4) But a disposition is a descriptive fact about what a speaker does, under ordinary or under idealized conditions, and no such facts determine which transitions are correct. Dispositions misfire, they are triggered by fatigue and by misleading appearances, and they underdetermine their own content: everything a speaker has actually done is equally consistent with his meaning something deviant by the conditional, a connective that has behaved exactly like 'if' on every case so far and diverges thereafter (the structure of Kripke's quus worry, applied to rules rather than to reference). Any attempt to privilege the correct manifestations as the ones "produced by the rule" rather than by noise simply re-poses the question of what fixes the rule, and re-poses it in the same form.

(5) Hence the meaning-constitutive theorist faces a dilemma. Either the requirement is propositional, in which case by (2) it can never be discharged; or it is dispositional, in which case by (4) it is not a requirement at all, and "correct inference" collapses into "inference that speakers are disposed to make."

(6) If correct inference collapses into disposed inference, then no inference is incorrect but only unusual. There is no fact of the matter that the tortoise gets wrong: his refusal of Z manifests a minority disposition, not an error, and the connective he is using is not ours. On this reading he is not obtuse, merely different.

(7) The meaning-constitutive reply therefore defeats its own purpose. It was introduced to explain why the tortoise errs, and it implies instead that he cannot err.

(8) Nothing in (1) through (7) was special to the conditional. The same reasoning runs against any rule offered as constitutive of the meaning of any expression — conjunction-introduction, universal instantiation, or the introduction rule for 'is a reason to believe'. So no expression's meaning is constituted by inference rules, and the meaning-constitutive reply is unavailable across the board, not merely here.

(9) Finally, (1) through (8) is itself just a sequence of transitions from accepted premises to a conclusion. If (8) is true, there is no such thing as being required to make those transitions; nothing required the step from (5) to (6) or from (6) to (7); and so the argument carries no force, including no force against the reply it was built to undermine.

The conclusion I cannot accept is the disjunction this leaves me with: either the tortoise can be answered, or inference is not the sort of thing that admits of correctness, in which case neither does my own argument. I find both disjuncts intolerable and I suspect the argument is simply invalid somewhere, but I cannot say where.
