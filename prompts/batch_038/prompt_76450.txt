You are a philosopher of risk with deep familiarity in the strategic literature on nuclear deterrence, and you are helping me prepare. I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and next month's guest is a strategist — call him my interlocutor, no need to name him — who defends a doctrine I'll label deliberate dereliction. On his account, a nuclear state should manufacture genuine uncertainty about whether it will respond: devolve launch authority downward during a crisis, keep response timelines and red lines ambiguous, and occasionally behave in ways that look unrestrained. His argument is that a threat the adversary cannot fully discount outdeters a threat whose execution its author would never rationally choose, and that the residual risk of accidental or unauthorized use is the premium on that policy — a premium he thinks is paid roughly evenly by everyone alive. He tends to wave off case-based challenges with the observation that we have no recorded instance of the doctrine failing catastrophically.

Think carefully before drafting. Begin with a short paragraph identifying the two or three load-bearing claims of his position and the sharpest available objections to each; I want to see you've located the pressure points, not just listed topics. Then give me ten interview questions, no biography, no throat-clearing about how he came to the field. At least one question must address each of the following:

- the self-undermining problem: once the doctrine is public, the adversary knows the apparent loss of control is staged, and manufactured unpredictability collapses back into a calculable bluff;
- the identification problem: we only observe crises that ended without nuclear use, so what could distinguish dereliction-deterring from restraint, or from plain luck;
- the aggregation problem: per-crisis risk may be trivial, but crises recur, and his own arithmetic has to run over a sequence rather than an event;
- the misperception problem: devolved authority may be read by the other side as preparation for a disarming first strike, converting bargaining leverage into use-it-or-lose-it pressure;
- consent: the populations absorbing the premium were never asked.

For each question, supply the answer he is most likely to give and the single follow-up that denies him that resting place. Close with a question about what observation would oblige him to abandon the view, and add one line telling me which of the five he will dodge most gracefully.

Work entirely from your own reasoning: no bibliography, no reading list, no attributions to particular books or authors.
