I work in the risk group of a mid-sized reinsurer, and last quarter I ran an informal preference elicitation on myself and two colleagues — originally as a way of checking whether our internal underwriting guidelines actually track our intuitions about unfamiliar exposures. Most of the bets are close cousins of the two problems Ellsberg used to generate his paradox, but I added several variants of my own, and the pattern of my answers turned out to be more complicated than I expected. Here are the eight cases and the verdicts I actually give, reported honestly, including the ones I am embarrassed by.

1. *Two urns.* Urn A holds 100 balls, 50 red and 50 black, composition certified by the house. Urn B holds 100 balls, red-black ratio unknown, with anything from 0 to 100 red consistent with what I am told. A draw pays $100 on red. I pay substantially more to draw from A, and I would decline B even at a $20 discount to A.
2. *Three colours.* One urn, 90 balls: 30 red, and 60 that are either black or yellow in an unknown split. I prefer a bet on red to a bet on black. I also prefer a bet on black-or-yellow to a bet on red-or-yellow. I hold both preferences at once and I know what that costs me.
3. *Free gift.* A ticket offered to me at zero cost, with no obligation, paying $1 if a ball drawn from a 1,000-ball urn of entirely unknown red fraction comes up red, and $0 otherwise. My gut reaction is suspicion rather than gratitude, and if you simultaneously offer me a certain ten cents, I take the certain ten cents. I recognise that this is close to incoherent and I cannot defend it.
4. *Hedged pair.* Same three-colour urn, but now I am offered the red ticket and the yellow ticket together as a single package. My aversion to the unknown composition vanishes: I take the package, and I would take it over the corresponding package from a known-composition urn if the known-composition package were even slightly cheaper. It feels as though ambiguity aversion requires an unhedged comparator sitting next to the ambiguous bet.
5. *Adversary.* Urn B again, except that its composition was chosen by a competitor who saw which colour I intended to bet on before committing. Now I do not merely shade my valuation: I assume the worst-case composition outright, and I refuse bets that a plain expected-value calculation would accept at mildly favourable odds. Unlike case 3, I feel no tension here whatsoever — this strikes me as obviously correct conduct.
6. *Expertise.* Case 1 again, except that the unknown composition of Urn B was generated by a procedure I have studied for twenty years — say, a deck shuffled by a player whose habits I know cold — and I have been told exactly how the generation worked. Under those conditions I am happy to bet on the "ambiguous" side against the certified 50/50 urn. Substitute a generation procedure I know nothing about, holding the formal description of the bet identical, and I flip straight back to case 1. So my aversion appears to be indexed to my competence rather than to anything in the state of the world.
7. *Delegation.* I will pay a colleague who is expert in the generating procedure to draw from Urn B on my behalf, at a premium; I will still not draw from that same urn myself. The urn is identical in the two situations, and only the identity of the person choosing differs.
8. *Amended treaty.* A reinsurance contract whose wording has been amended an unknown number of times, by parties I cannot reach, and where I suspect there exist clauses I have not read and occurrences the document does not obviously contemplate. My aversion here is by far the largest in the entire set — larger than anything in cases 1 to 3 — and I think it is rational, though I cannot say why, since formally I could just enumerate the states and assign each one a number.

What I want from you is the principle, if there is one, behind these eight verdicts. Consider the main candidate families: evaluating a bet by its worst case across a set of admissible priors rather than a single one; treating my uncertainty as a probability distribution over probability distributions, so that ambiguity aversion is really aversion to a higher-order spread; ordering acts by comparative confidence rather than by numeric probability; treating ambiguity as a property of the *source* of the uncertainty, so that a bet is ambiguous only relative to sources I do not understand; and treating case 8 as something categorically different, where the state space itself is not fixed. For each family you take seriously, say what it does to cases 1 through 8. In particular: does any of them explain why case 3 is worse than it has any right to be while case 4 comes out clean; do they make the competence-dependence in cases 6 and 7 a real structural feature or a confusion on my part; and do they preserve the sharp asymmetry I feel between case 5 and case 1, where the only difference is who chose the composition and when? If no single account survives all eight, I would rather have a simpler account that tells me plainly which of my verdicts to abandon than an elaborate one that keeps all of them at the price of unmotivated machinery — but show me both options before you choose.

Please also give me your predictions on three cases I have not tried. (a) A two-stage bet in which the unknown composition of Urn B is itself drawn, before I choose, from a known uniform distribution over compositions: do I become neutral between Urn A and Urn B, or does the aversion survive the randomisation? (b) A bet on Urn B in which the composition will be revealed to me after I have chosen but before I am paid. (c) A sequence of draws from Urn B in which I see the colour of each ball as it comes out: does my valuation of later bets move in a way that exposes me to a sure loss across the sequence, and if so, which of my eight verdicts is responsible?

Write the reasoning out as ordinary prose first — around 800 to 1,000 words, arguing rather than listing — and then close with a single JSON object, no prose after it, using exactly this schema: `recommended_account` (a short name plus a one-sentence statement of the decision rule); `formal_gist` (how the rule is defined over acts, states and priors, at most four sentences); `verdict_table` (an array of eight objects, one per case, each with `case` as the integer 1–8, `principle_says` as one of "prefer_known", "prefer_ambiguous", "indifferent", or "refuse_outright", `i_said` as the same vocabulary describing my reported verdict, `agrees` as a boolean, and `driving_clause` as one sentence saying which part of the rule produces the result); `overturned` (an array of objects with `case` and `why_it_must_go`, empty if you save all eight); `new_predictions` (an array of three objects with `case` as "a", "b" or "c", `prediction`, and `confidence` as "high", "medium" or "low"); `dynamic_consistency` (two or three sentences on whether the recommended rule licenses preference reversal as information arrives); and `runner_up` (an object with `account`, `what_it_saves_that_the_winner_does_not`, and `what_it_costs`).
