I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine — mostly psychology and cognitive science these days — and I've got a slot coming up in about three weeks that's supposed to be about how much stock we can put in people's reports of their own experience. My editor sent me an advance copy of a trade book coming out in the spring, by a science writer I've followed and liked for years, and the chapter on introspection has this passage that I've now read maybe five times and still can't decide whether it's good compression or just sloppy. Quoting from the galley:

"Introspection is broken, and the evidence has been sitting there for fifty years. In 1977 Nisbett and Wilson showed that when people explain their own choices they are mostly making it up: subjects shown four identical pairs of stockings gravitated toward the ones at one end of the display, then insisted, with real feeling, that position had nothing to do with it, and not one of them admitted he was guessing. What we call knowing our own minds turns out to be the same story-spinning machinery we use to explain other people, aimed inward. A Swedish team sharpened the point with what they called choice blindness. Subjects are shown two faces and pick the more attractive one; the experimenter swaps the cards behind a clipboard and hands back the face the subject rejected; most subjects never notice, and many go on to explain at length exactly why they preferred it. Eric Schwitzgebel, the philosopher who has made a career of this problem, concludes that we have no reliable introspective access even to our own conscious experience — that on questions about what we are feeling we perform at roughly chance. Which means, for anyone who reports on the mind, that verbal self-report should be treated as noise: if you cannot measure it from the outside, in behavior or physiology or reaction times, it is not data."

I'm inclined to buy that ending, honestly — it's the kind of hard line that makes a piece much easier to write, and I've been burned before by quoting people's recollections of thier own motivations as if those were facts. But something in the middle wobbles for me and I can't put my finger on it. So: can you go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works in this area would sign off on, what's a defensible simplification for a general audience, and what's wrong or quietly doing extra work? A few things I specifically want nailed down: does the 1977 result really say what this says it says, or is there a distinction in it that the passage flattens? Does failing to notice a swapped photograph license the conclusion being drawn from it, or is that a jump between two different kinds of question? Is "roughly chance" a fair way to state Schwitzgebel's view, and is this debate actually settled in the direction the book implies — I have a dim memory of a psychologist who ran studies with him and came out on the other side, though I may be inventing that. And then: once you fix whatever needs fixing, does the last sentence still follow, or does the book owe me an argument it hasn't given? Please don't hand me a bibliography or a reading list — no time, and half of it would be paywalled anyway. I just want your read on which claims are load-bearing, which are decorative, and where the honest disagreement still is, so I don't publish something that pretends there isn't one.
