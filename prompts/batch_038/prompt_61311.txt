I'm retooling my grad seminar on the Mencius–Xunzi debate for the spring and I've realized the whole thing runs on one pair of metaphors that I keep handing students as if it were an argument.

The standard line — mine included, and a fair chunk of the secondary literature's — is that Mencius is the gardener and Xunzi is the carpenter. Mencius gives you sprouts that only need tending (the four duan in 2A6); Xunzi gives you crooked wood clamped against a straightening board and clay thrown into a vessel (the opening chapter on learning, and the chapter arguing that human nature is bad). Then everyone slides straight from the images to a claim about the theories: plants have an internal developmental program, so for Mencius virtue is native and teaching just nourishes what's there; artifacts get their form from outside, so for Xunzi virtue is imposed by ritual and by the sages, with nothing in the nature of the person answering to it. Innatism versus constructionism, basically, settled by metaphor.

Two problems with my own framing. A very sharp student pushed back last term and said the potter doesn't manufacture the clay — so Xunzi's image already gives nature a job, supplying both the material and the capacity to be worked on, which is a bigger concession than I'd allowed. And Mencius's own imagery isn't exactly unforced organic growth: it includes axes and grazing cattle stripping Ox Mountain bare (6A8) and the farmer from Song who yanked his seedlings up to help them along (2A2). I also suspect the plant/craft contrast is quietly importing a modern nature–nurture picture that neither text shares, where "internal" means already present and needing only release, and "external" means the subject contributes nothing at all.

So: audit the pair of analogies for me. Give me the correspondence explicitly — what in each image plays the role of human nature, of the person's own effort, of ritual and instruction, of the finished virtue, and of the time the process takes — and then seperate out the disanalogies that actually bear on the innatist/constructionist conclusion from the ones that are just decoration. Where does each metaphor smuggle in something the target case lacks: a designer with a prior plan, a fixed end state, a purely passive patient, a form that's separable from its matter? Does the conclusion survive once those are on the table, or does it turn out that the two texts are less opposed than the images suggest — or opposed about something other than where virtue comes from? And if gardener versus carpenter can't carry that weight, is there a better shared image, one both sides would accept, that would let me teach the real disagreement without metaphor-smuggling? I want to be able to say in the first class why my own favorite comparison is partly a cheat.
