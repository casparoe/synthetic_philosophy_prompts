I'm teaching a 75-minute decision theory session to 24 undergraduates in a philosophy core course; they know what expected value is and essentially nothing else — no logarithms, no probability theory beyond that. Design an in-class betting game that makes them feel the difference between maximizing expected value on each individual round and maximizing the growth rate of their wealth across the whole sequence. Students must carry their wealth forward between rounds, be shown exact and generous odds on a repeated coin flip, and choose their own stake each round; they must never be told what the right fraction is, and the word "optimal" should stay out of the handout until the debrief. The stakes should be real but not cash — tell me what the tokens convert into at the end and how to structure that conversion so the class doesn't turn into a public wealth ranking that humiliates whoever busts. Give me the round-by-round structure, the materials, and a debrief that maps particular moments onto the distinction, including the moment when the class average has risen while the median has collapsed.

Then deal with three problems I expect to hit: what a busted player does for the remaining half hour; how to handle the student who bets everything and gets lucky, since he will read as a refutation rather than a survivor; and whether a known final round should tempt them to go all-in, and what I should do if it does. Close with the strongest objection a sharp student can raise — that she lives her life only once, so she should care about the average over possible outcomes rather than the average over time — and what I can honestly say in reply, plus the one feature of the phenomenon the game cannot reproduce and I have to supply by hand.
