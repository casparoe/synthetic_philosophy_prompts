I write novels — five published, literary-ish, nobody famous — and there's a thing that's happened with every single one of them that I've never been able to name, and its started to change how I draft. Here's the pattern. A sentence gets loose from the book. Someone reads it aloud on a podcast, or it ends up in a review, or it gets posted over a photo of a rainy window. Within a year there are three or four versions in circulation and they're all slightly off from what I actually typed. Say I wrote "Grief is a house you keep adding rooms to, mostly so you can go on living in it" — long, has a hinge in the middle, a little embarrassed by itself. What survives is "Grief is a house you build around the dead." Tighter. More aphorism, less thought. And the version that wins is never the one closest to mine, it's the one that's easiest to say out loud. People who only know the mutant will *correct* me when I quote my own line, very kindly, the way you'd correct a stranger about a movie. That part I've made peace with. Talk is lossy, fine.

What I can't name is the second half, because the second half goes the other way. The mutant travels back into the source. The paperback blurb uses it. A translator who came to the book through the popular paraphrase renders that shape into her language, and then the back-translation in a foreign-rights sampler matches the mutant instead of me. An anthology of lines about mourning prints the mutant under my name, with permission, and my name is now attached to a sentence I'd never have written. And in the tenth-anniversary edition my editor suggested, gently, that we consider "restoring" the line readers remember, since the real one now reads to people like a typo in my own book. So eventually the thing I didn't write is the text and the thing I wrote is the misquotation. Then the last twist, which is the one that actually keeps me up: I catch myself composing detachable sentences on purpose now. Short, self-contained, no hinge, easy to imagine surviving the shredder. I'm writing for the copy rather than for the book, and I can feel my prose getting worse in exactly the places where it's getting more quotable. My partner says I'm describing being well-known and should get over it, but I don't think that's it, because it happens to sentences nobody intended to make famous.

So — does this have a name? I know there's a whole literature about ideas spreading the way genes or viruses do, and I'm not asking about that, because that's about copying and what I've got isn't copying gone wrong. It's an asymmetry: the variants that travel better are systematically worse at being faithful, and then, uniquely, the winning variant colonizes the thing it came from and rewrites it. It isn't quote-mining, since there's no villain doing it and no agenda, just a slope. It isn't the telephone game either, because in the telephone game nobody takes the garbled result at the end and prints it back into the original manuscript. And it isn't plain misattribution, because at some point the misattributed version *becomes* correctly attributed. I want the actual carving, not just a label: what are the nearest established concepts, one or two of them, and what precisely separates what I'm describing from each; where does this stop being the phenomenon and start being ordinary paraphrase, ordinary drift, or ordinary editorial housekeeping; and if there's genuinely no term for the whole loop — spread-plus-writeback-plus-the-author-adapting-to-it — then coin me one, with a definition tight enough that I could drop it into an essay and no reader would assume I invented it on the spot. Give me two or three candidate names with the tradeoffs, and tell me which one you'd bet on, plus a boundary case that looks like it but isn't, so I know where the edges are.
