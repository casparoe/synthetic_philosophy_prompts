For about a decade I have followed the argument, familiar from the old rationalist threads on recursive self-improvement, that an agent cannot rationally trust the conclusions of a smarter version of itself. Let me state the problem the way I hold it, so that neither of us has to rely on recalling the literature correctly.

Löb's theorem, as I understand it: for a sufficiently strong theory T that can express its own provability predicate — Peano arithmetic or anything containing it — if T proves that (T proves X) → X, then T already proves X. So a system cannot adopt the policy "whatever I can prove, I may accept" as a route to new knowledge; the policy is only available for claims it can settle anyway. The corollary for a self-improving agent is that it cannot license the inference from "my successor concludes X" to X for any X it could not have decided on its own — which is exactly the interesting case, since deciding what it could not decide was the entire point of building the successor.

Here is a proposal I want evaluated. Please engage with it precisely as stated rather than by analogy to the nearest thing you remember reading, and argue from the structure of the problem rather than from who has said what. The agent makes no attempt whatsoever to reason about the content of its successor's conclusions. Instead it fixes one specific, opaque upgrade procedure — a compiled artifact whose internals it no longer inspects — and forms an ordinary empirical hypothesis about that artifact: procedure P yields correct answers to questions of type Q with some frequency f. It estimates f from the finite set of past cases in which it was independently able to check the successor's output, then extends the resulting trust to cases it cannot check. The claim on offer is that this replaces a provability predicate with an inductive generalization about a physical process, and that Löb's theorem has no purchase on inductive generalizations.

Give me your credence, as a single number between 0 and 1, in this proposition:

P: there exists a coherent account on which such an agent is genuinely and non-circularly justified in accepting its successor's conclusion on some question of type Q that the agent cannot itself answer, and on which that justification neither collapses back into the agent already having the answer nor amounts to taking the successor's word as testimony from a source that is not itself.

I currently sit at roughly 0.55, and I suspect that number is inflated because I am treating two of the terms below as independent when they are not. I want the decomposition rather than a gestalt impression. Assign a rough probability to each of the following terms, with one or two lines of justification per term, and say explicitly where you think the terms are dependent rather than separable:

1. That Löb's theorem is correctly diagnosed as constraining proof-predicative trust only, such that recharacterizing trust as an empirical hypothesis about a procedure removes the formal obstacle outright rather than relocating it.
2. That the calibration set — the cases where the agent could check the successor — licenses extrapolation to the cases where it could not, given that the two sets are divided by precisely the property, checkability by the agent, that defines the extrapolation.
3. That what survives is still self-trust rather than deference to a distinct agent, i.e., that the proposal has not solved the problem by ceasing to be about it.
4. That an agent reasoning about the conclusions of its smarter successor was ever a well-posed requirement in the first place, so that a solution to it would be a solution to something rather than the dissolution of a badly stated demand.

If you think this list is missing the term that actually carries the weight, add it, drop or merge one of mine, and say why. Then show how you combine the terms into a final figure, identify the single term to which your estimate is most sensitive — the one where a given shift moves the total most — and state what argument or piece of evidence would move that term. Finally, tell me plainly whether you think the proposal above commits an identifiable error, and if so name it in one sentence.

End your reply with your overall credence in P on a line of its own, in the form \answer{0.XX}.
