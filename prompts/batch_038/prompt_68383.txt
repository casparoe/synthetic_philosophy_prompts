Our lab ran a pilot that has turned into an argument I cannot escape, and the argument seems to consume its own premises. Subjects viewed fruit images near the ripeness boundary; one group was told the fruit came from a warm growing region, the other from a cool one, and the two groups placed the boundary between ripe and unripe at measurably different saturations despite identical proximal stimulation. From that I argued:

1. If the character of a visual experience depends on what the subject already believes, that experience cannot justify the belief; otherwise a belief would be its own evidence.
2. The pilot shows that experience depends on belief, and nothing about the design suggests the effect is confined to exotic materials, since expert and novice perception differ generally.
3. No residue of belief-independent experience can be marked off, because holding the stimulus fixed is itself a theoretical description of the situation rather than something given in it.
4. Therefore, for any perceptual belief, whether its supporting experience justifies it depends on the rest of what the subject believes.
5. If whether E justifies B depends on other beliefs, then E does not justify B non-inferentially.
6. Therefore no perceptual belief is non-inferentially justified.
7. But the pilot's own data — the readouts, the color patches, the coding of subjects' reports — are perceptual beliefs, so by (6) they are not non-inferentially justified either.
8. Therefore the argument has no non-inferentially justified premises, and refutes itself.

I would most like to keep (1) and (5), since giving them up seems to abandon the thought that justification cannot be circular. Identify the step or steps that fail, explain why each looked unobjectionable in advance, and price the available repairs: if I hold on to (2), must I deny that perception is encapsulated from belief, and if I accept (6), what is left to support the pilot?
