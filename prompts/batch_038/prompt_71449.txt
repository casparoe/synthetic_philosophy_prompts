I am preparing a handout for the first session of a graduate seminar in metaphilosophy. Nearly all of the students have settled habits of judgment about individual philosophical disputes — what counts as resolving one, what a dead end looks like, whether an old argument can simply be assumed — but almost none of them could name the view of philosophical progress those habits commit them to. I want an instrument that forces the commitment into the open.

Please write a ten-item sorting quiz with a two-axis scoring grid, formatted as a handout a seminar leader could photocopy, roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, in American English.

**Structure.** Ten items, three answer options each, divided into two batteries of five, but presented interleaved so that the handout does not signal which battery an item belongs to. Battery A items determine which *criterion* of philosophical progress the student's answers presuppose; Battery B items determine what their answers to particular cases commit them to about whether that criterion is in fact being met.

The criterion axis must have exactly these three values: (i) settling — a discipline advances by getting more of its central claims right and by ruling claims out; (ii) deepening — a discipline advances by making a subject matter intelligible, by showing what turns on what and how the pieces hang together, whether or not anything gets settled; (iii) tooling — a discipline advances by producing instruments (distinctions, arguments, formalisms, better-posed questions) that later workers can pick up and use even if the question that motivated them is abandoned.

The status axis must have exactly these three values: (i) yes, on the whole; (ii) yes, but unevenly and locally — some questions, some subfields, some generations; (iii) no, and not because we have been unlucky, but for a structural reason.

**Item design.** Every item must be a concrete situation a student can respond to without knowing any of the vocabulary above. No item may name a philosopher, a school, or a real dispute, and the words *progress*, *realism*, *quietism*, *pessimism*, *deflationary*, and *metaphilosophy* may not appear in any question stem or answer option. No item may ask the student to assess a discipline as a whole.

Please build the items around at least the following cases, inventing the details yourself:

- A student asks whether she may use, as an unqualified premise in her own argument, a three-page negative result published in a philosophy journal roughly sixty years ago — a paper showing that a familiar three-part analysis of some mental or epistemic state fails. Her supervisor says she may, but that she would have to re-argue it if challenged. What does this arrangement tell her?
- Over a century, twenty live positions on a question become six. Nobody has come to accept any of the six; everybody agrees about which ones are dead.
- In one discipline, first-year students spend their time on material produced in the last decade and treat the classic texts as history; in another, the classic texts are still read as live contributions and the newest work is read mainly for how it repositions them.
- A question that occupied a discipline for two centuries stops being asked — not answered, but displaced, because the vocabulary for asking it was replaced by a more tractable set of questions. Is the original question resolved, obsolete, or neither?
- Anonymous surveys of professional philosophers find overwhelming agreement on some questions and near-even splits on others, and the split questions generate most of the publications. What pattern of results would count as a discipline getting somewhere?
- A distinction introduced to solve one problem turns out to be indispensable in three unrelated areas, while the problem it was introduced for is now generally thought unsolvable.

Within an item, the three options should be recognizable as the three values of the axis that item probes. All three must be roughly equally attractive — no strawman a student would refuse to pick — and the option letter carrying a given value must vary from item to item.

**Scoring.** Follow the items with a three-by-three grid. For each of the nine cells give: a plain-English name of your own devising; one or two sentences stating the position; and one sentence on the most awkward thing that position is committed to. Then say which cells correspond to recognizable views, which are *unstable* — the criterion and the status verdict cannot coherently be held together — explaining briefly why, and which are empty but coherent, that is, unexplored rather than unattractive. If you think a cell should be subdivided, subdivide it and justify the move in a sentence. Include a tie-breaking rule for students whose five answers on one axis come out two–two–one or worse.

**Diagnostics.** Close with three notes: at least two pairs of cells the quiz cannot reliably separate, and for each, what an eleventh item would have to ask in order to separate them; what a student who splits evenly on the criterion axis should conclude about themselves; and which cell you expect to be over-represented among first-year graduate students, and why.

**Constraints.** No citations, no bibliography, no quoted passages, and no attributions to particular philosophers or claims about what any historical figure actually held. The handout must rest entirely on cases you construct.
