Right, bit of context first. I've been running the pre-departure side of a staff mobility office at a mid-sized UK university for about six years — we send maybe 120 people a year abroad on secondments, joint labs, fieldwork placements, anything from six weeks to three years. Part of what I produce is a "local norms pack" for each destination, and honestly the packs are now the most complained-about artefact I make. Returnees come back and say I instructed them to do something they found genuinely objectionable; hosts on the other end write in and say our people are stiff and rude; and our legal team periodically wants to know why we're telling staff to accept cash. All of which has forced me to admit I've never had an actual answer to the question underneath the whole enterprise, namely: when is a local convention something you *owe*, as opposed to something that's merely statistically common where you happen to be standing? I've read around this a fair bit over the years — the game-theory picture where a convention is just an equilibrium in a coordination problem and nobody owes anybody anything, the philosophy-of-law picture where convergent behaviour turns into a real rule at the point where deviations start getting treated as a reason for criticism rather than as a prediction error, the free-riding and reliance-interest arguments, plus about a decade of forum threads on Schelling points and whether "everyone does it here" is ever a reason to do it. What I want out of you is not another essay on that question. It's a procedure.

So: play the part of a moral philosopher who's done a decent amount of applied consultancy and who's comfortable writing for people with zero philosophy training. Before you draft anything, brainstorm out loud for a bit about the real structure of the problem — think carefully, don't rush to the deliverable. Specifically I want you to chew on the difference between (a) norms where compliance is self-reinforcing purely because everyone expects everyone else to comply, (b) norms where the cost of my non-compliance lands on some third party who had no say in the arrangement — the tip that comes straight out of a server's wage, the boarding position I decline to fight for that somebody behind me then loses, (c) norms that are just local habits with no compliance interest whatsoever, and (d) norms where the practice itself is doing something a visitor has independent reason to object to, and where the sanction for refusing falls mostly on the refuser rather than on anyone who relied on them. Please also handle the epistemics separately from the metaphysics, because that's where my advisers actually drown: they frequently cannot tell which bucket a norm is in, since everything findable online is written by tourists on holiday and their only other source is returnees with strong feelings and a story to tell. I want the procedure to say what to do under that uncertainty rather than quietly assuming the adviser knows.

Structure it as numbered steps an adviser runs in order on one norm at a time, with a stop condition at each step and an explicit statement of what counts as a *sufficient* answer to each question — I need to be able to tell somebody "if you can't establish X, you don't get to output Y". Budget is about fifteen minutes per norm, we cover fifteen to twenty norms per destination, and the adviser has no philosophy training and often no contact on the ground. There has to be a handoff point where the procedure stops producing an answer and escalates to a human being, because the output eventually ends up in writing that gets read by our ethics committee and by the traveller's line manager, so it has to be defensible. Ground it in whatever the philosophy actually has to say that survives contact with a fifteen-minute slot, but don't give me a reading list, a bibliography or any citations — I can't check them and I'd rather you didn't guess. And flag every step that is really a judgment call wearing a procedure costume; I'd much rather know where it's fake rigour than be sold something clean.

Then run it on these, all of which are live in current packs:

1. A city where sit-down restaurant service staff are paid well below anything we'd recognise as a floor and the tip is a large share of take-home, while our traveller is on a flat per diem that was costed on the assumption of no tipping — and who thinks the whole institution is extortionate and would rather not fund it.
2. A bus stop where there is no queue at all, boarding is a crush, and somebody who forms an orderly line reliably misses the bus. Our traveller's home instinct is to queue and they read the crush as rudeness rather than as a different equilibrium.
3. A city where crossing on a red man is universal, unsanctioned and completely normal, but where the law is strict and the on-the-spot fine is recorded against the host institution's sponsorship of the traveller — and our person is there for a school year with their kid, so there's a modelling question on top.
4. A partner institution where an arriving female colleague is expected not to initiate a handshake with senior male colleagues and to let the senior man order for her at working lunches. The sanction for refusing falls on her and on the relationship, not on anyone who relied on her — but the junior local women who are watching count for something too. I suspect your burden-transfer step is going to push towards "comply" here and I want you to say out loud if your own procedure over-reaches.
5. Standing example of the handoff category: an envelope of cash from a department administrator described as a settling-in allowance, not on any contract, where refusing is read as a slight on the department.

I want at least one of the outputs to be something the traveller will hate and something I'd have to argue them into. Give me the numbered procedure, a one-page flowchart-in-words version I could pin to a wall, a table showing each case with the bucket it landed in and the exact sentence as it would appear in the pack, and a short honest section at the end on what the procedure cannot do — where it will confidently produce a wrong answer, and how I'd notice.

British English throughout please, including the spellings, since big chunks of this are going straight into the handbook.
