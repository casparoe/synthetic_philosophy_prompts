For some months I have been working on a forecasting project whose central methodological dispute is between the inside view and the outside view. For questions of the form "will this large, long-lived political or monetary arrangement still be intact in thirty years?", one colleague wants to begin from the base rate for a reference class of comparable arrangements and then adjust for case-specific information, while a senior colleague declines to engage with the exercise at all, terminating each attempt with the same sentence: past performance is no guarantee of future results. He deploys it equally against anyone who cites historical frequencies of imperial contraction, currency-union breakup, or regime durability, and he treats it as decisive rather than as an opening move in an argument. What makes me suspicious is that the identical sentence is boilerplate that fund marketing materials are required to carry, where it plainly is not meant to undermine statistical reasoning about returns — and the discipline of base-rate forecasting is built on precisely the kind of inference he says the sentence forbids.

I am not looking for a defense or a refutation of either colleague. I want a disambiguation. Please set out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that this one sentence could be expressing. For each reading, do the following:

1. State it explicitly, spelling out what "guarantee" is being taken to mean (logical entailment, high probability, reliability under repeated sampling, or something else), whose past performance is at issue (an individual case, a class, or a generating process), and what work the negation is doing.
2. Classify it as analytic or near-analytic, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or self-undermining, and say briefly why.
3. Say whether it would actually license my senior colleague's dismissal of a base-rate forecast — and if it would, name the further premises or evidence he owes, such as a specific argument that the reference class has been broken by a change in the process generating outcomes, that its members are not exchangeable with the case in hand, that the observed frequency is itself a selected sample of survivors, or that the forecast is reflexive in a way that alters what it forecasts about.

Please also attend to two features of our setting that I suspect constrain which readings are even available. First, our reference classes are very small: a handful of long-lived polities, unions, or hegemons, each with distinctive initial conditions and no re-sampling, so it is not obvious what "the past performance" of such a class is supposed to be, nor whether the slogan has any purchase once stated. Second, some of the readings look like versions of the general problem of induction, while others are quite local empirical claims about particular processes; I want to know which of my colleague's uses actually depend on the global skeptical version, and whether he could use that version consistently while relying on frequencies everywhere else in his work.

Finally, identify the reading on which the fund-advertising disclaimer itself stands, and say whether that is the same claim my colleague is making or a different one. If the disclaimer's practical function turns out to be parasitic on some base rate or other — a statistical regularity about the persistence of performance differences — say so explicitly, since that would suggest the sentence is being used to defeat a form of reasoning it itself presupposes. Please close with a short ranked judgement about which single reading is most likely doing the work in his mouth when he says it at a methods meeting.
