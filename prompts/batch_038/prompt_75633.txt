I direct a mid-sized public planetarium and, in November 2030, we are co-hosting with our university's history of science department a two-day symposium marking the four-hundredth anniversary of Kepler's death — he died at Regensburg on 15 November 1630, aged fifty-eight. I have been asked to give the opening remarks myself, which is an odd choice, since I trained as a hydrologist and spent eleven years running operational flood forecasts before moving into public science; I have no Latin and no archival training. The room will be a genuine mixture: a third professional astronomers and people who forecast weather and hazards for a living, a third historians of early modern natural philosophy (several of whom work on astrology and will catch every loose claim I make), and a third secondary-school teachers and planetarium volunteers, most of whom are there because they have to explain Kepler to fourteen-year-olds. Please write the speech for me: eight to ten minutes of delivery time, roughly eleven hundred words, in English, meant to be read aloud rather than studied, with one or two places where a sentence is designed to land in a pause.

It has to be organized around a fact that anniversary rhetoric usually avoids. In 1619 Kepler published The Harmony of the World, in five books; the fourth is devoted to harmonic configurations in astrology, the fifth to the harmony of the planetary motions, and it is in the fifth that the third law appears. The claim that the earth has something like an animal soul which responds to the geometrical configurations of the heavens is not a superstitious footnote tucked behind the real work — it is an expression of the same conviction that produced the celestial physics: that the world is arranged in proportions a mind can perceive, and that perceiving them is not a spectator sport but something with consequences. So the speech should refuse two easy options at once. It may not treat astrology as a distasteful day job Kepler did to pay for instruments, and it may not treat it as a co-equal way of knowing that we have merely outgrown; the first condescends to him, the second to us. And keep it clear of the clichés — no man-of-his-time gestures, no tormented genius between two worlds, and no tidy analogy to p-hacking, to financial models, or to my own profession unless the analogy is made to do real work and then paid for.

There should be one moment of genuine discomfort that the speech declines to resolve. Kepler earned his living and much of his standing by casting nativities, including for a general whose campaigns killed tens of thousands, and for decades he wrote about reforming astrology rather than abandoning it — pruning whatever experience would not support while keeping what he thought it would. He also spent years defending his mother in a witchcraft prosecution, in a family and a region where that charge was not abstract. I want the tension stated plainly and left standing, because the honest thing about him may be that his self-criticism and his credulity were the same faculty. The ending, though, belongs to the teachers in the third of the room: when a student asks whether Kepler really believed in horoscopes, they should leave with something better than a shrug or a lecture about anachronism.

Please observe these constraints strictly:

- Do not put words in Kepler's mouth in quotation marks unless you are confident they are his and correctly rendered. Where you want his sense of something, paraphrase and mark it as paraphrase. No invented dialogue, no imagined scenes, no fabricated letter or diary passages; the historians will notice.
- Beyond what I have set out above, do not introduce specific titles, dates, thesis numbers, horoscope contents, or attributions. If you want to invoke his attempt to arbitrate between those who rejected astrology outright and those who defended it in full, do so in general terms unless you are certain of the particulars. A speech that is modest about detail and exact about its central claim will read as more scholarly, not less.
- One running image, not four. Give it a title. No stage directions beyond a single note if the lights are meant to come down at the end — the star field will be on.
