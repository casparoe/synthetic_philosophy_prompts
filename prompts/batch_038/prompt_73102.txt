**The position I want stress-tested.** I hold the following as a package, and I label the components so that you can refer to them precisely:

- **(a)** Phenomenal properties are real and irreducible: they are not identical to, and not reductively analysable in terms of, functional or physical properties, and there is a genuine explanatory gap between physical process and experience.
- **(b)** The physical domain is causally closed: every physical event that has a cause at all has a sufficient physical cause at that time.
- **(c)** Therefore phenomenal properties are causally inert. They neither produce nor contribute to any physical event — no neural process, no bodily movement, and, importantly, no verbal report.
- **(d)** The paradox of phenomenal judgment is not a genuine problem for (a)–(c). When I judge that I am in pain, or that my current experience has a reddish, throbbing character, the judgment is caused by the neural realiser of the experience, not by the phenomenal property itself; and because realiser and phenomenal property are linked by psychophysical law, the judgment reliably tracks the property anyway. On my view, reliability of this law-guaranteed kind is all that first-person authority over experience requires; the causal chain does not need to run *through* the property known. I therefore deny that epiphenomenalism leaves us without knowledge of our own experience.
- **(e)** I also refuse the weaker consolation prize. I do not think phenomenal properties are causally *relevant* in a difference-making or proportionality sense either — not in any sense that would license the ordinary claim that my pain caused me to wince, or that the redness of my afterimage caused me to reach for the colour chart.
