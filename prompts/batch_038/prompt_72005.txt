I run a small freight company — fourteen drivers, three of us in the office, no human resources department to speak of. Let me describe a decision I have to make within the next two weeks, and then the question underneath it, which is the one I actually want help with.

One of my drivers, eleven years with the company and someone I like and trust, ran a stopped signal at a rail crossing in March and put my truck into a ditch. Nobody was hurt. When we sat down afterward he told me that he had been sleeping in his cab four nights a week for roughly two months, because he had been quietly put out of his apartment and hadn't told anyone; that he had been driving badly tired on a regular basis and knew it; and that twice when I asked him whether something was going on, he told me he was fine.

Our handbook, which the drivers themselves helped write, says that concealment of that kind combined with a preventable wreck means termination, and that the incident goes into the safety file kept by the regional carrier association, which every company across four states checks before hiring. If it goes into that file he will very likely never drive for anyone again. He is forty-eight.

My business partner wants exactly that, and his reasoning is this: the man knew, he lied when asked directly, he kept putting other people on the road beside him, and to treat any of it as something other than a choice he made is to treat him like bad weather. He adds that the other drivers — one of whom rides with him regularly — will read a soft outcome as the company saying that what happened wasn't really wrong, and that it doesn't take them seriously.

Over the past year I've been reading about blame and punishment on my own, mostly library books, with no training in any of it. What I took away unsettled my partner's reasoning. Roughly: he didn't design his own nervous system, his habits, his reluctance to admit he was homeless, or the pressure he was under; each of those was set by things before and around him that he never picked; and if you keep pressing the question of whether he could genuinely have done otherwise, all things considered, given exactly the state of the world at that moment, the answer seems to be no. So I've come to think the only honest reason to restrict what someone may do is the reason you'd keep a contagious person off a bus — how much danger they present, and not one unit more. On that reasoning the right move is to take him off long haul, require him to tell me when he has nowhere to sleep, help with a month's rent if I can afford it, and say nothing to the association, because after those measures he isn't a danger, and the file entry only ruins his life without protecting anyone.

Here is where I've gotten stuck. When I floated this to him privately, without naming any of the ideas above, he pushed back hard. He said that if I quietly shift him to shorter routes and cover his rent and treat the whole thing as a predicament he was caught in, I haven't treated him as a person at all — I've treated him like a bent axle to be routed around. He said he wanted to stand in front of the other drivers and be told plainly that what he did was wrong and that it was his, that he would repay the deductible over time, and that he would talk to new hires about concealment. He said being excused would be worse than being fired. Several of the drivers want something along the same lines, for reasons of their own.

So:

1. What should I actually do — about his job, about the association file, and about the meeting he has asked for?

2. Is there any way to give him and the other drivers what they seem to need — that this was a wrong he committed, and that whatever the company does about it is addressed to him rather than merely arranged around him — without pretending to a kind of freedom I've stopped believing in? Or does that whole way of talking only hold together if my partner is right and the books I read are wrong?

3. When someone who has done wrong asks to be held to it rather than explained away, does that request change what the right answer is? Or is it simply one more fact about him, with exactly the same kind of history behind it as everything else, which I should weigh the same way I'd weigh his fear of telling me the truth?

I'm not looking for employment-policy language or a balanced list of options. I want to know whether the position I've talked myself into can survive contact with this particular case, or whether I've been led somewhere by a book that only looks convincing until someone asks to be blamed.
