> **(A)** There are two versions and they do different jobs. The Gay Science one is aimed at the reader as a personal test: a demon creeps into your loneliest loneliness and tells you this life, exactly as you're living it, will come back innumerable times with nothing new in it — every pain, every joy, every sigh, everything tiny and everything huge, in the same order and sequence, including the present moment and the demon himself. Then he asks how you react: do you curse him, or is this the one thing you'd want confirmed forever? That version works on you as an individual and it works through your attitude, not through argument. The Zarathustra version is a scene rather than an address: the dwarf sits on his shoulder at the gateway called "Moment," there are two long lanes, one behind him into eternity and one ahead, and the dwarf's point is that time is a circle so whatever is happening now has already happened endless times. Later, in the convalescence chapter, his animals say basically the same thing but cosily — everything goes, everything returns, the wheel of being rolls forever — and Zarathustra is repelled by them, because they've turned the heaviest thing into a comfortable little refrain and skipped the part where it has to be willed and endured. His nausea is specifically about the recurrence of the smallest man: not that pain comes back, because he can say yes to pain, but that the mediocre, the pitiful, the almost-human comes back forever too and there's no redemption that edits it out. That's what breaks him.
>
> **(B)** For Nietzsche it isn't a faith or a mood, it's a consequence of physics, and the Gay Science passage makes the argument right there: force is finite, time is infinite, so every combination of forces has already occurred endless times and must occur again. It's the same shape as the Poincaré recurrence result — a bounded system with finite states eventually returns arbitrarily close to where it started — so the modern version of his argument is stronger than he knew. Which means the test only really bites if the cosmology holds. If the doctrine turned out false — say the universe keeps expanding into heat death and nothing ever repeats — then the demon's question loses its teeth, because you'd know you only have to live this once and you could just endure it or optimize it. So the whole thing is hostage to physics, and honestly current physics isn't obviously on Nietzsche's side.
>
> **(C)** The one-change variant is basically the same test, maybe even a cleaner one. If you had to pick the single edit you'd carry into every future life, you'd be forced to state what you actually regret, which is exactly what the demon's question is getting at. And it has a practical upshot the original lacks: it tells you to prune. Cut the choices and relationships you'd have to keep reliving and you raise the average quality of the replay, so the test becomes a design problem — build a life whose balance sheet is worth endorsing, then you can say yes. Nietzsche's own answer, amor fati, points the same way: love what's necessary rather than wishing it were otherwise. So I'd accept the variant as equivalent.
