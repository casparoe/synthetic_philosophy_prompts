I write a weekly column on research methods and the ethics of prediction for a general-interest science desk, and I'm building an interactive sidebar to accompany a piece on the incompatibility results for algorithmic fairness — the theorems showing that calibration within groups, equal false-positive rates, and equal false-negative rates cannot all hold except when groups share a base rate or the predictor is essentially perfect. What I need is not a map of the metrics but a decision tree that sorts a reader into positions about what those theorems *mean*.

Please give me six or seven branch nodes, each phrased as a question a reader could answer without knowing which way it leads, and each anchored in a concrete case rather than an abstraction. I'd like the cases to include: a recidivism score that is well calibrated for both groups yet has unequal false-positive rates; the same score applied to a population where base rates happen to be equal; a care-management algorithm trained on prior spending as a stand-in for health need; a hiring screen where the threshold is chosen to maximize profit; and a predictor that is simply, exactly right. Let some nodes probe commitments rather than cases — whether a base rate should be treated as a fixed feature of the world or as itself the object of measurement, whether a fairness condition is an aim or a symptom-check, whether a score handed to a discretionary human decision-maker changes what fairness demands.

At the leaves I need at least these positions, each with a descriptive name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment: the theorems as a deep indictment of quantitative fairness; as an unsurprising trade-off no more scandalous than bias–variance; as a proof that differential base rates are the injustice to be surfaced; as downstream of the choice of target variable, so the mathematics is the least of the problem; as a case for procedural or deliberative fairness in place of any single metric; as scope-limited and therefore no excuse; and, if it earns a place, an abolitionist leaf that says the tools should not be run at all. Build the logical space first — the questions and the answer-combinations they license — then locate the positions in it, and tell me which combinations are empty and why: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate.

Throughout, distinguish what is settled as a matter of proof from what remains contested among specialists who know this literature, and do not let an interpretive reading appear as the consensus view. Citations are not needed; I care about the structure, not the bibliography.
