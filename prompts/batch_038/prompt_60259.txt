I chair the curatorial panel that sets the interpretive conventions for our national history museum, and I would like an analysis of our own record before we rule on a contested new case.

Eight years ago we began closing each gallery's wall text with a credit list, headed "Forces at Work," in which non-human entities may be credited alongside persons and institutions. Our dispositions to date:

1. The 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora and the ruined harvests of the following year: credited as an actor.
2. Yersinia pestis, in the Black Death gallery: credited as an actor.
3. The variola virus, in the gallery on contact and conquest: credited as an actor.
4. The same virus, in the later gallery covering a besieged frontier garrison in 1763, where the documentary record shows blankets taken from the fort's smallpox hospital being handed to Native emissaries: no credit. The text attributes the episode to the officers and does not name the virus.
5. The horse, in the Plains gallery: no credit; its role was written into the account of the peoples who bred, traded, and rode it.
6. Coal, in the industrial gallery: no credit; described as a resource, with credit going to owners, engineers, and miners.
7. The monsoon system, in the Indian Ocean trade gallery: no credit; the panel credited instead the navigators' seasonal knowledge of the winds.
8. The Little Ice Age: no credit; rendered as a shaded background panel headed "conditions," on the stated ground that it was a climate people lived inside rather than a force that struck them.
9. The drought years of the 1930s on the southern Plains: credited as an actor, over two recorded dissents whose authors wanted it rendered either as conditions or as inseparable from the plowing that preceded it.

Note that our refusals have taken two distinct forms, and we have never articulated the difference between them: entities demoted to background conditions, and entities folded into the agency of human actors.

The new case is atmospheric carbon dioxide in the Anthropocene gallery, which runs from the mid-twentieth-century acceleration of emissions to the present, so that the process in question has not closed. Three proposals are on the table. One member would credit carbon dioxide as an actor, since it was never enlisted to any human purpose and has turned against the purposes that produced it. Another would give it the background-condition treatment. A third would fold it into the industry and the consumers whose decisions produced it, on the ground that within the gallery's period the gas's chief effect on human affairs has run through human knowledge of it — the measurements, the models, the treaties — so that crediting the molecule double-counts agency already credited to people.

Please address the following.

(a) Reconstruct the candidate rules jointly consistent with all nine dispositions. Composite and conjunctive rules are welcome, and I want to know which candidate principles our record kills outright and which it demotes to necessary but insufficient. Say explicitly what a rule must do to generate the two different forms of refusal.

(b) For each rule that survives, state which of the three dispositions it yields for carbon dioxide, and identify where in the existing record that rule is most strained.

(c) Give your recommendation, name which of the nine cases is the closest analog, and state the respects in which that analogy breaks down.

(d) State what principle we would be adopting, and what we would be foreclosing, under each option. In particular: does crediting carbon dioxide concede anything to the worry pressed by the two dissenters in case 9 — that a non-human credit can absorb responsibility that belongs to identifiable human decisions? And would refusing it expose that our practice has all along been keyed to the kind of entity (microbe, eruption) rather than to any account of agency at all?

(e) Propose one further case, real or constructed, that would best discriminate among the surviving rules, and explain why nothing already in our record can do that work.

Assume our aim is consistency with our own precedents rather than correctness about agency in the abstract — but if you think consistency here would entrench a mistake, say so and say where.
