I'm a second-year graduate student in a history of science program, and last week's seminar on priority disputes left me more confused than when I arrived. We had been working through the Newton–Leibniz calculus controversy and the 1858 arrangement that put Wallace's paper alongside Darwin's material, and I asked two questions: whether the fact that the calculus was arrived at independently shows anything about how much credit either man deserves, and whether we have any reason to think the theory would have emerged within a decade or two regardless. My professor waved both away with the same gesture — the idea was in the air, so who got there first is not a deep question — and most of the room nodded, including, embarrassingly, me. Since then I've noticed that our reading list leans on Merton's claim that independent multiples are the dominant pattern in science and that singletons are the residual cases requiring special explanation, and that "in the air" gets used around the department as a summary of that claim, though I'm not at all sure the two say the same thing.

Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring back to seminar, the distinct precise theses that "it was in the air" might be expressing? For each reading I'd like a formulation with the quantifiers made explicit — inevitable for whom, by when, in what form, and with what probability — a verdict on whether it is a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive empirical claim, or simply false, and a note on what sort of historical evidence would confirm or undermine it. I'm especially interested in the places where the slogan splits apart: a claim about the actual past, namely that the tools, open problems, and notational resources were already available to a recognizable circle of practitioners, seems considerably weaker than a counterfactual claim that removing Newton and Leibniz from the world still yields the calculus; and both of those seem different again from a claim about how credit ought to be distributed. I'd also like the readings sorted into those that concern the inevitability of a result, those that concern the inevitability of its timing, and those that concern the inevitability of its particular framing or notation, since my suspicion is that the disputes about credit are mostly about the third. Please tell me, for each reading, whether it would actually license the two dismissals I was offered, or whether it would leave my questions wide open.

Finally, if you can do this without turning it into a survey: I'd like some guidance on the inference from the existence of multiples to inevitability. It strikes me that we notice the collisions and not the near-misses, and that the informative evidence would concern the distribution of time gaps between independent arrivals rather than the bare fact that some pairs landed within a year of each other — but I can't tell whether that is a real objection or a point that has already been met. While you're at it, compare "in the air" with its cousins, "the time was ripe" and the invocation of a Zeitgeist as the agent of discovery, and say whether those carry different commitments or merely different rhetoric.
