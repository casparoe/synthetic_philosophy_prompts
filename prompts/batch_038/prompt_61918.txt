On a school trip to the Natural History Museum I saw a drawer containing a single dried bird, labelled as the type specimen of its species. The guide explained that the name is pinned to that individual body: it stays attached to it even if the bird turns out to have been a hybrid, an albino, a juvenile, or one of a pair of species that look identical, and even if the population it came from has since changed. As I understand it, anyone naming a new animal species today has to designate an actual specimen, which is then deposited in a collection; a written description on its own will not do, though I have read that people argue about whether a photograph or a DNA sequence ought to count.

What confuses me is that this looks like a rule about pointing rather than about discovering. It is as though the joint in nature falls wherever one particular dead bird happens to sit. And yet taxonomists talk as though the boundaries they draw are out there to be found, and they split and lump species whenever the evidence changes, which suggests the type is meant to track something independent of itself.

Could you reconstruct as explicitly as you can the theory of natural kinds this practice presupposes — what it assumes about essences, about whether a species is a historical individual rather than a class of similar things, and about reference being fixed by ostension and a chain of usage instead of by a description? Then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with: could a convinced conventionalist, who thinks species boundaries are useful fictions we draw for the sake of communication, run exactly the same museum rules without embarrassment? And is anything internally unstable about pinning a name to one possibly atypical individual while claiming to carve nature at its joints?

Please also tell me what would change your mind here: which argument, or which fact about how taxonomists actually work, would push you to a different verdict, and where your reconstruction is most likely to have gone wrong.
