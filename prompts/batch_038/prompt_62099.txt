For several years I have allocated a fixed annual giving budget — and a modest amount of my own working time — under something like the evidential standards associated with effective altruism, while remaining substantially persuaded by the standard criticisms of those standards: that requiring strong evidence systematically favors whatever happens to have been measured, and that the same methodology which surfaces tractable wins also filters out the political and structural causes of the problems being treated. I am not asking for a defense or a refutation of effective altruism. I want to know whether my own case-by-case verdicts add up to a principle, and if they do not, which of them I should abandon.

Here they are, stated as concretely as I can.

1. A program distributing a well-studied preventive health commodity, with a cost-per-death-averted figure I trust to within a factor of two, versus a campaign to persuade one national ministry to adopt a clinical protocol whose adoption would, on my own estimate, do perhaps thirty times as much good, but whose probability of success and whose attribution I cannot estimate within an order of magnitude. I fund the first. I think this is the right call and simultaneously think it is partly an artifact of what has been studied.

2. The same campaign, now run by a group that has pre-registered its theory of change, published its forecasts with their calibration record, and submitted to third-party audit of its attribution reasoning. I fund this one, and more generously than case 1. Nothing about the intervention itself changed.

3. An education program whose outcome is captured by test scores, versus a transfer program whose outcome includes the absence of a documented humiliating procedure — queuing, means-testing, public verification of poverty. I believe the second produces more of what actually matters per unit of money and that I cannot measure it. I still do not fund it. I think my refusal is defensible but not clearly correct.

4. On earning to give: a friend who takes a lucrative trading position that would otherwise have gone to someone who would have donated nothing, and who donates forty percent of it — clearly creditworthy. The same friend taking a staff role at an assistance organization that would otherwise have gone to someone equally competent and equally committed — no credit, and perhaps a small negative. And a third version: the role is in a fast-growing research area, the seat would have gone to someone equally committed, but my friend is meaningfully better at the work — I award credit here, which I recognize sits badly with the second version.

5. I give to a municipal food pantry with essentially no evidence about its effects and feel no tension about it; I would refuse a global health program of exactly the same evidential standing. The difference in scale between them is enormous, and yet both verdicts feel right to me.

6. A program with a strong trial whose measured effect decayed to nothing within four years of the trial's end, versus a program with a weak trial whose gains appear durable. I prefer the durable one, even though the evidence for it is worse. So it is not evidential strength as such that I am tracking.

7. An intervention against loneliness among elderly people, evaluated in life-satisfaction reports, where I know the instrument partly records recalibrated expectations rather than change in condition. I have gone back and forth on this one for a year.

8. I think it is good to fund the study of an intervention that has not been measured — but only where the intervention is already independently plausible, and I cannot state what "plausible" means here without circularity.

Is there a principle, ideally separable into a rule for ranking options and a rule for setting the evidential threshold, that delivers all or most of these verdicts? Please state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each of its clauses, and tell me what it implies for questions I have not listed — in particular whether some share of a budget should go to reducing one's own ignorance rather than to acting on the ignorance one has, and whether the principle licenses a two-tier allocation in which unmeasurable goods receive a smaller but protected share. Then tell me plainly which of the eight it cannot accommodate, and press hardest on 4 and 7, the two I keep reopening. If you need a technical term, define it where you use it; do not send me citations or a reading list.
