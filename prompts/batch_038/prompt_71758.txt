I cover science and ideas for a monthly magazine and I'm reporting a long feature on whether the Allais paradox was ever genuinely answered or merely explained away. Before I commit several thousand words to a line of argument, I'd like you to make the case for a thesis I've arrived at and can't yet defend properly. I want an argument, not a survey: take a side and press it.

Here is the set-up I'll be working from, and which you should assume your reader already accepts.

Take the canonical pair of Allais problems. In the first, option A pays one million with certainty, while option B pays five million with probability 0.10, one million with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. In the second, option C pays one million with probability 0.11 and nothing otherwise, while option D pays five million with probability 0.10 and nothing otherwise. The robust pattern is A over B together with D over C, and that pattern cannot be produced by any utility function obeying the independence axiom, since the two problems share a common consequence.

Now suppose a theorist offers the following response, and suppose for the sake of the exercise that I have convinced myself the response is empirically correct in every detail. Call it the display thesis. The two problems as ordinarily printed are not descriptions of the same decision problem as the two problems printed as a single hundred-ticket grid, with the tickets grouped, aligned and labelled identically across both problems. Presented on that normalized grid, the common consequence is visible rather than inferable. In a study of twelve hundred subjects, the theorist reports, the violation rate falls from sixty-one per cent under the standard printing to seven per cent under the grid; the residual violators, interviewed afterwards, overwhelmingly say they read the first problem as involving two independent draws rather than one, and reverse their choices when the single-draw structure is stated aloud. The theorist concludes that the independence axiom was never under threat: the violations were artefacts of a description that concealed the common consequence, and once descriptions are normalized, expected utility theory is descriptively adequate as well as normatively unchallenged.

The thesis I want you to argue for is this. Even granting the display thesis in full — the sample, the collapse in violation rate, the interview transcripts, all of it — the independence axiom is not vindicated. The manoeuvre relocates the difficulty rather than resolving it. Either the normalized grid and the standard printing describe different decision problems, in which case the theorist has dissolved the data by substituting a new object of choice for the one people actually chose among, and has established nothing about preferences over lotteries as canonically described; or they describe the same problem, in which case subjects' rankings reverse under a redescription that leaves every prospect untouched, and it is description-invariance — the requirement the framing literature treats as a condition of rational choice in its own right — that has failed. On the first horn the display thesis is irrelevant to the axiom; on the second it is evidence against rationality, just different evidence. There is no third horn on which the theorist gets what he wants.

Please build that case, and build it to the standard I'd need in order to stake a feature on it.

Structure the response with the following sections, in this order, with roughly the lengths given:

- Standfirst — one sentence of thirty words or fewer, in the register of a magazine standfirst, stating the thesis.
- The thesis, precisely — about 150 words. Say exactly what is being claimed and, as important, what is not: this is not the claim that people really do violate independence, nor that expected utility theory is false.
- The display thesis at its strongest — about 250 words. Steelman it. Give the best reason for thinking a normalized display reveals preference rather than manufacturing it, and the best reason for thinking a normative theory is entitled to privilege canonical descriptions over the ones subjects happen to encounter.
- The dilemma — about 650 words. The main argument, as numbered steps. State each premise explicitly and tag it as a formal result, a conceptual claim, or an empirical bet, so that I can see which parts of the case are load-bearing and which are merely plausible.
- Objections and replies — about 450 words. Address at least four: (i) that a theory of rational choice may stipulate which descriptions are canonical without thereby changing the subject; (ii) that if violations also fade under repeated, incentivized play, what is being satisfied is invariance rather than violated by it, which removes the second horn; (iii) that the argument proves too much, since on this reasoning no framing effect could ever count as evidence about any axiom, which would make framing data epistemically inert across decision theory; and (iv) an objection of your own choosing that you judge most likely to defeat you — say why you picked it, and either answer it or concede what it costs.
- What would settle it — about 200 words. Specify what result, experimental or conceptual, would oblige me to drop the thesis, and say which of the two horns you think is the harder one to escape and why.

A few constraints. No citations, bibliographies, reading lists, or publication details — I can look those up myself, and a piece of writing that gestures at sources is worth less to me than one that reasons. You may name a theorist or a school where that is the most economical way of picking out a view: Savage's redescription in terms of changes in wealth, regret theory, rank-dependent utility, the dynamic-consistency worries Machina raised for non-expected utility models, the invariance requirement from the framing literature, the distinction between a normative and a descriptive failure. Use those as signposts, not as authorities; the weight has to come from the argument. Target roughly 1,700 words in total. Plain prose throughout, with the numbered steps in "The dilemma" as the only list in the piece.
