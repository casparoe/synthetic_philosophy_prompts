I'm a software engineer — I maintain the reference implementation of a wire-format spec for industrial telemetry (roughly: how a sensor out in a substation talks to a historian in a control room) — and I ended up as one of the technical members of our consortium's governance council a couple years ago after I fixed a nasty interop bug nobody else wanted to touch. I write code all day and I have zero formal training in anything social-scientific. Next month the council has to adopt a permanent rule for how we choose between competing technical proposals, and I've been asked to circulate something in writing beforehand. The draft bylaw currently says: every delegate submits a full ranking of all the proposals, we run instant-runoff on it, ballots are secret, and there's a clause saying delegates are expected to rank sincerely. Twelve voting delegates from member companies — who are, let's be honest, competitors — plus a chair who only votes to break ties. We do maybe six of these votes a year and everybody remembers roughly how everybody else voted last time.

Three things have muddied it. First, we paid an economist to run a half-day workshop for the council, and she told us that a theorem (Gibbard–Satterthwaite, I looked it up on the flight home) says a rule like ours is either a dictatorship in disguise or gameable, so we should stop pretending and just let the technical director decide, since at least that would be honest about who's deciding. Half the council is spooked by this, half thinks she was being cute. Second, a delegate I'll call Marek has counter-proposed a credit ballot: each delegate gets 100 credits to spread across the proposals however they like, highest total wins. He argues this inherits the incentive properties of a second-price auction, so nobody can gain by lying about their credits, and that it therefore dissolves the problem the economist raised. Third: in March, under a trial version of the ranked ballot, a delegate told me at the bar afterwards that he'd put a proposal he actually thought was reasonable in last place, because he figured that would knock out a rival in the first round. It worked. He wasn't even embarrassed, which is the part that stuck with me.

There's also a category of vote I don't know what to do with. A lot of our decisions aren't "pick one of four architectures," they're "pick a number" — max frame size, retry backoff ceiling, how stale a timestamp can get before we drop a reading. On those, delegates' preferences seem to fall off in both directions from some favorite value, and I have a half-memory that this shape is supposed to matter for whether the theorem bites, but I could easily be pattern-matching on something I skimmed once. So: write the memo I'll send to the council. It needs a bottom line we could actually put to a vote — which rule or rules we adopt, for which kinds of decision, and what happens to the sincerity clause — plus reasoning in language a smart engineer with no economics background can follow. Don't dodge the theorem and don't just gesture at it: I need to know whether the economist's "make it a dictatorship, it's more honest" conclusion actually follows, and whether Marek's credit ballot really gets the auction guarantee he's claiming or something weaker, and if weaker, what it does get. The numeric-threshold votes should get a concrete recommendation too, not a shrug. And be fair to whatever you're rejecting — give the strongest honest case for it (including the "manipulable in theory but almost never in practice" line, if you think that line has legs for a twelve-person electorate where everyone is a professional game-player) and then say why it doesn't carry the day. Don't send me a reading list or citations, I won't read them, and don't hedge into "it depends on your values" — its three weeks to the meeting and I need to hand these people something they can argue with and then vote on. Two pages, tops.
