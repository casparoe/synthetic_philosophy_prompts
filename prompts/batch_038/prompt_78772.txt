I do civic engagement work for a mid-size city and I read political theory on the side — lately almost all of it about sortition, citizens' assemblies, term limits, that whole debate about career politicians. There's a pattern I keep running into that I can't find a word for, and I want to know whether the field already has one.

The setup first. The standard pitch for replacing an elected legislature with a randomly selected body is that it kills the career politician: you're drawn once, you serve a year or two, you go home, nobody can build a power base. But then the standard objection is that amateurs can't run a state — no expertise, no institutional memory, no idea how the machinery works. And the standard answer to that objection is: give them a professional support structure. A standing secretariat. Briefing packs written by permanent staff. A curated expert panel. Trained facilitators who run the deliberation. Alumni of earlier cohorts kept on as mentors, or re-drawn, or hired to train the next cohort.

What I keep noticing is that the answer to the second objection quietly undoes the answer to the first one. The offices rotate; the people who decide what question gets asked, what counts as a feasible option, which witnesses get on the schedule, and what the final report actually says do not rotate at all. They aren't elected, they aren't drawn, they can't be voted out, and they're the only ones present for all of it. After a few cycles you don't get a legislature of career politicians, you get a permanent cadre of career enablers who hold no office and shape every cohort that passes through. Sortition doesn't remove the standing elite — it relocates it from the floor to the corridor and takes away its name tag.

Two things I've been reading that look like this to me:

- Ireland's constitutional convention of 2012–14. The 66 randomly selected citizens were the headline, but the agenda was fixed in advance by the resolution that created the thing, the secretariat was civil servants seconded from the Department of the Taoiseach, the research was run by a small standing academic group, and the recruitment itself was outsourced to a polling firm. Every one of those roles was permanent relative to the citizens' one-year turn, and none of them was randomly selected.
- The cottage industry of consultancies and nonprofits whose business model is recruiting and prepping citizen juries for whoever commissions them. Because there are only a few organizations with the panels and the know-how, the same handful of them end up being the gateway into "random" selection for a whole sector, and they carry the accumulated sense of what has worked, what framing survives contact with a real room, and which experts are worth calling.

Now, why the obvious labels don't seem to fit:

- Agenda-setting / framing: close, but that's about a decision on an agenda. I mean a standing structural position that outlives every turnover of the principals and gets stronger as the cohorts come and go.
- The Weberian point that officials rule because they have the files and the ministers come and go: right shape, wrong mechanism. A bureaucracy has legal authority over a policy area and answers to someone. These people have no authority whatsoever — they're designed to be neutral servants of a body that is supposed to be sovereign, and the whole legitimacy story depends on their input being just input.
- Capture, revolving door, rent-seeking: no. Nobody is being bought and there's no exit into industry to be bought with. The incentive is only to keep the process running and to keep being the ones who run it, which is much more benign-looking and much harder to police.
- The complaint that facilitators steer mini-publics: closest I've got, but as it's usually made it's about bias inside one assembly — bad moderation, cherry-picked witnesses, a loaded question. I'm after the multi-cycle version, where each individual assembly is run impeccably and the cadre forms anyway, purely out of memory plus turnover asymmetry.

So: is there an established concept for this — in the sortition literature, or in work on bureaucracy, deliberative democracy, or institutional design — that names the reconstitution of a permanent unelected cadre as a side effect of solving sortition's competence problem? It's definately not just "staff have influence." If there is a term, give it to me and draw its boundaries. If there isn't one, coin it: a definition, an explicit statement of what it is *not*, and at least two cases that look superficially similar but shouldn't count (say, an assembly whose support staff also rotate by lot, or one where the permanent staff have zero discretion over framing and just book rooms). I'd also like to know whether the thing is graded — is there some threshold of turnover asymmetry at which it starts to bite, or is it present in any sortition scheme that has staff at all? And does it have a natural remedy, or is the tradeoff between amateurism and cadre-formation just permanent?
