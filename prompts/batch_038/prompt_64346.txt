I work in failure analysis and reliability engineering — fifteen years of HAZOP studies, fault-tree construction, FMEA, and root-cause investigation of equipment failures on process plant. I am entirely fluent in that vocabulary and have no philosophical training whatsoever; the only philosophy I know is what colleagues have mentioned over lunch. I have been reading about al-Ghazālī's attack on the philosophers' account of causation in the Tahāfut — the fire and cotton passage, where he argues that the connection we take to be causal is not necessary, that God creates the burning on the occasion of contact rather than the fire producing it, and that what we call the natures of things is really a settled habit of succession which could in principle be interrupted, which is how he makes room for miracles. I understand Ibn Rushd's reply to be that this destroys knowledge, since knowing a thing is knowing it through its cause.

I want you to restate that position and that objection in the vocabulary I actually use — nodes and gates, initiating events and contributing factors, common-cause failure, latent conditions, basic-event probabilities, dependency structure, the difference between coincidence and causation in an investigation, battery limits, and the distinction between a root cause one can act on and one one cannot. Translate the following claims separately, one at a time, rather than giving me a general summary:

1. The connection between what is ordinarily called cause and effect is not necessary.
2. God creates the effect upon the occasion of the cause, not by means of it.
3. The Ash'arite doctrine al-Ghazālī inherits, that the power created in an agent does not itself produce the agent's act.
4. Al-Ghazālī's insistence that our knowledge of customary succession is genuine and reliable and that he is not abolishing it.
5. Miracles as the interruption of a habit without contradiction.
6. Ibn Rushd's objection that denying necessary connection between causes and effects is tantamount to denying that we know anything.

Then give me the residue report, in three buckets: what translates cleanly, what translates only by being distorted, and what has no expression at all in my framework. Keep the translation honest — do not import counterfactual, interventionist, or regularity-theoretic assumptions and present them as al-Ghazālī's view, and if my engineering vocabulary silently presupposes something he denies, flag that rather than smoothing it over.

Four specific questions I want answered on the basis of that mapping. First, a fault tree records dependency structure and probabilities and is silent on whether the dependencies are necessary — so can my vocabulary register any difference at all between al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd, or do the two positions generate identical trees? Second, an upstream cause common to every branch, which cannot be isolated or intervened upon, is not something we would ever put in a fault tree as a node; we would declare it outside battery limits. Is that where God sits in the translation, and what does that say about whether al-Ghazālī's claim is empirical, structural, or neither? Third, on my plant we informally say that when a failure pattern is too regular to be chance yet has no mechanism behind it we have found a hidden common cause. Does that phrase capture the notion of custom or habit, or does it distort it, and in which direction? Fourth — and this is what I most want your view on — is the residue a defect of my framework, something reliability engineering genuinely cannot express, or is it evidence that al-Ghazālī's claims are less substantive than they look, in the way that a disagreement which makes no difference to any fault tree might make no difference at all? If you think it makes no difference to the trees but a difference to something else, tell me what that something else is, in my terms if you can manage it.
