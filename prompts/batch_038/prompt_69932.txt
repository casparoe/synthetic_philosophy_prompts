I teach a philosophy of religion seminar, and this term I have paired the closing section of Yann Martel's Life of Pi with Pascal's wager. I want help making that pairing earn its place rather than sitting there as decoration, and I would like my own reading of it argued with rather than flattered.

Here is the reading I keep circling. The novel ends with Pi, in a hospital room, offering two incompatible accounts of what happened on the lifeboat: one populated by a hyena, an orangutan, and Richard Parker the tiger, and one populated by a cook, a sailor, his mother, and himself. Neither account can be verified, and the two investigators from the shipping company, when asked, say that the story with the animals is the better story — at which point Pi treats their preference as settling something about God. That looks like a wager: a commitment made under evidential indeterminacy, because of what the commitment is worth rather than because of what the evidence supports.

My worry is that the resemblance may be closer to a trap than to an insight, and I want the reading to test it against the argument itself. Pascal's wager is prospective, and it stakes an unbounded payoff on the truth of a proposition; Pi's choice is retrospective, made after the fact, about which account of a completed past he will carry, and its payoff, if it has one, appears not to depend on the chosen story's being true. If truth is irrelevant to the payoff, is this still a wager, or is it something else — an aesthetic preference, a fictionalist posture, the selection of a self-narrative — merely borrowing the wager's vocabulary? I have been calling this the difference between a prospective and a retrospective wager, and I would like you to say whether that distinction is real or whether it collapses under scrutiny, since a retrospective "wager" may have no stake to lose and therefore no structure to analyze. State precisely where the analogy holds, where it breaks, and what a defender of the analogy would have to surrender to keep it.

I would also like Pi's earlier religious situation treated in the same terms. As a boy he practices Hinduism, Christianity, and Islam simultaneously, and when his three mentors confront him he explains that he simply wants to love God. That reads like a hedge against the many-gods objection: hold every position at once and no cell of the matrix can be fatal. Does the hedge work, or does each tradition's demand for exclusivity render the portfolio incoherent? Then develop the structural contrast I think is the interesting one: in the religious case Pi's options are not forced, since he can have all three, whereas in the hospital room they are forced, since he cannot endorse both stories. William James's characterization of an option as living, forced, and momentous should let you say something exact about the difference, including whether the hospital choice is the only one of the two that is genuinely Jamesian.

Two further things. First, the novel's frame — the author's note, and the author-figure who goes looking for a story and comes home with Pi's — makes the selection recursive, since someone other than Pi also chooses the tiger story for payoffs of his own. Say whether that frame strengthens the wager reading or trivializes it. Second, tell me where the novel resists this entire line of approach, and where an honest critic must concede that I am reading a seminar into an adventure story; point me to specific scenes and moments I should put in front of my students, described rather than quoted. I do not want verbatim quotation from the novel, and I do not want citations, page numbers, or a reading list.

Close by saying what Martel's book contributes to the debate about religious belief under uncertainty that argument alone cannot supply: what the two-stories device reveals about wagering that the tidy cells and payoffs of the standard Pascalian matrix structurally cannot represent.

Please write in American English.
