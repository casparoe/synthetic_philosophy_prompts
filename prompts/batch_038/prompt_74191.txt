This spring the county historical society I direct will reopen its building, fourteen months after a fire in the annex destroyed roughly a third of the collection. I am giving the rededication address — ten minutes, about fourteen hundred words — and I want you to write it. Before you write a word, you need to understand what the most important loss in that annex actually was. In 1987 a retired schoolteacher deposited some ninety hours of taped interviews with Black and immigrant mill workers, on the express condition that they be catalogued and made publicly available. For the following thirty years our board declined to accession them, on the ground that magnetic tape was not a proper record, and the boxes sat unlabeled in the annex. Three of the people interviewed are still alive; one of them, aged ninety-four, will be sitting in the second row, and so will the schoolteacher's daughter, who has been asking for an answer in writing since the night of the fire. I sat on that board for twenty-two of those thirty years and never resigned over it.

The room divides into three blocs that barely overlap. The first is our old membership: descendants and longtime subscribers, mostly in their seventies and eighties, who want the evening to be about continuity, the building as their grandparents knew it, and the objects that survived. The second is the mayor, the county commissioners, and the donors' table, who have funded the new wing and who want an account of the night of the fire and of the eleven months it took to raise the rebuilding money — they want names, they want exemplars, and they would like the address to end on the future. The third is about two dozen people from a local labor heritage coalition, a graduate student who has published on the missing tapes, and a reporter from the weekly who will print whatever is said from the podium. The three of the surviving interviewees who can travel will be present, and the coalition has told me privately that they will walk out if the tapes are not mentioned by name.

I have read Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation closely enough to know what I want: the address should be built on the three ways history can serve life, and it should be honest about the fact that each of them, left to itself, turns into something that destroys the life it was meant to serve. But the address must never say so. There will be no philosophers in that room; naming Nietzsche at a rededication, or announcing a scheme, would convert the evening into a lecture and would hand the third bloc a reason to dismiss the whole thing as a rhetorical device. The three modes have to be present as an order of business the room can feel rather than as terminology it can recognize.

With that in mind:

1. Before the speech, give me a plan of about four hundred words: section by section, which mode governs that stretch, what you are asking it to accomplish in this particular room, and the specific degeneration of that mode you are steering around — in your own words, not in paraphrase of his. Where two modes come into open conflict, and in this room they will, tell me where and show me how the speech is meant to move across the seam. If you conclude that one of the three cannot honestly be given a place in this address, argue that rather than forcing it in.

2. Do not outsource the judgment. There may be no appeal to the verdict of history or of future generations, and no formulation that lets the society wait to be assessed. Someone has to say the thing in the present tense, in the first person plural, tonight.

3. Make no claim that the tapes can be reconstructed from other holdings, and no sentence that leaves a listener able to believe they can. Make no claim that our new accessions policy settles the matter, or that the society has made amends, or that the loss is in any respect a release.

4. There is exactly one place where the fact that the board was composed of people of their epoch may be mentioned, and it must be handled so that it does not become an exculpation. Write the passage in which the speaker says why the institution's having formed its own members is precisely what makes the failure the members' failure, and have him place himself inside that condemnation by name and by number of years. This is the hardest thing in the assignment; if that passage does not hold, the speech does not hold.

5. Use the monumental material, and use it truthfully — volunteers did carry the accession ledgers out through a window, and the town did raise the money in eleven months. But do not let the conduct of that night do the work of absolution for the thirty years before it, and do not end on the new wing.

6. Write it to be read aloud: sentences deliverable in a single breath, nothing that requires a footnote, and the admission of fault audible as such to someone standing at the back who is only catching fragments. I want the speech itself last, with the plan first.
