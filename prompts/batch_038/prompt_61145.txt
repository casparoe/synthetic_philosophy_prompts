I'm on a school-renaming committee in a small town and I could use help untangling a phrase that has taken over our meetings. The school is named after an 1870s state legislator — local doctor, founded the county hospital, treated everybody, generally a hero around here — who voted for a law requiring segregated schools and then wrote a column in the county paper defending it on the grounds that the races were different in kind. Every time somebody raises that, an older member of the committee says some version of "he was a man of his time" and moves right along, and the chair backs him up and calls the rest of us presentist.

Here's the thing that got me going: I went and read the actual record, and it isn't true. There were Black legislators in that same chamber who argued against the bill by name, a rival paper two counties over printing that he was wrong in the same month, and at least one man from his own party who voted no and said why. So he wasn't just swept along by his era — he was answered during it, and he answered back.

So what is "a man of his time" even claiming? It feels like its doing several different jobs and only some of them are any good, and I don't think anyone at the table knows which one they're reaching for. Can you lay out exactly five distinct readings of the phrase — what each one asserts, whether it's defensible on its own terms, and, crucially, whether it still gets you the dismissal given what I found above? I expect some of them to be flatly false, some to be true but doing no work at all, and atleast one to turn out to be a claim about how to write history rather than a claim about him. Numbered list, one or two sentences per item, no intro and no conclusion — I want to read it straight out at Thursday's meeting.
