You are a philosopher of science specializing in the epistemology of the historical disciplines, equally at home with a methods paper in archaeology and with one on confirmation. Please think carefully, and sketch the space of candidate principles for a few sentences before committing to one.

I retired four years ago after a career in experimental physics, and I have since been reading about how archaeologists claim to know anything, together with the long quarrel between the processualists and their critics. I find that I hold firm verdicts on particular cases and have no idea what rule generates them. All of the following are arrived at by gut rather than by argument.

1. Thirty fired-clay figurines from a single site all depict an animal that had been locally extinct for a thousand years. Verdict: license to say something about belief or story, not merely about local fauna.
2. In one village, every house doorway falls within four degrees of the same azimuth. Verdict: license to infer a shared building template; no license to infer a belief about the heavens, and none to infer a priesthood.
3. Stone weights from six sites in one valley fall on the same five values. Verdict: license to infer a shared standard — an institution — even though nothing at those sites represents it.
4. One burial out of four hundred carries copper ornaments. Verdict: no license to infer hereditary rank. The same excess repeated in each of six successive burial phases, in a row of adjacent graves: license.
5. A tool type occurs only in adult male graves. Verdict: license to speak of mortuary practice and of a category of person; none about who used the tool in life.
6. Every house in a village has a floor of burned grain, all of one date. Verdict: license to infer a single coordinated event; none to choose between attack and rite.
7. A pottery motif appears in one region, then eight hundred kilometres away two centuries later. Verdict: no license at all — neither migration nor transmission of belief.

Two pairs defeat me. Cases 2 and 3 are both unrecorded regularities, yet I read an institution out of one and refuse even a belief in the other. Cases 1 and 7 both involve representational objects, yet I let one carry belief and let the other carry nothing. My own tentative rule is that the difference turns on whether the people who produced the pattern could have been unaware of it, but that seems to give the wrong answer in case 6, and perhaps in 5 as well.

What I want is a principle, or a small explicitly ranked set of them, stated precisely enough to be falsified, that reproduces these verdicts — or most of them. For each clause, name the case that motivates it. Then apply the result to three cases I have not listed: pollen concentrated in a temple precinct, a vessel mended through drilled repair holes, and a substantial building left unfinished. Say what each licenses. Finally, state plainly which of my seven verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and whether I should abandon the verdict or complicate the principle.
