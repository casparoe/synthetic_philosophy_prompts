The talk is 45 minutes with 20 for questions at a philosophy of physics department. The thesis is deliberately not Bostrom's trilemma and I'll be annoyed if it gets folded into that. My claim is that the simulation hypothesis only turns into something like a scientific hypothesis once you stop talking about ancestor simulations and start talking about compute budgets: anyone running a simulation big enough to contain observers would be forced by cost to implement it lazily — details rendered on demand, at the coarsest resolution that keeps the inhabitants from noticing the seams. If that's right, a simulated world should carry the fingerprints of a lazy evaluator, and I argue three of our most famously weird physical features are exactly those fingerprints: state vectors that evolve smoothly and unitarily right up until some measurement-like interaction forces a definite outcome; no faster-than-light signalling, despite entanglement correlations that look for all the world like they ought to permit it; and entropy that always climbs in a closed system even though the underlying dynamics is reversible. So the slogan is that quantum mechanics is what an engine on a rendering budget looks like from the inside, and the second law is what garbage collection looks like. The argument roughly goes: (1) any simulation containing observers has to be run on finite hardware; (2) finite hardware plus observers means the cost of computing everything continuously is prohibitive while the cost of computing-on-demand is not; (3) a rational implementer therefore defers computation until an interaction forces a resolution; (4) deferred computation, seen from inside, presents as indefinite-until-interacted-with, as a speed limit on coordination between distant processes, and as a ratchet on bookkeeping you can't undo; (5) those are, respectively, superposition, no-signalling, and the second law; (6) so the simulation hypothesis makes a prediction that a brute-force simulated universe would not, and the prediction is borne out. My fallback line, which I'm not sure I believe, is that the hypothesis is falsifiable in principle because a world rendered continuously would show interference effects at macroscopic scales that we never see.
