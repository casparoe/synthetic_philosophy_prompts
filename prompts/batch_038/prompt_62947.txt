I've been sitting in on a clinical ethics seminar this term, mostly because my sister is a psychiatric nurse and she said I'd finally understand her job if I did. We've spent the last few weeks on advance directives and it's almost all been the dementia cases — the then-self versus now-self business, whether the person who signed the form is the same person lying in the bed, whether a life that looks contented from the outside can be the life the earlier self said would be unbearable. Honestly I find that whole set of cases kind of easy to talk past each other in, since everyone's intuitions about what makes a person persist do all the work before the argument starts. The version I can't shake is the psychiatric one. Someone with a recurring episodic condition — say bipolar disorder with a pretty predictable manic escalation pattern — writes, while stable, a directive that pre-authorizes involuntary admission and a named medication regimen for the next episode, and appoints their sister to be the one who triggers it. Then the episode comes. They're manic, they refuse, and here's the thing that's wrecking me: they don't obviously fail a capacity assessment. They can repeat back the diagnosis, the drug, the side effects, the last three hospitalizations. They can give reasons for their refusal. They just believe, with total conviction, that this time is different and they're fine. My seminar leader wants us to pick a side, and I want to be assigned the side nobody in the room wants to take, which is that the directive should be enforced.

So make that case for me, properly. Argue that the authorization given in the lucid interval outranks the refusal given in the episode. Part of what I want is an account of what the authority of the earlier self actually consists in here — is it a consent that persists until revoked, a transfer of decision rights that survives the transferor's change of mind, a standing instruction about which of the patient's own values gets to speak for them, or something else entirely? Because whichever mechanism you pick is going to do most of the load-bearing work, and I'd rather you name it than wave at "autonomy" generically. And the argument has to walk through the objections rather than around them: the personal-identity line, that the now-self is arguably a different subject with genuinely different preferences so enforcement is just paternalism aimed at a stranger; the capacity line, that if the patient passes the standard understanding-appreciating-reasoning-expressing-a-choice test there is no non-arbitrary place left to intervene, and that leaning on "appreciation" to disqualify them is question-begging dressed up as a finding; the betrayal line, that being forcibly medicated by people you love, acting under instructions you yourself wrote, might be worse than ordinary civil commitment because the violation has been laundered through your own autonomy; and the cold-hot gap, that the stable self simply cannot imagine what it is like to be the manic self, so their judgment about that state was never informed in the way that makes it authoritative.

Two more things. First, tell me where the argument has to be fenced in to stay honest — what conditions the directive has to meet before it's enforceable at all. Specificity of the treatment named? How recent the signature is? Whether a clinician outside the family has to certify that the triggering state has arrived? Whether the right to revoke exists only while lucid, and how you'd police that without collapsing back into the capacity problem? Carve-outs for anything with permanent or irreversible effects? I suspect a fairly tight fence, but show me yours. Second, and this is the part I actually care about most: admit where the case fails. Point at the one or two objections you can't fully answer and say what weaker claim you'd retreat to — the directive as very heavy evidence for the surrogate rather than as a command, or enforceable only for a short window, or something else. I don't want the whole thing hedged into "it depends on the details," I want an argument with a spine that knows where it's brittle. Something in the 900-1300 word range. No reading list or citations needed, I'm not writing a paper, I just want to see whether the position is defensible at all.
