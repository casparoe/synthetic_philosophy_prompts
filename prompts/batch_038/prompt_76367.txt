I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training; I own the pricing service for a two-sided marketplace that dispatches on-call tradespeople — plumbers, electricians, locksmiths, water-damage crews — to residential customers. The fare is a base rate times a demand multiplier computed from real-time request volume against available providers in a geohash, with a hard cap of 3.5×. Because the service runs in the US, my team maintains an emergency-mode flag that clamps the multiplier toward 1.0 in states under a declared emergency. Three features of the legal regime that flag implements have been nagging at me, and I want to know what account of the wrong in question they presuppose. Wherever you need a philosophical term, please reconstruct the position first and give me the label afterward rather than assuming I already have the vocabulary.

First, the on/off switch is political rather than physical. California Penal Code § 396 makes it unlawful, for thirty days after a presidential or gubernatorial proclamation of a state of emergency or a local declaration, to sell emergency supplies, building materials, housing, transportation, and similar items for more than ten percent above the price that seller charged immediately before the proclamation. Florida's § 501.160 treats it as prima facie evidence of an unconscionable price that the amount charged grossly exceeds the average price at which the commodity was sold or offered in the thirty days immediately before the governor's declaration. So the identical multiplier my service emits can be lawful on the Tuesday before a hurricane makes landfall and unlawful on the Wednesday after, with no change on my side of the ledger in cost, supply, or demand. The only thing that changed is that an official signed something. Meanwhile our 3.5× at 2 a.m. during a January freeze — a customer with a burst main and exactly one provider within forty miles — is legally unremarkable and, as far as I can tell, morally invisible to everyone involved.

Second, the test is baseline-relative, and the escape hatches differ in a way that looks philosophically loaded rather than accidental. Both statutes measure the offense against a historical price rather than against the seller's cost or against what the buyer's alternatives actually are. But California permits a higher price if the seller proves the increase is directly attributable to additional costs imposed by its own suppliers, or to additional labor and material costs, and only up to that cost plus the markup the seller customarily applied before the emergency. Florida additionally excuses an increase attributable to regional, national, or international market trends. On its face, then, California appears to hold that only cost-push increases can ever be legitimate, while Florida appears to concede that a price can be legitimate simply because the market as a whole moved — that is, because demand rose. One label, two states, apparently two different theories of what makes a price defensible.

Third, in Florida the prohibition is enforced by the state attorney or the Department of Legal Affairs, and the statute states explicitly that it creates no private cause of action in favor of the person damaged by the violation. The gouged customer cannot sue. Contract law's own doctrine of unconscionability, which uses closely related language, is structured the opposite way: the aggrieved party raises it.

What I'd like from you:

1. Reconstruct the account of the wrongness of disaster pricing that this architecture presupposes. If the wrong is taking advantage of desperate people, why is the trigger a signature instead of a measure of desperation? Does keying on the political event systematically over-include and under-include relative to the wrong, and in which direction does it err more often? Commit to one reading of the trigger: a crude proxy for buyer vulnerability, a proxy for something else entirely (the breakdown of the normal background conditions under which market allocation is supposed to be legitimate, or the violation of a temporary duty of social solidarity), or a purely administrative convenience carrying no philosophical content at all.

2. Say whether the baseline-relative test expresses a cost-of-production theory of the just price, a status-quo or entitlement theory under which a seller forfeits the moral permission to revise terms it was already charging, or no theory at all — just a bright line chosen because it is administrable. Use the California-versus-Florence difference in the exceptions as your evidence, and tell me which of the two states is more internally coherent and which is smuggling in a claim it would not endorse if asked.

3. Explain what the absence of a private right of action presupposes about who is wronged by gouging, and reconcile that with the fact that the practice is universally described as a wrong done to particular buyers. Is this a public-harm model, an evidentiary pragmatism, or a sign that the legislature does not actually believe the individual-transaction story it tells?

4. Identify the rival accounts this design quietly rules out or simply never engages — including, if they are relevant, the view that the objection is not about price levels at all but about what market pricing does to the character of certain goods and to the community that allocates them this way, and the view that there is no wrong because both parties gain and the alternative is empty shelves. Tell me which rival the practice is most incompatible with and which it merely fails to honor.

5. Design question, and the one I actually care about: suppose I replaced the declaration flag with a vulnerability rule computed from data I already have — number of alternative providers within a radius, essentiality of the requested service category, time since the request was opened, whether the customer has already been quoted and refused elsewhere. What theory would my system then be implementing? What would it get right that the statutes get wrong, and what would it get wrong that the statutes get right — including things like predictability, gaming by sellers, and the ability of a regulator to check the rule after the fact? Give me a definite recommendation on whether a marketplace should implement to the letter of the statutes or to the theory behind them, and state plainly which of the three features above is the least defensible.

Please be committed throughout. I don't want a survey of readings with the choice left to me: take a position on each numbered item and give your reasons. Do mark clearly which parts of your reconstruction are forced by the statutory structure I've described and which are speculation about what legislators were actually thinking, since I can't tell the difference from where I sit.
