I am at work on a novel set in a near-future jurisdiction whose appellate courts have formally adopted Epicurean thanatology as settled doctrine: death is no injury to the one who dies, all good and evil lie in sensation, and an estate therefore cannot recover for a decedent's death as such. One chapter opens with a document rather than a scene, and I need that document written.

Please compose an extract from the majority opinion of an invented appellate court in an invented jurisdiction, disposing of a consolidated appeal arising from a single fatal accident. About 1,200 words in total: a caption, a short statement of facts, numbered Parts, and a dissent of roughly 250 words at the end. The register should be that of a competent, mildly vain judge who enjoys writing.

The defendant manufactures a wearable device advertised as reducing the wearer's risk of dying, and the plaintiff's evidence includes the defendant's own long-running campaign urging consumers to dwell vividly on what they stood to lose. Two claims are on appeal: the estate's action for the death itself, and an action by the decedent's adult daughter for a falsehood published about her father after he died, which he never learned of and never could have. Her brief also argues, in the alternative, that her father was cheated of decades he would otherwise have had.

The constraints matter more than the plot, so please observe them strictly:

- The majority must be internally consistent Epicureans rather than strawmen, and a reader who knows this literature should recognize the strongest available form of the view. It should be able to hold that the fear of death is a real and compensable injury, that the length of a life is indifferent, that prenatal and posthumous nonexistence stand on precisely the same footing, and that any harm requires both a subject who suffers it and a time at which he suffers it.
- The comedy must come from doctrine applied with a straight face, never from wackiness. In particular the court should confront and dispose of: the timing problem, and its discovery that if no moment of injury can be located, the limitation period has never begun to run — this passage should be handled with total seriousness, as a genuine procedural dilemma; the deprivationist rejoinder that a person can be made worse off by losing a future he never experiences; the defense's symmetry reply to the daughter's alternative argument; and the consequence that the court's own rule makes an undiscovered betrayal of a *living* person no harm either, which the court should embrace rather than flinch from.
- The punchline should be doctrinally exact: the estate recovers nothing for the death, but the daughter's second claim is remanded in part, because the defendant's advertising did produce a compensable injury — a documented interval in which the decedent was conscious and afraid. The obvious loss fails and the strange one succeeds.
- The deadpan must hold throughout. No winking, no asides to the reader, no sentence signaling that the court suspects it is being absurd.
- No real names. No historical or living persons, no actual cases, statutes, or articles, and no verbatim quotation of any real text: the school's founder may be invoked reverently in the court's own prose, but the doctrine must be paraphrase throughout. Every authority cited should be invented in-world and obviously fictional.
- The dissent should articulate the deprivation account in a judge's voice, without naming a philosopher, a book, or a school of academic ethics, and should be permitted exactly one damaging question that the majority has not answered. End the extract there, before any reply, so the reader is left holding it.

The piece has to work for two audiences at once. A reader who has never encountered this debate should experience it as a slightly chilling legal document about a bereaved daughter who loses. A reader who has should be laughing at the jurisprudence, and should be able to tell that the author of the opinion understands exactly what the doctrine costs.
