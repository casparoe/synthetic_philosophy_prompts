I am a junior, and I write for my school's philosophy journal. Last month our principal opened a student-led assembly on climate policy, and partway through her remarks she quoted what she called an old Greek proverb: "A society grows great when old men plant trees whose shade they know they shall never sit in." It received the loudest applause of the morning.

Ten minutes earlier, a classmate of mine had stated the objection the assembly was meant to answer. His version ran roughly as follows: reducing emissions now imposes real costs on people who are alive today, while nearly all of the benefit accrues to people who are not yet born; those future people will, on any plausible assumption about economic growth, be considerably wealthier than we are; so transferring resources from a poorer present to a richer future is not obviously fair, and it is certainly not the kind of thing a proverb can settle. He also trailed off on one point that I could not reconstruct afterward — something about how the policies under discussion would themselves change which particular people are born, so that the beneficiaries are not a fixed group waiting to receive whatever we send them. Nobody picked that up. The principal engaged with none of it. She gave the proverb, and the discussion ended.

I would like to be able to say precisely what went wrong, or right, in that exchange. So: what distinct claims could that one sentence be making? Please give me a menu rather than your favorite reading. I want the trivial version, the substantive-but-defensible version, and the version that is simply false laid side by side so that I can tell them apart. For each reading, say what sort of claim it is (about motivation, about where value has to be located in order to count, about institutions as opposed to individual character, about probability, about age, or something else entirely); whether it is a platitude, a genuine thesis, or a mistake; and, most important for my purposes, whether it does any work against my classmate's objection or merely restates the conclusion he is disputing.

Be explicit about one feature in particular. The proverb stipulates that the planter *knows* he will never sit in the shade. I suspect that stipulation is doing quiet work, because most of what is actually said in defense of climate action concerns benefits the actors themselves may receive, or may not, depending on contingencies. If any reading depends on the stipulation rather than on the tree-planting, please flag it as such.

Please organize your answer in five titled sections, in this order: "What the proverb was being asked to do" (about 150 words, reconstructing my classmate's objection as charitably as you can, including the point he failed to finish); "The readings" (the bulk of the response, 800 to 1,000 words, numbered, each with its verdict attached); "Which readings actually answer him" (about 200 words); "Settled versus contested" (about 150 words); and "What I would say at the next assembly" (about 100 words, giving one sentence I could defend out loud in front of the people who applauded).

On the fourth section: please distinguish the claims here that are settled among people who work on these questions from the ones that are genuinely live. Intergenerational ethics strikes me as a field in which many confident-sounding positions turn out to be minority views, and I do not want to stand up in a debate and announce as common ground something that most specialists would reject. If a reading you have classified as a platitude is in fact disputed by competent people, say so rather than tidying it away — and where the disagreement is real, say roughly what the disagreement is about, without pretending there is a consensus that does not exist.
