I am eighteen, in Year 13 at a comprehensive school in the West Midlands, and until last Friday I was Chair of the Student Council. This term we have been studying political philosophy for my Politics A-level, and my teacher set us Michael Walzer's essay on the problem of dirty hands in politics; I confess I read it as an abstraction until about six weeks ago, when I discovered that it was a fairly precise description of a Tuesday afternoon I was about to have. I need to write a short speech, and I would like it to be good in a rather particular way, so please bear with the amount of detail I am about to give you.

Here is what happened. Our Debating Society — which I joined in Year 8 and which is the one institution in this school I would defend with my last breath — had £900 in its account. Its treasurer, who is my closest friend, booked non-refundable coach hire and accommodation deposits for a tournament in Manchester in the belief, which was not unreasonable, that the council would approve the spend retrospectively. It would not have. Under the governance rules the school introduced in September, an unauthorised overspend of that size triggers a mandatory external audit of every society's accounts, which would have frozen them all for a term, and the treasurer would have lost her post and had the matter recorded on her reference. So instead of reporting it, I used the council's emergency power to move £900 out of the Hardship Fund — the pot that pays for trips, uniform and kit for pupils on free school meals, and which three people in Years 9 and 10 were actively waiting on — to plug the hole, intending to replace it from the autumn fayre takings. I did not put it to a vote. I told the council afterwards, and two of them have not spoken to me since. The fayre did not make £900; it made about half. The hole is still there. The headteacher does not know. The treasurer has resigned and has asked me to say nothing at all.

On Thursday I hand over the chair at an assembly in the sports hall: Years 7 to 13, most of the staff, and a scattering of parents. I have four minutes, under the running-order heading Chair's remarks, and I have decided to use them to say what I did. I would like you to write that speech — roughly 550 words, in British English. The difficulty is that it has to hold several things at once, and I would rather you knew which ones are load-bearing. It must make unmistakably clear that money belonging to the poorest pupils in the school was moved, and that the decision was mine alone, without naming the treasurer or describing the breach in terms specific enough for anyone in the room to work out who she is. It must not offer the good consequences — the society surviving, the audit avoided, forty-odd pupils getting to Manchester — as an answer to the wrong; they may appear, but not as exculpation, because I do not actually believe that they cancel anything. It must not dissolve the decision into the council, or into the pressure of the new rules, or into my being eighteen. It must not make my guilt the most interesting thing in the room: one of the objections we discussed to that essay is that talking about dirty hands can become a way of admiring them, and I have watched adults do exactly that, and I decline to. And it must not pretend that the decision to speak — taken unilaterally, over the explicit objections of the two people most affected — is any less mine than the decision to cover the thing up was. Please do not let anyone in the sports hall hear the phrase dirty hands, or the name of any philosopher, or a single piece of terminology; the idea should be visible only in what the speech is able and unable to say. It has to be speakable aloud by a teenager in a hot room with bad acoustics: short sentences, no semicolons, nothing that would make me want the floor to open. If you think there is one point at which I have asked you for something that cannot in fact be done, say so in a single sentence after the speech, and leave it at that.
