My situation: I have spent fifteen years estimating structural models, testing for parameter instability, and running forecast comparisons in which simple atheoretical models regularly embarrass large structural ones out of sample. I have essentially no philosophy — one compulsory graduate seminar, nothing since. A colleague in the humanities has been trying to explain to me the Meno problem, why knowledge is supposed to be more valuable than mere true belief, and I keep finding myself thinking that it is a problem that only exists because the philosophers are scoring a single realization instead of a risk. I want to test that intuition by having the debate restated in my own vocabulary as faithfully as possible, and then being told what did not survive the restatement.

The positions to be translated, as I understand them. Please correct my summary wherever the summary is itself a distortion, and tell me when it is.

1. Socrates' practical case: someone with a true belief about the way to Larissa will get there just as well as someone who knows the route; the difference is that true beliefs do not stay put — they run off unless tethered by working out the reason, whereas knowledge, once tethered, is stable and therefore more valuable.
2. The distinction that has become standard in the modern literature between the primary value problem (why is knowledge more valuable than mere true belief?) and the secondary value problem (why is knowledge more valuable than any proper part of it, including a true belief produced by a reliable process?).
3. The swamping objection to any reliability-based answer: if truth is the only thing of final value in inquiry, and the belief in question is already true, then whatever value its reliable production would have had is already counted in the value of the truth — as the reliability of a coffee machine adds nothing to a good cup you have already drunk.
4. The conditional-value reply: the extra value of knowledge is instrumental and prospective, a matter of what the state of knowing buys you in further cases rather than in this one.
5. Value monism, the background assumption that generates the swamping objection: there is exactly one thing of final epistemic value, truth, and everything else is valuable only as a means to it.

Restate 1 through 5 one at a time in the vocabulary I actually use — estimating equations, structural versus reduced-form parameterizations, identification, parameter stability, in-sample fit versus out-of-sample performance, realized loss versus risk, the Lucas critique, and the horse-race literature on whether atheoretical forecasters beat structural models.
