Quick thing. I'm rebuilding a unit in my philosophy-of-science seminar on periodization — how we carve up time, and who gets to hold the knife — and the Anthropocene is going to be the centerpiece. What I want from you is not a survey; I have surveys, stacks of them. I want an essay that takes a position on a question I keep watching people slide right past: are "when did the Anthropocene start?" and "who gets to say?" two separate questions, or is the first one secretly the second? I got into a fruitless arguement at a workshop last month with a geologist who insisted the 2024 vote settled nothing philosophically and a historian who insisted it settled everything politically, and neither of them could tell me what they actually thought the right start date was. That's the failure mode I want your essay to avoid.

So here's the board, roughly as I'll set it out for the students. The candidate dates people fight over: the Neolithic, pushed back to the first irrigated rice paddies and ploughed fields, some eight thousand years ago; the Industrial Revolution around 1800; 1610, the so-called Orbis spike, which is a real dip in atmospheric CO2 in the Antarctic ice-core record that Lewis and Maslin attribute to reforestation across the Americas after the post-Columbian population collapse; and 1950, marked by bomb-test plutonium, which is the one the Anthropocene Working Group actually formalized, with a proposed golden spike in the sediments of Crawford Lake in Ontario. In 2024 the stratigraphers voted that proposal down — the subcommission rejected it by a wide margin, largely because the sedimentary record at the proposed site is thin and the proposed start date is too recent to function as an epoch boundary — and the international bodies confirmed the rejection while also saying, in effect, that the term would go on being used as a descriptor of human impact anyway. Hold onto that last part, it matters.

Here's the wrinkle I actually want you to chew on, and it's my own framing rather than something you'll find printed anywhere, so don't go looking for it: notice that every other boundary in the timescale is picked by a marker, not by a cause. Nobody asks what caused the thing that made the Cretaceous-Paleogene layer, and nobody would retract the boundary if it turned out the impactor was a comet rather than an asteroid — the layer is the layer, and the story behind it is a separate inquiry. The 1610 proposal looks different, because it seems to have been chosen in part for what it would say about who did what to whom, and then fitted to a wiggle in an ice core. So run the counterfactual. Suppose the wiggle is real but the causal story is wrong: suppose the dip turns out to be entirely drought-driven — and there is a live competing hypothesis along those lines — with no depopulation, no reforestation, no genocide anywhere in the causal chain. Does the Orbis proposal collapse? If it does, you're letting a moral and political consideration veto a stratigraphic one, which is exactly what geologists say their discipline can't allow. If it doesn't, then why 1610 rather than 1780 or 1950, all of which are just as wiggly? I want you to pick a horn.

Second wrinkle, on the who-decides half. Read the 2024 rejection charitably and uncharitably. Charitably: the Anthropocene is real but not yet *recorded* in a form that clears the evidentiary bar — an instrument problem, not a metaphysical one, and the vote was a disciplined refusal to date something whose signature is still accumulating. Uncharitably: the vote exposed that "epoch" was the wrong category all along, because you can't draw a lower boundary around a process that is still running, and the whole exercise was a category error in a lab coat. Which reading is right, and does it matter that the body doing the voting is staffed by people whose professional norms were built for a past with nobody in it — norms that are very good at excluding agency, and therefore arguably bad at recognizing the one interval in which agency is the thing to be measured? If you think the comittee's composition is epistemically irrelevant, say so and defend it; if you think it's relevant, tell me what a legitimate decision procedure here would look like.

Structure it with these sections, in this order, roughly these lengths:
