Our national authority for listing threatened species is rewriting the rules of its scientific review panel, and I have been asked to circulate a proposal. The trigger was last month's deadlock: a reviewer recused himself over a past consulting relationship, leaving ten voters split 5–5 on a grassland songbird, and neither our statute nor our bylaws says what happens next. The listing is on hold, and the committee must decide both what to do about that species and how eleven reviewers should reach a yes/no verdict in future.

Three designs are on the table: (a) keep the current practice, in which the eleven deliberate around a table until they reach consensus and only the outcome goes on the record; (b) after reading the same dossier, all eleven vote simultaneously and independently, with no discussion, majority decides; (c) split into three subpanels of three, each deliberating internally, and combine the three subpanel verdicts by majority.

Two features of the panel complicate the choice. All eleven were trained in the same handful of programs and run the same population-viability software, so when one of us is wrong about a species' trajectory the others are likely to be wrong in the same direction. And two members are markedly better than the rest at reading survey data: under (b) their votes weigh no more than anyone else's, while under (a) they tend to dominate the room and pull the panel with them. One colleague insists that discussion is what makes our verdicts survive judicial review, since it surfaces evidence that never reached the dossier and lets a reviewer discover she was simply wrong; another insists that discussion is precisely what wastes the value of having eleven heads, because everyone converges on whoever spoke first.

Draft the memo I will send the committee. It should recommend a disposition for the songbird and a design for the panel going forward — one of the three, or a fourth you think beats them — with reasons members could actually argue against, and it should give the strongest case for the option you reject rather than dismissing it. Say what our rules should require about recording individual votes and margins, and state the assumptions your recommendation depends on along with the evidence that would show them failing.
