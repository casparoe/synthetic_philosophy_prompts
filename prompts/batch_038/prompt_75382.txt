I am auditing a graduate seminar on early modern philosophy, and our last two meetings have been consumed almost entirely by Berkeley's challenge to conceive of some object — a tree standing in a park, a book shut in a closet — existing all the while unperceived by anyone. The seminar has settled, far too quickly for my taste, on the familiar diagnosis: Berkeley trades on a scope ambiguity, since one can perfectly well conceive that an object exists unconceived without conceiving of it as unconceived, so the challenge is either trivially satisfiable or trivially impossible depending on where the operator is placed. I have to present a paper at our reading group in three weeks, and I want to defend a different diagnosis instead — one a friend of mine, who works on the semantics of demonstratives, sketched on a napkin over the weekend and which I have so far been unable to articulate properly.

Call it the anchoring reading. The thought is that Berkeley is not asking what propositional content can be entertained, and he is not merely sliding between conceiving and perceiving; he is demanding a conception whose object is fixed independently of the very act by which it is conceived, in the way a demonstrative picks out that particular ship on the horizon, or a photograph of an empty room depicts a room whose emptiness is not a product of the photographing. When the materialist answers the challenge, on this reading, the object she offers is anchored by nothing but the act of offering it, and so the presentation of the object and the presentation of its independence from the presentation collapse into a single act. The conclusion is therefore not that unperceived existence is inconceivable, but the narrower claim that no conception can, in virtue of being a conception, present its object as existing unconceived. Please make the best available argument for this reading and for the further claim that it earns its keep: that the conclusion, though far short of immaterialism, is enough to disable the materialist's central appeal to an unperceived substratum. State the anchoring condition precisely enough to be applied as a test, inventing whatever notation you find convenient, and give two worked examples from outside Berkeley's own case — one conception that is anchored and one that is not.

Three constraints. First, the argument may not appeal to God, to the supposed passivity of ideas, or to any likeness principle relating ideas to things; I want to know what survives once those are set aside. Second, it must meet three objections head-on rather than gesture at them: that the anchoring reading merely relocates the scope confusion into a vaguer idiom; that the condition proves too much, since on its own terms one could never conceive of anything as having a property the conception itself does not supply; and that even if the condition holds it is a thesis about how we refer, and hence perfectly compatible with robust realism about matter. Third, no bibliography, no references to the secondary literature, and no quotations from Berkeley — paraphrase the challenge in your own words, since I have the texts in front of me and want the reasoning rather than the scholarship. Aim for roughly twelve hundred words, flag honestly the step at which your case is weakest, and close with two or three sentences stating what the materialist's best reply should be if everything you have argued is correct.
