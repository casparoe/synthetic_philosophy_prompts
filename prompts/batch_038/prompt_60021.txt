Acting as a philosopher of physics who's also patient with non-specialists — someone who has genuinely thought hard about supertasks, Thomson's lamp, and the "infinity machine" literature rather than just skimmed it — I need help with a story. I cover science and ideas for a general interest magazine and I'm writing a piece about whether a machine that does infinitely many steps in finite time could be a coherent idea at all: the hypercomputation pitch, where you grind through every natural number before lunch and then print out the answer to Goldbach's conjecture or whatever.

Two of my sources gave me essentially the same line and I want to stress-test it before it goes into print. The line is: "Thomson's lamp is just Zeno's paradox with a light switch. Everyone agrees Achilles catches the tortoise, and everyone agrees a bouncing ball that loses a bit of energy each time will do infinitely many bounces and come to rest — nobody calls that a paradox. The intervals shrink geometrically and sum to something finite, that's the whole story, and the lamp 'puzzle' is people forgetting that." From it they conclude two things: (a) Thomson's argument that supertasks are impossible just fails, and (b) so there's nothing incoherent about a machine that finishes infinitely many computation steps before noon and then reports a result.

Please think carefully before answering, and if it helps, start with a little scratch work — a few lines brainstorming what the two cases really share before you commit to an audit. Then give me:

1. The correspondence table. What in the Zeno / bouncing ball picture plays the role of the lamp's toggle, the shrinking intervals, the finish time, and the final state? Be explicit about what "the state at the end" even means in each case, because I suspect that's where the trouble is.

2. Where the mapping breaks, and — this matters more — which of those breaks actually do damage to (a) and (b) versus which are cosmetic. One reader of my draft made an objection I couldn't answer: for the ball, where it ends up is fixed by the physics, you can just extend the trajectory continuously right up to the finish time, whereas for the lamp the sequence goes on-off-on-off forever, there's no quantity converging to anything, and so the state at noon is up for grabs. Is he right that this is the whole ballgame, or is he overclaiming? And does his point imply the ball never really completes its infinitely many bounces either — i.e. is the ball a genuine supertask at all, or does the idealization do the work?

3. What the analogy quietly imports from the source that the target doesn't have. Continuity? Determinism by law? Bounded speeds? A well-posed answer to "what happens at the limit point"? Tell me which of these are load-bearing for the argument my sources are making.

4. Whether (a) and (b) survive once the disanalogies are on the table. My hunch is that they come apart — that the ball might kill Thomson's argument without buying anything for the hypercomputation pitch — but I could easily be wrong, and if so say why.

5. If the analogy is bad, what's a better one that a general audience would actually get? Give me something I could drop into a paragraph, plus a line or two on what the new analogy can and cannot carry, because I don't want to just trade one misleading picture for a prettier one.

Roughly 900–1300 words, prose plus the one table is fine. No bibliography, no list of papers, no "for further reading" — I'm not citing anything and I don't want you guessing at references, I just want the reasoning. If a step in your argument is genuinely contested among people who work on this rather than settled, flag it in a sentence so I don't write it up as consensus. And if part of my sources' line is actually just right and I should keep it in the piece, say that plainly — dont sandbag the analogy to make your answer more interesting.
