I work in commercial mediation and our house rule for dividing a joint surplus is basically the Kalai–Smorodinsky thing without anybody ever naming it: we work out each side's walk-away position and its best conceivable outcome, then settle on the single t that puts both parties the same fraction t of the way from the first to the second, so the gains are proportional. Two bits of it have started to bother me. One is that a party's best conceivable outcome counts in full even when it rests on an option nobody would ever actually exercise — say they bought up a patent that only pays off if the other side cooperates perfectly, which will never happen, but it still raises their ceiling and so raises their share. Two is that the same mediators will let each side simply assert its best case pretty much unchallenged, while insisting that the walk-away number has to be backed up with real accounts and real alternative offers.

So: what theory of fairness does this practise presuppose? Is it the idea that you're owed something in virtue of what you could have got rather than what you contribute or what you can credibly threaten, or something else entirely? Reconstruct the implicit view, then tell me how much of it the rule strictly needs — could a contribution-based or a threat-based account license exactly the same procedure, or does the rule quietly rule them out? And if we decided unexercised options shouldn't count, what would have to change in how the rule is actually run day to day? Finally, is the asymmetry between the two numbers — best case taken on trust, walk-away audited — defensible on any principle, or does it just reward whoever can state a grander ceiling with a straighter face?
