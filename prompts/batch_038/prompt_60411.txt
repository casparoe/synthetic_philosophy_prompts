You are to act as an expert in applied ethics who has spent a substantial part of your career advising international development organisations on integrity policy — someone equally at home in the philosophical problems surrounding gifts, obligation, and bribery and in the practical reality of running a country office. Please think carefully before drafting, and let your first pass be diagnostic rather than rhetorical.

I chair the Programme Integrity Committee of a mid-sized European development funder. In nine days we decide a referral against the director of our country office in a West African partner country, and I must circulate a draft decision memo in advance. Work from the facts as our secretariat recorded them rather than from a tidied-up version of them.

The director is a national of the country where he works, recruited locally eleven years ago. The referral was brought jointly by two expatriate secondees serving two-year postings. Three matters are referred.

**One.** Eleven months ago he hired his wife's cousin as the project's full-time driver, at the standard national salary scale, after advertising the post locally and receiving four applications, two of which came from candidates who did not hold a licence valid for the vehicle class the project uses. He did not declare the relationship on the annual conflict-of-interest form, which asks about "family members employed on the project." He states that he read the form as covering relatives by blood or marriage in the first degree, and that in his own context a wife's cousin counts as family in a sense the form does not capture.

**Two.** On three occasions over two years, project staff paid small sums — €40, €65, and €110 — to secure the release of donated medical equipment held at a port and at two road checkpoints. Two of the payments were receipted by the collecting agency as "handling fees"; one was not. The director authorised all three, on the stated ground that the alternative was a six-week delay in a maternal-health programme. He reported all three in the quarterly narrative to headquarters, under "logistics," and not in the payments register, which he understood to apply only to payments made to public officials.

**Three.** He accepted an invitation to the wedding of a district official's daughter, attended with his spouse, and was seated at the head table. He contributed nothing. The district official is the counterpart who signs the project's land-use permits, which were granted eleven days later. Photographs of the seating circulated among local staff. The director says attendance was expected, that declining would have been read as a deliberate slight, and that the permits were long overdue and granted on their merits.

One further fact is in the file and cannot be left out of the memo: in the same period, our own head office presented a €2,000 "courtesy gift" to a visiting parliamentary delegation at home. It was recorded, approved at director level, and occasioned no comment.

The governing provision is Section 6.2 of our Code of Conduct, drafted by an outside consultant and adopted four years ago without debate. It reads: "A gift or payment is permissible where it is customary in the locality, proportionate to local income, disclosed in writing to the funder within fourteen days, and capable of being described by the recipient to a third party without embarrassment. A gift or payment whose value to either party depends upon its not being known is impermissible regardless of amount."

The committee is evenly split, and the split is not along the lines anyone predicted. One camp holds that the clause is a laundering device: everything disclosed becomes permissible, so a systematic pattern of small payments clears itself simply by being documented, and the second limb collapses into the first. They also hold that if a payment is wrong, recording it does not make it right, and that our standards travel with our money. The other camp holds that the clause is sound precisely because it tracks the difference between a public economy of mutual obligation and private extraction, that a €65 port charge and a €2,000 courtesy gift may be morally identical or morally opposite depending on facts the clause is trying to get at, and that a funder which applies its home standards to locally hired staff while exempting its own head office is engaged in something worse than inconsistency.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate. It must:

- recommend a finding on each of the three matters — upheld, upheld in part, or not upheld — with the reasoning for each stated so that a panel member who has not read the case file could follow it, and so that the director could answer it point by point;
- recommend a single overall disposition, including any corrective or disciplinary element and any element addressed to the complainants or to head office;
- state plainly what should happen to Section 6.2 — retain, amend, or replace — and, if replace, give the panel a test it could actually apply to an unfamiliar case inside a two-hour meeting;
- include the head office's €2,000 courtesy gift in the analysis rather than treating it as a distraction, and explain whether it is relevant as a comparison or as an objection;
- set out the strongest consideration on the losing side of the committee's split, in the form its own proponents would recognise, and say why the memo nonetheless comes down where it does.

Before writing, spend some time reasoning privately: list the considerations that genuinely bear on each matter, separate the disagreements that are about what the facts mean locally from those that are about principle, and identify where the two camps are actually talking past each other. Then draft.

Aim for something between 1,100 and 1,400 words, in the register of a formal committee document. Do not include citations, footnotes, references, or a reading list; the argument must carry itself. Do not deliver a memo whose operative recommendation is that both camps have a point, and do not resolve the case by splitting it three ways for the sake of collegiality. If you conclude that one of the matters cannot be decided on the present record, say so and specify exactly what further fact would decide it.
