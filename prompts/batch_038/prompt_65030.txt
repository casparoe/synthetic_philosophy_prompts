I direct the analysis unit of a state resilience fund that commits roughly $400 million a year to long-horizon risk-reduction work: surge barriers, grid hardening, pathogen surveillance, and a small number of speculative hedges. Our enabling statute requires that every proposal be scored by its expected net benefit in dollars, and that the board rank proposals by that score. Two features of the work have started to break that mandate.

First, our outside modelers increasingly deliver scenario tables in which payoffs grow geometrically while scenario probabilities decay geometrically, so the products never shrink. Second — and this is the part our counsel cares about — the ordering of the rows in those tables is a bookkeeping artifact. In one recent submission the scenarios were sorted by a consultant's internal identifier; sorting the identical table by payoff magnitude instead changed the reported expected value dramatically. Counsel reads our charter as requiring that a proposal's score be invariant to how an analyst enumerates or groups its scenarios, on the grounds that two enumerations of the same table describe the same proposal and cannot be scored differently.

So I need a documented procedure that (a) tells a junior analyst with no measure theory, working from a spreadsheet, whether a given table has a well-defined expectation at all; (b) if it does, how to compute and defend it; (c) if it does not, what to record, what to do with the proposal, and at which point the question stops being analytical and goes to a person; and (d) survives the obvious audit question, which is why two structurally identical tables are scored differently. For context on the operating conditions: the board meets quarterly and sees about forty proposals a cycle, so triage has to take an analyst twenty minutes or less per proposal; the Chief Risk Officer signs off on the scoring memo; and questions of value rather than fact go to a standing values committee of three board members, which meets monthly.

You are a decision theorist who has done this translation work before. You know the literature on games with infinite, divergent, and genuinely undefined expectations — the St. Petersburg family, its alternating-sign successor the Pasadena game, and the further constructions in which the positive part and the negative part of the payoff distribution each diverge — and you also know that almost none of it has ever been operationalized inside an institution that has to publish a number. Work carefully. Before drafting, think through privately which candidate remedies would break on which tables: capping payoffs, applying a discount rate, truncating the tail, reporting an interval-valued score, comparing by dominance only, restricting to "natural" orderings. Let that deliberation determine the structure of what you deliver, but do not show it, except insofar as it shapes the final section. Define every technical term at first use in language a bright non-specialist can follow. I do not want references or a reading list — I want something my analysts can run and something counsel can defend.

Please structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Design constraints** (~150 words). The requirements the procedure must satisfy, including the invariance requirement and the statutory demand for a single dollar figure, and the precise point at which they conflict.
2. **The triage test** (~300 words). The concrete quantities an analyst computes from the scenario table, in order, to sort every proposal into one of a small number of classes — well-defined and finite; infinite with a determinate sign; genuinely undefined; and any further class you need. It must be executable in a spreadsheet without measure theory, it must terminate, and it must say what to do when the modeler supplies a continuous distribution rather than a list of scenarios.
3. **The decision procedure** (~700 words). Numbered steps keyed to the classes from section 2: what to score, what to write into the memo, what counts as a sufficient justification at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a refusal to rank, and exactly which steps hand the decision to the values committee instead of resolving it analytically.
4. **Why invariance does most of the work** (~400 words). The theoretical rationale, including what specifically goes wrong if analysts are permitted to order scenarios by magnitude or to group them in pairs, and why absolute convergence rather than convergence is the correct gate.
5. **Worked cases** (~900 words). Run the procedure end to end on the three proposals below, showing the numbers an analyst would compute at each step and the resulting board treatment. Verify or correct the headline figure the sponsoring team reports in case C.
6. **What the procedure cannot do** (~300 words). Its failure modes, the proposals it will misclassify, and the places where a step that looks like computation is really a judgment call wearing a procedure's clothes.

The three proposals, all competing for the same tranche:

**A. Harbor Reach.** A conventional surge-barrier and pump program. All payoffs are bounded and cost is already netted out: net benefit of $900 million with probability 0.05, $150 million with probability 0.25, and −$40 million (overrun with no protection benefit) with probability 0.70.

**B. Cascade Facility.** A reinsurance-style instrument we would purchase for a $50 million premium. The counterparty's own tail model, discretized into scenarios indexed n = 1, 2, 3, …, assigns scenario n probability 2^(−n) and net payoff after premium of $1 million × 2^n. There are no loss scenarios.

**C. Ledger Swap.** A hedged position in which scenario n has probability 2^(−n) and net payoff of $1 billion × 2^n / n, positive for odd n and negative for even n. The sponsoring team reports a headline expected net benefit of roughly $690 million, obtained by summing the products in increasing order of n.

For case C in particular, tell me how the procedure ranks it against A and against B, and what it publishes, given that the statute demands one figure. I expect the result to be one the sponsoring team will contest, and I want the procedure to hold up when they do. Close section 5 with one or two sentences saying what argument would convince me to abandon the invariance requirement — that is, what case there could be for privileging the natural enumeration after all.
