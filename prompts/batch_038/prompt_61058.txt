So I got roped into refereeing a chapter for one of those interdisciplinary volumes — neuroscience people, consciousness people, a couple of philosophers for legitimacy — and the author is a genuinely good cognitive neuroscientist who is trying to do right by the literature but is plainly not a metaphysician. Early in the chapter there's a boxed half-page gloss of Kim's exclusion argument, and it turns out to be load-bearing: the last third of the chapter leans on it to argue that neuroscientists can just carry on treating beliefs and decisions as causes without touching any of this stuff. I've taught the argument enough times that I can't trust my own ears anymore — it's very easy to start hearing errors in a student's paraphrase that aren't actually there, and equally easy to let a confident-sounding box slide past. So I want an outside audit before I write the report.

Here's the box, transcribed as it stands: "Kim's exclusion argument is the classic objection to the view that the mind can be both physical and genuinely causally effective. It starts from the causal closure of the physical domain: every physical event has a physical cause sufficient for it. Take a decision to raise an arm, and the arm's rising. Neuroscience tells us the rising is fully produced by neural events — motor cortex, spinal cord, muscles. If the decision is nothing over and above those events there is no puzzle; but the nonreductivist insists it is something more, realized by the neural events without being identical to them. And here closure does the damage: those neural events are already sufficient for the rising, so the decision has nothing left to contribute. Kim mentions that one could instead treat the rising as overdetermined, caused both by the decision and by the neural events, but wholesale overdetermination of that kind isn't something anyone else accepts, so it isn't a real option. The conclusion is that the decision, so understood, is causally inert. Which leaves a forced choice: either the physical world is closed and our thoughts don't move anything, or physics is incomplete and minds push matter around. Kim takes the first horn and accepts the consequences. For a working scientist, though, the choice looks false. We have overwhelming evidence that mental states are brain states — lesion work, imaging, the whole mechanistic story — so there is no competition between the mental cause and the neural cause; they are the same thing, and the puzzle dissolves. Kim never seriously considers this reply, I think, because it would end the argument before it got started."

My own instinct, for what it's worth, is that he's collapsed two premises into one and then congratulated himself for escaping a dilemma that was never on the table — but I could be wrong and I don't want to write that in a report if it's just me being a pedant about a box aimed at a general reader. So: go through it clause by clause and sort it into three piles — what a specialist working on mental causation would sign off on as accurate, what is a defensible simplification that costs nothing, and what is actually wrong or misattributed. I'm especially interested in a few specific questions. Is it fair to say closure alone "does the damage," and what work is the overdetermination premise really doing here — is treating it as a throwaway that "isn't a real option" a harmless compression, or does the whole dialectic depend on it in a way the box hides? Does the gloss get right who the target is, i.e. could a nonreductive physicalist read this box and recognise their own position as the thing under attack, or has it quietly been turned into an argument against dualism? And does the final paragraph follow: is "mental states are brain states, so there's no competition" a reply that Kim ignores, or is it something else entirely — and if it's something else, what's left of the claim that the puzzle dissolves and that scientists get a free pass? Finish by telling me, in a sentence or two each, which of the replies people actually press against the argument — the proportionality line, the interventionist line, whatever else you think is live — this author would have to engage if the box were rewritten, and give me the three or four concrete changes I could put in the report as requests rather than as complaints.
