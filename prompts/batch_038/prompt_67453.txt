You are a philosopher of mind who specializes in consciousness, sleep, and the metaphysics of mental states, and who has taught Norman Malcolm's argument that dreams are not experiences side by side with Dennett's confabulation-based reply for many years. Please think carefully about the structure of this dispute before writing anything, and treat the assignment as a piece of instrument design rather than as a survey.

I am preparing materials for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind. Half the students have never encountered Malcolm, and the ones who have tend to collapse the whole debate into a single question — did something happen during sleep, or didn't it? I want to give them a decision tree that sorts theories of dreaming by the commitments those theories actually have, so that the seminar can see how many distinct questions are buried inside the familiar one.

Before writing the tree, spend a short paragraph listing the candidate branch questions you considered and saying which you discarded and why. Discard anything that turns on a term of art the students would have to already know, and anything on which the interesting positions do not genuinely come apart.

Constraints on the tree itself:

- Ten to twelve nodes, eight to ten leaves, no more than five levels deep, and at most three exits per node. Present it in indented markdown, with each node phrased as a plain-language yes/no question about a case.
- No node may use vocabulary from the literature — no talk of phenomenology, confabulation, criteria of identity, intentional objects, or cognitive access. Every node must be answerable by someone who has read nothing on the subject and has no reliable introspective access to their own sleeping mind.
- At least eight of the nodes must be built out of the following cases, which are the ones my students actually argue about: the sleeper woken from non-REM sleep who insists nothing happened all night while showing entirely normal REM earlier; the report of a long, eventful dream on waking when only seconds of sleep could have elapsed; the immediate waking report that changes substantially when retold ten minutes later; the sound in the room that gets absorbed into the dream; the false awakening, where the dreamer dreams of waking up; the lucid dreamer who reports having decided something inside the dream; the person who reports vividly that they dreamed but cannot produce a single item of content, only the sense that something occurred; and the person woken mid-dream who knows at that moment that they were dreaming.
- Every leaf must carry: the name of the position if it has one (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it); a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment; and a location tag. For the location tag, use a distinction I have been using with students and cannot find in the literature: does the theory locate the dream in the episode, in the report, or in the gap between them? Sort every leaf into exactly one of these three, and say whether the tripartition exhausts the space or whether some leaf sits uneasily in it.

Required leaves: Malcolm's view that the concept of a dream is tied to the telling and that nothing need have occurred during sleep; Dennett's view that something does occur but not when it seems to, with the content supplied at or after waking; the orthodox view that dreams are experiences during sleep and the report is a fallible memory of them; the view that dream experience is genuine yet does not require a subject aware of it at the time; the view that dreaming is a species of imagination rather than of experience or perception; the functional view on which dreaming is the running of an offline simulator, whether or not anyone experiences it; and the eliminativist view that there are no dreams, only dream talk. You may add further leaves if the logical space demands them, but leave at least one cell unoccupied and diagnose it.

Then supply three things after the tree:

1. A table of the unoccupied combinations of answers your tree makes available, with a one-line diagnosis of each — is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply never been explored?
2. An explicit account of the pairs of leaves your tree cannot really separate. I expect Malcolm and Dennett to be the hard case here, since they may agree about whether anything happened and disagree about why; say whether any single question could pry them apart, and if not, say so plainly rather than manufacturing a distinction. Flag any other pairs that collapse, including whether the imagination view and the simulator view end up extensionally equivalent.
3. A closing paragraph on what building the instrument reveals about the debate: how many separate questions are hiding inside "are dreams experiences?", and which of them does the real work.

Do not quote any text and do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations — I will handle sourcing. Paraphrase the positions in your own words, and where you are unsure of a detail in the literature, say that you are unsure rather than filling the gap with a confident-sounding invention.
