I am working through Newton's *Principia* in Andrew Motte's English translation (the 1846 Chittenden American edition), and I have been treating the Definitions as neutral technical scaffolding that the Scholium which follows them then loads up with metaphysics. Two passages are making me doubt that, and I would like help reading them.

From the explication of Definition III (*vis insita*):

> "Resistance is usually ascribed to bodies at rest, and impulse to those in motion; but motion and rest, as commonly conceived, are only relatively distinguished; nor are those bodies always truly at rest, which commonly are taken to be so."

From the explication of Definition V (centripetal force):

> "A stone, whirled about in a sling, endeavours to recede from the hand that turns it; and by that endeavour, distends the sling, and that with so much the greater force, as it is revolved with the greater velocity, and as soon as ever it is let go, flies away. That force which opposes itself to this endeavour, and by which the sling perpetually draws back the stone towards the hand, and retains it in its orbit, because it is directed to the hand as the centre of the orbit, I call the centripetal force."

Three questions. First, in the sling passage, is the stone's "endeavour to recede" simply a name for a measurable tendency disclosed by the tension in the cord, or is it a real striving attributed to the body? Does the answer matter for the dispute between substantivalism and relationism — that is, does this force talk already require a standard of non-acceleration that is not analyzable in terms of the stone's changing distances from other bodies? Second, here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the Definitions are not metaphysically neutral, because the claim that the tension varies with the velocity "as it is revolved" presupposes a determinate fact about the stone's true rotation, and nothing among the surrounding bodies fixes that fact. Tell me whether that is right, or whether I am simply reading the Scholium's absolute space backwards into text that does not yet contain it. Third, the closing sentence of Definition III seems to concede that bodies we take to be at rest are not truly at rest. Does that commit Newton to a notion of true rest that is in principle unobservable, or could a relationist accept everything in these two passages — a fact of the matter about true rotation, grounded in forces — while denying absolute place and denying any fact about uniform velocity? Put differently, how much of the substantivalism is licensed by these passages themselves, and how much is added later by the Scholium's distinction between absolute and relative quantities and its appeal to the effects that separate true motion from motion relative to other bodies?

Please keep to the text and its arguments; I do not want a reading list or attributions to secondary authors. What I do want is candour about status: separate the points where the textual situation is settled from those where interpreters of Newton and of this debate genuinely disagree, say explicitly if my hypothesis rests on a contested reading or on a conflation of a dynamical issue with an ontological one, and do not present one interpretive school's conclusion as though it were the consensus.
