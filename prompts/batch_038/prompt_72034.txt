My dissertation supervisor is drafting a chapter that sorts present-day moral campaigns according to whether they express ressentiment in the sense of the first essay of *On the Genealogy of Morality*. Her verdicts so far. A tenants' association whose only leverage is publicity and which describes itself almost entirely as the opposite of its landlords: ressentiment. A philanthropist endowing a hospital wing who speaks about what she is building and hardly at all about the diseases she opposes: not ressentiment. A monastic order that treats the body and its appetites as the enemy: not ressentiment but a case of the ascetic ideal, on the grounds that the monks are not reacting against anyone and their denial has an object of its own. A movement of descendants of a persecuted people that campaigns for its own history to be taught and never mentions the persecutors: not ressentiment.

Her new case is a well-funded advocacy organization founded by descendants of that same persecuted people, now among the wealthiest and best-connected in the country, whose entire output ranks its members against the descendants of the former persecutors and insists that those persecutors are the real losers, and whose constructive program amounts to nothing more than an invitation to become like its members.

Predict her verdict. Lay out the candidate rules that accommodate all four earlier verdicts — including one keyed to whether a group can still discharge its affect in action rather than settling for imaginary revenge, one keyed to whether its own good can be stated without reference to an enemy, and one keyed to the historical origin of its values — and say which she is most likely running and why. Identify the closest precedent, state what principle her ruling would commit her to in either direction, and finish with one further case that would separate the rules you take her to be choosing between.
