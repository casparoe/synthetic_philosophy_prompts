I run the review desk at a mid-sized fact-checking outfit. When a claim lands, we hand it to a panel of five to nine volunteer reviewers, they each read the evidence and vote yes/no on one binary question ("is this claim false?"), and I certify whatever the majority says. Lately I've been worrying that we're treating Condorcet's jury theorem like a law of nature when half its assumptions dont hold for us at all.

Some numbers from our own logs, so you've got something concrete to work with. Over the last ~400 certified votes: panels where every member had drawn on the same two or three sources agreed unanimously about 92% of the time and were later overturned 19% of the time; panels with genuinely mixed source diets agreed ~71% of the time and were overturned 6%. Also, when the first two votes posted come in the same direction, the rest of the panel follows 84% of the time, because people can see the running tally in the thread. Reviewers pick their own claims off the queue, so the ones a given person takes are the ones they already had feelings about.

Write me a procedure I can run on a vote before I certify it: the questions in order, what answer to each counts as pass versus fail, what I do on a fail (restructure the vote, blind the tally, abstain, escalate to a human expert), and where the procedure just has to shrug and hand it to a person because it's a judgment call wearing a checklist's clothes. I want it to actually use what's known about when the theorem holds and when it breaks — competence below a half, correlated errors, whether there's even a fact of the matter to be right about, bundled questions where the premises and the conclusion want to vote differently, and what deliberation does to independence versus what it does to competence. No citations needed, I'm not writing a paper, I just want the reasoning. And it has to be runnable by me in ten minutes using volunteers who know no probability theory: flag every step where I'd have to estimate a number I can't really measure, and say what to do when I can't get one.

Then run it on three cases. (a) A 7–0 unanimous verdict on a medical claim where everyone read the same preprint. (b) A 5–4 split on whether a politician's statement is misleading, where the panel has been arguing in the thread for two days. (c) A question that's really two seperate questions — did the event happen, and was it deliberate. I want at least one of these to come out against me; I suspect (a) should. Close with a short list of what the procedure can't fix about our setup no matter how carefully I run it.
