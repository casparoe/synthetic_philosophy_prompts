My dad has Parkinson's and a couple years in they added one of the dopamine agonists to his meds. Within about four months he had put something like eighty grand into online blackjack. Nobody in my family had ever seen the man place a bet in his life — he didn't even do the office pools. When my mom finally dragged him back to the neurologist they took him off it and it just stopped, cold, and the doctor said yeah, compulsive gambling is a known side effect of that whole class of drug. My parents separated over it. I'm the one in the family who has been carrying the anger for everybody, and for over a year I've been operating on a single argument: he couldn't have helped it, therefore I don't get to be furious at him.

Then, because apparently I have no coping skills, I fell down a free will rabbit hole and hit Frankfurt cases and the principal of alternative possibilities, and now the whole year of reasoning feels unstable. Because if those cases work, then the "couldn't have done otherwise" part of my argument isn't doing any work at all — the work is being done by something about where the action actually came from. And my dad's story is a story about where it came from, not a story about closed doors. I genuinely can't tell which of those two things my blame is keyed to, and reading hasn't settled it, because every example in the literature is a neurosurgeon with an electrode array and I can't picture it, so I can't get a real gut response.

So I want you to build me a matched pair I can actually run my gut on. Two scenarios, call them A and B, one paragraph each.

The specs:

- Same guy, same act, same setting, same motives, same knowledge of what he's doing and why, same history. The only thing that differs is that in A he really could not have done otherwise, and in B he could have. Everything else held down.
- Ordinary and realistic. A workplace, a small business, a family, a hospital — somewhere I could plausibly have been. The act should be morally serious but not a crime and definitely not a trolley: a betrayal, a lie that costs somebody their job, a refusal to help someone who needed it. Your call, but it has to be the same act in both cases and it can't be worse in A than in B.
- In A the thing that removes the alternatives has to be mundane: a person, a lock, a schedule, a contract, an institutional rule, a piece of bureaucracy. No implants, no surgeons, no hypnosis, no demons, and no drugs. Also no threats and no coercion — I don't want a duress case sneaking an excuse in the side door — and I don't want his desires or values rewritten either, because then A and B stop being matched and I learn nothing.
- He shouldn't know the blocking mechanism is there in A. If you think knowing would change the verdicts, tell me that instead of quietly building it in.
- Make A as flicker-free as you honestly can. I know the dilemma: either the early sign is causally sufficient and you've just given me determinism wearing a costume, or it isn't and there's leftover flickers for a PAP defender to grab.
