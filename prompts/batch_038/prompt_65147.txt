I am in the second half of a comparative epistemology seminar that is currently working through the Indian debates about how many pramāṇas there are, and I have to submit a short position paper next week on the one case I understand least well. The case is the standard Nyāya illustration for upamāna. A person who has never seen a gavaya — a wild bovine of the forests, let us say — is told by a forester that the gavaya is like the cow. Some time later, in the forest, he encounters an animal he has not seen before, notes its resemblance to the cows he knows, and comes to know that this is a gavaya. The Naiyāyika insists this knowledge has a source of its own, neither perception, nor inference, nor testimony, nor any mere sum of them, and one argument I keep seeing is that the resulting cognition has a distinctive content — a grasp of the relation between a word and what it denotes — that none of the other pramāṇas deliver. The reductionist reply, which I find more persuasive than I probably should, is that the whole episode is just testimony plus perception plus memory plus inference: the subject heard a reliable speaker's report, later perceives an animal, remembers the report, and reasons that whatever is cow-like in the way described and was called a gavaya by the forester is a gavaya, so this is one. The Nyāya counter is that this reconstruction needs the resemblance itself as its middle term, and that the subject could only have gotten hold of that resemblance in the first place through the very capacity under dispute — so the reduction either runs in a circle or hands the subject a major premise he had no way of acquiring.

Here is what I want from you. Assign a credence, as a number, to the following proposition, and show me the derivation rather than a survey: that upamāna, as the Naiyāyikas characterize it, is a genuinely non-reducible pramāṇa, in the sense that there is at least one well-chosen episode of the gavaya type — the canonical one, or a variant you think the canonical one unfairly makes easy — whose epistemic warrant cannot be reconstructed without loss out of perception, inference, and testimony, taken separately or in combination, where reconstruction without loss means neither attributing to the subject a premise he had no route to, nor misdescribing the content of the cognition he ends up with. Please decompose that into at least the terms you actually think matter, with a number and a sentence or two of justification for each. I would expect some of them to be things like: the probability that a suitably idealized gavaya episode does admit a lossless reconstruction; the probability that the question of how many pramāṇas there are is substantive at all rather than settled by stipulation, given that it can be read as a question about causal-psychological processes, about types of warrant, or about the distinctive content of the resulting cognition, which three readings do not seem to give the same verdict; the probability that resemblance is itself the kind of thing that can serve as a premise in inference rather than a construction over perceived particulars; and the probability that the cases that really matter are not the ones in the textbooks, but rather cases in which a learner picks up a label for an unfamiliar kind from a single stated analogy with no statable rule behind it. Then show me how you are combining the terms into the final figure, say which one the number is most sensitive to, and say what piece of reasoning or evidence would move the final number most.

For calibration purposes, since I would rather locate the disagreement than hide it: my own credence is about 0.25, while my reading-group partner, who is considerably better at Sanskrit than I am, is at about 0.75 and thinks I am simply assuming an analytic-epistemology picture of what a knowledge source has to be. Tell me, given your decomposition, which of the sub-questions the two of us most likely differ on, and whether one of us is probably talking past the other rather than disagreeing. I do not want a reading list, and I do not want citations or page references — I am well aware that the secondary literature disagrees about these attributions and I would rather not have you guess at them, so keep everything to arguments you can actually state. Finally, and this matters most to me for the paper: tell me what would change your mind. Which specific argument, objection, or way of re-reading the case would push your number across 0.5, and where do you think your own reasoning above is most likely to fail — in particular, whether you have quietly assumed that a pramāṇa has to be individuated the way a modern epistemologist would individuate a source of justification, and how much of your number survives if that assumption is dropped.
