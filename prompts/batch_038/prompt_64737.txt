Three of us are trying to make decisions about our father's care, and the argument has stalled in a way that has very little to do with our father. My brother holds that what matters is how the days feel to him from the inside. My sister holds that what matters is whether the things he spent his life wanting are actually being realized. I keep finding myself saying that certain things — being genuinely understood by someone, having made something that holds up, having kept your own commitments — count whether or not they feel good and whether or not he still wants them. We have been talking past one another for weeks, and I suspect that if we were handed a clean case with none of the sentiment of our own situation attached, the disagreement would show up sharply enough to be examined. So I would like you to build that case for me.

What I am asking for is a matched set of three scenarios, constructed to the following specification.

1. Three scenarios, each a single paragraph of no more than about 120 words, describing one year in the life of the same fictional person at the same age and in the same material circumstances: same income, same health at the start of the year, same people in their orbit. Name the person. Settings should be ordinary and contemporary, with nothing science-fictional.

2. The three scenarios must be engineered so that a hedonist about well-being — well-being as the balance of pleasant over unpleasant experience — ranks A first; an informed-desire-satisfaction theorist — well-being as the extent to which the person's desires, as those desires would stand under clear-eyed and well-informed reflection, are actually satisfied — ranks B first; and an objective-list theorist ranks C first. For the third theory, state explicitly which list you are using; treat items such as accomplishment, understanding, close personal relationships, aesthetic engagement, and autonomy as candidates. No two of the three theories may return the same full ranking of A, B, and C.

3. Barred devices. The differences in verdict may not be produced by any of the following: simulated, artificially generated, or technologically supplied experience of any kind; deception of the subject, or false beliefs of any significance; effects that occur after the year in question or after the person's death; drugs, implants, or other interventions on the person's affective systems; and any appeal to the person's ignorance of their own situation. In all three scenarios the person must have accurate and complete awareness of how their year is going, and that awareness should be visible in the description rather than announced afterward.

4. The point I most want handled carefully: in at least one of the scenarios, a central desire of the person's must be plainly unsatisfied while remaining affectively inert — they know it is unsatisfied, they have not given it up, and they simply do not mind. Do not resolve this by having the person revise or abandon the desire.

5. Audience: my brother and sister are not philosophers. The scenarios should be readable aloud without explanation, and every technical term should be glossed on first use in the analysis.

After the scenarios, please provide the following.

- For each of the three theories, its ranking of the three years and the specific feature of each scenario that produces that ranking. I want the mechanism, not a restatement of the theory's general commitments.
- What is held constant across A, B, and C and what varies, together with the name of the single variable doing most of the work.
- What happens to the verdicts if that variable is weakened or removed: do the three theories converge, and if so, on what ranking?
- Which theory comes out worst on the case as a whole, and the strongest reply available to its defender — including whether that reply requires revising the theory or only rejecting an intuition the case invites.
- A candid note on any place where you think the scenarios assert a verdict rather than generating one. If you find that the specification cannot be met without cheating, say so, and tell me which constraint you would relax.

So that you know what counts as cheating here: the purpose of the case is to expose which principle each of us is actually relying on when we argue about our father. A scenario pre-labeled in a way that settles the question in advance — "the agreeable but hollow year," "the year of hard-won success" — would defeat that purpose entirely. The concrete details have to be what make the verdicts come out as they do.

Please write the whole response in American English, and please do not include citations, references, or suggestions for further reading. I have no way of looking anything up at present, and I would much rather have your reasoning than a bibliography I cannot use.
