In three weeks I present a position paper to the governance working group at a frontier AI lab. The room is mostly ML researchers and evals people, two social scientists who study public opinion, and one product counsel; nearly all of them think of alignment as the problem of specifying and testing an objective, and they are sympathetic to the goal but impatient with anything they cannot operationalize.

My thesis: the values a deployed general-purpose system should serve ought not to be elicited by aggregating measured individual preferences at all. Instead, a standing sortition-selected citizens' panel, deliberating over months with expert testimony, publishes a charter of floors and vetoes — things the system may never do, and trade-offs it may not make silently — and the lab then picks its actual objective from inside that feasible set on grounds of technical tractability rather than moral weight. Representativeness counts; preference intensity does not; the charter binds the lab but is never itself the reward function.

Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or nine objections this particular audience will raise, in roughly the order they will come and in their own register — the evals person asking what I would measure, the RLHF engineer noting that any charter gets compiled into training signal and so re-enters the aggregation problem it was meant to bypass, the counsel asking what happens when the charter conflicts with local law. For each, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, a candid note on where that reply is softest, and a verdict on whether to concede, hold, or bracket. Finish with the single answer that has to land for the proposal to survive, what I should give away in the opening five minutes to defuse the room, and the objection I should be praying nobody asks.
