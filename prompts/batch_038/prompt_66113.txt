I retired four years ago after thirty-nine years in crystallography — mostly small-molecule structure determination, which if you think about it is one long argument with missing information, since you measure intensities and lose the phases and then have to claw the structure back out by tricks. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, badly and enthusiastically, and lately I've fallen down the rabbit hole of that AI-alignment literature my daughter keeps forwarding me. Two of the problems people there list as separate line items have been nagging at me: ontology identification and inaccessible information. My rough gloss on the first is that a machine builds its own categories out of whatever data and training pressure it gets, so a goal I state in my categories — make me a diamond, don't harm people — may not have anything in its head for the words to hook onto, and worse, its categories may shift under its feet as it learns more, the way ours went from atoms to electrons to whatever. My gloss on the second is that even when the machine does have the relevant information, we can't reliably get it out: we can't read the internals, and if we train it to explain itself the explanations are shaped by what sounds convincing to us rather than by what is actually true, so a well-behaved-seeming report tells us nothing.

Here is what I can't settle. I suspect these are the same problem wearing two hats, on the grounds that if its categories really are alien then its beliefs are automatically inaccessible to me, full stop. My neighbour, who used to do instrument design and is sharper than me, says no — inaccessible information is about the epistemic channel and ontology identification is about the target of the goal, and you can have a system with perfectly human-shaped concepts whose innards you still can't read, or a system you can read like a book whose bookkeeping contains no page labelled human flourishing. He then offered a test I like and distrust in equal measure: suppose a demon hands you a flawless readout of every internal state, no channel noise, no deception possible — does the ontology problem survive? I said yes, obviously, so they must be distinct; he said the readout would arrive in the machine's own categories anyway, so my thought experiment is empty and proves nothing. Separately I have a hunch that ontology identification is just Quine's point about there being no fact of the matter which of the world's candidate referents our terms pick out, restated in the language of expected utility, and that calling it a new technical problem is a bit of marketing. And I keep reaching for my own trade as an analogy: the phase problem, the inverse problem, regularisation and prior assumptions doing quiet work in the reconstruction. I don't know whether that analogy illuminates these two or actively misleads about them.

So: compare the two properly. What exactly goes wrong in each, at which stage — building a world-model, fixing the goal, interrogating the system, checking behaviour — and give me at least one concrete case where one bites hard and the other doesn't, because I think the existence of such a case is the only honest way to settle whether they are one problem or two. Then take a stand rather than surveying: tell me whether they are genuinely distinct, tell me which of the two is the more fundamental obstacle and why, tell me who wins my neighbour's demon argument, and tell me whether the Quine reduction and the phase-problem analogy hold up or whether I should throw them out. If my glosses above are wrong or sloppy in places, say so and fix them, since I wrote them from memory of a few blog posts and I know they are not careful. I want a definite verdict with reasons attached, not a balanced two-sided summary that hands the decision back to me — I have had enough of those already, and I am old enough to want to be argued with.
