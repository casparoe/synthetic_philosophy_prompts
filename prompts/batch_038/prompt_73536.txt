I'm in a methodology seminar this term and we just did the week on Friedman's essay about the methodology of positive economics and the F-twist charge (Samuelson's term, I think?). I can state the shape of the charge but I can't tell which of the standard replies actually block it and which ones just move it somewhere else. As I understand it, Friedman has two claims sitting awkwardly together: judge a theory by the accuracy of its predictions and not by the realism of its assumptions, but also a theory that was literally true in its assumptions would be useless, because it would have to reproduce every detail of the thing it explains. Then the twist: he wants to say a successful theory's assumptions are false-but-fine, and "fine" turns out to mean "the predictions come out right," so predictive success is doing the work that was supposed to be done by something other than predictive success. The critics then say he's quietly reintroduced a realism requirement — the assumptions do have to be true in the respects that matter, which is exactly what he denied. If that were right the whole debate should have collapsed, and it obviously hasn't, so I'm missing something.

Here are the six replies I've collected, in my own words. (1) The safe-false-assumption move: an idealization is harmless just in case, whenever it's false, the prediction it feeds into is false too — so falsity is never the issue, load-bearingness is. (2) Caricaturism: models aren't in the belief business at all, they're pictures, so asking whether "the firm maximizes profit" is true is a category mistake and the twist never gets going. (3) Tendencies: the assumption names a real causal factor that's always masked by other stuff, so it's literally false of every actual firm but not thereby empty. (4) Capacities: same family as (3), but the truth-bearers are capacities rather than laws, with the fundamental laws false and the phenomenological ones doing the work. (5) Split credibility from applicability: judge the model on whether it's internally credible first, and then seperately on whether it travels to the setting you care about, so success in one setting never licenses anything about the assumptions. (6) The verbal-dispute defense: the critics are already instrumentalists, so nothing is being twisted — "false assumption" just means "abstracted" and the fight is over vocabulary.

Score each of the six on two 0–5 scales and be decisive rather than even-handed: "twist-escape" (does it actually block the slide from predictive success to assumptions-are-fine, or does it relocate the slide?) and "Friedman tax" (how much of Friedman's own point it gives up — can you still say a wildly false model is a good model?).
