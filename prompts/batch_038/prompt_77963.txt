I sit on the committee that writes our health system's guidance for when a clinician can rely on an AI diagnostic aid without doing their own re-verification. Right now the whole thing hinges on one sentence, roughly: the clinician may treat the aid's output as known-correct when its reported confidence is above 0.95. It's falling apart in review and I have to circulate something before next Thursdays meeting.

Three camps. The single-number people say 0.95 across the board is the only thing thats auditable and the only version where the sentence means the same thing from one paragraph to the next. The sliding-scale people say the number has to move with what's at stake — 0.85 is plenty for "here's a differential, go work it up" and nowhere near enough for "you're cleared for an irreversible procedure." And then there's a member who used to teach philosophy, who thinks the sliding version isn't just unworkable but confused: two residents looking at the same output on the same evidence either both know it or neither does, and what's at stake belongs in the decision rule, not in the knowledge claim. She's also put a compromise on the table, which is that "knows" is context-sensitive, so the low-stakes room and the high-stakes room are each using it correctly and there's no contradiction to fix. My problem with that one is that a written policy doesn't live in a single context — it gets read out in a chart note, in credentialing, in discovery.

So: draft the memo. It has to come down on one structure — single threshold, sliding threshold, contextualist wording, or something else entirely — with real numbers and real proposed language for the sentence, not a menu of options. About a page. Take the confused-objection seriously rather than waving at it: if she's right about knowledge, say what that does to our wording and to the sliding-scale proposal; if she's wrong, explain why in terms a room full of clinicians and one hospital lawyer can actually follow. And tell me straight whether we can run a stake-indexed action threshold without taking any position at all on whether knowledge itself is stakes-sensitive, because if we can, I'd much rather not litigate that inside a policy document. Assume whatever we write gets quoted back at us in malpractice discovery.
