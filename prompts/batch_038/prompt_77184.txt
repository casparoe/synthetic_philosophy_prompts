Most rich democracies run a two-track system for supporting adults of working age, and the tracks are governed by utterly different rules. On the first track, a household whose income comes mainly from labor, or from the absence of it, must apply for means-tested support and then live under continuous behavioral supervision: a written agreement specifying how many hours per week must be devoted to searching for work or to approved training; mandatory appointments with an assigned caseworker who may unilaterally redefine what counts as adequate searching; payment reductions or cancellations for a missed appointment, a refused job offer, or a failure to produce evidence; a lifetime cap after which eligibility lapses whatever the person's circumstances; and, alongside all this, a fraud-detection apparatus — data matching against employers and banks, unannounced home visits to check whether a partner lives at the address, public tip lines inviting neighbors to report suspected cheats, and criminal prosecution rather than civil adjustment. Some of these programs additionally impose a resource limit, so that a claimant who saves is disqualified while one who spends the savings down becomes eligible again. On the second track, a household whose income comes from dividends, interest, rent, royalties, or the appreciation of inherited assets receives that income under no comparable regime. Nobody asks the recipient how many hours this week were spent stewarding the asset; nobody requires reinvestment or improvement as a condition of the return; there is no time limit after which the yield ceases; there is no inspectorate empowered to call on landlords or to examine whether a portfolio is being used productively; there is no register of diligent versus idle owners; and misreporting on this track is handled overwhelmingly through administrative settlement and civil penalties rather than prosecution and public denunciation.

What theory of social contribution, desert, and reciprocity does this asymmetry presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not merely gestured at: what does the practice treat as making a claim on the social product legitimate, and why does it treat the claim of the property owner as antecedent to politics and the claim of the unemployed person as conditional, revocable, and purchased with supervised conduct? Consider whether the practice requires a claim about the moral psychology of the poor (that idleness is chosen and must be corrected by external pressure), a claim about property (that asset income is a pre-institutional entitlement while transfers are gifts), or a claim about reciprocity (that only effort expended in the labor market counts as giving something back). Which rival views does the arrangement effectively rule out — a luck-egalitarian view that would treat both an inherited portfolio and an unemployable body as matters of brute luck, an unconditional basic income grounded in common ownership of inherited resources, or an account of contribution that counts unpaid care work alongside waged work? Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which of its features could be explained by administrative verifiability, by incentive and fraud-control arguments, or by the electoral composition of the sanctioned group, without any commitment to a theory of desert at all. Distinguish sharply what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with.

Finally, evaluate this counterfactual, which I am stipulating rather than reporting. Suppose a jurisdiction extended the first track's machinery to the second: every household drawing property income above a threshold must file a monthly statement of hours devoted to maintaining, improving, or reinvesting in the underlying asset; failure to file or demonstrable idleness triggers a progressively reduced after-tax yield; a dedicated inspectorate may inspect lettings and portfolios; and a public register distinguishes productive from passive owners. Is that extension a reductio of the reciprocity principle invoked to justify conditionality for the poor, showing the principle cannot be applied consistently? Or does the intuitive resistance to it reveal that the operative principle was never reciprocity in the first place — in which case, name precisely what it was, and say whether that something else can be defended as a principle of justice or only as a fact about power and stigma. Answer in no more than 800 words.
