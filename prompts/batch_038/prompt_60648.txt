I write about ideas and science for a general-interest magazine, and I'm drafting a piece on whether genuine moral dilemmas — cases in which an agent is simultaneously subject to two all-things-considered obligations that cannot both be discharged — are possible at all. The episode I want to run through that question is the 1972 Andes crash. A chartered flight carrying a Uruguayan rugby club along with its families and friends went down on a glacier; of the 45 aboard, 16 survived seventy-two days. There was no food. Early on they agreed among themselves that whoever died should have his body used by the rest; they then ate the dead, who were friends, teammates, and in several cases the mothers, sisters and wives of men still alive. An avalanche some weeks later killed eight more and forced the decision again, this time about the people they were closest to. After the rescue a Catholic priest judged that they had acted out of necessity and had not sinned, and the survivors themselves described what they had done in Eucharistic terms.

I want the mapping done carefully, and in both directions. First: is this a genuine conflict of obligations, or a conflict of prima facie duties that resolves cleanly once the facts are in? Does the pact hold up — can consent given prospectively, by the living about their own corpses, discharge a duty toward the dead — and if it can, does that dissolve the dilemma or merely relocate it? Second: what does the iterated structure do to the theory? The dilemma literature models a single decision point, whereas here the choice recurred and escalated. Third: what should I make of the residue — the survivors' lasting distress, agent-regret in Williams's sense, the priest's absolution — evidence of an obligation actually violated, or only of a horrific situation survived? Tell me what the standard argument from agglomeration and ought-implies-can leaves you holding in this case, and whether the episode confirms that argument, strains it, or belongs to a class of case it was never built to handle. Where the theory is simply silent, say so rather than making it speak.

Finish with a line of the form Verdict: <label>, where <label> is exactly one of: genuine dilemma; no dilemma, one obligation only; no dilemma, consent dissolved it; indeterminate.
