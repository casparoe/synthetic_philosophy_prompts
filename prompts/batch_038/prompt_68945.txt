Last night I spent three hours arguing with a close friend about epistocracy — restricted suffrage, weighted votes, the lot — and we got nowhere, in a way that tells me precisely where my own teaching tends to go wrong. He is mathematically literate and holds that the Condorcet jury theorem all but settles the matter: if the typical voter's chance of selecting the better of two options is below one half, then adding more such votes degrades the collective decision, and the rational response is to restrict or weight the franchise. My answer was that the theorem is a template rather than a verdict — that its assumptions are exactly what is in dispute, and that under realistic assumptions about how political opinions are actually formed it can cut the other way. He took this for hand-waving, and I could not make the structure of my response vivid to him. That failure was mine, not his, and I would rather not repeat it in front of a room.

In two weeks I have eighty minutes with a cohort of first-year public-policy master's students: no philosophy, some elementary statistics, and, I expect, a bare majority privately sympathetic to my friend's conclusion. What I want them to leave with is the crux I failed to convey — that the jury theorem is not an argument for either side of this debate but a list of conditions whose satisfaction is an empirical and political question. Your task is to design my explanation, not to give me one.

Tell me first the order in which the components must go in and what depends on what. I am particularly unsure whether the threshold condition — individual competence better than chance — has to be securely in place before the independence condition can even be discussed, and where to put the question of what counts as the correct answer at all, since raising it first risks making everything afterwards sound like special pleading.

Then identify the single running example that should carry the session, and say what it must contain and what it must conspicuously lack. I have in mind something like a town deciding whether to fund a flood barrier after a once-in-a-century flood, with one dominant local paper, a state hydrologist's report, and a property-value dimension that divides the town along predictable lines — but I cannot tell whether that is too elaborate for eighty minutes or not elaborate enough to make correlated error visible.

Then give me the misconception map. The ones they will arrive with, as I expect them: that voting is simply a criminal jury writ large; that more heads is automatically better; that the theorem says the group outperforms its most competent member; and that a short civics examination would restore independence by filtering the ignorant out. The ones they will form during the session: above all, that correlation among voters' errors is a technicality to be tidied away later. Say where in the sequence each should be headed off, and how. One I cannot yet answer myself, and I want your treatment of it: my friend's parting shot, that weighting votes by competence repairs correlated error all by itself, since it raises the average competence of the weighted pool.

Then a closing question, answerable in a few sentences, whose answers would let me distinguish students who have grasped the crux from students who have merely found me agreeable.

Finally, tell me what to leave out and say so openly. I had assumed the formal limit result, the taxonomy of epistocratic institutional designs, and the separate question of whether legitimacy might matter even if the epistocrat were right about accuracy — but if excising any of those would render what I do teach unintelligible, tell me that instead.
