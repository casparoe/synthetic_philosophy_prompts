Third year in philosophy of biology, working on individuality/multilevel selection, and I've hit the point where I have a big pile of verdicts and no principle behind them. I forced myself to answer yes/no on a bunch of concrete cases without hedging, and the pattern is embarassing. Here it is:

1. Quaking aspen clone — one root system, tens of thousands of stems, and you can cut a stem off and root it. I say: one individual.
2. Branching coral colony — fragments break off, survive, found whole new colonies. I say: not one individual.
3. Portuguese man o' war — zooids are obligately specialized, none of them can live or reproduce alone. I say: one individual. I can see 2 and 3 look like the same animal body plan and I'm still splitting them.
4. Honeybee hive: superorganism, counts. A city or a firm: does not count, even though the functional integration and the differential survival look the same to me on paper. My only reason is genetic relatedness, and I know how thin that is.
5. Dictyostelium slug: individual if clonal, not an individual if it's a chimera with cheating lineages in it. Same worry as 4, except here I'm letting relatedness decide individuality outright.
6. Conjugative plasmid with a toxin-antitoxin addiction module: yes, a real replicator with its own fitness interests, selectable in its own right.
7. Mitochondrion: no, a part. Except that in plants with cytoplasmic male sterility the mitochondria that sterilize males plainly get a transmission advantage, so I'm happily granting that selection happens at that level while refusing to grant unit-hood. I can't say why 6 gets the honor and 7 doesn't, since 7 has its own genome, its own mutation supply, and its own differential transmission, and 6 can't even replicate without its host's machinery.
8. My own colonic crypt stem cell lineages: not units, obviously, even though there's heritable variation among them and differential success.
9. The transmissible devil facial tumour: unit. HeLa cells in a flask: unit, someone even wanted to give them their own genus. But HeLa never infects anybody, its whole life cycle is a human pipetting it 1:20 every few days, which makes me suspect my criterion is really "an individual if a person is doing the passaging," which can't be right.
10. Segregation distorters, t-haplotype, SD: genes are the units here, yes. But I want the organism to be the primary unit and drive to be a breakdown case, and I have no non-question-begging way to say what makes it a breakdown rather than the normal situation showing through.
11. Polyembryonic parasitoid wasp with a sterile soldier larval caste: not one individual, because it develops into many wasps — even though it has exactly the soma/germ division of labour I use to promote the beehive.

So: is there a single principle — a spec, with clauses, that delivers all or nearly all of these? I want each clause motivated by the case that forces it, not a slogan about individuality being "a matter of degree." Then run it on four cases I haven't listed and tell me what it says: snowflake-yeast clusters from a gravity-selection experiment, where multicellular clusters get picked every round; a CRISPR gene drive construct spreading through a mosquito population; an aphid plus its obligate vertically transmitted endosymbiont, which never lives outside a host; and the B-cell clones inside a germinal centre during an immune response. And then the part I actually care about: be straight about which of my eleven it cannot save. If it can only save all of them by becoming baroque, tell me which one to abandon and why that's the cheapest loss. 8 vs 9 and 6 vs 7 are the pairs I most expect to break it.

Don't give me a menu of pluralisms to choose between and don't survey the options — commit to one account and defend it, and flag the places where you're stretching it. No citations or reading list, I want the argument, not the bibliography.
