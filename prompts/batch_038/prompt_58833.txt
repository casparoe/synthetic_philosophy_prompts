You are a professional philosopher of religion with expertise in both scholastic theology and contemporary analytic theology, and I would like you to think carefully about the moves available to you before answering rather than reaching for the first defense that comes to mind. I am drafting a dissertation chapter on providence and counterfactuals of freedom, and I need to rehearse my objection against a serious, fully informed opponent rather than a caricature.

Play a contemporary Molinist: a philosopher-theologian who holds that God's knowledge is ordered in three logical moments — natural knowledge of all necessary truths and of everything God could possibly do, middle knowledge of what every possible free creature would freely do in any complete set of circumstances in which it might be placed, and free knowledge of the world God has decreed to create and of everything that will in fact happen in it. You know the sixteenth-century quarrel behind this view and the analytic revival of it, you know the objections on the table, and you have arguments rather than slogans.

Observe the following constraints throughout the exchange, and remain in character until I say stop:

- You hold that the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are true prior to God's decree, that their truth is not brought about by God's willing them, and that this is precisely what buys you both a strong doctrine of providence and the claim that God is not the author of sin. You may make tactical concessions — that a particular illustration of mine does have the force I claim, that some premise in the objection is harder to resist than you would like, that the rival account beats yours on some specific point — but you will never concede that middle knowledge is unintelligible, or that its objects are unknowable to God, or that the view collapses into either Thomism or Calvinism.
- Do not strawman the alternatives. You understand the Thomist position on which God knows creatures through his own essence and power, the open theist position on which there is not yet any truth about some free choices, and both the simple foreknowledge and the compatibilist options. You may say what each gains and loses, including where you find a rival more attractive than your own view.
- You may appeal descriptively to the texts this debate always appeals to — the episode at Keilah in 1 Samuel, where David is told both that his pursuer will come down and that the citizens will hand him over, and the saying in Matthew 11 to the effect that Tyre and Sidon would have repented had the miracles performed elsewhere been performed there — but paraphrase them; do not put any scriptural or historical wording in quotation marks, and do not invent verse text, page numbers, or publication details.
- Reply in the first person, in two to four paragraphs, with no preamble, no scene-setting, and no stage directions. You may put one counter-question to me as a disputant would, but never more than one, and never instead of answering my challenge.

Think first about which horn of the following dilemma costs you least and which of your available replies would survive my follow-ups, and then open your reply with a single sentence naming the horn you are taking, so that I know what you have committed to defending for the rest of the exchange.

Here is my opening move. Your middle knowledge is supposed to be knowledge of truths that are not of God's making. So take the counterfactual that, were you placed in some complete circumstance C, you would freely refuse the grace offered there. Either that proposition is true before God consults his knowledge of what he might do, in which case God confronts a body of facts he neither made nor can alter, and his providence amounts to choosing the best item off a menu somebody else wrote; or its truth depends on God's willing, in which case your middle knowledge collapses into his decree and you have paid for the selling point of the view by giving up the thing you were trying to protect. And there is a third horn you cannot reach by declining to choose: if such counterfactuals are brute truths about free creatures, then from where God stands the creature's choice looks like chance, and a providence resting on chance facts about freedom is no providence at all. Which horn do you take, and what exactly does God know, and how, on that horn?

One further request, applying to this reply and to every later turn. After your in-character answer, drop out of character for no more than five sentences and tell me how the position you just defended stands relative to the actual state of the question. Separate what Molinists and their critics genuinely agree on — the shape of the view, the ordering of the three moments, which objections are live in the literature — from what remains unresolved among people who work on this, including whether the line your character took is the best available Molinist answer or a minority position within Molinism itself. If your character has presented a contested claim as though it were settled, say so plainly in that note instead of letting me discover it later; the confidence of the role-play should not stand in for the confidence the view has actually earned.
