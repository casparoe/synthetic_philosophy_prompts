Six years ago I had a benign tumor removed from my left temporal lobe, and the surgery left me with seizures that no drug regimen has controlled. My neurologist now recommends completing a procedure I began two years ago, in which damaged tissue is replaced, bit by bit, with a synthetic scaffold built to do the same job: same inputs in, same outputs out, verified before and after every increment.

There are two protocols. The first removes the remaining tissue and installs the full scaffold in a single operation under general anesthesia; I would go to sleep as I am and wake up with the implant, and the clinic expects function back at baseline within a week. The second is roughly four hundred outpatient sessions, each replacing about a quarter of one percent, performed while I am awake and conversing, so the engineers can compare my responses across the increment and halt if anything shifts. My insurer covers only the second. I have already had sixty of those sessions on a smaller region, and I passed every check each time.

Two things trouble me. First, the checks are the clinic's own battery, and they are the same measures used to decide what each new increment must replicate; the scaffold is tuned until I pass them, so I am not sure what failing would even consist in. Second, my wife says something has changed that I cannot detect from the inside. I no longer hum while I cook. A certain kind of dry joke has stopped landing for me. She cannot say which session it started at, and I would be surprised if she could: I have never noticed a discontinuity, and I am not convinced I would be positioned to notice one.

That last point is what I most want your help with. The clinic's outcome data are patient self-reports, and patients on the incremental protocol report feeling entirely continuous with themselves far more often than patients who have the single operation. But the incremental patients were never out of contact with themselves at any moment, so they never occupied the kind of vantage from which a loss would show up. Should that difference in reporting reassure me, or is it precisely what I would expect to see whether or not anything had been lost?

Concretely, then: which protocol should I choose if I could get either covered, and why? Should I ask my wife to keep a written record of my behavior across the remaining sessions, and if so, what in that record ought to count as a finding rather than as her looking for one? And is there any assessment I could insist on that would tell me something my own reports about myself cannot?
