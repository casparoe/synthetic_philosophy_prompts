Thirty-some years in a lab doing electrophysiology on invertebrate nervous systems, retired about four years back, and I've fallen into philosophy of mind the way other retirees fall into golf. I've written down where I've actually landed, mostly for my own benefit, and I want to know whether the thing holds together or whether I'm fooling myself. Here's the list:

1. Whether a system is conscious is an objective matter, full stop — a fact about the physical setup, in the same league as whether it's alive or whether its running hot. Not a matter of how we describe it or what we happen to care about. In principle it's an empirical question you could settle by getting the right measurements on the machinery.

2. What does the work is the organization, not the stuff. Get the right causal structure and you get experience, whether it's built out of neurons, chips, or something nobody's thought of yet. Substrate chauvinism is just bad biology, and I've seen plenty of it.

3. All the same, I don't think computation is a thing that's out there in the physics. When I say my old bench instruments computed nothing, and that a rock isn't running any program, I think that's simply correct — because "implements function F" is a description we lay over the physical facts from the outside. The atoms aren't up to anything of that sort.

4. Consciousness isn't a switch. It comes in degrees, and the moral weight of a creature's experience ought to scale with the degree: more and richer experience, more weight.

5. But I don't think degrees are comparable across very different designs. Asking whether a bee's aversive response is one-fortieth of a person's headache looks to me like a meaningless question rather than a hard one.

6. Where I actually draw the line in practice: arthropods and cephalopods get some benefit of the doubt; nematodes, plants and thermostats get none; and a big system that isn't organized like a mind — a warehouse full of servers, say, or a giant lookup table that could pass for a conversation — gets none either, no matter how many operations per second it racks up.

7. Policy-wise I'm for being conservative: where there's a live question about whether something suffers, act as though it might, becuase the cost of being wrong falls entirely on the thing that's suffering and none of it on me.

So, audit it. Which of these actually pull against each other, and how badly — rank them for me, don't hand me a flat list where every crack looks equally deep. For each real tension, say which commitment has to go and what I'd be giving up by dropping it. And please also flag the pairs that only look inconsistent and aren't, because I suspect I'd otherwise spend a month tightening the wrong joint. I genuinely don't know where the soft spot is; that's why I'm asking.
