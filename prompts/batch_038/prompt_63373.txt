I'm in a reading group that's been working through stuff about mechanistic interpretability, and last week one of the members wrote up a short handout to start the session. He's a philosophy grad student, not an ML person, but he's smart and he'd clearly been reading. Here's roughly what he had:

> Neural networks don't just store statistical mush; they compute with features — concepts like *dog*, or *Golden Gate Bridge*, or the relation *greater-than* — that are physically present in the model's activations. The early interpretability program assumed one neuron corresponds to one concept and went hunting for the dog neuron. What it found instead was polysemanticity: a single neuron responds to dogs, and also to cars, and also to the color blue, with nothing tying those together. That discovery refutes the idea that features are really there. If the network's own units don't carve the world at its joints, the joints were only ever in the eye of the interpeter. The rescue is superposition: because a network has to represent far more features than it has dimensions, it packs them into overlapping directions, so a feature is a direction in activation space rather than a unit. And once you've identified such a direction you've identified something the model genuinely represents, whether or not the model actually uses it in producing its outputs — which is why you can turn one of these directions up and watch the model start shoehorning that topic into everything it says. So interpretability is in the business of discovering facts about representation, and skepticism about features is just a philosophical hangover.

Half of us thought that was sharp, half thought it was a mess, and we never got past the first paragrph. Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone actually doing this research would sign off on, what they'd want to hedge, and what's flatly wrong? A few things I especially want pinned down:

- does the step from "neurons are polysemantic" to "so features aren't really there" work at all, and how does polysemanticity relate to superposition — is superposition a rescue of features, or the explanation of why neurons look polysemantic in the first place, or both?
- is the "whether or not the model actually uses it" clause something the field would accept? It struck me as odd, because his own example (cranking a direction up and seeing the behavior change) sounds like it's precisely about use.
- what's the status of "features are physically present in the activations"? Is a feature the same kind of thing as a direction someone finds with a probe, or is there a gap there, and if so who gets to say which directions count?

And if some of the handout turns out to be wrong, does his closing line — that interpretability is discovering facts about representation, and that the skeptical worries are just leftover philosophy — still survive the corrections? I lean toward thinking features are real-ish, but I don't trust my leaning here, so please don't just ratify it.
