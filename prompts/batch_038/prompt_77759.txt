I serve on the six-person Integrity Review Panel of an international NGO that administers donor-funded maternal and child health programs, and on Friday the panel must resolve a case on which it is currently deadlocked two-to-two. I intend to circulate a written opinion in advance of the meeting. Please draft that opinion in my name.

The panel's jurisdiction is limited: we may uphold, overturn, or modify the findings of the internal audit and set the consequent remedies, and we may adopt interpretive guidance binding on future panels, but we may not amend the Standards themselves. Assume the audit's factual findings are accurate and do not invent further facts.

**The findings.** Over three years the director of our country office in a West African state where we work through district health offices did three things, all of which the audit flagged:

1. **Attendance allowances.** He paid cash per diems to district health officers and to elected members of village health committees who attended our training workshops, at rates set by the host government's own statutory per-diem schedule for civil servants attending official meetings — roughly three times the local daily wage. The audit concluded these were improper payments to public officials. Its stated ground is that the allowances functioned as inducements, because recorded attendance rose sharply in the year the payments began.

2. **A funeral contribution.** He authorized the purchase of a goat, rice, and cooking fuel, drawn from the logged "community relations" budget line, for the funeral of the mother of a district medical officer with whom the office deals weekly. The expenditure was properly logged and within the budget cap. The audit concluded that a contribution to the household of a public official on a personal occasion crosses from community relations into private benefit.

3. **A driver.** He hired, without a competitive process, the nephew of the district chief who controls decisions about clinic siting and land access. The director maintains the nephew was the only applicant holding a license and prior experience, and that a refusal would have been publicly read as a rebuff and would have cost the program its land access. Our Standards require open competitive recruitment for appointments longer than six months.

**The governing text.** Our Standards define a prohibited payment as any transfer of value to a public official or to a member of their household that is intended to secure favorable treatment, and they separately permit community-relations expenditures for "customary hospitality," provided they are logged and capped. The donor's anti-bribery clause is incorporated by reference and is drafted in zero-tolerance terms.

**The two camps.** Two members hold that the findings are straightforward and that the office should recover the payments and terminate the director; that any accommodation here licenses the next, larger accommodation; and that a panel which carves out exceptions for one region is applying a different code there than it applies elsewhere. Two members hold that the audit treats all reciprocal gift-giving as bribery; that allowances, feasts, and kinship hiring are the ordinary currency of legitimate obligation in the setting and are what makes the program deliverable at all; that sanctioning the director would enforce a norm no one in the district recognizes and would destroy a functioning program; and that the Standards' own reference to customary hospitality shows the drafters knew the code could not be applied without local calibration.

**What I want the opinion to do.**

- Distinguish two ways of characterizing the wrong at issue — corruption as the illicit conversion of public office into private revenue, and corruption as the subordination of an office's entrusted purposes to the personal purposes of its holder — and identify which of the two our Standards actually encode, since I suspect each camp is tacitly arguing from a different one. Show where that identification changes the analysis of each of the three findings.
- Address the audit's inference in finding 1: whether behavioral responsiveness to a payment establishes corrupt intent, or whether it is equally consistent with the payment being an incentive or a wage supplement, and what turns on that difference.
- Take account of a fact the audit recorded but did not discuss: the host government's per-diem schedule was itself introduced in the 1990s by donor agencies to staff their own workshops, and was adopted into national civil-service rules thereafter. Say what follows for the argument that these payments are protected local custom.
- Propose a forward-looking test the panel can apply to unanticipated cases, stated as criteria a member could actually operate — not as a list of values. It should say how the panel is to decide which local conventions are eligible to modify the application of the Standards and which are not, and it should state explicitly whether the test it produces is universalist or convention-sensitive, and in what sense.
- Commit to a disposition and remedy on each of the three findings. The three need not come out the same way; if you conclude they should, explain why the apparent differences among them are not morally relevant.
- State the strongest consideration against your recommended dispositions in a form that a member of the opposing camp would accept as their own view rather than a caricature, and explain what weight you assign it and why.

Format it as a formal internal memorandum with a short disposition summary at the top that the panel could adopt verbatim as a motion. Roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words. Write in a measured, argumentative register suitable for a divided body: no bullet-point lists of alternatives, no hedging toward a menu of options, and no citations, references, reading lists, or appeals to outside authorities — the reasoning must stand on the facts given and on argument alone.
