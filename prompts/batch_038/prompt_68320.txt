I teach ancient philosophy — Aristotle mostly — but the department has me covering six weeks of a first-year seminar on knowledge and its enemies while a colleague is out on parental leave, and next Thursday is the ethics-of-belief day. I last taught Clifford and James in about 2009 and I'd rather not rebuild the session from scratch, so my plan was to hand out a piece that ran in one of those general-audience magazines over the summer; a friend forwarded it, it did the rounds on my feed, it's well written and it made the whole 19th-century quarrel feel urgent, which is exactly what I want from a handout. Before I send it to the copier for forty students I want to know how much of it I'll spend the hour walking back.

Here's the bit where it sets up the debate, typed out: 'The quarrel between W.K. Clifford and William James is really about how much evidence a person needs before a belief stops being a vice. Clifford, a Victorian mathematician, held that a belief is immoral whenever the evidence for it falls short of certainty, and he made the case with a shipowner who knowingly sends emigrants to sea in a vessel he suspects is unsound. When the ship goes down, Clifford says, the owner is guilty of their deaths — and he would have been just as guilty had it reached port safely, because a belief held without evidence corrupts the very habit of judging, and sooner or later society pays the bill. James, writing later, granted all this for laboratory questions but insisted religion is a different case: since no amount of evidence can settle whether God exists, we are free — sometimes obliged — to take up the belief that makes our lives work, and his slogan that faith in a fact can help create the fact means that believing can literally bring truths into existence. Strip out the rhetoric and the disagreement is about costs: Clifford thinks the worst thing a belief can do is be false, James thinks the worst thing is to miss a truth. That's why James has the better of it today. Nobody can actually live by Clifford's rule — it would make it wrong to trust your doctor, to board a plane, or to assume your friends like you — and Clifford himself obviously believed most of what he knew on other people's testimony, which is just an admission that the rule is unlivable.'

I'm instinctively with Clifford and I suspect that ending is cheap, but I can't tell whether my instinct is tracking something in the views or just my own temperament, and that's the part that worries me, because the students will hear the confidence and not the argument. So: go through it claim by claim and tell me which bits a scholar of either author would sign off on unchanged, which are harmless simplifications that cost nothing at this level, and which actually turn one of the two positions around. I'd especially like your read on the 'short of certainty' characterization; on whether the shipowner story is doing the job the article assigns to it; on whether James's license really is scoped to religion and really amounts to choosing beliefs at will; and on whether that closing testimony-and-paralysis point is an objection Clifford has no answer to, or whether it's aimed at a rule he never proposed. Then tell me, practically: if I hand this out, which two or three wrong ideas will be hardest to dislodge once they're in the room, and does the article's verdict survive the corrections — is there any sense in which these two are even answering the same question? No bibliography or suggested readings, I know where to look; I just want the audit.
