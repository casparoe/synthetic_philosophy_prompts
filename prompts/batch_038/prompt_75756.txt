I'm a software engineer with no philosophy training. Building governance tooling for a large open-source project got me reading about sortition, and I wrote out an argument for it to discuss with a colleague. It now seems to prove far too much, and I can't find the exit.

(1) An elected legislature is produced by a selection process that systematically favors candidates with money, media access, and party backing; the resulting body is not a fair sample of the people it governs, and its members are calculating their next campaign from the day they take office.
(2) A legislature chosen by lot from the eligible population would be a fair sample, and no organized interest could buy seats in advance, since nobody knows who will be picked.
(3) What makes a body's decisions binding on those subject to them is that the body is composed like the population and insulated from organized money.
(4) So a legislature chosen by lot is more legitimate than an elected one, and we should move toward abolishing elections.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(3) is specific to legislatures: a lottery-selected central bank, court of appeals, hospital ethics board, or committee deciding which of my team's services to deprecate satisfies (3) at least as well as an elected legislature does.
(6) Filling those bodies by lot would be a disaster, and the people who defend sortition for legislatures agree with me about that.
(7) So (3) cannot be what confers legitimacy, which removes the support for (4), and sortition has no principled justification at all.

Tell me which step is wrong and what rejecting it costs. Is the argument invalid — if so, where exactly is the gap — or valid with a premise I should give up? If I have to choose, I'd rather keep (2) than (3): (2) looks like a fact about how selection works, while (3) looks like something I invented on the spot.
