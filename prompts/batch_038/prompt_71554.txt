I'm giving a talk next week in our department's epistemology work-in-progress series. The thesis: Jeffrey conditionalization doesn't actually solve the problem of uncertain evidence, it just relocates it — the rigidity assumption (your conditional probabilities given the observation partition stay fixed) is a substantive commitment rather than a neutral default, and it's exactly the shifty cases Jeffrey was worried about, dim lights and ambiguous colour patches, where it fails. Second half of the talk: because of this, succesive probability-kinematics updates don't commute, and that non-commutativity is a genuine problem for the view, not a technical curiosity.

The room is mostly formal epistemologists who've absorbed the minimum-relative-entropy characterization of Jeffrey's rule and will think my worry misses the point entirely, two biostatisticians from the medical school who'll say I've reinvented a hierarchical model with a noisy likelihood, and one ML person who does variational inference. No decision theorists, nobody who cares about Dutch books for their own sake.

Put together my prep sheet: the seven or eight objections this crowd will actually raise, ranked by how much damage they do, each phrased the way that subgroup would phrase it, with a 90-second reply and a straight note on where the reply is softest. Tell me what I should just concede in the opening ten minutes so it doesn't come up as a gotcha later, and end with the question I'm praying nobody asks — I suspect it's about what normative force is left once you give up rigidity, but I may be wrong.
