I have been working slowly through Keynes's *A Treatise on Probability* (1921), and I have reached the point where I can parse his sentences but not his concept. I would like help with the notion of the weight of an argument, as Keynes develops it in Chapter VI, together with the claims about measurement and comparability in Chapter III. Everything below is my own paraphrase — I am deliberately not reproducing his wording — so please tell me plainly where the paraphrase is unfaithful to the text.

As I read him, Keynes holds two theses that seem to pull apart. First, a probability is not a state of mind but an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis; such relations are usually not numerically measurable, and two of them are often not comparable at all, so that neither is greater, lesser, or equal to the other. Second, alongside probability there runs a distinct dimension, weight, which tracks how much relevant evidence the argument rests upon. Weight is supposed to rise with the arrival of new relevant evidence even where that evidence leaves the probability unchanged or lowers it; and weight is supposed to be something other than the subjective feeling of confidence, since Keynes also appears to hold that, other things being equal, we rationally prefer the conclusion supported by the weightier argument.

Here is the case I cannot make sense of. Suppose the hypothesis h is a mundane empirical claim. In Case A, a single witness of known high reliability — call it 0.9 — testifies that h; against a neutral prior my credence in h becomes 0.9. In Case B, a hundred witnesses, each barely better than a coin flip at about 0.505 reliability and conditionally independent given the truth, split sixty to forty in favour of h; the arithmetic is approximate, but my credence comes out near 0.6. Case B plainly rests on far more evidence, so I want to say it has the greater weight. Yet Case B is also the argument I would bet less on, the one whose conclusion feels less settled, and the one whose probability is lower. Weight has come apart from probability, from evidence-directed confidence, and from betting disposition simultaneously, and I cannot see what is left for it to be.

With that in mind:

(a) Explain, at a level of intuition rather than exegesis, what quantity weight is meant to pick out, and why Keynes would insist that a rational agent needs it as a separate parameter rather than as a gloss on probability. Does weight attach to the evidence base, to the argument as a whole, or to the relation between the two?

(b) Diagnose my example. Is the tension genuine, or has the case smuggled in a premise Keynes would reject — for instance, that the probabilities in Case A and Case B are comparable in the first place, or that the witness reports can be aggregated into a single measurable magnitude at all? Note also that I am unsure whether Keynes's weight depends only on the quantity of relevant evidence or also on how that evidence is distributed between the favourable and unfavourable sides; tell me what turns on that.

(c) Evaluate two natural formalizations. First, weight as something like effective sample size or posterior concentration: in a binomial model with a Beta prior, some quantity grows with data and governs how far the posterior can still be moved. Second, weight as sharpness under imprecise probabilities: represent the agent's state by a set of probability functions, let incoming evidence narrow the set, and identify weight with the narrowness of the resulting credal set. For each, say which of Keynes's claims it recovers and where the analogy breaks — in particular whether it can accommodate both the thesis that weight rises monotonically with relevant evidence while probability may fall, and the thesis that many probability relations are not measurable at all.

(d) Give the decision-theoretic upshot. If weight is genuinely distinct from probability, can it license ambiguity-averse behaviour of the Ellsberg kind as rational rather than as a bias? And is there any coherence pressure — a Dutch book, a money pump, a diachronic inconsistency — against letting weight influence choice, or do those arguments simply fail to engage because they were designed to constrain degrees of belief and Keynes's probabilities are not degrees of belief?

(e) Consider the reductionist reply: weight is just a second-order probability, a credence about the reliability or stability of one's first-order credence. State the strongest version of that reply, and then explain what Keynes's denial of universal comparability does to it, given that a second-order probability is itself supposed to be a magnitude.

A note on what I am not after. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or attributions to later commentators; I have no way to check them and they are not the point. My formation is in the rationalist-blog tradition, so my reflex is to translate everything into credences, updates, and coherence arguments, and that reflex seems to be exactly the wrong instrument here. I want the conceptual work done in prose, an account of weight that survives contact with the example above, and a candid statement of where the notion resists formalization. Where interpreters genuinely disagree, say that they disagree and identify what the disagreement turns on, rather than adopting one reading silently.
