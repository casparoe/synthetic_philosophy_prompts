At a reading group on the history of logic, two of our members got into a prolonged argument about Frege's political diary — the private notebook he kept in the year before his death, never prepared for publication, containing virulently antisemitic entries and attacks on democracy and parliamentary government. I sat through the whole exchange without saying anything, and I would like an impartial referee. Here is how it went:

1. Marcus opened that the diary is philosophically inert. The *Begriffsschrift*, the *Foundations of Arithmetic*, and the papers on sense and reference concern proof, definition, and meaning; their content is no less sound for having been written by a man whose private notebook is vile. To think otherwise is the genetic fallacy — where a belief came from is not evidence bearing on whether it is true — and if we applied Marcus's opponent's standard consistently we would have to stop teaching mathematics by anyone with a bad conscience.

2. Dana replied that the genetic-fallacy charge is blunter than he needs it to be. She is not offering the diary against a theorem; she is offering it against a doctrine that is not a theorem at all, namely his anti-psychologism — the picture on which the logical belongs to a realm untouched by the human, the historical, and the merely particular, a picture he laid down as a standing rule in the *Foundations*. The diary, she said, shows what that picture was doing for him: the same reflex of excluding the plural, the particular, and the merely human from the space of reasons appears in his contempt for democracy and for Jewish citizens. One temperament, two products.

3. Marcus said temperament is not a premise, and asked her to name a single doctrine in the logic or the semantics that she would have to state differently, or that would lose support, if the diary had never been written. Absent that, all she has is an atmosphere of resemblance, and resemblances of atmosphere can be manufactured between anything and anything by describing both sides loosely enough. He added that on her own account Frege has already supplied the answer: his rule is precisely that the conditions of discovery are irrelevant to justification, so she is condemning him by a principle he held and applied — which is the opposite of showing that his philosophy and his politics share a source.

4. Dana said the difficulty runs the other way. If thoughts are objective and belong to neither the outer nor the inner world, they are supposed to be equally accessible to every rational being; that is what makes logic binding on all thinkers alike and makes agreement across differences of psychology, history, and interest intelligible. His politics denies the parallel claim about persons, treating the nation as an organic whole whose unity precedes its members and treating the idea that a polity's direction can be settled by aggregating the standpoints of individuals as a fiction. He therefore owes an account of why plurality is defeated in the one case and harmless in the other, and that question is generated only by the two bodies of writing taken together.

5. Marcus answered that this concedes his point. She began by saying the diary bears on the philosophy and ended by saying it raises a question the philosophy does not answer — but every serious philosophy leaves questions unanswered, and a private notebook of 1924 is no more a threat to the *Basic Laws* than a hostile book review. He also pointed to her asymmetry: had the diary turned out to be impeccably liberal, she would not have said the philosophy thereby gained support it previously lacked, so she is counting the diary as evidence only in whichever direction damages him.

Neither would budge, and I do not want to settle for splitting the difference. Please give me exactly six numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first five rating moves 1 through 5 on a scale running from clearly mistaken through partially right to clearly right, each with a brief justification of that rating; and a sixth stating the most important thing both of them are overlooking — in particular, whether they have conflated the question of whether the diary bears on the truth or support of his doctrines with the question of whether it bears on how his doctrines should be read, and whether they are treating his philosophy as one thing when it is several things with different degrees of exposure.
