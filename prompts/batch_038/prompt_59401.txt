I am editing a short anthology of philosophical fiction and have an unfilled slot on non-classical logic. I would like you to write the story for it. Below is the conceit; please treat its details as binding rather than as suggestions.

**The world.** A public registry in a jurisdiction that has formally abandoned the principle that anything whatsoever follows from a contradiction. Because that principle is gone, the state can afford to record contradictions rather than suppress them: a deed, a verdict, a border, a person, a debt may be entered on the register as both obtaining and not obtaining, and the legal machinery continues to function. The clerks' actual work is containment. From a recorded contradiction, only the consequences the entry was written to license are permitted to propagate; everything else has to be actively dammed, entry by entry, and a great deal of the office's procedure exists for no other reason.

**The protagonist.** A mid-level registrar, competent and unremarkable, not a logician and never to be made into one. Long enough in the post to have developed instincts about which entries are stable and which are quietly corrosive. Give this character a working life and petty grievances; the ideas should arrive as job, not as lecture.

**The plot.** It should turn on the difference between two kinds of trouble, and on the fact that only one of them is recognised as trouble at all. The first is an entry of the ordinary sort: something recorded as both true and false, sitting inert, infecting precisely the narrow set of inferences it was filed to govern, and causing nobody any surprise. The second is a new instrument, a clause in a petition drafted by someone cleverer than the office expects, of roughly the form *if this petition is granted, then the registry must grant whatever the petitioner goes on to specify*. Two features matter and both should be dramatised rather than announced: the clause contains no negation anywhere, so the containment procedures your registrar has spent a career perfecting — procedures built entirely around negation and its misbehaviour — simply fail to engage with it; and if the conditional may be used twice, once to detach a conclusion and once to be fed back into itself, then the petition licenses an arbitrary conclusion of the petitioner's choosing. At that point the register is not merely inconsistent but worthless: every entry becomes derivable, and twenty years of careful containment is undone in an afternoon. Your registrar has to see this in time and decide what to do, and the reader should feel the difference between a system that can afford some contradictions and a system that cannot afford this one, without anyone in the story naming the phenomenon or explaining it to a colleague who happens to be a professor.

**Format.** Please return exactly two sections, in this order:

1. **The Story** — roughly 2,000 to 2,600 words. Title it as you see fit.
2. **Choices** — roughly 450 to 700 words of commentary on what you did and why.

In **Choices**, commit. Do not survey the options you considered and leave me to adjudicate among them; state the decisions you made and defend them. At minimum, address these four: (a) what your registrar ultimately does about the petition, and why that is the right thing for a *story* to do even if it is not what a logician would recommend for a *system*; (b) how much of the machinery you allowed the reader to see, and where you chose opacity over clarity; (c) whether you wrote the registry as a good institution, a corrupt one, or neither, and what the story consequently takes the honest recording of a contradiction to be worth — a gain, a luxury, a slow capitulation; take a position on this in the story's own terms; and (d) where you took liberties. If the register you built diverges from how a working paraconsistent logician would design one, or if you leaned on a contested reading of what such a system could or could not survive, say so plainly. I would rather have the divergences flagged than smoothed over, but I do not want them cut out either.
