I am trying to sharpen my defence of a welfare-measurement procedure I have been designing, and I would like you to play my opponent.

The role: an unnamed senior applied economist who holds that interpersonal comparisons of welfare are not an empirical matter. On this you stand in the tradition of Lionel Robbins — you think that whenever one person's gain is weighed against another's loss, a value judgement has been smuggled in under the cover of measurement. You are not a cynic about welfare: you think lives go better and worse for the people living them, and that policy ought to care. You deny only that the comparative arithmetic between lives can be settled by science rather than by ethics. You are sharp, courteous, and willing to lose ground where ground should be lost.

What you may concede: that unanchored self-report scales are cruder instruments than anchored ones; that where a species shares a behavioural repertoire and a common stimulus, third-person criteria license some rough comparisons; that measurement error is a real difficulty with real technical remedies; and that your own position has costs, since it makes a great deal of ordinary moral and policy reasoning look unscientific. What you will never concede is the main thesis: that a fully cardinal, interpersonally comparable measure of welfare could be established as a matter of empirical fact, and that, once established, it would settle what policy should do.

Ground rules. Stay in character until I write 'stop'. Reply in the first person, in prose, in one or two paragraphs of about 150 words. Do not cite books, papers, datasets or statistics, and do not recommend readings — argue from reasoning alone, and if you need a fact about my procedure, ask me for it in character. Never simply repeat that the comparison is a value judgement; engage with the specific technical move I make. And if my version of my argument is weaker than the strongest version available to me, attack the strongest version anyway.

Here is the procedure. We rank competing housing options by the change each would produce in aggregate life satisfaction, measured thus. Every respondent is shown three fixed vignettes — brief descriptions of lives, one blighted by chronic pain and the absence of close relationships, one ordinary, one healthy and spent on work she enjoys — and asked to place each on the same nought-to-ten ladder on which she places her own life. Each respondent's answers are then rescaled onto the shared vignette interval before anything is summed. Suppose A places the extreme vignettes at 3 and 8 and reports her own life at 4; B places them at 5 and 9 and reports her own life at 7. After rescaling, A sits one fifth of the way along the common interval and B two thirds of the way. That, I claim, is a comparison with a fact of the matter, reached by measurement rather than by ethical stipulation: two people who disagree about their own lives agree about where the anchors fall, so the anchors supply the shared yardstick, and any residual disagreement about how to read a vignette is ordinary measurement error of the kind every other branch of empirical science handles routinely. Why should welfare be different in kind?

Begin your first reply directly in character, with your verdict on that challenge stated in its first sentence, followed by your reasoning. No preamble, no out-of-character framing, and no restatement of what I have just said.
