So I'm slogging through chapter 2 of the first (1798) edition of Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population and I keep getting tangled up in how he actually establishes the two ratios. Two bits, back to back:

> "In the United States of America, where the means of subsistence have been more ample, the manners of the people more pure, and consequently the checks to early marriages fewer, than in any of the modern states of Europe, the population has been found to double itself in twenty-five years. This ratio of increase, though short of the utmost power of population, yet as the result of actual experience, we will take as our rule, and say, that population, when unchecked, goes on doubling itself every twenty-five years or increases in a geometrical ratio."

> "Let us now take any spot of earth, this Island for instance, and see in what ratio the subsistence it affords can be supposed to increase. We will begin with it under its present state of cultivation. If I allow that by the best possible policy, by breaking up more land and by great encouragements to agriculture, the produce of this Island may be doubled in the first twenty-five years, I think it will be allowing as much as any person can well demand. In the next twenty-five years, it is impossible to suppose that the produce could be quadrupled. It would be contrary to all our knowledge of the qualities of land. The very utmost that we can conceive, is, that the increase in the second twenty-five years might equal the present produce. Let us then take this for our rule, though certainly far beyond the truth, and allow that, by great exertion, the whole produce of the Island might be increased every twenty-five years, by a quantity of subsistence equal to what it at present produces. The most enthusiastic speculator cannot suppose a greater increase than this. In a few centuries it would make every acre of land in the Island like a garden. Yet this ratio of increase is evidently arithmetical."

Two more sentences I keep coming back to, one from earlier in the same chapter and one from the preface (where he talks about himself in the third person):

> "Consequently in no state that we have yet known has the power of population been left to exert itself with perfect freedom."

> "If he should succeed in drawing the attention of more able men to what he conceives to be the principal difficulty in the way to the improvement of society and should, in consequence, see this difficulty removed, even in theory, he will gladly retract his present opinions and rejoice in a conviction of his error."

What I want help with:

1. The two ratios are meant to be doing the same sort of work but they're built in exactly opposite ways. The American figure comes from experience and is then declared to be *less* than the real capacity; the island figure comes from nowhere near experience and is declared to be *more* than the truth. Walk me through what each hedge is protecting against, and what the pair of them together does to the claim that one series is geometrical and the other arithmetical. Is he stating two laws, or two bounds?

2. Given "when unchecked" plus his own admission that the power of population has never actually been left free anywhere, is there any observation that could contradict the doubling claim? Say explicitly what the American numbers are being offered as evidence *of*, since on his own account they cant be evidence about an unchecked population.

3. The "far beyond the truth" concession looks like it should hurt him, and I think it actually does the opposite, but I cant articulate why. Spell out the move. Then give me the cost: if he's wrong about it in the direction things actually went — food output rising much faster than an arithmetical series for a century or two — what in the argument survives and what dies? Specifically, does the point where the two series cross get pushed further out, or does it disappear, and which of the two hedges from (1) is responsible for the answer.

4. That preface line, "removed, even in theory" — what does it tell you about what he took his own thesis to be answerable to? Is that an honest statement of scope or a way of pre-emptively disqualifying counter-evidence?
