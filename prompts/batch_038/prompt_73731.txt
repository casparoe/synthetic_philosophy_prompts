I'm giving a talk in about three weeks at a workshop on welfare measurement and social philosophy — mixed room, not a philosophy-only crowd. Maybe four welfare economists who trained in social choice theory and are alergic to interpersonal comparisons of utility, one political philosopher who thinks any veil-of-ignorance move is just consequentialism wearing a hat, a happiness economist who measures wellbeing on 0–10 self-report scales and will want to know what my claims cash out to empirically, and a handful of grad students who've read Broome and Sen closely and will be the ones asking the actually technical questions. There's also a committed total utilitarian who's on my side but for reasons I think are bad, which is its own hazard.

My thesis: the Harsanyi impartial-observer argument should be read as a template, not as a proof of utilitarianism. If you keep the empathetic identification of the observer's preferences with the individuals' preferences but let the observer aggregate over social states rank-dependently rather than by plain expected utility, you get prioritarianism instead — so the theorem's structure is neutral between the two, and the distributive work is done by whatever risk transformation the observer applies to the distribution of outcomes. That's supposed to blunt the usual complaints: that the theorem smuggles in cardinal full comparability, and that it's really just the observer's own risk attitudes doing the normative lifting. My line is that those objections don't kill the argument, they relocate where the argument has to be made — you have to defend the observer's transformation, not the identification assumption.

Sketch of the argument as I'll present it: (i) the observer, under empathetic identification, has the preferences of the individuals across the possible social states; (ii) the observer is a rational agent under uncertainty in the expected-utility sense; (iii) the observer's weighting function over the ranked outcomes of the social states *is* the distributive principle — linear gives average utilitarianism, concave-ish gives prioritarianism; (iv) therefore the theorem underdetermines the distributive view and the real question is about the observer's risk attitude, which is a normative question, not a technical one.
