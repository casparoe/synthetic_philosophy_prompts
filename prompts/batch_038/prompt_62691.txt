I spent thirty-nine years at the bench in molecular genetics and retired three years ago; I have since been reading philosophy of biology with more time than discipline. One question has come to occupy me disproportionately: whether it is anything more than a manner of speaking to say that DNA is a code. My former colleagues used the phrase several times a day and would be irritated by the question, and the philosophers I have been reading seem to divide in ways I cannot yet map. I should be grateful for a decision tree that would let me locate anyone's answer, including my own, which I suspect is not the one I give at dinner parties.

Please construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a roster of named views. I want the branch questions first, and only afterwards the positions placed at the leaves, together with any combinations of answers that turn out to be unoccupied; for each empty cell, a sentence diagnosing whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it has simply not been tried.

Constraints on the nodes:

- Each must be answerable by a working biologist or an intelligent layperson without knowing where it leads. None may ask, in effect, whether the term is literal or metaphorical; that is what is to be diagnosed, not the instrument for diagnosing it.
- Each must be anchored in a concrete case. I should like the following to appear somewhere among them: a synonymous codon substitution that leaves the protein unchanged but alters how much of it is made; a genome rewritten in the laboratory so that a codon which formerly signalled termination now specifies an amino acid not found in nature; a viral sequence read in two overlapping frames at once; a heritable trait transmitted by a misfolded protein with no change of sequence anywhere; a strand built in a synthesizer that has never been inside a cell; and the genome of the last individual of an extinct species, sitting in a museum drawer, which will never be read again.
- Ten to twelve nodes, eight to ten leaves, and no more than two leaves of the purely dismissive sort, the ones on which the question is verbal and there is no fact of the matter.

One axis I want represented, because it is my own and I do not know whether it will bear weight: I have come to suspect that the decisive question is not whether the pairing of codon to amino acid is arbitrary, but whether the system contains two separable roles — an inscription mechanism that could have written any of a range of messages, and a reading mechanism whose behaviour would differ under each — and whether those roles are instantiated by different parts of the cell. Call it the separable-roles condition. Please include a node that tests it, and tell me candidly whether it discriminates anything or merely relabels the dispute.

For each leaf I want four things: a name, which may be an established one or one you coin; a single sentence stating what the position makes of the word code; its most awkward commitment; and one cash-out, a claim stated without any semantic vocabulary that would be false were the position false. The last is my protection against the whole exercise dissolving into a quarrel about words.

Please also flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and any node whose place in the sequence does real work, where asking it earlier or later would sort the same person differently. I do not want a bibliography or a census of who holds what; I am after the geography. If it please you, end with a compact key translating the commonest patterns of answers into leaves, since I intend to administer this to a reading group of retired scientists, every one of whom will be certain he occupies the sensible position.
