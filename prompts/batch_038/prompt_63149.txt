I'm a staff engineer and I own our incident review process. We do what we call blameless postmortems: after an outage, the writeup explains things in terms of systems, tooling and incentives, nobody's name goes in the contributing factors section, and the framing is that whoever shipped the bad config did the reasonable thing given what they could see at the time. But at the same company we hand out public shoutouts to the person who stayed up until 3am fixing that same outage, we stack-rank everyone in the review cycle and put people on improvement plans for behavior we'd just called reasonable in the postmortem, and if someone clicks a phishing link during a security incident there suddenly is a name attached and there are consequences. So it feels like we're running two different accounts of who's responsible for a bad outcome and picking between them based on whether the damage landed on the company or on a customer.

Can you spell out what the blameless postmortem actually commits you to? Specifically, does it need the claim that nobody could have done otherwise, or does it survive perfectly well if people have free will and we're just choosing to be kind about it? Then tell me which of the things I described above are flatly inconsistent with it versus merely awkward, and whether one coherent story can cover all of them.

One more thing. The practice seems built to make sure nobody feels guilty or ashamed after breaking production, on the theory that its just going to make people hide stuff. But the best engineers I know visibly feel awful afterwards and that awfulness seems to be exactly what makes them careful next time. Is there a version of this that keeps the guilt and drops the shame, or is that a distinction without a difference when your manager is sitting in the room? What would our process have to look like if we decided guilt was a feature to cultivate rather than a risk to engineer away?
