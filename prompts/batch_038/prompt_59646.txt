hi — for my ethics-and-economics seminar we built this argument against interpersonal utility comparisons and I can't find where to stop it.

(1) A claim about people's welfare is factual only if what makes it true could in principle be pinned down by measurement or verification.
(2) All that ever gets pinned down about one person's welfare is the ordering of their own outcomes — nothing selects a zero point or a unit for their utility scale.
(3) So each person's utility function is only defined up to a positive affine transformation, and which transform is the right one is a free, fact-less choice.
(4) Sums, ratios and comparisons across people shift when you transform scales, so any claim resting on them isn't pinned down by anything factual.
(5) So there's no fact about whether A's gain outweighs B's loss.
(6) By the same reasoning there's no fact about whether my headache today is worse than the migraine I'll get next week, since nothing pins the scale for future-me either — and if I take Parfit seriously about fission, future-me isn't obviously me at all.
(7) So prudential trade-offs over time are just as indeterminate as interpersonal ones.
(8) But it can't be a coin flip whether I take the aspirin now or in an hour, and it isn't a matter of fiat that the single dose should go to my roommate with the migraine instead of the one with the dull ache.
(9) Therefore (5) is false and interpersonal comparisons are fine after all.

My professor says (9) doesn't follow and I'm inclined to agree, but I can't say why, and half the class thinks the whole chain is just valid with a bad premise somewhere near the top. I'd rather not give up (2) or (8) if I can help it.

So: which single step do you reject, why did it look so innocent going in, and what does each plausible candidate for rejection actually cost you — the economics, the metaphysics, and whatever you have to say about the aspirin case?
