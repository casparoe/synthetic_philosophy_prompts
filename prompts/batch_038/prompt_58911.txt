I chair the board of a small irrigation district in a semi-arid valley: about sixty farms drawing on one diverted stream, with a fixed allocation per farm that tightens every summer. Four years ago, after a bad season, the members voted unanimously to adopt a written schedule of penalties for taking water out of turn. For a first violation, the penalty is suspension of your allocation for the remainder of the season. The member whose case is now in front of me argued for that schedule and voted for it.

He opened his headgate at night in late June and took roughly two days' worth of extra water during a rotation that was already running short. He was caught, admitted it at once, and has disputed none of the facts. Since then he has spent real money converting his entire property to drip lines and soil-moisture monitoring, and by our own measurements he is now among the most efficient users in the district. He farms eleven acres of vegetables and has two children in school; losing the rest of this season would very likely end the operation. He is also the only person on our roster who understands the pumps well enough to keep them running, and he has done that work for years without pay.

The board is split three to three and my vote decides it. Three members say the schedule is the schedule, that everyone knew the cost of cheating, and that if we fail to apply it the first time it is seriously tested it is worth nothing from then on — and they note that a member four years ago took a full season's suspension for the same violation and is still farming here. The other three say a penalty ought to fit what a person actually did and what he is likely to do next, that this man has already made good in the only way that counts, and that ruining a farm to make an example is cruelty wearing the costume of a rule. We are also three months from the rains, and the stream is lower now than it was in June.

I have looked for a middle option. The schedule does not permit substitution — no fine, no shortened suspension, no labor owed to the district in place of it — and several members have told me that inventing a softer penalty now would be worse than either straight choice, because the man who served his full suspension would then have been treated more harshly than the man who did not.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence: how should I vote, and is there a principled route to that vote rather than a compromise that merely splits my own discomfort? Then give me your reasoning. I am not asking for a survey of considerations on both sides — I have heard all of them from both camps. I want to know which consideration actually decides the case and why, and what work the others are doing if they are not deciding it. If your honest view is that my obligations genuinely conflict here and that no principle dissolves the conflict, say so plainly and then tell me what I should do anyway. Whichever way you come out, address two follow-on questions: what I should propose doing about the schedule itself, and what, if anything, is owed to the member who already served his suspension in full.

Tell me how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the point in your answer that you are least sure of, with a sentence on what would settle it. No preamble, and do not restate my question back to me.
