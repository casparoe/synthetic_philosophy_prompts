I work in the financial-crime and customer-outcomes function of a mid-sized lender, and I have been asked to sign off on an arrangement that I cannot quite articulate my unease about, so I should like help reconstructing what it actually assumes. Some months ago we began using a machine-learning model to decide routine matters: whether to reduce a customer's credit limit, whether a flagged transaction is genuinely fraudulent, whether an application needs manual review. Because the model's own workings are opaque to us, we have licensed a separate language-model system whose job is to take the features the deciding model weighed and the outcome it reached, and to compose a fluent, case-specific explanation of it. That explanation is placed on letter-headed paper, signed by a case officer, and sent to the customer. Nobody in the chain has worked the reasons out: the officer reads the draft for obvious errors and signs it, and the vendor's own documentation is candid that the text need not correspond to any computation the deciding model actually performed. The internal standard we have been given is that the rationale must be faithful enough, which is glossed as follows: a competent analyst presented with the same information about the customer would very likely have reached the same decision on grounds of that general kind. A colleague in legal assures me the arrangement is unremarkable and that every lender she knows has one.

Here is what puzzles me. In the same firm, a human officer who gives a customer reasons for a decision and then, when the decision is challenged, produces better ones is in serious trouble: the reasons offered at the time are treated as the reasons on which the decision was taken, and improving them afterwards is treated as a species of dishonesty. The wider legal culture agrees, in broad outline at least. Where a public body must justify a decision on review, it is generally confined to the grounds it relied on when it acted, and its lawyers may not construct a sounder justification for it later, however sound that justification would have been. Our practice towards the machine seems to require precisely what we forbid our humans to do: a rationale manufactured after the event, by an agent that did not make the decision, for the purpose of making the decision look reasonable.

So: what theory of reasons does the machine-side practice presuppose, what theory does the human-side prohibition presuppose, and can one institution hold both without contradiction? I am particularly interested in the possibility that the first practice assumes reasons can exist without reasoners — that a rationale is a property of a decision's inputs and outputs, and of the space of considerations that would licence it, rather than a report of any particular agent's deliberative history — whereas the second assumes the opposite, namely that a reason is essentially someone's reason, bound to the actual grounds on which a mind moved. Please reconstruct the implicit theory on each side as carefully as you can; separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it, since a firm might hold that the letter is not a report of the reasons at all but a justification tendered to the customer, in which case the two norms need not collide; and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that rival accounts of explanation — causal, inferential, normative, or frankly pragmatic — would each licence what we do. Two further requests. First, is the standard of being faithful enough even well defined: what account of explanation would turn it into a real test rather than a gesture, and does anything in the practice depend on which account turns out to be right? Second, tell me what would have to change institutionally if we adopted the strict view that reasons must always be some agent's reasons: who in our arrangement would have to be the reasoner — the signing officer, the firm as a corporate agent, the model itself, or nobody at all — and what precisely would we have to stop doing. If a comparison helps, mathematics routinely accepts theorems on the strength of arguments so long, or so dependent on machine checking, that no single person has ever gone through the whole of them; consider whether that is the same phenomenon of a reason floating free of its reasoner or a different one, on the ground that any mathematician could in principle take the reasoning up and make it her own.
