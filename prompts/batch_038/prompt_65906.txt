I am trying to pin down what I mean when I call a piece of behaviour sphexish, and I would like to do it the way one settles any slippery predicate: by reporting my verdicts on a batch of cases and seeing whether they add up to a principle. My working gloss is loose — action driven by a fixed routine that is triggered by local cues and keeps running after the point of running it has lapsed — and my tentative principle is this: an agent behaves sphexishly in a situation just in case (a) its action is governed by a routine installed for conditions that no longer obtain, (b) the change is detectable from information the agent already has, and (c) the agent fails to detect it. I suspect this is wrong in instructive places. The cases:

1. The digger wasp of the familiar anecdote. She drags her paralysed prey (a katydid, in the usual telling) to the mouth of the burrow, goes inside to check it, comes out and drags it in. If an experimenter moves the prey a few inches while she is underground, she finds it, hauls it back to the mouth, goes in again, comes out, drags it in — and will repeat this as many times as you interfere, never once carrying the prey straight inside. Sphexish: the paradigm.

2. A warehouse picking arm which, when a bin arrives rotated slightly outside its training distribution, attempts one particular grasp, fails, resets, and attempts the identical grasp again, for hours, at full speed and with no drop in confidence. Sphexish.

3. A very strong chess engine that in an open position plays a squeeze no human would find, but in a locked position with fixed pawns and no available plan shuffles its king between two squares until something falls. Sphexish — though only in that pocket, and I cannot say what licenses me to withhold the charge from the rest of its play.

4. An airline captain's read-aloud checklist, performed item by item on her ten-thousandth departure, which on this occasion catches a fuel-panel setting her eyes had already slid past twice. Not sphexish. Here the rote is the virtue; I would fault her for omitting it.

5. My grandmother's mourning routine: the same words in the same order, the same photographs, the same meal, at every funeral she attended over thirty years, including those of people she had met twice. Not sphexish — it read as devotion. Yet it is, as far as I can tell, exactly as cue-triggered and exactly as indifferent to the particulars as the checklist.

6. A man who answers whatever his partner says with the same three rebuttals, in the same order, and wins most of the arguments anyway. Sphexish — and I have no evidence any of the three rebuttals was false.

7. A master player's two-second intuition: the right move arrives immediately out of thousands of chunked patterns, and when asked why she offers a plausible rationalisation that careful introspection studies suggest is constructed afterwards. Not sphexish. That is what expertise looks like, even though structurally it is a lookup table with a narrator bolted on.

8. A chatbot that answers a question it has never seen by fluent recombination, never declines, and does not notice that its third paragraph contradicts its first. Partly sphexish; but when its answer genuinely tracks the wording of the question I withdraw the charge, and I cannot say what the difference between those two situations consists in.

9. A thermostat. Nobody calls a thermostat sphexish, and neither do I, though it is more rigid than anything on this list. So the predicate seems to require that the thing was supposed to be flexible — which makes me worry that sphexishness is a fact about my expectations rather than about the agent.

10. A man who has driven the same route to work for eleven years. When the bridge closes he finds a better route within a day and keeps it for the next decade. Not sphexish. But the revision was triggered by a barricade, and I am confident he would not have noticed a drift with no barricade in it: ten minutes a day, creeping, for a year. Sphexish with respect to the drift, not sphexish with respect to the closure — and I do not know whether that is two verdicts about one agent or a sign that my case is misdescribed.

11. The one I keep going back and forth on. A man early in recovery from alcohol dependence decided, sober and in advance, that he would not answer calls from one particular number, and he has not answered it since — mechanically, on the first ring, while wanting to, and still not answering nine years later when the risk is long gone and when, on at least one occasion, the call turned out to be his sister using that phone. Every structural feature I listed under the wasp is present: a fixed response to a local cue, no fresh assessment of the situation, persistence after the conditions have lapsed, and a lapse detectable from what he already knows. But I want to call this the opposite of sphexish. I want to call it an act of will.

What I would like:

- State the principle, or principles, that best fit these verdicts — precisely enough that a third party could apply it to a new case without consulting my intuitions — with the clause-by-clause motivation drawn from the cases above. If no single principle does the work and I am in fact deploying a cluster, say so, and say what holds the cluster together.
- Test my tentative principle from the opening paragraph against the list, identify exactly where it breaks, and say whether it can be repaired or must be replaced.
- Report what your principle delivers for cases I have not listed: a search-and-rescue drone returning repeatedly to the coordinates where its sensors last registered a survivor, long after the thermal evidence has gone cold; a liturgy recited weekly by a congregation that no longer believes part of it; a research field's standard methodology, applied competently for decades after the phenomena that motivated it ceased to be the interesting ones.
- Construct one further case of your own that discriminates between the rival principles you are weighing, and say which way you predict I would rule on it and why.
- Tell me plainly which of my verdicts your principle cannot save, and which you think I should give up. I would rather be told that 5 and 11 cannot both stand than be handed a principle with eleven epicycles.
- Finally: does your principle make sphexishness a property of the agent, of the environment, or of the relation between them — given that the wasp is sphexish only because someone with a katydid is standing there, and that any system flexible enough to look non-sphexish at one resolution presumably decomposes into rigid subroutines at a finer one? If the answer is the relation, what survives of the thought that showing a person to be sphexish shows something about their freedom?
