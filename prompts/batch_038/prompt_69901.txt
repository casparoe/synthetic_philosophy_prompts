Ok, so I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training, and I've been stuck on this for about a week and my partner is done hearing about it.

Situation: I got offered a spot on a winter-over crew at an Antarctic research station. 14 months, systems work, and once the season closes there's no leaving. I read some stuff about transformative experience (L.A. Paul's thing I think) and built myself an argument for why this decision isn't rationally decidable. Then the argument kept growing legs and now it proves way more than I can swallow. Here it is:

1. To choose rationally between options I have to be able to put a utility on each outcome, and I can only put a utility on an outcome if I can know what that outcome would be like for me.
2. Some experiences are the kind where you can't know what they'd be like from the outside. No description, no testimony from people who've had them, no amount of mentally simulating it gets you there. And they change you enough that the person who'd be living the outcome isn't the person doing the choosing.
3. Antarctica in winter is like that for me. I've read the winter-over accounts, I've talked to two guys who did a season, and I still have no idea what it would be like, and whoever walks back onto the ice runway in spring is definately not exactly the me sitting here.
4. So I can't rationally choose to go. And by 1 and 2, the same holds for any option whose outcome includes an experience I've never had.
5. But basically every real option includes an experience I've never had. A dish I've never eaten, a city I've never lived in, a book I've never read, a job I've never done.
6. So basically no real choice is rationally choosable.
7. Worse: learning anything is one of those options. Reading a book, taking a course, running an experiment at work. Each one changes what I care about afterwards, and from where I'm sitting I can't know how. So the rational play is to decline all of them and stay as ignorant as possible for as long as possible.
8. And if 7 is right, I couldn't have rationally read my way into this argument either. So nobody who followed the reasoning got here rationally, which means whoever asserts 6 or 7 is asserting something their own situation contradicts.

Either something's broken somewhere, or the only rational life is a maximally sheltered one and I can't even rationally hold the view I'm asking you about. Which step do you kill, and what does killing it cost you?
