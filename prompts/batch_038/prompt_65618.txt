Write a short story, 900 to 1,200 words, in American English, about the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem — though the story itself must never name the theorem, never name its authors, and never use the phrase "strategy-proof." The result has to be discovered on the page by a character who does not start out knowing it.

Narrator: the town clerk of Kettle Falls, in first person, who must personally sign the certification and is hunting for a voting rule nobody can game. The ballot question is what to do with the derelict mill site: A = demolish it for a parking deck, B = restore it as a market hall, C = leave it as a ruin and riverbank preserve. There are 100 voters in three blocs with these true preferences:

- 40: A > B > C
- 35: B > C > A
- 25: C > A > B

Requirements:

1. The clerk must try at least two rules — plurality, a top-two runoff, Borda counts, a fixed agenda of head-to-head votes, anything you like — and each of the two you dwell on must be broken on the page by misreporting whose arithmetic a reader can redo from the bloc sizes above. Show ballots and tallies as ballots and tallies, not as summaries. Keep the arithmetic exact; a rule that survives this profile untouched may appear, and its brief survival is part of the drama.
2. Include a scene in which a resident proposes the escape of arranging the three options along a single left-to-right axis so that every voter's preferences fall away from one peak, and the clerk works out, from the numbers, why that door is shut in this town.
3. End with the town adopting a rule that genuinely cannot be beaten by lying about one's preferences, and paying a recognizable price for it.

No lecturing, and no dialogue that is secretly a textbook: the insight must arrive as trouble for particular people.

End with one final line in the exact form `Moral: ` followed by a single sentence of no more than 25 words.
