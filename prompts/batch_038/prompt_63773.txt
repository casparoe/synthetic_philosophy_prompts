help me think through a game theory thing I keep going back and forth on.

Standard program equilibrium, as I understand it: two players face a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, but instead of picking C or D directly they each submit a program. Every program gets handed the other program's actual source code as input, then outputs C or D, and the payoffs come from the outputs. Two facts about this setup worth keeping in view. First, constant-defect programs are still an equilibrium — they ignore thier input, so nothing really changes relative to the ordinary Nash story. Second, there are also cooperative equilibria that just aren't available in the ordinary game: e.g. two copies of a program that searches for a proof the opponent cooperates and defects if it finds no such proof will, via a Löb's-theorem-style trick, find the proof about each other and both cooperate, and neither can gain by unilaterally swapping in some other program.

Now here's a variant I'm making up, call it fingerprint equilibrium. Programs are still submitted and still deterministic, but nobody gets to read the opponent's source. Instead each program is shown a behavioral fingerprint: the opponent's output when run against a fixed public list of, say, a hundred probe programs — constant-C, constant-D, tit-for-tat, a bunch of other bots — and that fingerprint is the only information it gets. So you're transparent about behavior but not about code.

Compare the two equilibrium concepts properly. Which equilibria survive the move and which don't; is the cooperative equilibrium in the fingerprint version the same kind of animal as the Löbian one, or something else entirely; and — the part I actually care about — is fingerprint equilibrium a genuinely weaker notion of transparency, or does the gap between "you can read my code" and "you can watch what I do in a hundred test runs" turn out not to matter nearly as much as it looks? I have a suspicion there's a cheap exploit involving programs that behave one way against the probes and another way in the real game, but I want you to work out whether that actually kills cooperation or just reshuffles it into some other form. Then take a position on whether fingerprint equilibrium is a real distinct concept worth keeping or a disguised version of something we already have, and argue for it.
