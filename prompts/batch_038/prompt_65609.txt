For two years a reductionist and a realist about dispositions in our metaphysics group have argued past one another, and they have now agreed to produce a joint closing document instead of continuing. The reductionist defends the reformed conditional analysis in the Lewisian tradition: to be disposed to break when struck is for a hedged counterfactual to be true — hedged by a proviso that holds the object's relevant intrinsic character fixed and excludes interference from outside the object — and there is nothing here over and above the categorical basis together with the laws. The realist denies this: dispositions are genuine properties standing in an irreducible relation to stimulus types and manifestation types, so that a disposition can be fully present while every counterfactual one might write down about it comes out false. Draft the document the two of them should be able to sign.

Work under a protocol both have accepted. They argue in a shared vocabulary containing a three-place predicate D(x, s, m) — x is disposed to manifest m when stimulated in way s — a two-place predicate B(x, b) relating an object to a categorical basis, and ordinary counterfactual resources; each side states its position as axioms or axiom schemata over that vocabulary, and neither may appeal to a notion not expressible in it.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign in identical wording — on what exactly makes the unhedged conditional analysis false; on whether finking, masking, and mimicry (an object that lacks the disposition yet would behave as though it had it) are one phenomenon, two, or three; on whether an object's duplicate in a different environment can differ in what it is disposed to do; and on how far ordinary disposition ascriptions are context-sensitive.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the residual disagreement in wording the reductionist accepts as fair to her view; the same disagreement in wording the realist accepts as fair to his; the axiom or schema on which they differ; and whether that difference is substantive or merely a difference in how the shared vocabulary is to be read.

3. A crux register built around the three engineered cases below. For each, record both parties' classification in advance, before they have discussed it, then note where the classifications coincide and where they diverge, and what would settle the divergence:
 - Self-sealing pane. A brittle composite that does begin to craze when struck, but whose dispersed second phase, activated by the stress wave of that same impact, flows into the crack faces and bonds them faster than the crack can propagate. The pane survives. Is the craze pattern a partial manifestation? Is this a mask, a fink, or a third kind of case?
 - Gradual basis swap. Two elastically identical springs; over the course of a year one has its lattice replaced molecule by molecule with a structurally distinct but elastically equivalent one. The same counterfactuals hold throughout, yet the basis at the end is not the basis at the start. Is it numerically the same disposition?
 - Missed blow. An impact rig fitted with an actuator that detects an incoming hammer and deflects it just before contact, on every trial, leaving the pane's microstructure and everything else about the pane untouched. The pane is never struck and never breaks. Is the hedged counterfactual true of it, and what determines the answer without smuggling back in the very notion the analysis was meant to explicate?

4. For each crux, the argument, case, or empirical result both agree would bear on it — including at least one crux on which they agree that a finding from materials science or from fundamental physics, rather than a further thought experiment, is what would move them — with each side's prediction of what that finding will show, recorded beforehand.

5. A closing section identifying which parts of the dispute the collaboration now takes to have been verbal, and saying what made them hard to recognize as verbal from the inside.

Do not supply citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; the document has to stand on its own reasoning.
