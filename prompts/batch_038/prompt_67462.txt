I am in my final year of secondary school and I serve as secretary of our Philosophy Society, a student-run group of roughly thirty members between the ages of fifteen and eighteen that meets on Thursday evenings. For our next meeting we have taken up the question of whether lying and misleading are morally different — that is, whether it is worse to say something one believes to be false than to say something true in a manner one expects will leave the hearer with a false belief. In our preparatory discussion the group split into what appeared to be four or five camps, but nobody could say where the boundaries fell, and several members observed the puzzling fact that they agreed on nearly every example while disagreeing on why they agreed. I have accordingly been asked to prepare a diagnostic instrument for the meeting, and I would be grateful for your assistance in designing it.

I am not asking for an essay on the topic. I am asking for a classification device in five parts, to be used in the order given.

**Part one: the axes.** Before writing a single question, set out the claims along which the positions in this debate genuinely come apart. I expect at least the following to appear, though you should add any I have missed: what makes a lie wrong in the first place (the deception it causes, the violation of an assurance the hearer was entitled to rely on, the breach of a norm attaching to assertion as such, or the harm); whether the difference between lying and misleading is one of kind or only of degree; whether the hearer's entitlement to the truth varies with the relationship and the circumstances; what state of mind is required (believing the statement false, merely lacking belief in its truth, or being indifferent either way); whether the deception must succeed; and whether the distinction between what is said and what is merely implied carries moral weight of its own.

**Part two: the questions.** Ten questions, each presenting one concrete case with exactly three answer options. Every question must be answerable by a member who has never encountered the debate, so the questions themselves must contain no technical vocabulary: save terms such as implicature, insincerity, and assertion norms for the key. No question may ask what a member thinks the definition of lying is, or which camp or philosopher they favour; each must sort by what the member says about a situation. Each question should be tied in the key to the axis or axes it probes. Please avoid the two examples everyone reaches for — the inquirer at the door whose intentions are murderous, and the deposition testimony of a head of state — and instead draw on cases of the following kinds, using at least one of each: a truthful but omissive statement in a commercial transaction; an ambiguous phrase that the speaker knows the hearer will resolve in the wrong direction; a lie told to someone who has forfeited any claim to be told the truth; a lie the hearer sees through immediately; a misleading statement the speaker corrects voluntarily before the hearer acts on it; a case where the misleading produces more harm than a blunt lie would have; a speaker who does not care whether what is said is true; a speaker who asserts something they believe false and which nevertheless turns out true; and a case in which silence or a refusal to answer is compared with an evasion.

**Part three: the tree.** Build a decision tree from the axes to a set of leaves. Label every branch with the question that separates it from its siblings, and every leaf with a descriptive name for the position, together with the standard name if the position has one in the literature. Beside each leaf, give one sentence naming its most awkward commitment: the case from part two that it handles worst. Please locate within the tree, or explain why it cannot be located there, the early modern casuist practice of silently restricting the meaning of one's own words — the practice our members met under the name of mental reservation — and also tell us what the accepted term of art is, if there is one, for the practice of producing a false impression by means of statements that are strictly true.

**Part four: the gaps.** Identify at least three combinations of answers that the axes make available but that no one seems actually to hold, and say for each whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. Then flag honestly any pair of leaves that my ten questions cannot separate, and say what additional question would be needed to separate them.

**Part five: the key.** A scoring key mapping patterns of answers onto the leaves, including a rule for mixed patterns and a designated tie-breaker question for each pair that runs close, followed by a short paragraph per leaf beginning with the words "what your result means," suitable for handing to a member who has just finished the quiz.

Three further constraints. The quiz must be printable, so please keep each question and its three options within about eighty words. Please do not append a reading list or any citations, since our members have no access to a research library and would only be misled by guesses. And write throughout in plain, formal English; the aim is an instrument our members will actually use on themselves, not a summary of the state of the debate.
