I sit on a faculty advisory panel reviewing a proposal to replace our state's upper house with a standing citizens' chamber: some one hundred and twenty members drawn by stratified civic lottery, serving staggered two-year terms, holding a suspensive veto and agenda-setting powers over the elected assembly. At Tuesday's hearing the proposal's chief advocate disposed of my objections concerning competence and accountability in a single line — 'sortition is democracy, election is aristocracy' — and then added that the objections were themselves evidence of aristocratic bias. The remark runs through the whole sortition literature, and it descends from Montesquieu's observation that suffrage by lot is natural to democracy while suffrage by choice is natural to aristocracy, which Rousseau accepts but grounds differently: in a democracy office is a burdensome charge that the law, rather than anyone's will, should distribute. I should like to know what the sentence is actually asserting before I decide whether it answered me at all. I already know that the classical evidence is less tidy than the slogan implies — Athens elected its generals and put lot-selected officials through scrutiny on entry and accounting on exit — so please do not simply repeat that point back to me.

Give exactly five numbered entries and nothing else: no preamble, no closing remarks. Each entry should state one precise claim the slogan might be making — they may differ in the sense assigned to 'aristocracy', in whether the claim is conceptual, causal-sociological, historical, or normative, and in whether it concerns the procedure itself or the bodies the procedure produces; tag that claim as a platitude, as substantive and defensible, or as false or unsupported; and say whether it would license either of the two moves my colleague made, namely that the reform restores democracy rather than departing from it, and that objections from competence and accountability are symptoms of bias rather than reasons. Where you think advocates characteristically intend more than one of your readings, indicate which within the entry. Keep each entry to three sentences or fewer.
