Two philosophers have argued past each other for years about doing and allowing. One holds that the difference between bringing a harm about and merely failing to prevent it marks a real, non-derivative moral distinction. The other holds that the distinction only ever tracks variables that can be pried apart from it — cost to the agent, the agent's prior involvement, whether a threat is already underway, and how many people stand to suffer if nothing is done — and that once these are matched, no moral work is left for it to do.

Draft the document this collaboration should yield. Open with numbered claims both can sign: about what explanatory work the distinction is being asked to do, about which variables the stock examples fail to control for, and about at least one class of case — the withdrawal of treatment at a patient's request, or double prevention, where one thwarts someone else's thwarting of a good — in which both agree that the doing/allowing description is itself unclear. Then set out the residual disagreements in wording each side would accept as a fair statement of its own view. Then give the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move someone, each paired with a case or case-pair both agree bears on it, and with each side's prediction recorded in advance about what the other will say. If some of the dispute is about the word "doing" rather than about morality, identify which part and rewrite it so that only the substantive remainder is left. Close with what each side would accept as counting against its own position, and whether any such evidence is in fact obtainable.
