You are a philosopher who works on judgment aggregation, collective intentionality, and group agency, and who has advised real decision-making bodies — tribunals, licensing boards, prize juries, regulatory panels — on how to structure their votes. I want you to treat this as a consulting engagement with a client who has a live institutional problem, not as a survey of the literature. Think carefully before you answer; work the vote profiles through yourself first, but hand me the finished procedure and analysis rather than your raw deliberation.

**The setting.** I chair a nine-member accreditation panel. We sit twice a year, over two days each time, and decide roughly twenty programs per sitting. Our determinations decide whether a program's students can receive state funding, so the stakes for the institutions are severe. Members are practitioners — clinicians, program directors, a couple of retired judges — not logicians; nobody in the room will follow a formal argument, and the protocol you write has to survive being read aloud at 4:30 on the second day. I certify the report and my signature is what makes it operative.

**The standard.** Bylaw 3 provides that a program is accredited if and only if it satisfies all three of:

- **F** — the faculty collectively meet the qualification and staffing standard;
- **O** — graduate completion and placement outcomes meet the threshold;
- **R** — facilities, clinical supervision arrangements, and financial resources meet the standard.

Bylaw 7 (the "waiver") provides that the panel may nonetheless accredit where **exactly one** of F, O, R is unmet, if at least six members vote in favor of waiving. Six is an absolute number fixed against the full nine-member panel.

Two features of the appeals process drive everything. First, the state appeals board reviews our **findings on F, O, and R de novo**, and will overturn a finding only if it is clearly erroneous; it does **not** re-hear the accreditation conclusion as such. That is why I have been instinctively drawn to voting criterion-by-criterion and letting the conclusion fall out: the findings are the part that has to be defensible in writing. Second, the appeals board has told us the published report must state the panel's reasons, not merely its tally — which raises, sharply, in whose voice those reasons can be written.

Also relevant: several members have told me they do not in fact vote on accreditation as the strict conjunction Bylaw 3 states. They say they weigh F, O, and R holistically, so that a marginal failure on one can be offset by strength on another, and that Bylaw 7 is a grudging acknowledgment of what they are already doing. Others insist the conjunction is the rule and that "holistic" is just a way of voting no while looking reasonable. I have no idea how to adjudicate that in the room.

**Three profiles from our last sitting.** Members are numbered 1–9. Please verify my tallies; I did them on the back of an envelope.

*Profile 1 — the midwifery program.*
Members 1, 2, 3: F met, O met, R not met.
Members 4, 5, 6: F met, O not met, R met.
Members 7, 8, 9: F not met, O met, R met.
Criterion tallies: F met 6–3, O met 6–3, R met 6–3. Every one of the nine voted **against** accreditation when I put the overall question, because each saw one criterion failing. Waiver: no member moved it, and my staff lawyer advised it was unavailable because no criterion had failed collectively.

*Profile 2 — the rural nursing program.*
Members 1, 2, 3, 4: F met, O met, R met; overall yes.
Member 5: F not met, O met, R met; overall **yes** — he thinks the faculty shortfall is trivial and would vote to waive.
Members 6, 7, 8, 9: F not met, O met, R met; overall **no** — they regard F as non-negotiable and would not waive.
Criterion tallies: F not met 5–4, O met 9–0, R met 9–0. Overall question: accredited 5–4. Waiver moved (exactly one criterion unmet), and carried only five votes in favor, so it failed. Result announced: not accredited. Member 5 asked me afterwards, in the corridor, whether he had made things worse by voting sincerely on F, since one vote the other way on F would have carried the program without needing the waiver at all. I could not answer him.

*Profile 3 — the allied health program.*
Member 6 disclosed a paid consultancy with the sponsoring institution's simulation laboratory — which bears **only** on R. I recused her from the entire matter rather than deciding the scope of the recusal, and I now think that was a mistake I made for administrative convenience.
On F (all nine, had she voted): met by 1, 2, 3, 4, 6; not met by 5, 7, 8, 9. With her recused: 4–4. Bylaw 4 says a criterion is met only if a majority of those voting affirm it, so a tie means not met.
On O: met, unanimously among the eight.
On R: met 8–0 among the eight.
Overall question, asked of the eight: accredited 5–3.
Waiver: exactly one criterion unmet (F, on the tie); five of the eight willing to waive; six required. Failed. Result announced: not accredited.
Note what turns on the recusal decision I never reasoned about: had Member 6 voted on F, it would have passed 5–4, all three criteria would have been met, and no waiver would have been needed.

**What I want from you.**

1. **Diagnosis first.** For each profile, state what the criterion-by-criterion route yields, what the overall-question route yields, and what the waiver does, and identify precisely where and why they come apart. Tell me whether, in each case, the set of findings we published is a set of judgments that any single member actually holds, and whether that fact has any bearing on what I can honestly write in the report. I have read that this phenomenon goes by one name in the legal literature and a different name in the philosophical literature, and that there is an impossibility result to the effect that a rule escaping it must either be dictatorial or treat some propositions differently from others — which is just what voting on premises and then deriving the conclusion does. Please correct or sharpen that gloss before you rely on it, and tell me whether it actually constrains my options here.

2. **The procedure.** An ordered sequence of steps and questions the panel can run in the room on any case: what to ask, in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops at "not accredited," where it produces a deferral or a re-vote, and where it hands the decision to me or to counsel because the question is one of institutional design or values rather than arithmetic. It must explicitly cover: whether and how to test that members accept the connection Bylaw 3 asserts, and what to do if they don't; the order in which votes are taken and why that order is not neutral; whether criterion ballots should be open or secret given what happened to Member 5; recusals of limited scope, and whether a member recused from one criterion may vote on a conclusion that incorporates it; ties, and thresholds expressed as absolute numbers of the full panel when the voting body is smaller; and what goes into the published findings and rationale, in whose voice, when a de novo reviewer will read them.

3. **Run it on all three profiles.** Give me the outcome your procedure produces in each, including Profile 3, where I expect it will tell me something I do not want to hear — either that the announced result stands, or that I must un-announce it, and I would rather know which and why.

4. **A one-page protocol for members**, in plain language, that could be handed out at the start of a sitting without embarrassing anyone who has never heard the word "aggregation."

5. **Limits and failure modes.** What the procedure cannot fix; where it will misfire; which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure; and whether any redrafting of the standard — the number of criteria, the logical form of the connection, the waiver threshold, the tie rule — could make the divergences impossible in advance. Be explicit about your confidence on that last point, and do not assert a general theorem unless you are sure it applies to a conjunctive standard with a waiver clause.

Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I will be working offline and cannot look anything up. Define technical terms on first use. Where my description of the bylaws is ambiguous in a way that matters, say so and tell me how to fix the drafting rather than silently choosing a reading.
