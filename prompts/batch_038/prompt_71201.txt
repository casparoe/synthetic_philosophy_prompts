I write literary-ish sci fi, mostly short pieces, and I've been stuck on one idea for months. Its basically Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result — the thing that says two genuinely rational people who started from the same priors can't knowingly end up with different beliefs about the same question, as long as each one knows the other's belief, and knows that the other knows, and so on all the way down. Disagreement, on that picture, is always a symptom: somebody's hiding something, somebody's not updating properly, or they never actually started from the same place. I want a story that makes a reader feel that as a loss rather than understand it as a theorem.

Here's the seed. Near future, a private mediation firm rents out a rig — call it a handshake, a mirror, whatever you like — that two people wear during arbitration and divorce settlements. It doesn't read minds for content, it just lays each party's current confidence about the disputed facts fully open to the other, and recursively: not only what I believe, but what I believe you believe I believe, the whole tower, live. The firm's brochure promises disputes end in an afternoon, and mostly they do. Follow one session, roughly 1500 to 2500 words, two people and the mediator, one contested thing (a house, a custody split, the cause of an accident at a plant they both worked at — your call).

The part I care about most: the rig works exactly as advertised and the session still goes catastropicly wrong, and the reason has to be one of the theorem's escape hatches rather than a malfunction or a hack. Pick one and make it the engine of the plot — either the two of them turn out not to share priors at all, because what they've lived through has built them different starting points, and the device exposes that instead of smoothing it over; or the transparency fails to be genuinely common knowledge at some level that matters, because one of them is performing a confidence they don't have, or can't actually report their own; or one of them isn't updating the way the machine assumes, so the convergence just stalls mid-session while everyone watches. Then fold in the emotional cost of agreement: at least one character should want to keep disagreeing, because the disagreement has been load-bearing for something else — a marriage, a verdict, a reason to get up. And if you can do it without it feeling like a lecture, have somebody notice one institution quietly dying in a world where everyone wears the rig, just one, in passing: markets need people to value the same asset differently for anyone to buy or sell, juries need to be able to hang, a wager only gets offered by someone who thinks they know something the other doesn't. Notice it the way you'd notice a shop on your street that shut down.

Hard constraints: nobody says the words common prior, Bayesian, or rational agent, and nobody names a theorem or a mathematician — the ideas have to survive purely as behavior, procedure and dialogue. No resolving speeches, no narrator stepping in to explain the machinery. End on something physical and small: a form being signed, the rig powering down, a sentence one of them starts and can't finish. Third person, past or present, whatever you want.
