I teach research design in a historical-sociology department where two incompatible-looking explanatory practices coexist peacefully: half my colleagues publish pooled regressions across dozens of comparable cases, the other half publish single-case accounts built by tracing an outcome backwards through documents, and in seminar we grade both kinds of student work as excellent without being able to say what they have in common. Every few years someone raises the old philosophy-of-history question of whether historical explanation is a species of law-governed explanation or something structurally different, and the conversation goes nowhere. I want a diagnosis of that stalemate rather than a survey of it.

Let me state the two positions as concretely as I can, and please correct me where I have them wrong. On the covering-law side: to explain an event is to show that it was to be expected in virtue of initial conditions together with general laws; historical explanations look lawless only because they are elliptical sketches of such arguments, with the general premises left unstated and supplied from ordinary practical knowledge of human beings and institutions. On the other side, at least three distinct objections that I cannot tell apart: first, that an action is explained by exhibiting the agent's situation and reasons such that the action was the appropriate thing to do, which is not subsumption under any generalization about what people in such situations do; second, that the explanandum itself is not fixed independently of the account, since what makes a stretch of the past into one event rather than another is the later vantage point from which it is organized into a story; third, that which of many true antecedents counts as the explanation is settled by an implicit contrast and by norms about what was expected, so explanation is context-relative in a way that a law-based model cannot accommodate.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides were never answering the same question. The covering-law side was asking what makes an account explanatory at all, in the sense of what entitles it to be called knowledge of why; the other side was asking what makes an account informative to a particular audience on a particular occasion. If that is right, the impasse is not depth but non-overlap, and the reason it was never diagnosed is that both sides shared a hidden premise — that explanation is a relation between a set of sentences and a sentence reporting a single event.

Is that the correct diagnosis, or is the real fault line elsewhere: about the relata (events versus facts versus processes), about the status of 'in principle' in an account that permits unfulfilled explanatory sketches, or about standards of evidence that no amount of conceptual analysis can arbitrate? Was the dispute genuinely stalled, or was it dissolved rather than resolved — and if dissolved, by what, and does the mechanist and process-tracing vocabulary my field now uses inherit one side's commitments while claiming to have escaped both? Finally, and most importantly: given your diagnosis, what would count as progress here? I am looking for something my department could actually deploy — a distinction that cuts the knot, a formal claim about what asserting a mechanism commits one to counterfactually, a reframing that makes one side's question disappear, or a reason to expect no progress and to treat the coexistence of the two practices as the correct permanent settlement. I would rather you argue against my hypothesis than accommodate it.
