I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the defence policy of small European states, and I want it to work as a sorting instrument rather than a survey. The object of classification is the position a person actually holds on the relation between a state's posture toward war — armed neutrality, alliance membership, or non-alignment without a declared doctrine — and the way it raises manpower: universal conscription, selective conscription, a professional force with voluntary reserves, or a total-defence obligation that is mostly civilian.

Please write a decision tree whose trunk is a starting commitment every student in the seminar shares: my state is small, cannot win a war of attrition, and wants to avoid being drawn into one. Each branch node is a single question; each leaf is a named position.

Constraints on the nodes:

- Every node must be answerable by a student who has never heard of any of the leaves. Phrase them as concrete cases or as arithmetic, not as restatements of the conclusion; nothing of the form "do you support conscription?" or "is neutrality worth defending?"
- Nodes should turn on the distinctions that actually do the work here. At minimum: whether credible deterrence requires forces that already exist before the crisis or only forces that can be generated during one; whether a militia's willingness to fight depends on the population's prior civic identification with the defence mission or on nothing beyond training and equipment; whether a short universal service that produces a large trained reserve substitutes for a smaller standing professional force or merely defers the same shortfall; whether an exemption from war resting on a state's own capacity to raise the cost of invasion differs in its manpower requirements from one resting on other powers' interest in preserving it; whether alliance membership removes the manpower requirement or redistributes it; and whether conscription, where it survives, survives on non-military grounds — social mixing, civil-protection labour, the discipline of a cohort.

Leaves that must appear, each with its name and one sentence stating its most awkward commitment: the Swiss militia-plus-armed-neutrality position; the Finnish retention of universal male conscription through and after NATO accession; the Swedish suspension of conscription in 2010 and its 2017 reinstatement as a selective, gender-neutral obligation nested inside a total-defence framework; the Austrian combination of constitutionally declared neutrality with conscription confirmed by referendum; the Irish combination of military neutrality with an all-volunteer force; the market objection that a draft is a hidden tax levied on the young rather than a duty; the civic-republican claim that soldiering is constitutive of citizenship rather than instrumental to security; and the position that armed neutrality is obsolete and that its manpower logic died with it.

Build the logical space before the names: lay out the nodes and the answer-combinations they permit, then locate the actual positions within that space. Identify at least three combinations that no state occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has tried it. Finally, flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate, and say what question would separate them if one exists.

Format: an indented tree, each node one question on its own line, each leaf a bolded label followed by a single sentence. Hard limit: 900 words for the whole handout, with no leaf exceeding 35 words.
