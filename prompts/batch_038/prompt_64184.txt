I'm in a fortnightly reading group on cooperation under uncertainty — half of us are CS people, half are philosophy people, and we mostly fail to translate each other, which is half the fun. Last week's topic was safe Pareto improvements. The setup we're working with: two principals each hand a game over to a representative; neither principal knows which game nature will actually pick, they only have priors over a finite set of candidate games; and the default instruction to a representative is simply "play whatever game turns up as well as you can." An SPI is a modification of those instructions that steers the representatives towards a jointly better outcome in the games where the default would be bad, while leaving things alone in the games where the default is already fine. The selling point, as we've been stating it, is that you thereby buy cooperation with no downside — you cannot be made worse off for having signed up. Then one of the philosophy people produced the following argument to the effect that this is a fantasy, and after an hour and a half nobody in the room could say whether it's a proof or a piece of sleight of hand. Here it is, in his numbering:

(1) A modification of a principal's instruction-set is *safe* for that principal just in case, in every game nature could pick and whatever instruction-set the other representative happens to be operating under, the principal does at least as well as they would have done under the default instruction — and strictly better in at least one such game. (2) The SPI modification satisfies this. Its instruction reads: play the agreed joint plan in any game where that plan beats the default for both of us, otherwise play the game as well as you can. So in every game the representative either plays something both principals ranked above the default, or plays exactly what they'd have played anyway. Nothing is forgone. (3) Notice that (2) was established with no assumption at all about the other side's instruction-set. Safety is therefore unilateral: my adopting an SPI cannot hurt you, and your adopting one cannot hurt me, whoever we are and whatever we believe about each other. (4) Hence the pair of SPI instruction-sets is a Pareto improvement on the pair of defaults: each side is at least as well off in every game, and at least one side strictly better off in some. (5) So a principal ought to adopt an SPI whatever their prior over games and whatever they think the other principal will do; there's no situation in which holding out is the rational play. (6) By the same token, *which* joint plan the principals agree on can't be a safety question either. Any plan they light on delivers, in the redirected games, something both ranked above the default, and in the rest, the default. So when two SPIs are on the table, choosing between them is a matter of taste or of distributive bargaining, never a matter of avoiding harm. (7) But its not hard to build cases where both principals end up strictly worse off after adopting SPIs than they would have been under the defaults — the representatives end up playing a given game differently from how they'd have played it, or they settle on a plan that each principal only ranked above the default given a belief about the other which the plan itself destroys. (8) So SPIs aren't safe. And since (3) was meant to be the *general* reason they can't hurt, this isn't a defect in one particular construction: it shows no instruction-set modification of this kind can carry a gaurantee of no loss. The whole programme is a dead end.
