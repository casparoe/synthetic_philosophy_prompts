I write about science and ideas for a magazine, and for the last year or so I've been reporting on family caregiving -- mostly adult daughters with a parent who has dementia. There's a thing I keep running into that I don't have a word for, and my editors keep cutting the graf about it, partly because I can't say what it is.

Here's the pattern. A woman reorganizes her whole life around her mother: quits the the job, moves into the house, learns the meds, learns how to talk her down from the 3am panic. She gets genuinely good at it -- attentive, quick, reads the mood before it turns. And then somewhere around year two you notice her own preferences have stopped getting produced. Not suppressed, not overruled -- they don't come up in the first place. Ask what she wants for dinner and she says whatever; ask what she'd do if the caregiving ended and she goes quiet and then starts describing her mother's day instead. She isn't resentful and she isn't wrecked. She'd say she's fine, she'd say this is what love looks like. The blankness only surfaces later, after the death, when she finds she can't answer basic questions about herself and starts kind of assembling a self out of whatever's at hand -- a hobby she never had, a taste she's not sure is hers.

What makes me think this isn't just a psychology story is that the care-ethics writing I've read treats this direction of change as the good outcome. Attentiveness, being open to the reality of the other person, letting their needs set your ends -- that's the virtue of the practice, not a hazard of it. So what I'm describing is a virtue that's been exercised so successfully that the capacity it runs on has worn out. The receiving has gone on so long there's nothing left to receive with.

Is there an established concept for this? I know the near misses and none of them are quite it. Burnout and compassion fatigue are about depletion and they improve with rest; this doesn't seem to. Codependency implies somebody's extracting value, and usually implies the caregiver is enabling a bad actor -- here the relationship is loving and the person being cared for is not in any position to take anything. Adaptive preferences, maybe, but as I understand that one it's about ratcheting your wants down to fit a situation you can't change, and here the wants aren't lowered, they're absent, and it's a situation she'd endorse. Self-effacement is a disposition to under-claim credit, which is different from having nothing to claim. And "loss of identity" is what everybody says about everything, so it's useless to me.

I'm not after a label for its own sake. I want the carving: what the nearest real concepts are, what exactly seperates this from each of them, and whether anyone has an account of the mechanism -- how does doing the virtue well consume the thing that makes the virtue possible? If nobody's named it, say so plainly and coin one, with a definition and boundary cases, what would and wouldn't count. And practically: what would I have to see or ask in an interview to tell this apart from ordinary exhaustion, because right now I can't, and I'm not printing it until I can.
