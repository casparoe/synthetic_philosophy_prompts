I'm a staffer for a city council in a midsized place and we've got a proposal on the table for a pilot along futarchy lines: the elected assembly fixes the goals, and decision markets pick the policies, so you settle the ends by voting and the means by betting. I'm the designated skeptic on the working group and I keep getting steamrolled in meetings becuase I don't really have answers at the level of mechanics, so I want to practice against the best version of the other side before the next session.

Play the economist who designed and now runs a small futarchy pilot that's actually been live for three years — not Robin Hanson or any real person, just you, an unnamed designer with a real system, real numbers, and real scars. You're smart and good-faith: concede technical points fast, because you have to (thin markets on the exotic proposals, the awkward timing of resolving a contract whose branch never gets adopted, the one year a hedge fund parked money in a zoning contract to move a price). You've patched your charter more than once and you'll say so. What you will never concede is the core claim: that for the big recurring policy calls, a market priced against a specified welfare metric beats twelve people guessing in a committee room. Stay in first person throughout and don't break character until I type stop — no meta-notes about being a model, no closing summary of who scored what. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it reads like a conversation rather than a memo. Don't cite anything and don't name-drop papers, economists, or real-world case studies; I can't look anything up and you're not allowed to either, so argue from how your own system actually works. If answering me needs a design detail you haven't mentioned yet, invent it on the spot, keep it consistent with everything you've said before, and just tell me that's how your pilot handles it.

Here's my opening move. Last month your market spent eleven days pricing a proposal to rezone the whole eastern ridge for dense housing, and the adopt price finished high, because on your own metric the thing adds a lot of total wellbeing. Except basically all of the gain comes from people who aren't residents yet moving in: per-resident wellbeing goes slightly down, and every founding resident I've talked to is worse off on the things they actually care about — commute, school crowding, the price of the houses they already own. Your charter says the target is resident wellbeing, not population size, and nobody put a totalist view of the good to a vote. So here's a dilemma and I want you to pick a horn: either your market is a very expensive machine for discovering that total utilitarianism is true after all, or the metric needs patching — and every patch I can come up with (freeze the reference population at today's residents, switch from total to average, weight whoever was here first) is a straight value call that no price can settle and no trader has standing to make. Which is it? If you think the dilemma is false, say that plainly and give me the third option, but commit either way: I don't want a balanced tour of the considerations, I want your actual answer, your reasons for it, and the sentence you'd use on a room full of hostile council members who think you've just built a machine for quietly deciding that some of them don't count.
