Consider a dispute that has generated a large literature and essentially no movement: whether lying is morally worse than merely misleading, or whether the line between what is said and what is implicated tracks nothing of moral consequence. I convene a group that has now worked through it twice, and the argument has stopped advancing in a way I find more interesting than the question itself. Every consideration has been stated, and each has been met with the same reply it always gets.

One camp holds that the difference is real and structural. Assertion, on this view, carries a warranty of truth that implicature does not: the liar puts herself on the hook for something she believes false, and in doing so bypasses the hearer's capacity to scrutinize her, whereas the misleader leaves the truth on the table and lets the hearer assemble the false belief herself, from premises she was free to reject. The other camp denies that anything morally relevant separates the two. Both speakers intend the same false belief and succeed in producing it; both present themselves as informing; and if there is an asymmetry, it runs the other way, since the misleader recruits the hearer's own reasoning as the instrument of the deception and then shelters behind literal truth. Tellingly, both camps press the same datum in opposite directions: the misleader's fallback, that she never said the thing in question, is for the first camp proof that the lie is a commitment one can be held to, and for the second camp proof that misleading is the more evasive and so the worse practice.

To sharpen matters we built pairs that remove assertion altogether, so that the same false belief is induced non-linguistically: a wedding ring worn after the marriage has ended, a car left in the driveway overnight, a calendar that is accurate in every entry but arranged to suggest a meeting that never occurred. The warranty story predicts no distinctive wrong in these cases, and nobody in the group is willing to accept that. We also varied how detectable the implicature was, reasoning that an appeal to the hearer's rational agency should make misleading less objectionable as detection becomes easier. Our judgments came out flat across that variable, which surprised both camps.

My hypothesis is that the stalemate is not about deception at all but about the object of assessment: one camp is evaluating a speaker's assumption of responsibility within a communicative practice, the other is evaluating the effect of a transaction on a hearer, and both are answering different questions under the same comparative heading. I suspect I am flattering my own camp, and I want that checked rather than accommodated.

So diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, a fight over the extension of a word; a disagreement about what moral assessment attaches to; a disguised empirical question about detectability and blame; a clash of bedrock intuitions about the significance of saying something; or a sign that the comparative question as posed is ill-formed? Argue from what each camp actually claims, not from a tour of the literature, and then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction, a discriminating case, an experiment, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and say why. Commit to a single diagnosis and defend it: I would rather have a definite claim to argue with than a distribution of plausibility across the options. Say also, briefly, what would make you give it up.
