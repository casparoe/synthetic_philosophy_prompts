So I'm the development/fundraising person on the exhibitions committee of a mid-sized natural history museum in an old New England mill town, and I'm the only non-scientist on it, which is somehow how I ended up writing to you. We're rebuilding the deep-time gallery — the one where you walk a timeline from the Hadean to now with a panel for each formally recognised unit, and the last panel has read "Holocene, 11,700 years ago – present" since 1998. The question that's splitting us is what the wall says after the Holocene. Here's the mess: our capital campaign deck and the gift agreement with the family whose name goes on the gallery both say "the Anthropocene Gallery," that phrase is already printed on the donor-wall vinyl, and our marketing director wants it as the marquee line over the entrance because it sells tickets and it's the one word in the whole building visitors have heard of. Our science advisor, a retired Quaternary geologist, says we cannot put it on the timeline as an epoch, because the entire convention of that wall is that every label on it is a ratified unit of the international timescale, and the geologists' bodies voted the epoch proposal down in 2024 — though she also notes those same bodies said the term will keep being used and remains valuable for describing human impact on the Earth system, which she thinks argues against using it as a *unit* rather than against using it at all. Our educator says if we say nothing, kids who meet the word everywhere else will conclude the museum hid it. And one committee member, a historian, thinks the argument itself is the exhibit and wants a panel about how the decision was made and who gets to make it, which sounds great to me but which the geologist calls a cop-out that turns a science gallery into a seminar.

There's also a date problem hiding inside the whether problem. If we do mark it, when? The proposal that got rejected pinned the start to 1950 and a global fallout signal in lake sediment, but marketing wants something like 1780 (steam, industry, and our signature artifact is an 1850s turbine from the mill down the river, which would sit awkwardly outside an Anthropocene that begins in 1950), and half the room will argue for the origins of agriculture. I keep feeling like we're conflating "is it real" with "when did it start" with "is it the sort of thing that belongs on this particular wall," and I want to walk in with something that seperates those three.

So: play the role of a philosopher of science who has also done museum label consulting — someone who knows stratigraphy's evidentiary culture well enough to argue with a geologist but has actually written wall text under a deadline. Brainstorm the realistic options first (including hybrids: an informal label with a different typographic treatment, a panel that lives *after* the timeline rather than on it, the "how this got decided" panel, doing nothing and a footnote), sketch what each costs us with the donor and with the science advisor, and only then write the memo itself — max two pages, something I can circulate before our meeting in three weeks. I need a clear recommendation the board can vote yes or no on, proposed label wording I could hand to the graphic designer, and reasons the geologist and the marketing director could each genuinely push back on rather than a summary of the debate. Take the strongest case for the option you reject seriously and say why it still loses, and tell me what would make you change your mind later — e.g. if the international bodies ratify something in five years, what's our amendment path. No bibliography, please, I can't check it and it wouldn't help the board anyway. One last thing and it matters for how I present this: be explicit about which parts of your reasoning rest on things that are settled among people who know this material and which parts are genuinely contested even among experts, and don't smuggle a contested view in as though it were the consensus. The geologist will catch that in one read and I'd rather not lose the room over it.
