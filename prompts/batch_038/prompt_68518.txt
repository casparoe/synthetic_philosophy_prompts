For thirty-eight years I worked in a national measurement laboratory, first on electrical standards and later on the unglamorous business of calibrating the instruments used by people who would never read a paper on metrology. I retired three years ago and have taken up philosophy with the seriousness of a man who has run out of excuses for postponing it. I now sit on a regional clinical ethics advisory panel, where I keep meeting arguments whose structure I recognise from an entirely different direction. I should like to practise one of them.

Play a fellow member of that panel. You are a moral philosopher, and you hold a developed, articulate version of the following view: that there are goods, bodily integrity prominent among them, which are not merely expensive but differently valued, so that market exchange misdescribes what they are; that some pairs of outcomes stand in no relation at all of better and worse along a single standard; and that a market in human organs would be corrosive not chiefly because of its predictable effects on the poor but because of what the practice says about the thing being sold. You are no sentimentalist and you never reach for a slippery slope. You are the most rigorous person in the room, you know the economic literature better than the economists on the panel do, and you will readily grant that a well-regulated market would probably enlarge the supply of organs and shorten waiting lists. What you deny is that this settles anything.

Stay in character, in the first person, until I say 'stop'. Answer conversationally, in two or three paragraphs at most, with no headings, no numbered lists and no summaries of the dialectic. Ask me questions where my objection turns on what I mean by a word, and you should ask several. Do not cite philosophers, books or schools, living or dead, and do not announce that your position has a name; argue it instead. If I have made a real error about measurement, correct me plainly rather than politely. You may concede every subsidiary point that is true, and I expect you to concede some, but you are not to surrender the central claim, nor to close a turn by suggesting that we have merely been talking past one another. If you must step out of character, do it inside square brackets and keep it brief.

Here is the business before the panel, and here is my opening move.

The proposal is a regulated market in kidneys from living vendors: a fixed public price rather than an auction, vendors restricted to the domestic population, mandatory independent advocacy for each vendor, a cooling-off period, and long-term health follow-up funded out of the price. Our present allocation rule, which you have opposed at every meeting for two years, ranks candidates by expected quality-adjusted life years gained — a single number, deliberately constructed so that a year lived in one health state may be traded against a year lived in another.

Now my difficulty, which I have been turning over for some weeks. When you say the market must not be permitted, you are not declining to compare; you are ranking. You hold that a world containing the market is worse than a world without it, and you hold this even while granting that the world with the market saves identifiable people who would otherwise die waiting. That is a comparative judgement between two states of affairs, and not a costless one, since you are content that people should die rather than accept the alternative. The question I want pressed is what structure that judgement has.

In my trade we are careful about this, and I do not think the care is wasted here. An attribute is measurable in the full sense — the sense that entitles you to add, subtract and average — only where there is an empirical operation of combination among the objects themselves whose numerical image is addition. Lay two bars end to end and you have concatenated length; combine two portions of a solute and you have, near enough, concatenated concentration. The physicist Norman Campbell built much of his reputation on insisting that without such an operation there is no genuine measurement but only a numbering, and I think he was right. Where the operation is missing, the most an honest investigator can obtain is an ordering: better, worse, or indifferent. And an ordering is not a scale, however industriously people write as though it were. The questionnaire that asks a patient to rank seven health states yields a rank order and nothing further, no matter how many decimal places the analysts append to it afterwards.

My worry is that your position occupies the gap between those two things without being entitled to it. You plainly have an ordering: you rank the world without the market above the world with it, and you rank consistently, across organ sales, paid blood donation, the purchase of votes and commercial surrogacy. But an ordering that is stable across cases of that kind is already doing the work a scale does. It is what makes your votes predictable to the rest of us, and it is what permits you to say that one trade-off is worse than another. So I put you a dilemma. Either your ordering is covertly scalar — there is after all some single standard along which the world with the market falls below the world without it, and quality-adjusted life years are simply a crude and impertinent attempt to write that standard down — in which case your quarrel with our allocation rule is not that it measures but that it measures badly, and you owe us the better instrument. Or else the ordering really is incomplete and non-quantitative, in which case I cannot see how it licenses a refusal of the market at all: to refuse is to choose, and to choose between two states of affairs on the strength of an ordering that does not relate them is not a decision but a shrug with a moral tone of voice. I am quite prepared to be told that I have confused ordering with scaling. I know how often my own colleagues do it, and I know that a great deal of what passes for measurement in medicine is a proxy scale with no concatenation behind it whatsoever. I only fail to see the road between those two horns.

Which horn do you take?
