I'm doing a masters module on the philosophy of physics and I've talked myself into co-presenting a seminar on determinism, chaos and predictability with a friend who's a physics guy and is extremely confident about everything. He has an argument that he says kills off Laplace's demon and then does something interesting on the side about agency. I've been asked to defend it in the second half, and I've started to suspect it proves far too much, but the annoying thing is that every time I try to object out loud I end up agreeing with him again by the end of my own sentence, which is not a great sign.

Here's the argument roughly as he gives it. (1) Determinism is the thesis that the complete state of the world at a time, together with the laws, fixes the state of the world at every later time. (2) If a world is deterministic, then an arbitrarily powerful computer given a complete description of its state at some time could work out its state at any later time; if it couldn't, "fixes" in (1) wasn't doing any work. (3) So in a deterministic world there could be a predictor that gets every future detail right, including details about itself, since the predictor is just another physical part of that world. (4) Now suppose in such a world we build a little device D, wired to the predictor's output, whose whole job is to print the opposite of whatever the predictor says D will print at noon tomorrow. (5) The predictor says D prints X at noon; by (3) that's right, so D prints X; but by (4) D prints not-X. Contradiction. (6) Since (1) is just a definition and (4) is only a bit of wiring with no magic involved, what has to go is (3): determinism does not buy you predictability, not even for a computer of unlimited power sitting inside the world. (7) Press him and he says the same trouble comes from outside too: any predictor, internal or not, that is right about a world containing a D-device gets refuted by that D-device, so a complete theory of such a world could never be cashed out as predictions about what its inhabitants will do, so the world can't be one where everything about what people do is settled by the past in any sense that constrains them. (8) Then he bolts on chaos: sensitive dependence means you can never pin down an initial state to infinite precision, and any finite error grows exponentially, so the practical version of (3) fails as well. Conclusion he wants: the demon is dead twice over, and that leaves room for genuine agency, because nothing about the past settles what I do.

Somewhere in there I've stopped believing things I'm pretty sure are true, but I can't get purchase on the middle. The contradiction in (5) looks like a real contradiction and not a trick, and I can't see how to hold onto it without conceding (6); and then I can't see how (6) doesn't drag (7) along with it, since (7) looks like (6) with the word "internal" quietly deleted, and deleting a word doesn't usually break an inference. I'm also not certain (4) is as innocent as he claims — can you actually wire a device to invert a prediction about its own not-yet-produced output without smuggling in something that can't be done? — but I don't want to be pushed into saying the device is impossible, because that smells like anti-mechanism through the back door. So: which step would you reject, why did that step look unobjectionable when you first read it, and what do you pay if you reject something else instead? In particular I want to know whether any of the options costs you anything about determinism itself as opposed to about predicting it, and whether predictability ever had any bearing on free agency in the first place. And separately, does the chaos material in (8) actually add weight to the argument, or is it doing a completely different kind of work from the D-device and just borrowing the D-device's plausibility? My preference, if I have to choose, is to keep (1) and (4) and eat whatever falls out of that. Could you write the answer in British English please — I'm at a UK uni and I'd rather not spend an evening converting spellings.
