Retired three years ago from a job I spent most of my life in — process engineering and failure analysis. Thirty-some years of designing experiments, chasing down why a batch went bad, and argueing with people about whether we'd established a cause or only a correlation. Now I read philosophy in the evenings and I'm in a small group with four other ex-scientists and one very patient librarian. We got onto causation and fell straight into a mess: half of us think the interventionist stuff is just a description of what we already do in a lab, dressed up as metaphysics, and the other half think counterfactual dependence is the real thing and interventions are only a way of finding out about it. Neither side could say what would settle it.

So I want to build a decision tree that sorts us. Starting point: "I accept that a well-designed controlled change in my process establishes a causal claim about it." Ten questions maximum, each a concrete case with two or three options, phrased so somebody can answer without knowing any of the labels — no node should be "are you a counterfactualist?" Every branch has to be a commitment that makes a difference somewhere, and for each node I want you to name the kind of case that forces the split.

Use at least five of these, since they're the ones we actually fought about:

- a redundant compressor fails and the backup, which would have started 200 ms later, never gets its chance;
- a tech didn't log a pressure reading and three weeks later a valve lets go — nobody touched anything;
- our interlock shut down a drive, which thereby stopped the drive from tripping a breaker that would have killed the cooling loop;
- the banded iron formations, or any claim about four billion years ago that nobody could manipulate even in principle;
- a bad lot of parts doubles the failure rate, but on this particular unit the failure was set off by a perfectly normal overload;
- the ideal gas law runs just as well backwards as forwards, so why is only one of those directions causal;
- when I write "longer dwell time lowers porosity" in a report, am I making a claim about a relation in the world or summarizing what happened to individual batches.

The leaves should carry their usual names, and I want all of these reachable somewhere: Lewis-style counterfactual dependence (including whether his rule about not letting small miracles into the nearest worlds does any real work or is just plumbing), interventionism as an account of what causation is, interventionism as an account of explanation and inference that stays quiet about what causation is, process or conserved-quantity views, straight probabilistic causation, Humean best-system regularity, causation as a primitive needing no analysis, the two-concepts pluralism, and the view that causal talk is folk convenience with nothing answering to it. If two leaves can't be told apart by any answer pattern the tree permits, say which pair and why, rather than pretending the instrument is finer than it is.

Also mark every cell in the space that the questions open up and nobody occupies, and for each say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered.

Last thing: don't hand me a menu and leave me holding it. Tell me which leaf you'd put me at and which you'd put yourself at, then commit — which one is right for a person who's going to keep writing failure reports instead of publishing metaphysics, and why. And tell me which of my seven cases above you think is a badly posed question and how you'd repair it. I'd rather hear that than have you quietly route around it.
