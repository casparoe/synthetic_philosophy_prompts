Been going around in circles on the sphexishness example — Hofstadter's word, from the Fabre-style story about the digger wasp that hauls a paralyzed cricket to the mouth of the burrow, goes inside to check on things first, and if you nudge the cricket a few inches while its back is turned it finds the cricket, drags it back to the threshold, and then has to go in and check again, so the loop never ends. Dennett deploys it to make us feel better about ourselves: sure, that wasp is a little robot, but we've got layers and layers of self-monitoring stacked on top, so we're not sphexish, and free will survives.

I want the other side. Argue that the wasp does no work at all for the compatibilist, and that if it shows anything it shows the pessimist's point. Concretely, defend this: every increase you grant the wasp's flexibility is paid for by pushing a fixed subroutine up a level rather than deleting one, so there's no non-arbitrary point at which sphexishness turns into freedom — the gap between the wasp and us is loop length and nesting depth, not a difference in kind. Then deal with the two objections I think are actually hard: (a) we can notice we're stuck in a loop and step out of it, which the wasp can't, and (b) we can be told about the wasp and change what we do for reasons, which the wasp also can't. If your best move on one of them is to concede, concede it cleanly and tell me what's left standing of the thesis.

Don't lean on the entomology. Assume for the sake of argument the anecdote is inflated and real wasps adapt after a dozen or so trips — the argument should survive that, and I want to see where it gets weaker when it does.

500 words max. Open with your thesis in a single sentence, then the argument.
