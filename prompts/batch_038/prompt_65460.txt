In my philosophy of perception seminar this term I have become a convert to disjunctivism, and specifically to the negative version: a hallucination that is introspectively indiscriminable from a veridical perception of a yellow lemon shares no fundamental kind of conscious state with that perception, and the most that can be said about the hallucination is that it is indiscriminable from such a perception. For my final paper I want to know how much this view actually risks, so I am looking for a map of its exposure rather than a survey of the debate.

Take each of the following in turn and say whether it would be fatal, serious, or merely awkward if it landed, and whether a committed disjunctivist would concede it as telling against the view or explain it away:

- The causal-matching consideration: if the same proximal neural activity can generate both the veridical perception and the matching hallucination, is disjunctivism committed to a claim that could turn out empirically false, or can it absorb the result?
- Screening off: if whatever explains the subject's reports, dispositions, and beliefs in the bad case is equally present in the good case, does the disjunct do any explanatory work at all?
- Whether the argument from illusion forces the issue: if a hybrid account — a common factor for illusion, disjunctivism only about hallucination — proves coherent and better supported, has the view collapsed into a rival?
- Whether characterizing the bad case solely in terms of indiscriminability insulates the view: could any observation or argument show that the two cases do share a common kind, and if not, is that a defect in a thesis of this sort?

Close by saying what the view predicts or licenses methodologically that a highest-common-factor theorist's does not, and give an honest verdict on whether disjunctivism risks anything at all.
