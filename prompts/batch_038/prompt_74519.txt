Chalmers' method of elimination asks us to take the term in dispute out of the argument and check whether the disagreement survives in what's left. If it survives, the dispute is substantive; if it evaporates, it was verbal all along. Applied to ontology the result is meant to be deflationary. When the mereological universalist and the nihilist argue about whether there are statues, they can eliminate the disputed existential talk and agree on everything else — how the matter is arranged, what happens if you kick it — so what's left over is either verbal, or a merely pragmatic quarrel about which concepts to employ. Hirsch gets somewhere similar by a different route, and Sider resists both by insisting that some expressions carve reality at the joints better than their rivals do.

My worry is that elimination isn't a neutral procedure. To eliminate a term T you need a replacement vocabulary that both sides accept as expressing the same content-minus-T. But in the disputes that actually matter, the candidates for that replacement vocabulary are disputed in exactly the same way. A nihilist who rejects "statue" because she rejects the relevant sortal commitment may equally reject "arranged statuewise", on the grounds that "statuewise" smuggles the sortal back in through the adverb. Chalmers has resources here — he can say the arrangement talk is neutral because it's just about how the particles are — but the nihilist's complaint is precisely that "how the particles are" isn't neutral either, since it presumes that the only real quantification is over simples.

So let me stipulate that a dispute has an *elimination depth*: the number of successive eliminations you have to perform before the disagreement disappears. A dispute about whether "bank" picks out a financial institution or a river edge has depth one. A dispute about whether some interrogation technique counts as torture, I'd suggest, also has depth one but leaves a residue that is substantive rather than ontological — and that's where Plunkett and Sundell's notion of metalinguistic negotiation is useful: some disputes survive elimination not because they're ontologically deep, but because the parties are negotiating which concept to govern their practice, and that negotiation is a real disagreement about how to think and act.

My claim is that for the ontological cases elimination depth isn't merely large but *indeterminate*: there's no fact of the matter about when the procedure terminates, because at every stage the allegedly neutral vocabulary can be challenged on the same grounds that challenged the last one. And I want that to be the diagnosis of why these disputes feel substantive while resisting resolution. The feeling tracks the indeterminacy in the procedure, not a joint-carving question waiting to be answered.

The upshot is that Chalmers can't have it both ways. He wants the disputes that bottom out — the ones where the parties simply disagree about which concept to use, with no further residue — to be merely pragmatic, which is what buys him the deflated ontology. But he also wants the notion of a non-verbal dispute to be robust enough to explain why some disagreements are substantive rather than merely apparent. If termination is indeterminate, the second demand can't be met without importing a normative account of concept choice that looks an awful lot like the framework-relative Carnapian picture he thinks he's escaping.
