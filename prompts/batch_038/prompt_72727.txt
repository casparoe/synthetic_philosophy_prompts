I am the acquiring editor for narrative nonfiction history at a mid-sized trade press. Proposals whose engine is a counterfactual now reach me at a rate of roughly ten a month: books arguing not what happened but what would have happened had some documented pivot gone otherwise. Our list's standing depends on holding the line between the disciplined kind — the econometric exercise that computes what American output in 1890 would have been in the absence of railroads and finds the shortfall remarkably small — and the decorative kind, where a chapter of confident speculation is insulated from any evidence that could disturb it. I have no training in the philosophy of history; the two colleagues who sit with me on the acquisitions board have less; and I have about ninety minutes per proposal before the weekly meeting.

Write me a screening procedure I can run on such a proposal inside that time and reduce to a one-page memo. Assume I will not consult anything you point me to, so the procedure must stand on its own and name its ideas in plain terms rather than by directing me to sources.

It should contain:

1. An ordered sequence of questions, each with a stated pass condition: what counts as a sufficient answer, and what a failing answer looks like in practice. Order them so that the cheap disqualifiers come first.

2. Gates rather than scores. I need to know at which questions a failure ends the procedure with "do not acquire as pitched," at which it means "acquire only with specified conditions written into the contract," and at which the question is not factual at all but concerns what the book is trying to be — and therefore mine to settle rather than the procedure's.

3. A treatment of the two objections that can kill a counterfactual and pull in opposite directions: that the antecedent is not genuinely available, because reaching it requires rewriting so much prior history that the resulting world is not recognizably a continuation of ours; and that the consequent is not determinable, because once the pivot is altered everything downstream shifts and any answer becomes as good as any other. Add the third case, where the world snaps back: other forces were jointly sufficient for approximately the same outcome, so the negated event mattered little. Explain how one procedure can apply all three without collapsing into an all-purpose veto, and what evidence separates "it snaps back" from "nobody can say."

4. A step testing whether the counterfactual is doing explanatory work — identifying which actual conditions were load-bearing, and how much they bore — or is ornament the argument could shed without weakening. Give me a test I can put to an author in a letter without specialist knowledge of my own.

5. A step testing for motivated choice of pivot: the suspicion that the counterfactual was selected because the world it produces flatters the author's politics. Include a symmetry test, and say what a pass looks like when the author's thesis is honestly and avowedly political.

6. Two devices I already use informally and want built in and made precise. First, a rewrite budget: the number of changes to the documented record the author must make before reaching the pivot, each itemized and sourced. Second, a visibility date: the point by which the divergence would surface in the kinds of evidence historians actually possess. Tell me what budget figures are defensible, and at what point the count ceases to mean anything.

7. Explicit marking of the steps that are judgment calls dressed as procedure — where "sufficient" really means "sufficient to me" — together with a guard against those steps quietly doing all the work.

8. A hand-off rule. I can commission a specialist reader's report at $1,200 and a three-week delay, no more than six per year, and the publisher signs off on any advance above $40,000. State which screening outcomes require a report, which require sign-off, and which I may close myself.

9. A closing section on limits: what a clean pass does not entitle me to claim about the book's truth, and what I should do when two specialists report in opposite directions.

Then run the procedure on three proposals and show me the resulting memo for each.

(a) A manuscript arguing that the July crisis of 1914 would have produced a general European war even had the assassinations at Sarajevo failed, on the grounds that mobilization timetables, alliance commitments and staff planning over-determined the outcome.

(b) A celebrated stylist's book contending that European history from 1789 to 1989 would have taken a benignly different course had one revolutionary government been overthrown in its first year. The prose is superb, the antecedent on inspection requires rewriting the preceding century, and my publisher wants the book badly. This is the case where I expect the procedure to tell me something I will not like. If it does, say so without softening, and tell me what would have to change in the book rather than in the procedure for it to pass.

(c) A short scholarly monograph that uses counterfactuals purely as an instrument for establishing which among several actual causes was load-bearing, and that never asserts what would otherwise have happened. I am concerned the screen will reject it for failing to answer its own question. Show whether it does; if it does, repair the screen and show the repair working on the other two proposals.
