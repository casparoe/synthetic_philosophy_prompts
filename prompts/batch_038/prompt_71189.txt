I spent thirty-odd years measuring trace gases in the upper atmosphere, mostly out of a lab with a budget that would make a grad student laugh, and since retiring I've been volunteering two days a week with a small environmental monitoring outfit — river chemistry, a scattering of fixed sites, one part-time statistician who is really a hydrologist. They've asked me to draft a "how we do inference here" document, because two of their reports got torn apart last year by a regulator's consultants and the whole staff is now in that mood where they want to throw out p-values entirely and pick up something else, anything else. I've been reading a lot about this since I stopped working — I audited a philosophy of science course last spring and got hooked — and the thing I keep running into is that everybody agrees the standard toolkit is in trouble, and nobody can say what a replacement actually has to deliver. It's all critique and no spec, which is not how we'd do things in a lab.

So here's what I want from you: write the desiderata. What would any framework for drawing conclusions from data have to be able to do to count as an adequate replacement for the null-hypothesis-testing workflow in a place like ours? Fix on the actual conditions, because adequacy is relative to them: sample sizes that are often in the single digits per site, data that arrives sequentially season after season rather than all at once in a planned batch, several dozen quantities measured at every site whether or not we predicted them, staff who are competent but will never internalize the theory, decisions that have to be made anyway (dredge the sediment, don't dredge; declare the trend, don't declare it), and a standing assumption that somebody who dislikes us will re-read our numbers looking for a way to make them look dishonest. Give me the cases it has to get right — the pair of experiments with identical data but different stopping intentions, peeking mid-study, multiplicity across sites, a genuinely null effect, a real effect too small to matter, and the model being wrong in a way we can't see — and the distinctions it must not collapse, like evidence versus decision, or long-run error rates versus degree of belief, or "we found nothing" versus "we found that there's nothing to find." Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really just the house prejudices of one camp dressed up as logic. I'm particularly suspicious of the demand that inference obey the likelihood principle, and of the argument that if you accept the sufficiency principle and the conditionality principle you're committed to it whether you like it or not: is that a real constraint on a working framework, or a piece of philosophy that a lab can politely ignore? Same question for long-run calibration — if a procedure can't be shown to control errors over repetitions that will never actually happen, does that matter to me?

Finally, and this is the part I most want: flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. Let me throw in four of my own that I've been arguing about with a friend, and tell me which are legitimate and which I should drop — (a) a technician should be able to execute the workflow correctly without understanding why it works, (b) the method should let you state "this is inconclusive" without the whole project grinding to a halt, (c) the verdict shouldn't change just because we happened to plan to collect one more season of data, and (d) an adversary who reruns our analysis should get the same numbers we did. I suspect (a) and (c) are in tension and I can't quite see why. Wrap up with maybe half a page on how the three obvious contenders — preregistration plus p-values with effect sizes and power, a fully Bayesian setup with stated priors, and a decision-theoretic cost-loss framing — do against your list, but keep that brief and don't send me a reading list or a bibliography, I've got more than enough to read already and my library access ended when I handed in my badge.
