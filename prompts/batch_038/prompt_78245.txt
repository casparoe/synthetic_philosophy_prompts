I teach philosophy of mind and mathematical logic, and I have been asked to give the toast at the retirement dinner of a colleague of thirty-eight years, whom I will call Miriam. Her entire research programme was a defence, in successive and increasingly careful versions, of the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument.

The room will hold about ninety people. Roughly a third are logicians and philosophers of mind, several of whom have published against her and will be listening for accuracy; another third are former students now scattered across industry, including one who builds language-model agents and who has told me privately that she regards the whole debate as settled against Miriam; the final third are family — her husband, her two daughters, an eleven-year-old granddaughter, and a great many people who have never heard of Gödel and have no intention of starting tonight.

Three things about her position, which the toast must not garble, since a third of the room will be checking. First, the version she favoured rested its weight on the second incompleteness theorem rather than the first: her recurring claim was that the mechanist thesis, if it is to deliver its conclusion, must licence a degree of self-transparency that no consistent system can have of itself, so that the honest upshot is a dilemma — either I am not a machine, or, if I am, I can never come to know which one I am. Second, in her last decade she came to think the argument's real interest lay not in its conclusion but in what it exposed: the idealisation it demanded from finite, error-prone, mortal reasoners to sound, self-transparent ones, an idealisation that both sides of the debate had been making quietly for years. Third, she was famously no friend of Penrose's physical conjecture about neuronal microtubules; she thought the quantum-gravity story an unnecessary embarrassment that the logical argument did not require, and she said so in print. She also despised being idealised. She used to make the point — I am paraphrasing her, not quoting her — that a retirement speech which describes its subject as having been consistent for four decades has described somebody nobody ever met.

The task: write the toast. Five minutes, about 650 words, ready to be read aloud. It must be warm and funny at least once, and it must not become a seminar; nobody in that room should feel lectured at. It must contain exactly one joke that trades on self-reference or consistency and that works on both halves of the audience at once: the logicians hear the technical point, the family hear an affectionate story about a stubborn woman, and the eleven-year-old can be told afterwards, in one sentence, what it meant. The philosophical material has to do structural work rather than decorate the edges. Because her argument turns on the impossibility of self-certified consistency and on the distance between an idealised reasoner and a real one, the toast should be constructed so that it cannot idealise her — so that its affection comes precisely from the failure of the idealisation, and so that describing her accurately is itself the tribute. It should acknowledge her refusal to sign the physical conjecture without turning that into a swipe at anyone living or dead. And it should be something she could listen to without judging it either false about her or condescending to the room.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. **The toast** — about 650 words, in the voice it will be delivered in.
2. **What each part is doing** — about 300 words, following the toast in sequence and identifying which segment of the room each move is aimed at, where the joke sits, and how the idealisation theme is bearing the structure rather than ornamenting it.
3. **A sceptic's audit** — about 250 words: list every claim in the toast concerning the argument that a philosopher who thinks the argument fails could still endorse without embarrassment, and flag, with a proposed replacement, any line that quietly asserts or concedes its conclusion.
4. **What I left unsaid** — about 150 words on the stories, superlatives and characterisations you excluded because she would have counted them as flattery or as false, and why excluding them costs the toast nothing.

Append no bibliography and no reading list. Do not place anything in quotation marks as though it were her own wording, beyond the paraphrase I have supplied.
