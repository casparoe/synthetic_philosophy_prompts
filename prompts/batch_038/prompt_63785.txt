I retired four years ago from a physiology department in which I spent some thirty-five years studying how taste information is carried: single-unit recording from the chorda tympani and the rostral nucleus of the solitary tract in rodents, and a fair amount of human psychophysical work with trained panels. The vocabulary I think in is signal detection theory — two-alternative forced choice, hit and false-alarm rates, d-prime and criterion, receiver operating characteristics, dose-response functions, adaptation, receptor turnover — together with the population-coding controversy that ran through my whole career, namely whether a taste quality is carried by dedicated labeled lines or by the pattern of activity distributed across a broad ensemble of fibers. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy of mind in a desultory but earnest way; a former colleague and I meet fortnightly to read together, and we have just worked through Dennett's paper on quining qualia, and in particular his case of Mr. Chase and Mr. Sanborn, the two coffee tasters whose employer paid them to keep the product constant year after year and who, in later life, agree that the coffee no longer pleases them as it once did but disagree about why: one holds that it tastes exactly as it always did and that only his own liking has altered, the other that the taste itself has drifted away from what it was.

What I should like is a systematic translation of the argument Dennett builds on that case — and of the general thesis that qualia, conceived as intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible properties of experience, are an incoherent posit — into the vocabulary I actually use. Take the argument apart into its distinct claims and premises, render each one in the language of peripheral transduction versus central decision rules, sensitivity versus criterion, reactive discrimination versus reflective judgment, and labeled-line versus across-fiber coding, and label each rendering as clean, distorted, or unavailable. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want: which claims map onto distinctions I already make for a living, which only map if I allow a certain amount of bending, and which have no expression whatsoever in my framework. I have a working hypothesis of my own that I want tested rather than flattered. It is that Chase is a pure criterion shift with sensitivity intact, that Sanborn is a genuine decline in sensitivity of the sort one expects with age and receptor turnover, and that the philosophical puzzle evaporates once the sensory variable and the decision variable are prised apart — an entirely ordinary instance of the two coming loose from each other, which my discipline has measured for a century. Tell me whether that is right, or whether it merely relocates the difficulty.

Three constraints on how you go about it. First, no smuggling: signal detection theory presupposes a two-stage architecture, a sensory evidence variable and a decision rule operating upon it, and if a given step of the translation succeeds only because that architecture has been quietly assumed, say so explicitly rather than counting it as a triumph, since part of what I want to know is whether Dennett's argument is aimed at precisely that architecture. Second, be honest about the diachronic problem: nobody can place the coffee of 1965 beside today's coffee in a forced-choice block, so a d-prime for the question "has it changed?" is not defined in the way a d-prime for "is this quinine or water?" is defined; tell me whether my framework has any legitimate means of handling a comparison against a remembered standard, or whether this is a real expressive limit, and if it is a limit, whether Dennett's argument needs the comparison to be well defined in order to go through. Third, no bibliography, no catalogue of who has replied to whom over the decades — I cannot chase references and have no wish to. Conclude with a verdict, stated plainly: is the residue a defect in my vocabulary, a thing it cannot say that is nonetheless there to be said, or is it evidence that the posit it fails to express was never determinate enough to be true or false? And if it is a defect in my vocabulary, specify what measurement I would have to add in order to close the gap.
