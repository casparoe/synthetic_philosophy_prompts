I write literary fiction — four novels that all circle the same handful of people — and I keep what I call the bible: one document with every version of every character across every draft, plus the changes made for the audiobook, the French translator's substitutions, and the stage adaptation I sold the rights to. So I have something like eight Annas sitting in a spreadsheet. Draft-one Anna gets on the train. Draft-three Anna stays and it kills her. Stage Anna is forty years older. The translator renamed her. In my head they're all Anna, and I can tell you which of her traits are load-bearing — the ones where if you move them it stops being her at all — and which ones float free.

My partner does philosophy and keeps trying to get me to care about an argument between two people about what it means for the same thing to be like this in some other possible situation. One of them says: when you ask what Anna would have done if she'd gotten on that train, you're really asking about some other, similar Anna in some other scenario, and how similar counts as similar enough depends on what you care about at the time. The other says no — it's literally Anna herself you're asking about, and pointing me at a lookalike is a dodge, because nobody asks a question about Anna and wants an answer about somebody who resembles her.

Translate that whole disagreement into my vocabulary: drafts, retcons, the bible, load-bearing traits, adaptation, my authority to just decide things, and the fact that the published book outranks the drafts even though the drafts are where the same characters supposedly were. Be as faithful as you can and don't quietly smuggle your framework's assumptions in and hand them back to me labelled as translation.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Which of their claims maps onto something I already do? Which ones you had to bend to make fit, and where exactly did the bending happen? And which ones can't be said at all in how a novelist talks about a character surviving a rewrite?

Some places I suspect it'll tear — tell me if I'm wrong:

- Their similarity thing doesn't chain. Draft-one Anna resembles draft-two Anna, draft-four resembles draft-five, but draft-one and draft-five share almost nothing, and still I say it's one continuous Anna. Does either view have room for that?
- They apparently think every scenario is equally real from the inside, nobody's world is the true one. For me the printed book is the real Anna and the abandoned drafts are the maybe ones. Is their symmetry useless to me, or is my sense of one privileged version just bad metaphysics?
- In draft six I swapped which twin does what and changed nothing else. Different situation, or the same one with the name tags moved? Do the two views answer that differently, and should I care?
- I invent people. One of these guys seems to think scenarios are things we stipulate and the other thinks they're already out there. But I don't stipulate anything about a real person and I don't discover Anna either, I make her. Does either framework have a slot for that?

Finally: when something doesn't survive the translation, tell me which way it cuts. Is craft-thinking missing a distinction, or is their fight thinner than it sounds? My own suspicion is that when I say the play's Anna "isn't really Anna" I'm doing both things at once — weighting traits by importance, like one of them, while insisting I'm talking about her and not a lookalike, like the other one. Either I'm confused, or the two of them are arguing about something smaller than they think. Which is it?
