For two years I have coordinated intake at a volunteer-run legal clinic, and I have repeatedly failed to change one practice: every client is asked to narrate their entire case to three volunteers in sequence, which most of us consider demeaning to the client and which triples the time each intake takes. After reading Lewis on convention, I drew three conclusions, and I would like them checked before I draft yet another proposal. First, since the practice persists only because each volunteer expects the others to continue it and prefers to conform given that expectation, it must be a convention in Lewis's sense, and therefore a solution to a coordination problem that serves our common interest. Second, since Lewis holds that conventions are arbitrary in that some alternative would serve the same purpose, it must be confused to argue that one intake procedure is better than another; the only real question is which procedure we have converged on. Third, since mutual expectations are what sustain a convention, nothing short of everyone's expectations shifting at once can move us to a different one, which would make my proposed two-month pilot pointless. Which of these inferences does Lewis's account actually support, and where have I quietly swapped his view for something else?
