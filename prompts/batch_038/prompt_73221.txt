I sit on the evidence review panel of a mid-sized insurer. Roughly once a quarter we are pitched an externally developed scoring instrument — alternative-data credit models, "culture fit" screens, vendor-built indices linking weather to behaviour — and a growing number of these pitches arrive in the same rhetorical shape. The vendor concedes that the older folk method from which their instrument descends was largely discredited; they advertise that they have amputated the discredited parts; they retain a slim core of parameters; they attach a new and mathematically elegant mechanism story that nobody could have told thirty years ago; they report a modest but non-zero backtested signal; and they position themselves as the sober middle between credulous enthusiasts and reflexive debunkers.

I want the panel to evaluate that shape by reasoning about a pattern rather than about a vibe, so I have chosen a historical case I know reasonably well: Kepler's attempt to reform astrology. As I understand it, Kepler drew much of his income from his duties as district and court mathematicus, which obliged him to produce annual prognostications and nativities — including one for Wallenstein in 1608, extended in 1625 — while privately rejecting most of the inherited apparatus. In his tract on the more certain foundations of astrology (*De fundamentis astrologiae certioribus*, 1602) he discarded the twelve unequal houses, the zodiacal signs as sources of power, and the traditional planetary dignities, and located whatever efficacy exists in the angular configuration between celestial bodies as seen from Earth. He then rebuilt the aspect list from geometry rather than authority: configurations corresponding to constructible regular polygons stay, divisions of the circle admitting no such construction are struck, and on that basis he added new aspects — the quintile and biquintile from the pentagon and decagon (72° and 144°), the semisquare and sesquiquadrate from the octagon (45° and 135°). The mechanism he substituted was harmonical rather than mechanical: a soul-like vital faculty in the terrestrial globe, and in living bodies, that responds to these ratios as the ear responds to consonance; the same commitments run through *Harmonices Mundi* (1619), which devotes substantial space to astrology. And he insisted the surviving core be checked against records: he gathered weather and nativity data and argued that aspects should be tested against weather and temperament rather than against particular political events, which he assigned to the disreputable "judicial" branch of the art. In *Tertius Interveniens* (1610), written into a quarrel between an astrology-friendly physician (Röslin) and an anti-astrological natural philosopher (Feselius), he attacked the superstitious practitioners and the wholesale rejecters alike, on the ground that discarding the whole practice together with its abuses throws away something of real value.

I want the transferable consequences drawn out: what should Kepler's reformed astrology change about how our panel handles these pitches? I am not after a general history of astrology, and not after a verdict on whether Kepler was right about the heavens influencing the Earth. I want the lessons for evaluating any practice that has been reformed by amputation, re-mechanized by its own defenders, and then re-tested under protocols its defenders designed. Please give me reasoning rather than references: no bibliography, no reading list, and no quoted passages from Kepler's works.

Please organise the response in the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. **Kepler's reform as a set of moves** (about 250 words). Decompose the episode into four to six discrete structural moves — for instance, amputation of the inherited ontology, substitution of a more unified mechanism, migration of the predicted target toward vaguer and higher-base-rate outcomes, self-designed testing protocol, occupation of the moderate middle, dependence on a paying audience that wants reassurance. Name each move neutrally and describe it; do not judge it yet.

2. **Diagnostic table** (about 250 words, mostly tabular). One table with a row per move from section 1 and four columns: the move; how Kepler performed it; how a modern vendor pitch performs it; and the single sharpest question our panel should put to the vendor.

3. **Implications for the panel** (about 700 words). This is the core. Numbered implications, each opening with a claim about what follows, then one or two sentences of support grounded in the Kepler case, then the concrete procedural consequence for how we run a review — what we demand before a vote, what we refuse to count as evidence, what we record. At least two implications must cut against the reflex either to trust a self-critical reformer or to dismiss the whole family of instruments.

4. **Where the analogy breaks** (about 200 words). At least three respects in which Kepler's situation differs from a commercial vendor's in ways that matter for transferring the lessons, including the fact that the same harmonical commitments were feeding a research programme that was simultaneously producing genuine, independently checkable astronomical results.

5. **Bottom line** (about 100 words). Three or four unhedged sentences the panel could act on at its next meeting.
