I am a final-year biology undergraduate writing my dissertation on phylogenetic comparative methods, and I am also taking a course on big history. The comparative vocabulary is the one I am fluent in: characters and character states, models of trait evolution such as Brownian motion and Ornstein–Uhlenbeck processes, phylogenetic signal as measured by Pagel's lambda or Blomberg's K, ancestral state reconstruction, branch lengths read as rates, borrowing and hybridisation as departures from a strictly bifurcating tree, and above all the non-independence of tip data — Galton's problem — as the central inferential hazard in any cross-species comparison. The historical material I am far less sure of.

Please translate three theses from that course into my vocabulary, as rigorously as you can. First, Harari's account in *Sapiens* of the Agricultural Revolution as a trap: a change that multiplied the population while making individual lives worse. Second, Diamond's argument in *Guns, Germs, and Steel* that continental axis orientation and the uneven distribution of domesticable plants and animals determined which societies acquired germs, writing and military technology. Third, Graeber and Wengrow's counter-argument in *The Dawn of Everything* that political form is far more labile and self-consciously chosen than either allows — that societies switched regimes seasonally and abandoned agriculture deliberately, and that what reads as an irreversible slide out of egalitarianism is better described as the loss of the freedoms to leave, to disobey and to reorganise social life.

For each thesis, tell me what it becomes as a hypothesis about trait evolution: what the characters are, what the tree would be, which model of change it presupposes, and what would count as evidence for or against it. Then give me the residue report — what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression at all in comparative-method terms. Be scrupulous about the direction of fit: do not silently rewrite the historians' claims into ones they would reject, and do not treat the absence of a well-behaved tree as though it settled anything on its own. Finish by telling me where you think the residue really sits: is it a genuine limitation of my framework, which cannot represent agency, reversal and deliberate choice, or does its inexpressibility suggest that some of these grand narratives were never making causal claims capable of being tested?
