I help run a Thursday-evening talk group at our public library. Nothing official — I put up a flyer in the café and about fourteen people drift in, ages somewhere between twenty-two and seventy, and maybe a third of them were born somewhere else (two from Poland, one from Nigeria, one from Vietnam, a guy named Eduardo who's from Peru and worked twenty years on fishing boats). We read pop-philosophy-ish stuff and argue about it over bad coffee. I'm not academic at all, I manage a hardware store, but I've gotten weirdly obsessed this year with that whole line of research pointing out that almost everything psychologists claim to know about human moral instincts was measured on a very narrow slice of humans — the acronym I keep running into is some letters like WEIRD, and I'm pretty sure it stands for western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic, which is basically the room I sit in. What gets me isn't the fact that people elsewhere answer differently. It's that you can't feel it from the inside. When I picture a hard case, the morally relevant parts arrive already highlighted — who's related to whom, who owes what, whether anyone's watching — and it feels like I'm just seeing the case, not adding anything to it.

Six months ago I tried running that famous one about the brother and sister who sleep together once, use protection, both enjoy it, nobody's harmed, and the pair keep it a secret forever. Complete disaster. Within four minutes half the room was yelling about disgust and the other half was accusing them of being prudes, Eduardo said something in Spanish that made two people laugh and one person furious, and we never got anywhere near the actual point, which was supposed to be noticing that your judgment arrives before your reasons do. So I want to design something better, and I have a half-formed idea I'd like you to build out or shoot down. Instead of one shocking case, I want a set of matched pairs: the same dilemma written two ways, one version told with names and family and reputations and who owes whom, the other version told with strangers and quantities, structured so they're the same decision on paper. People answer each card privately on an index card, no talking, and then we reveal. My hunch is that the same person will answer the two versions differently and be genuinely rattled by it, because they were sure they were responding to the case and not to how it was told. Then — and this is the part I can't figure out — the group has to notice that if a rewording inside one culture can flip your answer, the culture you grew up in is doing something like that to you permanently, except you can't run the matched pair on it, because you have no version of yourself raised elsewhere to compare against.

So: design the actual session. Ninety minutes, one facilitator (me, no helpers), budget is index cards, a whiteboard, and whatever's in my kitchen junk drawer — no printing money and no app, several of our regulars don't want to look at a phone. Give me the card texts or at least the recipe for writing them so the pairs really are matched and not sneakily different, the order things happen in, how the private answering and the reveal work mechanically so I can't accidentally steer it, and a debrief that maps what they just did onto the bigger claim rather than just asserting it. Two things I need handled explicitly. First, with a room that mixed, any activity where "other cultures" become the exhibit turns my actual friends into specimens — Eduardo already half-joked about being the sample last time — so the design needs to not depend on someone volunteering their homeland as data, and I'd like to know what to say if it starts going that way anyway. Second, and this is the one that keeps me up: if all of it is soaked in culture, including my hunch that the soaking matters, what's supposed to be left standing at the end? I don't want the debrief to just land on "everything's arbitrary, have a cookie," but I also don't want to fake a solution I haven't got. Tell me honestly what that night can and can't deliver, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and which piece of the idea the activity just cannot reproduce so I have to say it out loud myself. Please don't send me a reading list — nobody's going to read it and I'd get the names wrong anyway.
