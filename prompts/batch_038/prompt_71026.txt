I head model validation at a mid-sized commercial bank. My committee signs off on roughly twenty internal models a quarter — probability-of-default, pricing, liquidity stress — and a large share of the files contain the same move from the model owner: an assumption is conceded to be unrealistic and then defended on instrumentalist grounds, that what entitles a model to be believed is its predictive performance for the class of phenomena it is intended to cover, not the realism of its assumptions. That appeal is sometimes right, and we have no principled way to tell when. Worse, we have let it go circular: backtesting is the only reason we accept a model's idealizations, and when the model subsequently underperforms the same owners explain the shortfall by pointing back at an idealization or a regime change. The assumption therefore never loses — it licenses acceptance when the numbers are good and excuses failure when they are not. We call that the F-twist internally, and closing it is the point of this exercise.

What I want is a decision procedure my committee can run inside a forty-five-minute slot per file. The constraints are these: four members, two of whom have no technical training in the subject area of the model under review; validation may not commission new empirical work or re-estimation, so every question must be answerable from the file and the owner's answers in the room; our supervisory guidance expects validation to evaluate conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring, and outcomes analysis, and I have to be able to say which of those three my procedure discharges; I sign the recommendation and carry it to the risk committee. Each gate must terminate in one of exactly three dispositions — reject the model, approve it restricted to a stated domain of use, or refer it to me as a question of judgment rather than fact — and must say which.

Run the procedure on three live files:

A. A probability-of-default model that treats a bureau credit score as a causal driver and includes a fitted interaction term nobody on the team can interpret or motivate. It backtests well over four years, including the most recent downturn. The owner now wants to extend it to a product line it was not built for, and the business has already been promised that extension. This is the file my committee most wants to keep.

B. A liquidity stress model that assumes the bank can raise secured funding at a fixed spread over benchmark. The owner concedes the assumption is false in stressed conditions. The model's entire backtesting record consists of calm quarters, because the bank has not been through a stress period since the model was built; it nonetheless sets the stress buffer, and that buffer is currently binding on two business lines. There is no alternative model, so an adverse disposition means running the buffer on judgment until one exists.

C. A hedging model for short-dated index options that assumes continuous trading and no jumps, used only for that book. The distortion is admitted, quantified as immaterial at those maturities by the owner's own sensitivity runs, and my committee has nevertheless spent two years asking the owner to justify it.

Deliverable: exactly five numbered items, one per gate, in the order the committee should ask them, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no summary of the methodological debate — assume I know it and want only the translation. Each item may run to three sentences: the question the gate puts, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, and the disposition that follows if no sufficient answer is supplied. Where a gate is the one that decides a file above, name the file and give its outcome inside that third sentence rather than after the list. At least one of the five gates must be one the procedure cannot pass through on evidence at all, and must state plainly what judgment it hands me instead. And if you expect a gate to misfire in practice — letting through a model it should stop, or stopping one whose idealization is harmless — say which gate and why, inside the five items.
