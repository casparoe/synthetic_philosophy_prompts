I am preparing a handout for a philosophy-of-science reading group, and the argument below — assembled from familiar pieces of Bayesian confirmation theory and then pushed further than its sources push it — seems to me to prove far too much. I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey.

Set-up: an agent at a time has a credence function P and an evidence base, a set of propositions she accepts on the strength of experience; confirmation is cashed out in the standard probabilistic way.

1. Evidence E confirms hypothesis H for an agent only if P(H | E) > P(H).
2. If E belongs to the agent's evidence base, then P(E) = 1; one does not merely have high credence in one's own evidence.
3. If P(E) = 1, then P(H | E) = P(H) for every H. (Since P(H ∧ ¬E) ≤ P(¬E) = 0, we get P(H ∧ E) = P(H), and dividing by P(E) = 1 changes nothing.)
4. Therefore no member of an agent's evidence base confirms any hypothesis for that agent.
5. This is no local peculiarity. Every agent at every time has some evidence base, and everything in it satisfies (2). So nothing believed on the strength of experience has ever confirmed anything for anyone.
6. The same holds of the evidence base taken as a whole, and it holds precisely in the cases that motivate the enterprise. In the paradigmatic episodes of astronomical theorizing the anomalous datum was on the books for decades before the theory that explained it was proposed; at the moment of proposal the datum was already believed and, by (4), inert.
7. Nor is there an obvious patch. If the agent is told to conditionalize not on E but on the proposition that H entails E, that proposition too is believed as soon as the entailment is seen, and (4) applies to it in turn. Every surrogate for E is swallowed the moment it is accepted.
8. Confirmation, then, is available only in the instant before an agent comes to believe her evidence — a state nobody can occupy while reporting on it — and so the Bayesian has no account of confirmation at all.

I do not believe (8), and I cannot see which premise to abandon. (3) and (4) look to me like arithmetic rather than philosophy, and I am attached to (1) and (2): I want confirmation to be a genuine shift in credence and not a notational relation, and I want evidence to be something an agent can be certain of, or the story of learning from experience starts to slip. That leaves the later steps, but nothing in them strikes me as questionable.

Three questions. First, identify the step or steps you would reject and say exactly what the argument assumes there that it has not earned — a scope shift, an equivocation on the relata of "confirms," a suppressed index to agent and time, whatever it is. If instead you think the argument is valid and the premises must simply be false, say so and say which one is false. Second, for each of the three repairs on offer — refusing credence one for empirical evidence, treating confirmation as a relation between a hypothesis and an evidence base from which the datum has been removed, and conditionalizing on the entailment relation rather than on the datum — state what the repair costs the Bayesian elsewhere, and whether it actually rescues the astronomical cases of (6) or only makes (8) less embarrassing. Third, tell me whether the argument defeats itself: if it shows that no evidence ever confirms anything, it has identified nothing distinctive about old evidence at all, and I want to know whether that generality is a mark of strength or the trace of a slide.
