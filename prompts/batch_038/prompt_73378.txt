I'm on a small working group giving advice to a constitutional assembly, and before I read one more comparative-politics paper I want the spec rather than the candidates. Here's the case, treat it as hypothetical: 61-seat unicameral legislature, three regional blocs that each pull roughly a third of the vote, first election almost certainly produces no majority and no natural coalition partner for anyone. They have to ratify a peace deal whose land-transfer schedule and enabling laws need to survive at least two full electoral cycles, so whatever executive form they choose has to let the government make promises that a later bare majority can't cheaply unwind. There is no independent judiciary worth the name yet — the courts are staffed by the same parties, so entrenchment-through-judicial-review is off the table as a commitment device. And the budget has to pass every year or the civil service stops getting paid.

So: what would a theory of presidential-versus-parliamentary institutional choice have to deliver before it could issue a defensible recommendation in a situation like this? I want the requirements list, not the answer. Which phenomena it has to explain, which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it mustn't collapse (I'm thinking at minimum of the difference between a system being deadlock-prone and a system being deadlock-prone *in this electorate*, and between removing an executive who has lost support and removing one who deserves removing), what empirical regularities about regime survival it can't contradict, and what it has to be able to dismiss as an artifact rather than treat as a real difference between forms. Give me the consideration or case behind each requirement.

Then mark which of them are criteria that people who work on this actually agree on, and which ones are really the preferences of one camp smuggled in as neutral requirements — the ones about fixed terms being a bug versus a feature, and the ones about clarity of accountability, are where I suspect this shows up most. If some subset of the requirements can't all be satisfied together given the facts above, say which subset and why; that's the part I most want to see. And if you had to pick one requirement that does the most work in narrowing the field, name it.

One thing I'd add to the case: whatever it recommends has to be able to score mixes, not just pure types. I've floated something where the assembly can dismiss the executive only by a two-thirds vote for the first four years and by simple majority after that, and nothing I've read has a category for it. A theory that can only rank the two ideal types and goes quiet on hybrids is failing a requirement, I think — tell me if you disagree and why.

Short scorecard at the end is fine, two or three lines per contender, keep it genuinely short.

Also please be explicit, throughout, about which parts of this are settled among people who study it and which parts are live disputes. I'm going to be in a room with lawyers from three different blocs and I really don't want to accidentally present one camp's contested claim as the professional consensus.
