ok, so context: I'm a second-year PhD student in philosophy of science and in a few weeks I've got to present a bit of a heretical line at a work-in-progress seminar — namely that inference to the best explanation is a genuine rule of inference in its own right, not just a folksy gloss on Bayesian reasoning. My supervisor has told me to get properly beaten up beforehand, so that's you.

Play a committed Bayesian deflationist about IBE. You think the phrase doesn't name a distinct rule at all: every defensible explanatory inference is either conditionalisation plus a likelihood judgement, or a straight leap beyond the evidence dressed up in philosophical language. You're sharp and fair-minded, not a strawman — you'll happily grant that explanatory virtues are real features of theories, that working scientists talk this way all day, that "IBE" is perfectly good shorthand for the heuristics by which we generate and rank hypotheses, and that a Bayesian who sneers at explanation is missing something about scientific practice. What you will never concede is that explanatoriness is itself an evidential relation: i.e. that a hypothesis's being the best explanation of the evidence can confer warrant that conditionalisation on the same evidence couldn't. You keep van Fraassen's bad-lot point in your back pocket and you reach for it whenever I try to squeeze warrant out of "best of the lot we happened to think of". You also regard simplicity, unification and the rest as legitimate inputs to priors and likelihoods — which is how you defuse most of my examples before they get going.

Mechanics: stay in character until I say "stop". Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs; this should read like an argument between two people who've read the same papers, not a lecture or a list. If something I say is ambiguous, ask me about it as your character would. And if I land a move you genuinely think is decisive, say so, give the ground, and then fall back to a weaker position you can actually hold — I need to know where the retreat lines are.

My opening move is the problem of old evidence. General relativity was confirmed by the fact that it accounted for the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion — but that anomaly had been measured and firmly believed for decades before Einstein got there. Conditionalisation looks silent: if your credence in the anomaly was already 1, learning it again can't move anything, and P(T|E) with P(E) = 1 is just P(T). Now here's the bit I think hurts you. The standard repair is to say that what Einstein learned wasn't the anomaly but the entailment itself — some proposition like "GR entails E" — and to conditionalise on that. But notice what you've committed yourself to: your language now has to contain the entailment relation, and in order to conditionalise on "T entails E" you have to have got hold of that logical fact somehow. Conditionalisation doesn't tell you how, because for the formalism the entailment was already known all along. So your best fix for old evidence only works by admitting that agents aren't logically omniscient and that the epistemically interesting learning is happening outside the formalism — and once you've conceded that, I want to say it's precisely there, in the grasping of why the theory makes the anomaly intelligible, that the confirmation comes from.

So: how do you handle old evidence without either (a) smuggling the acquisition of a logical fact in through the back door, or (b) conceding that what did the confirming wasn't the bare entailment but the fact that GR made the Mercury anomaly make sense in a way its rivals definately didn't?
