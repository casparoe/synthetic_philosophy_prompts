ok so I'm a junior and I sit on my school's student senate, and we're having a genuinely ugly fight about who gets to vote in a referendum. The referendum is on whether we should be allowed to leave campus during lunch. The senate chair says only juniors and seniors get ballots, becuase the referendum is a senate matter and the senate is elected by upperclassmen. When I pushed back he said it's exactly like a private club deciding who its members are — the club sets its own membership rules and nobody calls that undemocratic — and then he added that if freshmen get a vote, why not teachers, and if teachers, why not the front office, and pretty soon it isn't student government at all. That slope thing landed with a lot of people and I couldn't answer it on the spot.

I've since been reading about the boundary problem in democratic theory — the idea that you can't settle who's in the demos by taking a vote of the demos, because whoever votes on that question has already assumed the answer — and I'm pretty sure his club analogy is carrying way more weight than it can hold. Can you audit it for me?

What I want: the correspondence table first, what in the club is supposed to map onto what in the student body and what maps onto the senate's authority to draw the line. Then the places the mapping breaks, but sorted — tell me which disanalogies actually threaten the conclusion he's using it for and which ones are just nitpicks a smart person would ignore. Then these specific questions:

- does the club analogy already presuppose the thing we're fighting about, i.e. that there's some group whose entitlement to decide is settled before the deciding starts? If it does, is that fatal or can he dodge it?
- is the freshmen-to-teachers-to-front-office slope the same argument wearing a different coat, or a separate one? What would actually stop the slide without just begging the question in the other direction?
- "everyone affected should get a say" versus "everyone subject to the rules should get a say" — I keep seeing these two pulled apart. Which one gives a cleaner verdict on the freshman who has to live with the lunch rule, and on the teacher who'll be ordered to enforce whatever we decide but has no ballot?
- if the club is the wrong picture, is there a better analogy I could use in a two minute speech, or is the honest answer that there isn't one becuase the situation genuinely has no parallel?

Please keep it in language I can say out loud to teenagers without sounding like I swallowed a textbook — no jargon dumps, no name-checking of books I haven't read — but don't soften the arguments either, I'd rather have the hard version and lose gracefully than a comfy one.
