our reading group has asked each of us to write down our distributive-justice views so the others can attack them, so here's mine. I'd like to know whether it hangs together.

1. Justice means the distribution should be insensitive to brute luck and sensitive to option luck. That's the whole idea for me.
2. But I read "option" objectively: what counts is whether the choice was informed and made under adequate control, not whether the person happened to think of what they were doing as a gamble.
3. So: the careful driver who crashes on a stretch of road she knew went icy in winter gets compensated — she did everything right. The reckless one who crashes without ever having formed any view about the risk at all get nothing.
4. The currency is resources, not welfare, since welfare is contaminated by adaptive preferences and by tastes people cultivate. Hence the wine-lover who acquired the taste gets nothing, while someone with an expensive taste they never chose, or a congenital disability, does.
5. I'm a starting-gate person: equal shares at the outset, and whatever free exchange produces afterwards isn't a question of distributive justice.
6. But I do think structural disadvantage — say a woman channelled into low-paid care work by the way her society is arranged — is brute luck from her point of view and should be compensated.
7. I don't buy the harshness objection; leaving the imprudent to the consequences of their choices is part of respecting them. All the same I want an unconditional floor, owed on grounds of equal citizenship rather than distributive justice.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real ones by severity, and for each say what the cheapest revision would be and what it'd cost me — then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I'm not wedded to any of them, so don't spare one just because it's near the top of the list. Audit only, no tour of the literature. British English please.
