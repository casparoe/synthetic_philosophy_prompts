so, bit of background first — I spent 35 years in an analytical chemistry lab doing method validation, and since I retired I've gone down a rabbit hole of philosophy of science, especially the whole replication crisis thing, which reads like a horror story to a man who was made to run the blank twice before he was allowed to say anything at all. I want a comic piece out of it, but I'm fussy about the statistics actually being right, because most of the jokes I read about this stuff are wrong in a way that ruins them for me.

Write me a parody of one of those mail-order wine merchant catalog pages — you know the sort, tasting notes, a vintage chart, staff picks, case offers, delivery and storage information — except the shop doesn't sell wine. It sells published findings, bottled. Invent the merchant; I keep calling it Harker & Null in my head, but rename it if you come up with something better.

The bottles are studies. So I want a big fruity red that comes in at p = 0.049 and has to be drunk this season, because left another year it will have drifted over the line and be undrinkable. A tiny production number from a producer working with a dozen bottles' worth of material, which somehow tastes enormously of a huge effect. A natural-wine preregistered thing — honest, austere, faintly disappointing, entirely unsold. A sparkling made by optional stopping, where the proprietor kept going back to the tank and tasting until the bubbles were right. Six to eight entries in that register, and let the vocabulary do the work: nose, palate, finish, terroir, cellaring potential, the lot.

Then the vintage chart, where the years are rated on something like how many of that year's bottles still taste the same a decade on. A staff pick. Pairing suggestions, which should be pairings with things a study gets consumed alongside — a press release, a policy brief, a conference keynote. Serving and storage advice along the lines of serve at alpha = 0.05, keep away from direct sunlight and from direct requests for the raw data. And a case offer that is the file drawer problem sold as a bundle: we taste twenty, we ship you the one that worked, the other nineteen stay in the back and we'd rather not discuss them.

The things I actually care about:

1. Every joke has to be a true feature of the practice, exaggerated — not something made up because it sounds funny. A statistician reading it should wince with recognition, not at errors.
2. Deadpan all the way through. The merchant is completely sincere, a craftsman proud of his range. He never winks, never apologises, never says anything equivalent to 'of course it's all a bit silly.'
3. It still has to read as a plausible catalog page to someone who has never heard of a p-value. The surface should be straight enough that a lay reader senses something is off without being told.
4. No real people, no real papers, no real journals or labs, and absolutely no bibliography or reference list — you'd only be guessing anyway. Invent everything. If you find yourself wanting to cite a source, turn it into a producer's name or a château instead.
5. Around 600–700 words, laid out with the little headers, bolded tasting-note names and bullet points a real catalog would have.

One more thing, and its the part that annoys me most, so I'd like it handled carefully: one entry — maybe a producer-profile sidebar, or the intern's note at the bottom — should be a perfectly correct and perfectly unexciting description of a well-powered study with a small effect size and a sensible confidence interval. The merchant cannot summon any enthusiasm for it. The joke is that the good wine is boring and he knows it and he resents it, but he must not say so outright. Let the flatness of his prose do it.

And if the merchant talks about confidence intervals, either keep him correct or make his confusion legible — a reader who knows the difference between a confidence interval and a credible interval should be able to tell he's just mixed them up. That's funnier to me than getting it right, actually, so long as the mistake is one a real person would make.
