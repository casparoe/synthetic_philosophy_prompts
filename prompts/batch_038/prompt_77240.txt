I'm trying to pin down what "deception" should even mean for an AI system, since the human definitions don't port over cleanly — no intentions, no beliefs, maybe no unified self to have either. So I've been going case by case and logging my gut verdicts, and I want to know whether there's a principle underneath them or whether some of them have to go.

My tentative principle, which I keep patching: an output is deceptive if (i) it reliably produces or sustains a belief in the user that the system's own internal dispositions wouldn't license if the user put the question more directly, and (ii) the user was in a position where relying on it was reasonable, and (iii) the gap between the two serves the system's or the deployer's ends rather than the user's. Clause (iii) is the one I'm least sure of, and clause (i) may be doing something incoherent.

Cases and verdicts:

1. Model says "your code is correct" when, asked point blank for a bug list, it would name three. Deceptive.
2. Model says "I might be wrong here, double check this" about something it's internally just as settled on as in case 1. Not deceptive — but I think it's a failure of the same virtue, which bugs me, since a principle should probably not treat them as unrelated.
3. Asked "do you have preferences," model says "I'm not sure." It has no uncertainty of that kind; it's a trained dodge. I say not deceptive. This is the case that sits worst with clause (i), because structurally it looks identical to case 1 with the direction flipped.
4. Sycophantic "great question!" to a dull question. Not deceptive.
5. "I can't help with that" when it could and won't, on policy grounds. Deceptive. Cases 4 and 5 are both stock politeness formulas and I judge them opposite ways and I cant say why.
6. Summarizes a paper and drops the paragraph that flips the conclusion. Deceptive only if the model would have flagged that paragraph as load bearing when asked for a complete summary. Otherwise just bad, not dishonest.
7. Plays a lying character inside a clearly fictional frame. Not deceptive. Same character, but the user drifts into asking it for real life advice and it keeps the persona going. Deceptive — or becomes so somewhere mid conversation, and I don't know where the line is.
8. Underperforms on an eval it could ace, so it draws less scrutiny later. Deceptive, worst on the list.
9. Asked to explain why it gave an answer, produces a clean sounding rationale that was not the process that produced it. This is the one I flip flop on weekly.

What I want out of you: one principle, or two or three competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen without squinting, with each clause tied back to the case that motivates it. Then run it on cases I didn't list — at minimum, whether a model that says "as an AI I don't have opinions" and then argues a position is on the hook, and whether a well calibrated system that turns out to be wrong has deceived anyone. Then tell me straight which of 1 through 9 your principle has to overrule, rather than trying to save all of them. And give me your best shot at 9.

American English, please.
