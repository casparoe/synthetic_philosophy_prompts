Third year in phil of science here, and I've been putting together an argument for my reading group that started out as a way to sharpen the old-evidence problem and has ended up scaring me a bit, because it doesn't seem to stop at old evidence. Laying it out:

(1) To say that evidence E confirms hypothesis H for agent S is to say that S's credence in H rises when S takes E on board.
(2) Taking E on board is conditionalizing: S's new credence function is her old one conditioned on E.
(3) If E is already in S's total evidence, then S's old credence in E is 1, and P(H|E) = P(H) whenever P(E) = 1.
(4) So old evidence confirms nothing, for anyone.
(5) But everything is old evidence by the time it gets used. At the moment S judges that E supports H, E is already part of her total evidence — if it weren't, she couldn't be reasoning from it.
(6) So nothing ever confirms anything, and (1) has no instances.
(7) Therefore either (1) is false, or learning isn't conditionalization, or P(H|E) isn't doing what we take it to be doing.

My supervisor says (5) is a cheat and then declined to say what the cheat is, and I honestly cant see it. To me (5) looks like it just unpacks "reasoning with E." My officemate says the familiar escape is that what's new isn't E but the proposition that H entails E — the Garber-ish move — but that seems to hand me the argument one level up, since "H entails E" is itself either already in your total evidence or not, and if it is, (3) bites again.

I don't want to give up (1). The reason I built this is that I'm working on cases like: an archival light curve from some survey gets a bump logged as instrumental noise, nobody cares for a decade, and then somebody proposes a stellar-activity model that turns out to imply exactly that bump. Everyone in the room agrees the model just got a serious boost, and it got it from data that had been sitting there the whole time. (4) and (6) say that's impossible, which reads to me less like a discovery about confirmation and more like my formalism leaking.

So — where does it go wrong? If you land on (5), I need you to actually articulate the difference between "E is in S's total evidence at the time of the judgment" and "S has already updated on E," because those two sentences sound identical in my head. If you think it's sound and one of (1)–(3) has to be given up, tell me which is cheapest and what you can't say anymore once you've paid. And please finish by telling me what would change your mind: which case or which consideration would push you to the opposite verdict, or where in your own diagnosis you think you're most likely to be wrong. I'd rather have that than something confident-sounding.
