Ok, context first. I run a fortnightly-ish reading group that's an unholy mix — three econ PhD students, two philosophers, and a bunch of people who got into decision theory through poker and forecasting — and last week someone wheeled out Good's theorem on the value of information: roughly, that if you're offered a costless sample of evidence before you decide, and you update by conditionalizing, and your utilities don't themselves depend on what the evidence turns out to say, then taking the evidence and then choosing is never worse than choosing right now, with equality only where the evidence wouldn't have changed your choice anyway. He used it to argue that seeking more evidence is always the rational move, full stop, and that anyone who demurs hasn't understood the result. Half the room pushed back, half agreed, and after ninety minutes nobody could say what exactly the disagreement was. So I want to build a little self-sorting flowchart that people can do on their own in ten minutes and then compare, so the next session starts with "right, you four dropped assumption three, you three dropped assumption five" instead of the same circular fight. It's going to be a printed handout and also a clicky form version.

What I actually want: a decision tree, ten to twelve questions deep at the worst, where every node is a concrete situation with two or three answer options — not a menu of assumptions the reader picks from. Someone should be able to work through it without any idea which exit they're steering toward, and without having seen the theorem before, so please also write me a four-or-five sentence preamble in plain language stating the result and listing the assumptions it rests on (costless, no delay; conditionalization; fixed utilities; value attaches to consequences only and not to how you got there; expected-utility maximization / dynamic consistency; single-agent, so nobody else's situation changes). If you think I've got that list wrong, or that something should be split in two, just split it and tell me. Each leaf should be named in ordinary words by the assumption it gives up — or, for one leaf, by giving up none of them — and should carry three things: what the reader has actually committed themselves to, the single most awkward consequence of holding it (the case where it tells them to do something that looks flatly crazy), and whether it's a genuine exit or a fake one. No citations, no author names, no reading list, nothing anyone would have to go look up. And if a cell of the space turns out to be empty — a combination of answer-drops nobody actually holds — say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered.

Scenarios I'd like in there, in whatever shape suits you: (a) a free, painless aptitude test where the person knows that a positive result will make them reorganize their life around the thing and a negative one will kill their interest entirely, so what they care about is not the same before and after; (b) an ambiguity-averse person who has deliberately spread their bets across two unknown-probability urns and is offered a free draw from one of them that will resolve its composition and thereby wreck the hedge; (c) a test whose result the person you're negotiating with also gets to see, so being informed makes you legible and exploitable; (d) a free self-assessment that the person knows will make them second-guess themselves and perform worse — the learning has a causal effect on the performance, not just on the belief; (e) a trap one: the test is free but takes three weeks and the offer expires in the meantime, which I want routed to a leaf that says you didn't escape the theorem, you broke "costless"; (f) something where the evidence can't be cleanly conditionalized on — dusk, a sailboat, you get a partial impression rather than a proposition; (g) a genetic result that is also information about a sibling who has asked not to know. And please include one node that forces apart "I'd turn down a free test because knowing would spoil the pleasure" from "I'd turn down a free test because knowing would change what I value" — I think those are different exits and everybody in the group conflated them last week. Finally, after the tree, give me a short map of the logical space: which combinations are genuinely available, which are empty and why, and, most importantly, which two leaves a scenario-only instrument cannot seperate. I'd bet money there's at least one pair — dropping conditionalization and dropping expected-utility maximization probably look identical from the outside — and if so, tell me what kind of question *would* split them, even if it isn't the sort of thing you can fit in a ten-minute handout.
