I am in my fourth year of a political philosophy doctorate, writing on the ethics of blame, and for the last two years I have also sat on my university's panel for online conduct — the body that handles referrals when a member of the community's social-media activity becomes a matter of institutional concern. Three of us read each referral cold, we get twenty-five minutes before a vote, none of us has legal training, and our recommendation goes to a dean's office that ratifies it almost without exception. We see perhaps a dozen cases a year in normal terms and considerably more in a bad month, and the realistic range of outcomes is narrow: a formal statement of censure, a requirement that the person issue a correction or apology, withdrawal of a teaching assistantship or of the department's sponsorship of a talk, a recommendation that a scholarly society rescind an invitation, or nothing at all. My co-panellists and I keep relitigating the same question case by case, and I want to stop doing it. One of us, who came from legal theory, holds that if what we impose is burdensome and is imposed because of a past wrong, it is punishment in substance whatever we call it, and therefore owes proportionality and process. Another holds that a university, a platform, and a profession are all voluntary associations, and that withdrawing standing from a member is no more punishment than uninviting someone from a dinner party is; on that view the only duties we owe are notice and a fit between the restriction and the risk. A third thinks the whole frame is wrong and that what we are doing is neither punishing nor excluding but managing a hazard. Please write a decision procedure this panel could actually run on a referral, one that resolves which frame governs rather than assuming it.

The procedure should proceed through diagnostic questions in a fixed order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and it should be built on the distinction between sanction as punishment — which, if it applies, imports proportionality, due process, and the expressive aim of condemning a wrong, on something like Feinberg's account of what punishment expresses — and sanction as the alteration of the terms of association or as risk management, which imports only reasonable notice and fit. It must be explicit about the aggregation problem, which is where I think our current practice is least honest: a journal rescinds an invitation, a department rescinds a talk, an employer rescinds an offer, a thousand people post, and no single one of those actors is punishing anyone, yet the target can end up worse off than any court could lawfully have made them. I want the procedure to say what, if anything, follows for the one actor sitting in front of us, and whether it matters that our own contribution is marginal to the total harm and that withdrawing would not help the target. Strawson's distinction between the participant stance and the objective stance is worth keeping if it earns its place: in particular I want the procedure to detect when a body is managing someone as a risk while describing what it does in the language of holding them responsible, and the converse, when it is condemning someone under the cover of risk language, since both moves let an institution impose the costs of punishment while declining its constraints. Beyond that, strip out any conceptual machinery a non-specialist panellist cannot deploy in twenty-five minutes, and tell me what you stripped and what breaks as a result. Mark every point at which the answer turns on a value judgment the procedure cannot derive, and say where the procedure terminates in a flat "no" rather than handing the case to a person.

Then run it on three referrals. First, a senior faculty member whose decade-old posts have surfaced, who has apologised unprompted, and who is now the object of an organised external campaign, where our only realistic options are a formal censure or silence. Second, a graduate student who repeatedly targeted a junior scholar with harassing replies and who argues, not implausibly, that removing him from the department's mailing list and barring him from the workshop series is punishment for speech. Third, a researcher who has been broadcasting dangerous medical misinformation to a very large following while remaining, so far as anyone on the panel knows, entirely decent in person and a good colleague — a case where the risk frame and the blame frame point in opposite directions. I expect the procedure to hand down at least one result I will dislike; if it does not, it is flattering us rather than doing work, and I would rather know that about my own panel now. Finally, tell me plainly where this procedure is most likely to misfire in practice — which kinds of case it will systematically get wrong, and which of its steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure — and tell me what would change your mind about the central classification: what argument or consideration would convince you that aggregated social sanction really is punishment in the sense that imports proportionality, or that it never is, and where in your own reasoning you think the weakest link sits.
