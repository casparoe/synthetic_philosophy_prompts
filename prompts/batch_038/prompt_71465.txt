I'm about halfway through a novel set in the 1260s and my Dominican has to talk a baron into killing his liege, a count who inherited the title fair and square and then spent eleven years squeezing the county dry. I've given him this argument and my medievalist reader says its too good — that as written it doesn't just license this killing, it licenses every killing. Here's the speech:

1. Authority is given for the sake of the common good; a ruler who turns it to his own benefit is no true ruler but a tyrant, and his government is not peace but an attack.
2. The community that set the office up keeps the power to unmake it — to depose or restrain a ruler who perverts its end. So the tyrant's office gives him no immunity, and killing him is not the killing of a lord.
3. Tyranny is not a single act but an attack that never stops; it is going on while we speak.
4. Anyone may repel an attack that is going on, even with lethal force, so long as he intends the defence and not the death.
5. You intend only your people's deliverance; the count's death is the unavoidable price of it, not your aim.
6. Therefore you may do it yourself, tonight, without waiting for any commission — indeed even against the town's wishes, since a town that consents under the lash is not consenting at all.

Which step should I have him strike or repair? I'd rather keep 2 and 3 than 6 — I like the idea that it's the office itself that dissolves, and I need the attack to feel present-tense in the scene. But I'll cut whatever has to go. And when you price the options, price them for the drama as well as for the doctrine: if he needs the community's commission I have to write a whole council scene, and if he can't intend the death then he can't be an assassin at all, which is arguably the book.
