Our team lead keeps arguing against giving any real autonomy to LLM agents by leaning on the digger wasp story: the wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow, goes inside to check things over, and if you slide the cricket a few inches while it's in there, it comes out, finds the cricket, drags it back to the entrance, and goes in to check again — on and on, never just picking the thing up and walking inside. His line is that our agents are the wasp and any changed environment is the moved cricket, so everything should stay scripted and supervised. Can you audit the analogy? I want the mapping spelled out — what plays the wasp, the cricket, the nest inspection, the experimenter — then where it breaks, and I want the disanalogies sorted into the ones that actually undercut his conclusion versus the ones that are just noise. Also tell me what the source case quietly imports that an agent doesn't have: a closed action repertoire, one fixed goal represented one way, a rationale that lives in the wasp's evolutionary history rather than anywhere in its head. Two things I'll put on the table as fair game. One: the version philosophers run with reached Dennett secondhand from a 1963 book about the brain, not from Fabre's own observations, and at least one critic (Fred Keijzer, I think) has argued the wasps do start adapting after enough repititions and that re-checking a disturbed nest is plausibly adaptive rather than blind — so how much of the analogy's punch survives if the source story is partly a myth that kept going because it was rhetorically handy? Two: sphexishness looks like it depends on which perturbation you choose, and you can make almost anything look rigid if you test it far enough off distribution — which would make the charge unfalsifiable rather than damning. Does his conclusion survive all that, and if the wasp is the wrong picture, what would be a better one?
