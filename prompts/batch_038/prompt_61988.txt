I'm a backend infra engineer, no philosophy training beyond what I've read on my own, and I'm giving a 20-minute talk at our local dev meetup. Audience: ~40 working engineers, mostly backend/platform folks, two tech leads who run a big monorepo, one guy who did a formal-methods PhD and will definitely know the game theory (and has probably at least heard of Lewis), and a developer-experience person who cares about onboarding. Nobody else has read any Lewis.

My thesis: style and process norms in a codebase are *not* Lewisian conventions. Lewis's paradigm is a pure coordination game — everyone gets the same payoff at any equilibrium, so it genuinely doesn't matter which one gets picked, and salience does the picking. But a codebase norm looks like battle of the sexes: the infra team and the product teams prefer *different* equilibria (different formatter config, different error-handling idiom, different branching model) and one side actually loses something when the other's wins. So salience isn't what settles these, and I want to argue what settles them is organizational power: whoever owns CI wins. Roughly my three moves are: (1) show a real disagreement from my own repo where both sides were right on their own metrics, (2) claim Lewis needs common interest for salience to do work, (3) say the "convention" only looks shared because the losing side got outvoted in a tooling decision, not because they coordinated.
