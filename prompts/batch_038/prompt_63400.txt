Retired from geophysics — 35 yrs, mostly gravity field modelling and orbit determination, which is why the "self as a center of gravity" line grabbed me the first time I ran into it. I've been reading philosophy seriously for about two years now and I've put together a position on the self, and I want someone to stress-test it rather then explain it back to me.

Here's the package as best I can state it:

1. The self is an abstraction — a posit we introduce to make sense of the behaviour of a certain kind of body — and not an ingredient of the world. I'm sold on the center-of-gravity analogy for this.
2. What makes me the same person now as the one who was twenty isn't a persisting substrate, and it isn't really a chain of memory links either; it's that there's one ongoing story being told and I'm its protagonist. The narrative is what does the holding-together.
3. That story isn't just bookkeeping though. My character genuinely causes things — it's why I gave the wallet back, why I stayed in the marriage. The narrative self has to earn its keep by doing explanatory work, and I won't accept a view on which it does none.
4. Blame and punishment require that it be the same person. You can't justifiably hold someone accountible for what a numerically different individual did.
5. At the same time I don't think infants, corvids, or my mother in late dementia have selves in anything like the sense I do, and I don't think for a moment that puts them outside moral concern.

Which of these actually pull against each other? Rank the tensions worst-first, and for each one say what I'd have to surrender and what breaks downstream once I do. I'm not precious about any single thesis; I just want to know which one and what the repair costs. And please don't flag everything — I'd bet a couple of these only look contradictory, so tell me which ones dissolve and why. Finally, tell me where your own audit is most likely to be wrong: what argument or consideration would make you reverse one of your severity calls?
