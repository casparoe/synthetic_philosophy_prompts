I'm drafting the scope-and-methods section of an internal proposal on mechanistic anomaly detection — the program of building detectors that flag, from a model's own internal activations and without behavioral supervision, when something relevant about the model has changed relative to a reference condition. A colleague read the draft and told me that my criteria for when two internal processes count as the same mechanism are mutually inconsistent, and that I slide between at least three different notions of mechanism identity depending on which argument I happen to be making. I don't think she's right, but the review is on Thursday and I'd rather find out now than in the comments. What follows is the position as I've written it down, stripped of hedging. Please audit it as a set: which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only look like they conflict, what the cheapest revision is in each case and what it costs me, and which single revision would clean up the largest number of problems at once. Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and if I can only sacrifice one item, tell me which one it has to be. I don't want a summary of the research program, and I don't want to be told the program is ambitious — I want the internal structure.

Here are the commitments:

(1) Mechanism identity is fixed by causal role. Two internal processes are the same mechanism just in case they make the same difference to the model's outputs across the full range of contexts it might be run in. Shared implementation is neither necessary nor sufficient.

(2) Accordingly I accept multiple realizability inside a single model: the same function can be carried by different components in different layers, or by overlapping distributed code, and I don't want a detector that is hostage to a fixed set of neurons.

(3) For cost reasons, however, the detectors I actually propose are signature-based. They read the activation pattern of an identified set of learned features on a single forward pass and score its distance from a reference profile. No counterfactual interventions and no probing across contexts: interventions don't scale, and they require knowing in advance which contexts matter, which is precisely what we lack during a real deployment shift.

(4) Mechanisms are objective, mind-independent features of the trained model rather than artifacts of our descriptive scheme. The arbitrariness of a learned feature dictionary — its dependence on dictionary size, objective, and the data it was fit on — doesn't threaten this, because any faithful decomposition will serve: the mechanism is there regardless of which coordinates we use to see it.

(5) The reference condition is fixed by the model's own statistics on the distribution it was trained or fine-tuned on. Nothing outside the model — no specification, no description of intended behavior, no human judgment about what the model ought to be doing — is permitted to enter the definition of normal.

(6) That said, the anomalies I care about are shifts in what the model is trying to do rather than shifts in what it can do. A detector that fires because the model has acquired a new skill is producing a false positive; one that fires because a strategy has changed is producing a true positive.

(7) The detector must be unsupervised in construction: no labels of any kind feed into fitting it. Labels appear only afterward, in the evaluation suite, and I've arranged that the evaluation tasks share no data with the reference distribution.

(8) I distrust a model's verbal reports about what it is doing, yet I treat its internal activations as the ground truth against which my detectors are calibrated. She flagged this as the most obviously circular item in the draft.

(9) Two verdicts from experiments I've already run, both of which I stand behind. Case A: on a held-out distribution, a set of features I had previously identified as a coherent in-distribution circuit fires in a similar pattern but with visibly different downstream routing; I judged it to be the same mechanism, on the grounds that the feature signature matched. Case B: elsewhere, two circuits with essentially matching feature signatures produced different behavioral effects under patching; I judged these to be different mechanisms. Neither verdict felt ad hoc to me at the time.

Two further things I'd like settled explicitly. First, she argues that (5) and (7) are jointly self-undermining: if the only yardstick is the model's own trained-in statistics, then a model whose reference distribution was itself assembled from a shifting mixture of tasks has no fact of the matter about what counts as anomalous, and my claim to be measuring the same mechanism across two different reference sets is empty. Tell me whether that charge lands, or whether it trades on running together what fixes the baseline with what fixes the identity of the thing being baselined. Second, I want to know whether (6) is available to me at all given (1), (4), and (5) — specifically, whether any way of cashing out trying that doesn't smuggle in the normative idiom (5) forbids ends up collapsing (6) into something a purely capability-oriented detector would already deliver, in which case I've been claiming a distinction my own framework cannot draw.
