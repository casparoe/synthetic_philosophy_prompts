For a graduate handout in a seminar on markets as epistemic devices, I need a decision tree that starts from one observed fact — two sophisticated counterparties voluntarily exchange a risky asset at a price both accept, each expecting to come out ahead, and each aware that the other is a rational expected-utility maximizer — and ends at a named account of which hypothesis of the familiar no-trade and agreement results has been given up. Give exactly five numbered forks and nothing else, no introduction and no conclusion: each item should pose the fork's question in one sentence, then in one or two further sentences name its two or three exits and the leaf each one leads to. Two constraints on the questions themselves. First, each must be answerable in principle by someone watching only the trade tape and the traders' own reports of their beliefs, not by already knowing which school of thought one belongs to. Second, no fork may name a theorem, an economist, or a leaf; the labels live only at the exits, and among the exits I want at least these: priors that genuinely differ, rationality known to only finite order rather than commonly, the trade not being speculative at all, one side knowingly absorbing less well-informed flow, and non-Bayesian attitudes toward an ambiguous payoff — plus any others the tree needs. In the fifth item, after stating that last fork, add one sentence identifying a pair of leaves your five questions cannot in fact separate observationally, and one sentence saying whether any reachable combination of answers lands on a cell nobody defends, and if so whether that is because the view is incoherent or merely unattractive.
