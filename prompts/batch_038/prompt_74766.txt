I'm preparing a set of worked illustrative examples for an upper-level study guide that accompanies a course on the foundations of game theory, and I need help with one entry in particular. The section it belongs to is titled "When does iterated elimination have a unique answer?", and the surrounding entries deal with strict dominance, where the order of deletion provably makes no difference. The entry I want written concerns the breakdown of that result once weak dominance is allowed. Every treatment I've seen makes the same two moves: it asserts that order-dependence arises, and it demonstrates the fact with a game so degenerate (rows with identical payoff vectors, or players who end up indifferent among everything that survives) that a student cannot tell whether the phenomenon reflects anything deep about rational choice or is merely an artifact of a badly chosen example. I would like this entry to be honest about exactly which of those two readings is correct.

To keep the discussion anchored, build the entry around the following stylized three-by-three game, which I've adapted from a mock negotiation exercise we run in section. Three regulatory bodies — a harbor authority, an inland-transport authority, and a border agency — each choose whether to file a joint petition for jurisdiction over a shared cargo-inspection regime (J), file alone and preempt the others (A), or stay out (S). Filing alone secures the whole mandate for the filer but forces the others to withdraw; a joint filing splits the mandate and requires compromises nobody particularly wants. The resulting bimatrix, with the harbor authority as the row player and the border agency as the column player, is:

|  | J | A | S |
|---|---|---|---|
| **J** | 1, 1 | 0, 2 | 1, 0 |
| **A** | 2, 0 | 1, 1 | 2, 0 |
| **S** | 0, 1 | 0, 2 | 0, 0 |

The example is worth the space it takes because it is not degenerate in the usual way: staying out is strictly dominated for the row player (it is beaten outright by filing alone, whatever the column player does), filing jointly is strictly dominated for the column player, and once those two rows and columns are gone the reduced game contains no further strict dominances at all — but it does contain two distinct weak dominances for the column player, since filing alone and staying out each weakly dominate the joint petition, and each does so by tying in a different place. Deleting one of them leaves the row player with a unique choice; deleting the other leaves her with a different unique choice; and, because the two weakly dominated strategies are not payoff-equivalent in the reduced game, deleting both instead leaves a set with two surviving profiles rather than one. Three different orders of elimination, three different answers, all of them legitimate by the letter of the definition.

What I want from the entry, then, is the following. First, walk through the game as above and lay out the three elimination orders explicitly, so that the order-dependence is exhibited rather than asserted. Second, diagnose where the indeterminacy actually comes from: is it the permission to delete weakly dominated strategies as such, or is it the presence of ties in the payoff structure, and what does the distinction tell us about the justification for iterated dominance in the first place — specifically, whether the argument is that a rational player would never choose a dominated strategy, or the stronger claim that a rational player could not coherently *believe* an opponent would choose one, and whether the weaker of those two claims is enough to license the deletion of strategies that merely tie. Third, bring in the familiar worry, associated with Brandenburger among others, that a mixed strategy may be read either as a deliberate randomization device the player physically uses or as a description of what her opponents believe she will do, and explain whether that ambiguity matters here — whether reading mixtures the epistemic way rescues iterated weak dominance, leaves it untouched, or makes the situation worse. Fourth, state what the honest verdict on the game is: whether it shows that iterated weak dominance fails to be a well-defined solution concept, or that it is well-defined but simply does not single out a unique prediction, and what a student should conclude about using it as an argument that some profile will be played. Please write this as a self-contained study-guide entry of roughly 1,200 words, with clear internal headings, and with no citations or reference list; referring to positions and arguments by name is fine, since I will add the bibliography myself.
