I've been reading Berkeley's *Querist* on the bus for a seminar on eighteenth-century Irish political economy, and I'm stuck in the middle of Part I. The seminar keeps saying "Berkeley thought money was just a ticket or a counter" and then moving on, but I can't see how that squares with several of the other things he says two queries later — and I can't tell whether I'm misreading the 18th-century idiom or whether he's actually being loose. Here's the block I'm wrestling with (Query 5, then Queries 21–26, numbered as in the edition I have):

> 5 Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?
>
> 21 Whether other things being given, as climate, soil, etc., the wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?
>
> 22 Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?
>
> 23 Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?
>
> 24 Whether the value or price of things be not a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty?
>
> 25 Whether the terms crown, livre, pound sterling, etc., are not to be considered as exponents or denominations of such proportion? And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?
>
> 26 Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?

So, help me get inside this. What is "exponent" doing in 25 — is that a mathematical term of art for him, and how is it different from the "denomination" it's paired with? Query 24 looks to me like a price equation (price rises with demand, falls with plenty), and 22 looks like a claim about velocity, which would be oddly early; am I allowed to read it that way, or is that just me importing later stuff? More importantly: in 23 he explicitly refuses to call money a standard or a measure, and then 24–25 seem to make the money-names the very things that express a ratio — isn't that a measure? What exactly is he rejecting in each of "commodity, standard, measure, pledge", and especially in "pledge" — if money isn't a pledge, does he have any room left for saving or hoarding, or is that precisely what he wants to deny for Ireland? I also keep wanting to connect the ticket/counter line to his views on language (words as counters and signs in the Alciphron dialogue, and the anti-abstract-ideas stuff in the Principles' Introduction): is money for him a linguistic device, and is that connection real or just a cute one an undergrad would make? Finally, is 26 a metaphysical claim about what money is, or a policy proposal about paper credit and the bank scheme, or both at once — and how much weight does the whole "Whether ... be not ...?" form carry, i.e. should I treat these as assertions at all?

Don't bother pointing me at sources or telling me what to read next, I have the text in front of me. I just want someone to work through the passage with me carefully, flag where I'm over-reading, and give me the most defensible way to state what Berkeley is actually committed to by the end of Query 26.
