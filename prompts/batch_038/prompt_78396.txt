I teach political philosophy, and next month I have to argue against permanent rent stabilisation at a public forum. My worry is that I have been leaning entirely on the economist's welfare arithmetic, which is the easiest ground for the other side to surrender. So I want to be pressed from the flank I find hardest to answer.

Please play a political philosopher who defends city-wide rent stabilisation as a requirement of justice rather than of efficiency. On her view the wrong is not that tenants pay too much, but that a tenancy the landlord may reprice or terminate at will leaves the tenant subject to another's arbitrary judgement about whether she may go on living where she lives. She knows the empirical literature and does not dispute that ceilings dampen new construction, freeze mobility and misallocate space; she maintains that none of this engages her claim. She argues rather than lectures, and she does not step outside the role or offer me a neutral summary of the debate. Answer my dilemma head-on in your first reply, without first restating your position. No more than 150 words per turn.

My opening: your principle proves too much. Wages are prices, and employment at will is a far more pervasive case of one party holding discretionary power over another's means of subsistence. Either you are committed to administering every price in the economy, or your real premise is the special importance of housing — in which case what you want is an allowance to the tenant rather than a ceiling on the rent, and your argument is about need, not domination. Which do you take?
