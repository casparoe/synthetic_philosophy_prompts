I spent 38 years in a water-quality lab, mostly counting things and then arguing with people about whether the counting was any good, and I've only been reading philosophy seriously since I retired, so keep that in mind. Tuesday nights I go to a discussion group at the public library — fourteen or fifteen of us, mostly retirees. Last month somebody brought in a piece about that experiment where a company's boss is told a new program will raise profits but also damage the environment, and he says he doesn't care about the environment one way or the other, he just wants maximum profit. They run it, profits go up, environment gets damaged, and something like 82 percent of people asked say he damaged it intentionally. Swap damage for benefit — identical indifference, identical profit motive — and only about 23 percent say he benefited it intentionally.

We actually ran a crude version of it ourselves that night. Marta (family lawyer) read out three cards and we wrote down yes or no. Card one was the damage case. Card two was the benefit case. Card three was one I helped her make up: the program will raise profits and also clean up the river downstream, but the filtration gear is expensive and eats into the margin, and the boss says he doesn't care about the river either way, fine, he'll eat the cost, he wants the profit. Results, in order: 11 yes, 3 yes, 6 yes.

Then Marta and Dev (who spent his career designing surveys) went at it for close to an hour and I couldnt get a word in edgewise. As near as I can reconstruct it:

(1) Marta: this result kills the standard picture where you first settle the descriptive facts and only then evaluate them. Here the evaluation is already inside the fact-finding — whether we call something intentional depends partly on whether it was bad. That's not a glitch in the instrument, that's how the concept works.

(2) Dev: it's the question format. Hand somebody a bare yes/no question about a man who just wrecked the environment and "yes" is the cheapest available way of registering that he's blameworthy. Ask it differently — make them pick between several descriptions of what he did, or rate agreement on a scale — and the gap shrinks a lot. That's people using a word loosely in conversation, not people having a moralized concept.

(3) Marta: loose usage would be loose in both directions. If "yes" just means "I disapprove," the same conversational slack should let people say yes in the benefit case to register approval. It doesn't. A bias that only runs downhill isn't slack, it's structure.

(4) Dev: pragmatics doesn't have to be symmetric — communicating blame is worth the effort in a way communicating credit isn't, so a purely conversational story predicts a one-way effect all by itself. And your moral reading is in trouble regardless, because the same asymmetry shows up where nothing moral is at stake: the outcome breaks a rule of a card game, or a dress code, or some arbitrary house policy. Nobody is expressing blame there. So the driver is "this went against a norm," which is a much weaker claim than yours.

(5) Marta: weaker, but it's still my claim. "Went against a norm" isn't a descriptive property of an event either. So the ordinary notion of doing something on purpose has a normative component built in, whether the norm is moral or a dress code.

(6) Dev: there's at least one fully descriptive option. In the damage case he accepted a cost in order to get what he wanted, and accepting a cost is what doing something on purpose looks like from outside; in the benefit case the good outcome came free, a byproduct he never paid anything for. And there's the character version: people say intentional when the outcome tells you who the agent is, and being unmoved by damage tells you a great deal about a man while an accidental benefit tells you nothing. Neither of those needs norms or morals.

(7) Marta: your character version is your norm version in different clothes. Which features of a person count as the real him is itself an evaluative call — you don't get to relabel it descriptive just because you stopped using moral vocabulary.

(8) Dev: then settle it with data instead of definitions. That's what card three was for. If this is about accepted costs, making the benefit expensive should push people toward yes. If it's about norm violation or blame, it shouldn't budge. It budged: six of fourteen, up from three.

(9) Marta: six out of fourteen, in a library basement, isn't a result, it's noise. And even taken at face value it moved halfway and no further, which says the cost factor adds to the norm factor rather than replacing it.
