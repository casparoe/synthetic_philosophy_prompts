I am a software engineer. I have no formal philosophy background — no courses, no degree, nothing beyond what I have picked up incidentally — but I do spend my working life inside a vocabulary that I would like to use here, so let me describe it precisely enough for you to calibrate.

I reason about systems as dependency graphs. Modules depend on modules, definitions depend on definitions, and a build fails if the graph contains a cycle. I have written tooling that detects and rejects such cycles. I also know the places where that tooling is too crude: mutual recursion between two definitions is acceptable when it is guarded and terminating, and an infinite data structure such as a stream is acceptable when it is productive — when every finite prefix can be generated on demand — even though the structure as a whole is never completed. Separately, I have used proof assistants enough to know that you can postulate an axiom or leave a hole marked as admitted, that the kernel accepts it without protest, and that everything downstream then inherits its trustworthiness; and I know that an inconsistent postulate is not flagged at the moment you add it, but only later, when a large portion of the development collapses.

The subject I want translated is the epistemic regress problem and its three standard responses: foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism (the last defended in recent analytic epistemology chiefly by Peter Klein). My rough reading — correct me where it is wrong — is that foundationalism holds that the graph must bottom out in nodes requiring no incoming edges, coherentism holds that cycles are permissible provided they are of the right kind, and infinitism holds that the descent may continue for ever provided it behaves well. I have also encountered a case, not unlike a lint rule I once wrote that flagged mutually corroborating test suites as a dependency cycle, in which two bodies of evidence support one another and yet the arrangement does not feel defective to me; I do not know whether the philosophers have a name for that intuition or whether it is simply an error.

Please produce the following, in British English.

(a) A faithful translation of the three positions, and of the principal arguments deployed between them, into the vocabulary above. Address in particular whether the regress concerns chains, general graphs, or something my graphs cannot represent at all — and how the sceptical version of the argument is meant to work: is the sceptic claiming that no admissible graph exists, or that the three admissibility criteria are jointly unsatisfiable?

(b) An assessment of one specific hypothesis. I suspect that infinitism is not an infinite loop but corecursion: that what the infinitist requires is productivity — arbitrarily long finite prefixes, available on demand — rather than an actually completed infinite traversal, so that the dispute between infinitism and coherentism is roughly the dispute between guarded descent and cycles. Tell me whether that mapping holds up, or whether I am bending the philosophy to fit my tools.

(c) A residue report. Sort the significant claims in the debate into three classes: those that translate cleanly; those that translate only with distortion, stating exactly what you had to bend; and those that cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all.

(d) One adjudication I particularly want. My framework has no way to express a node that justifies itself other than a self-loop, which my linter rejects as trivially circular. Foundationalism therefore appears not merely distorted but inexpressible. Is that because foundationalism genuinely amounts to a self-loop, so that the circularity charge against it is simply correct? Or because my vocabulary is too thin — it has no category for a node whose status is not conferred by its edges at all?

(e) A declaration of everything you had to smuggle in. Where my vocabulary hands you an assumption for free, flag it rather than passing it off as translation: for instance, that dependencies are checked statically rather than evaluated on demand; that the graph contains no agent who must be able to traverse it; that edges are syntactic dependencies rather than normative relations; or that my framework draws no distinction between a reason merely existing and a reason actually being used by someone.

(f) A verdict. Does the residue indicate that my framework is missing something, or that the three-way dispute is less substantive than it appears — perhaps three admissibility criteria for the same graph, differing only in which failure modes they treat as disqualifying? Say which, and say what evidence would settle the question.

Two constraints. Do not give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations; where you need to refer to a philosopher or a work, do so only if you are confident of the attribution, and otherwise describe the position instead. And wherever you are reconstructing rather than reporting, mark it as such.
