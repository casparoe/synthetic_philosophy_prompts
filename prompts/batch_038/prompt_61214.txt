I am the clinical lead for a regional breast-screening programme: roughly forty thousand women a year, twelve readers, and a workflow in which every set of mammograms is read independently and in the blind by two of them, with any discordant pair — one recalls, one does not — going to a third reader for arbitration. I trained as a clinician and have no academic background in philosophy; I came to this material by accident, through an argument at a book group that turned into something I could not put down, and I have spent the last year convinced that a large share of what my colleagues argue about in arbitration meetings is not about the images at all. It is about what a reader is supposed to do once she learns that someone equally qualified looked at the same image and came out the other way. I have since found that this has a name in the epistemology literature — higher-order evidence — and that there is a related puzzle that describes our arbitration room almost exactly: cases where what the evidence about your own reliability tells you to believe conflicts with what the thing in front of you tells you to believe, so that the correct verdict requires you to hold a belief you yourself judge unsupported. I have read that some philosophers think such a state is straightforwardly irrational and that a rational agent cannot be in it, and others that it is precisely what the situation demands, and I cannot work out which of those two pictures our forms should be designed around. Our own service adds three wrinkles that I have not seen discussed anywhere. First, we are piloting an arrangement in which an algorithm performs the first read and a human the second, and I have no settled view of whether a dissent from a machine is a peer dissent, a faulty-instrument reading, or a category of its own — the answer changes what a disagreement even means. Second, our readers' own re-reads of the same films months later disagree with their original reports often enough to be measurable, which means each reader is also a disagreeing colleague of her own past self. Third, we know that certain states — the end of a long list, a week with an unusual run of recalls, being interrupted mid-case — impeach a reader generally rather than about any particular film, and at present we have no way of handling that except by hoping.

What I want is not an essay but something I can print on one side of an A4 sheet and staple into the arbitration folder: a decision procedure a reader can run in the two or three minutes between cases, with no philosophy training, no arithmetic, and no access to anything she does not already have in front of her. It should proceed in ordered steps, and each step should say what the reader must be able to state before she may move on to the next, what she does if she cannot state it, and which way the step defaults. It must answer, in whatever order the logic requires: when learning of a disagreement obliges a reader to move and when she may stay where she is; how to tell a genuine disagreement about the film from two readers using different thresholds or different words for the same thing; how much weight a machine's dissent carries and on what conditions that weight changes; what a reader's own past dissent counts for, given that she cannot interview her former self; and how to handle the general impeachers, where nothing has gone wrong on any particular case but something has plainly gone wrong with her. It must terminate either in one of our three real actions — recall, no recall, escalate to a named senior clinician — or in an explicit handover of the question to a person, and it must be clear which of the terminations are matters of evidence and which are matters of policy that no reader should be settling alone. Above all, I want the steps at which the procedure will require someone to report a confidence she does not feel, or to sign a decision she thinks the image contradicts, marked as such and justified or removed; if the honest answer is that some akratic output is unavoidable, then say so, and tell me whether our documentation should record the reader's private first-order judgement alongside the arbitrated one or deliberately suppress it. Then tell me what you had to discard from the higher-order-evidence debate to make this runnable, what that discarding costs us in accuracy, and where the procedure will misfire at volume — the failure modes I should expect over forty thousand cases a year, and the conditions under which I should take it out of service.

Finally, run it on three cases, showing the steps rather than just the verdicts. One: two readers disagree about a subtle architectural distortion, and the arbitrator — who is at the end of a nine-hour list — sides with the reader who called it normal, so the woman is not recalled; two years later the cancer presents clinically. Two: a reader is confident of a finding and has written it up, the algorithm flags nothing, and a senior colleague mentions in passing that she thinks this reader has been over-calling all month; the reader does not feel she has been over-calling. Three: on a case already closed as no recall, a reader's own second reading six months on flatly contradicts her original report, and she is the only person who will ever know. At least one of these should come out in a way I will dislike and would be tempted to overrule from my office; tell me which one, whether overruling would be defensible, and what I should change about the procedure if it is not.
