I spent thirty-five years describing marine gastropods, and since retiring I have taken up the philosophy of biology. There is a pattern I watched unfold across my entire working life for which I have no name, and I should say at the outset that a label alone will not satisfy me; I want to know where its edges lie.

The pattern runs as follows. Take a genus of shore snails worked over since the nineteenth century, with a list of eleven species stable for decades. Then protein electrophoresis arrives, and the same jars in the same museums yield fourteen. Then mitochondrial sequencing, and the count falls to eight under a new arrangement. Then genome-wide markers, and it rises again — to a different set, not to fourteen. The specimens have not changed. At each step the analysis is internally sound, the authors competent, and nobody claims the earlier work was mistaken, only that it interrogated a different slice of the organisms. The same drift occurs on the morphological side: a monograph's lumped species is split once geometric measurement replaces the identification key. What troubles me most is that one cannot say which list is correct without first declaring which kind of evidence is entitled to decide, and that declaration is precisely what the comparison of evidence was supposed to deliver.

Two neighbours I want held apart from it. Conservation authors write of taxonomic inflation, but that seems to concern counts rising when subspecies are promoted for practical reasons; what I describe includes counts falling, and cases where every motive is immaculate. And I gather philosophers maintain that several competing species concepts are equally legitimate — yet that is a thesis about concepts, whereas mine is about a measurable drift in the output of a discipline as its instruments change.

Is there an established term for this, and if so how exactly is it marked off from those two neighbours and what would fail to be an instance of it? If there is none, please coin one, define it, and tell me which of my cases fall outside it — in particular whether it extends to bacterial systematics, where the organisms may have had no fixed boundaries to begin with.
