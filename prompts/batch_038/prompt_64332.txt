I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and once a year my editor makes me host one of the onstage conversations at a festival we're a media partner of — thirty minues, mixed crowd, about half of them work in tech and the other half would rather hear about octopuses. This year my guest is a philosopher of mind who has become the person AI people cite when they want to say something interesting about large language models without ever using the word consciousness. Her thing is Dennett's four kinds of creatures, the rungs of his generate-and-test tower: Darwinian (natural selection did the designing), Skinnerian (reinforcement history did it), Popperian (the creature has an internal model of its world and can kill off bad options in simulation instead of in the world), and Gregorian (the creature picks up mind tools from outside itself — language, writing, maps, money, the whole cultural toolkit, named after Richard Gregory). She reads the rungs as tracking where the design information comes from, not how smart or how conscious a creature is, and from that she draws what I think is her most interesting and most attackable claim: the Popperian rung is optional. A system can arrive at Gregorian directly by ingesting the sedimented results of millions of Popperian creatures' hypothesis-testing, which is roughly what pretraining on the internet does — you inherit the tools without ever having had to grow the simulator that generates and vets them. She's also written a bunch arguing that cephalopods are genuinely Popperian but constitutionally incapable of going Gregorian, and I want to use that against her, because if the ordering isn't fixed for octopuses she owes me a reason it is fixed for models.

Can you draft eight to ten questions for me? Not the softballs — nothing about what drew her to philosophy, and nothing she can answer with a charming anecdote and move on, because she is very good at that and will do it if you let her. Each question should hook onto something specific in the view as I've described it, and for each one give me the answer she's most likely to give in a line or two, plus a follow-up keyed to exactly that answer rather than to some other answer she might have given. I want coverage of at least these pressure points: the fact that RLHF is literally operant conditioning, so her Gregorian-first systems have a Skinnerian basement and no Darwinian one at all — no metabolism, no death, nothing the system is for — and whether the tower can float free of its bottom rung; whether her taxonomy has any discriminating power, i.e. can she state criteria that would demote a model to merely Skinnerian, and name a non-octopus case of Popperian-but-never-Gregorian; the slide between two readings of the rungs, a descriptive taxonomy of information sources and a scale of cognitive achievement, since she tends to reach for whichever one blunts the objection in front of her; the point that Gregorian creatures adopt and set down external scaffolding, whereas a model arguably is the scaffolding and can't put language down; the difference between having a simulator and having absorbed other creatures' simulations; and the consequence she may or may not want, namely whether Gregorian standing carries any moral status with it, and if not, why the taxonomy stops cashing out exactly there.

Format it as cue cards I can actually read off a lectern while a technician points a light in my face: numbered, question in bold-ish plain text, then likely answer, then follow-up. At the end, tell me which two of these have to land for the conversation to be worth anything, which one is closest to being a strawman or a cheap gotcha so I can decide whether to cut it, and which one she's most likely to charm her way out of — with a note on how to steer back. And if you think there's an obvious question a well-read audience member would shout from the third row that I've missed, add it as a bonus with the reply I'd want her to give.
