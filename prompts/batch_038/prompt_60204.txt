For the past year I have been sitting on a citizens' working group drafting the franchise provisions of a charter for a new cross-border authority — call it the Valley Authority — which will regulate public transport, wastewater, and drinking-water abstraction across a river basin that straddles two states that have never shared any institutions. The assembly we are designing will have real teeth: it can impose a payroll levy on employers operating in the basin, cap abstraction upstream, and set fares and service levels. Our chair keeps remarking that we have stumbled into the old problem of who constitutes the demos without any of the usual shortcuts, since there is no prior constitutional practice here to borrow from and nationality is split down the middle of the territory. My colleagues and I have been trading arguments for months with no convergence, so I did something different: I stopped arguing and simply wrote down my own verdicts on concrete cases, on the assumption that my intuitions are probably more orderly than my reasoning. What I want from you is the principle those verdicts add up to, if there is one.

1. A third-country national who has lived and worked in the basin for twenty-two years, on whose wages the payroll levy is collected, and who has never held the nationality of either contracting state: votes.
2. A national of one contracting state who owns a second home in the basin, spends roughly six weeks a year in it, votes in national elections at home, and has no other connection to the place: does not vote.
3. A sixteen-year-old resident who works full time in the basin and whose school and job are both served by the Authority's buses: votes. A nine-year-old resident: does not vote.
4. That nine-year-old's parents get no additional votes and no proxy vote in her name, even though she will live with the Authority's water and climate decisions for five decades longer than they will.
5. A resident serving a ten-year sentence for aggravated assault in a prison outside the basin who will come home afterwards: votes. A resident convicted of buying ballots in the Authority's own first election: loses the franchise for the duration of the sentence and five years beyond it. I am at peace with that asymmetry and cannot account for it.
6. The basin's largest employer, a corporation whose discharge permits the Authority controls and on whose continued presence the entire regional economy depends: does not vote, notwithstanding that any given decision affects it more than it affects any individual.
7. A person who lives inside the basin, has his own well, drives his own car, has no children in school, and is untouched by anything the Authority does: votes anyway.
8. A town forty kilometres downstream, outside the charter's territory, whose drinking-water intake sits below the Authority's release gates: ought to have some vote, though I cannot say with what weight. But the same reasoning reaches everyone along the estuary and the fishing fleet two hundred kilometres further out — ten million people — and I am not willing to give them votes.
9. A commuter who lives outside the basin in one of the contracting states, rides the Authority's trains daily, and has the levy deducted at source: votes. This sits badly with case 2, since both are non-residents with an economic interest in the basin.
10. A rotational guest worker on a three-year contract, fully subject to the Authority's regulations while present and always intending to leave: votes while resident, and loses the vote the day he departs. I accept that cliff edge even though the day before and the day after look morally identical to me.
11. A lifelong resident with advanced dementia who no longer recognizes the basin or forms any view about how it should be run: does not vote. Where in the course of decline the franchise should lapse I genuinely do not know, and that vagueness bothers me far less than it probably should.

My working principle, such as it is, is that the vote belongs to whoever lives under the Authority's rules for the long haul and cannot realistically exit them — which I recognize says nothing precise about either long haul or exit. So: state the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best reproduces these eleven verdicts, and state it precisely enough that I could hand it to a colleague and get the same answer on a case neither of us has seen; identify for each clause which numbered verdict it is doing work for, and whether it is ultimately tracking who is affected or who is coerced. Then tell me plainly which of my verdicts no reasonably simple principle can save, so that I know which intuition to abandon rather than which epicycle to add — I would rather drop one verdict than end up with a five-clause formula nobody can apply. Please pay particular attention to the pairing of 6 with 7, and of 8 with 9, and say what your account yields for two questions we must rule on next month: whether the Authority's own civil servants, who are subject to its rules more continuously than anyone, get votes in the elections that govern them, and whether a neighbouring basin that expects to accede in five years should have observers with voting rights in the meantime. If the honest answer is that my list is a grab-bag with no principle behind it, say so directly, and tell me which three verdicts I should reverse to get closest to a coherent view of the boundary.
