I am giving a twenty-minute talk to an undergraduate philosophy-of-science society in a few weeks on whether special relativity has anything to contribute to the free will debate, and I have built the talk around the argument usually credited to Rietdijk and Putnam in the mid-to-late 1960s. Rietdijk's 1966 paper in Philosophy of Science announces a rigorous proof of determinism derived from the special theory, and Penrose pressed the same line with the example now known as the Andromeda paradox, in which two pedestrians pass on the street. My reconstruction goes like this. A pedestrian walking briskly toward Andromeda, some 2.5 million light years away, has a plane of simultaneity tilted with respect to mine by an amount that grows with distance, so that the stretch of Andromedan history she counts as present runs roughly three or four days ahead of the stretch I count as present; at the Andromedan event lying on her plane, place a second observer moving with respect to her, whose own plane of simultaneity cuts back through the Earth at a moment falling in what I call my future; and since, on the standard reading of the theory, no inertial frame is physically privileged, the events on her plane are as real as the events on mine, and his as real as hers. Chaining these steps, the event of my accepting or declining a job offer three weeks from now is real now, in precisely the sense in which the events of last Tuesday are real. I have drawn the two tilted planes on a Minkowski diagram, which will be the only piece of geometry the talk assumes.

I take it this is what makes the argument worth a talk rather than a paragraph: it transfers the fixity that everyone concedes to the past onto the future, so that the fatalist no longer needs any premise about truth, bivalence, or foreknowledge and can claim the result from the structure of spacetime alone. People sometimes say that the argument at most shows the future exists, not that it is settled, but I cannot see any daylight between those two claims. What exists can no more be made not to exist than what has happened can be made not to have happened, so existence and fixity come apart only verbally. A physicist friend who read my slides told me that this is exactly where I have gone wrong, that I have run together two claims that merely sound alike, and that once they are separated the talk collapses into a restatement of the old fatalist puzzle with a spacetime diagram attached to it. She declined to say more than that, which is why I am asking you.

So, granting the reconstruction above: what is actually left for my compatibilist opponent, and where should I expect the room to push back? Does she have to deny that the future is real, deny that quantum theory is genuinely indeterministic, or concede that deliberation is a kind of theatre in which the choice is an accompaniment to the outcome rather than a cause of it? And which of the familiar replies to fatalism are genuinely closed off by the fact that my version rests on empirical physics rather than on logic or theology — I mean Aristotle's sea battle and the denial of bivalence for future contingents, and the Ockhamist treatment of divine foreknowledge as a soft fact about the present? Two smaller matters I would like your view on as well. First, I currently dismiss the objection that in relativity the present is a purely local notion which cannot be extended to a global hyperplane, on the grounds that what Mermin calls in It's About Time "the single most important lesson to be learned from relativity," namely that no inherent meaning can be assigned to the simultaneity of distant events, is a point about what can be known and signalled rather than about what exists, and so cannot touch an argument whose conclusion is ontological; tell me whether that dismissal is as safe as I think it is. Second, half the room will have done first-year mechanics but nobody will have seen a Minkowski diagram before, so if the argument cannot be repaired in the form I have given it, I would like to know what I should put in its place that still earns the twenty minutes.
