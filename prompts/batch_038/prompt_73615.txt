I'm a PhD student in philosophy of language putting together a handout for a seminar on the semantics/pragmatics interface, and what I want is a decision tree that sorts *pieces of communicated content*, not a survey of who said what. Roughly eight to ten leaves, built out of the logical space first: what's part of the sentence's semantic content, enrichment that has to happen for the sentence to say anything at all, generalized conversational implicature, particularized conversational implicature, conventional implicature, presupposition, indirect-speech-act force, implicature that some people argue is really part of what is said — and then note which combinations come out empty and diagnose each one (incoherent, just unattractive, or nobody's bothered).

Every branch node has to be one of the actual diagnostics: cancellability, both the explicit and the contextual kind, calculability, nondetachability, reinforceability, whether the content survives being stuffed under negation or a conditional or an attitude verb, whether it's tied to a lexical item with an obvious semantic alternative, plus anything else you think earns its place. Phrase each node as a question you could answer about an utterance without knowing which leaf it's steering you toward, and then push at least five test utterances through the whole tree and show the path — I want a scalar one ("some of the students passed"), one with "even", one with "but", a cold-room style indirect request, and a case where the sentence doesn't express a complete proposition until you've done some pragmatic work on it.

Be upfront about the node pairs your tree can't really separate, and about the diagnostics that disagree with each other on the same utterance. On that last point I don't want a menu: commit to which test you'd trust most when they conflict, say why, and if you think one of the leaves I listed is bogus, cut it and tell me where it goes. I lean toward thinking a good chunk of what gets called conversational implicature is really part of what is said, so push back on me if that's wrong. American English, please.
