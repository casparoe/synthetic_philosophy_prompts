I'm auditing a foundations-of-QM seminar and the running arguement in our group is between a many-worlds person and a Bohmian; we had it out at the pub after last week's session and it never actally got anywhere, so I'd like to see what a properly-run version looks like. Write a dialogue between two such people — call them Nadia (Everettian, unitary quantum mechanics only, no collapse, no hidden variables, decoherence does the work of defining branches) and Elias (Bohmian, particles with definite positions guided by a universal wavefunction) — and make both of them genuinely sharp. No strawmen: neither should get to win by smugly pointing out that the other's view is just the same formalism with different bookkeeping, neither should lean on consciousness or on "the measurement problem is a pseudo-problem," and please don't let either one escape into GRW or any dynamical-collapse model — our seminar leader keeps trying to do that and it derails everything.

Set it concretely instead of in the abstract. Have them start from one specific experiment they're both trying to account for: a spin-1/2 particle prepared in sqrt(2/3)|up> + sqrt(1/3)|down> along z, which goes through a Stern-Gerlach plus macroscopic amplification, so that by the end you have two non-interfering branches and a bettor who gets paid if the outcome was up. Walk through what each theory says is really happening there — where the particle is, what the other branch amounts to, how the 2/3 frequency arises — and then let them fight about whether the Born rule is a theorem or a smuggled-in extra postulate on each side. I want the probability stuff to be the load-bearing part: Nadia pressing Elias on quantum equilibrium (why should the initial configuration of the universe be typical, and isn't the typicality measure just the Born rule wearing a disguise?), and Elias pressing Nadia on why a rational agent should weight branches by squared amplitude instead of counting them equally, and whether the decision-theoretic and self-locating-uncertainty style arguments actually derive anything or just restate the answer they wanted.

They should respond to each other's real points rather than trade prepared speeches — each should land at least one remark the other finds genuinely uncomfortable, and each should try to pick up the other's best argument and turn it around. Have them close by agreeing on exactly one thing: the sharpest crux they can name, the single question that would actually distinguish the two views — and then immediately bickering about whether that crux is answerable even in principle, given that Bohmian mechanics reproduces the usual statistics whenever quantum equilibrium holds and only seems to diverge if you're willing to speculate about non-equilibrium situations nobody has ever prepared. Around 1200–1800 words. And keep the physics honest: whenever one of them makes a technical claim about decoherence, the preferred basis, nonlocality, Bell's theorem, or empirical equivalence, it should be one a working foundations person would recognise as correct.
