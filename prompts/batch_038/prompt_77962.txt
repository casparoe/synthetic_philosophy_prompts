Our bioethics reading group put together an argument that seems to demolish the therapy/enhancement distinction, and half of me is thrilled while the other half is sure we cheated somewhere. Can you find the cheat?

Setup: my friend insists the baseline has to be purely statistical — normal functioning means statistically typical functional efficiency for a reference class, with no ideals smuggled in by the doctor. Then:

(1) Medicine's job is therapy: an intervention is legitimate medicine iff it restores the patient to normal functioning; going past normal functioning is enhancement, and enhancement isn't medicine's business.
(2) If "normal functioning" were set by what physicians or society approve of, (1) would just say medicine should do whatever medicine approves of, so the baseline has to be factual — statistically typical functioning for some reference class.
(3) Reference classes aren't handed to us by nature. The same person can be classed by age, sex, birth cohort, neighbourhood, school, diagnosis, or by possession of some trait.
(4) So for any intervention and any outcome it produces, we can name a class in which that outcome is typical: kids who have the implant, adults who took the drug, that district, that generation.
(5) Hence, relative to a suitably chosen baseline, every intervention restores normal functioning — glasses, vaccines, steroids for a healthy sprinter, a memory implant. Nothing counts as enhancement.
(6) So "therapy" and "enhancement" don't mark any difference in the world; (1) classifies everything as therapy or nothing, depending on how you slice the class.
(7) Therefore any objection to enhancement that runs through (1), including the familiar one that enhancement is simply outside medicine's remit, is empty — opponents have to find a wholly different argument or drop the objection.

I'd really like to keep (2), since I don't want the line resting on professional approval, and I'd like (7) to stay a genuine constraint rather than a shrug. I distrust (4) and (5) but can't say whats wrong with them, because the classes in (4) all sound like perfectly ordinary ones you'd use in any statistics class. Which step do you reject or repair, why did it look innocent, and what does each other exit cost? I especially want to know what a defender of (1) can say about those classes without making the baseline ad hoc.

Give me a JSON object with exactly these fields: "breaking_step" (the number of the step where it goes wrong, or "none — valid" if you think it's valid); "flaw_name" (short label for the kind of move that fails); "why_it_looked_fine" (one or two sentences); "steps" (an array with one entry per numbered step 1–7, each {"step", "status" = keep | repair | reject, "note", "cost"} where cost is what the position pays for giving that step up, or "n/a"); "defense_of_1" (the best non-ad-hoc reply available to someone keeping (1), and what it concedes); "kind" (either "invalid — subtle flaw" or "valid — a plausible premise has to go"); "bottom_line" (one sentence verdict on whether the distinction survives).
