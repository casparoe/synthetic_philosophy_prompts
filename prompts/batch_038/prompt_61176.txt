I am auditing a graduate reading group on the epistemology of fundamental physics. We are working through the ninth chapter of Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis* ("Hypotheses in Physics") in the 1905 English translation — the Walter Scott edition with Larmor's preface — and pairing it with the contemporary debate over whether theories like string theory or multiverse cosmology can be supported without novel empirical confirmation. The translation is elegant but slippery, and I want help understanding the opening section, "The Rôle of Experiment and Generalisation," before I commit to a reading in my response paper. Two passages in particular have the group split. The first:

> "It is often said that experiments should be made without preconceived ideas. That is impossible. Not only would it make every experiment fruitless, but even if we wished to do so, it could not be done. Every man has his own conception of the world, and this he cannot so easily lay aside. We must, for example, use language, and our language is necessarily steeped in preconceived ideas. Only they are unconscious preconceived ideas, which are a thousand times the most dangerous of all. Shall we say, that if we cause others to intervene of which we are fully conscious, that we shall only aggravate the evil? I do not think so. I am inclined to think that they will serve as ample counterpoises—I was almost going to say antidotes. They will generally disagree, they will enter into conflict one with another, and ipso facto, they will force us to look at things under different aspects. This is enough to free us. He is no longer a slave who can choose his master."

The second:

> "Thus, by generalisation, every fact observed enables us to predict a large number of others; only, we ought not to forget that the first alone is certain, and that all the others are merely probable. However solidly founded a prediction may appear to us, we are never absolutely sure that experiment will not prove it to be baseless if we set to work to verify it. But the probability of its accuracy is often so great that practically we may be content with it. It is far better to predict without certainty, than never to have predicted at all. We should never, therefore, disdain to verify when the opportunity presents itself. But every experiment is long and difficult, and the labourers are few, and the number of facts which we require to predict is enormous; and besides this mass, the number of direct verifications that we can make will never be more than a negligible quantity."

What I would like, roughly in this order. First, an argument reconstruction of the "preconceived ideas" passage: what are its premises, and what precisely is the conclusion? In particular, what work does the master/slave sentence do — is Poincaré claiming that we neutralise bias by deliberately adopting several mutually conflicting hypotheses (a pluralist or coherence-based safeguard), or only the weaker claim that conscious hypotheses are less dangerous than unconscious ones? One member of the group insists this is a proto-Duhemian point about theories being tested as wholes; I suspect it is narrower, about idealisation and about which background assumptions we are entitled to choose. Tell me which reading the sentences actually support and which clauses decide it.

Second, I cannot make the second passage consistent with the chapter's opening: "Experiment is the sole source of truth. It alone can teach us something new; it alone can give us certainty." If only the observed fact is certain and direct verification reaches a "negligible quantity" of what we predict, in what sense is experiment still the sole source of truth *and* of certainty? Is Poincaré assigning certainty to singular observational reports alone and grading everything else by probability, and if so which notion of probability is in play — the "probability of its accuracy" of a prediction sounds epistemic, yet the whole section is framed in terms of analogous circumstances recurring. Relatedly: the same section invokes Bacon's *experimentum crucis* approvingly while insisting that a fact never repeats and that prediction always requires interpolation. Does that commit him to denying that any single experiment can be decisive, or is his target only the fact-worship he parodies in the John Lackland exchange? Please explain what that parody is doing, and whether its point concerns prediction or the individuation of facts.

Third, and this is what matters for my paper: how much of the current non-empirical confirmation programme can be built out of this text? That programme holds that a theory can gain genuine support without new empirical data — from internal consistency, from explanatory unification, and, most controversially, from the absence of any rival that solves the same problem — which is how its advocates argue for string theory and how some defend the empirical respectability of multiverse cosmology. Which sentences in the two passages give such an argument real leverage, and which cut directly against it? I am especially interested in whether "the first alone is certain" together with the "negligible quantity" remark can be pressed into service as a licence for non-empirical support, or whether they instead presuppose that empirical confirmation is available in principle and merely scarce — a distinction that seems to me to decide whether Poincaré is any use to the defenders of the multiverse at all. Finally, please flag anywhere the 1905 English may mislead me about the French original (I suspect "generalise" and "interpolation" are doing technical work), and be explicit about where you are confident in your reading and where the text genuinely underdetermines it. I do not want a bibliography; I want the reasoning laid out so that I can argue with it.
