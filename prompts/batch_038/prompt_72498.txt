I am giving the opening remarks at our natural history museum's annual benefactors' evening, at which we will formally announce the description of a small insectivorous bird known only from one montane valley and a single adjacent ridge. The species epithet honours a ranger who walked that valley for thirty years and is in the room with his family. The audience will be roughly a third systematists, a third officials from the ministry and the valley's community land trust, and a third donors with no biology at all.

The announcement is contested, and the room knows it. The describers worked under a phylogenetic species concept and diagnosed the population by consistent differences in tail proportion and song; two colleagues who will be sitting in the front row hold that this is precisely the wrong move, because the characters grade across an unsampled stretch of forest and, on any biological species concept, the birds may be conspecific with a widespread relative. The ministry and the land trust are here because our national conservation fund releases habitat money only for taxa formally listed as species, and they have been assured that this one qualifies.

Please write the speech: about four minutes spoken aloud. It must honour real fieldwork, and it must hand the valley's people something they can rely on, without asserting as settled that the taxon is a species in the sense the two camps dispute. Let the underlying disagreement do structural work — deciding what the speech may promise, what it must withhold, and how the word "species" is and is not used — rather than appearing as an aside on the philosophy of biology. Do not dissolve the problem by declaring that species are individuals, or lineages, or useful fictions, or by recommending pluralism as a happy ending; my colleagues have heard all of these and will groan. Close with two sentences I can give a reporter who asks me afterwards whether it really is a species.
