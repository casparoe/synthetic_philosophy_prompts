I am convening the opening week of a graduate seminar on inference in archaeology — specifically, on how one gets from a static material record to claims about past belief, practice, and institutions — and I want to frame the week with a work of fiction rather than a methods paper. The novel I have chosen is Umberto Eco's *The Name of the Rose*. I would like a piece of criticism that reads it as a treatise on archaeological reasoning, and that is willing to say where the analogy breaks.

Some of the material I have in mind, as a check that we are talking about the same book. William of Baskerville arrives at the abbey in 1327 and immediately demonstrates his method by reconstructing the passage, size, and identity of the abbot's escaped horse from tracks in the snow, broken branches, and tufts of hair — before he has seen the animal or spoken to anyone who has. The successive deaths are all read through traces: the blackened tongue and fingers that make poison visible on the body, the armillary sphere beside the herbalist's skull, a blood-stained cloth left in a cell, a body in a barrel of pig's blood whose placement does not match the mechanism of death. The library is an institution legible only from its architecture and its ordering — the words above the doorways, books arranged by region of origin, the rule that only the librarian may enter, the walled-up room behind the mirror. There is an ox heart left on the kitchen floor after Adso's night with the village girl, which the inquisitors, Salvatore, and the monks each read very differently. And there is Bernard Gui's extraction of a confession from the cellarer, which yields an account of all four murders that is complete, coherent, institutionally welcome, and false.

Please address the following, in whatever order makes the argument work best.

First: is William's method actually the archaeologist's method, or does the novel merely borrow detective conventions that happen to look like it? I want the comparison made precise against the real methodological furniture — bridging arguments between statics and dynamics, the warrant for ethnographic and historical analogy, site formation processes and the temptation to read a deposit as a snapshot of ordinary life rather than as an abandonment or catastrophe assemblage.

Second: the library. Treat it as an assemblage from which an institution must be reconstructed — access rules, the sequestration of dangerous knowledge, the transmission of a tacit competence in the labyrinth. Then run the counterfactual that the novel's ending supplies. After the fire, what would an excavator of that abbey, working only from charred debris and standing walls, be able to recover? Would the poisoning mechanism leave any detectable trace at all? Would the catalogue's ordering principle survive, or only the rooms? And is the surviving deposit the kind of thing that licenses inferences about how the library was used?

Third: the apocalyptic schema. William comes to read the deaths as following the sequence of the trumpets of Revelation. I want an account of the epistemic status of that schema, and of the different possibilities the ending leaves open — that the pattern was the killer's design, that it was a coincidence the killer opportunistically adopted as a sign, or that it was substantially an artifact of William's own ingenuity. Then set that failure beside two real controversies I raise with the students: the dispute over whether notches on Upper Palaeolithic bone and antler record lunar cycles or are by-products of butchery and tool-working, and the worry that a sufficiently rich sky and a sufficiently flexible horizon will generate "significant" alignments at any prehistoric monument. Is William's error the same error? What guards, if any, does the discipline have against it, and does the novel endorse a guard or merely punish the man?

Fourth: Bernard Gui. His confession fits every datum and is produced by an institution with a schema already in hand. What, on the novel's own showing, distinguishes a good inference from a well-fitting one — and does the novel have an answer, or does it simply prefer William's temperament to Gui's?

Fifth: the outer frame. An unnamed modern narrator presents a manuscript whose authenticity he doubts, and an aged Adso returns decades later to the ruins to salvage scraps and assemble from them a diminished library. Is Adso's salvage the same epistemic activity as the archaeologist's, or does the frame import a forgery-and-provenance problem that sits uneasily beside the inference problem?

And finally, the honest part: where does the novel resist this reading? It is, after all, a book about signs, texts, riddles, and inscriptions, in which material objects are persistently treated as already text-like — which is precisely what archaeologists deny about their evidence. Does Eco collapse the object/text distinction, and is that a liability for my framing or the point of the book? Is the ending better read as a methodological caution or as something closer to nominalist resignation, in which case I am imposing a discipline's concerns on a novel that has other fish to fry? Close by telling me what the novel contributes to the debate about inference from objects that a methods argument cannot — if it does.

Two requirements about how you handle uncertainty. Please distinguish clearly, throughout, between what is settled among people who work on archaeological method and what is genuinely contested — the reach of analogy, the testability of symbolic and cosmological readings of assemblages, and the status of inference to the best explanation where no independent check is available are live disputes, and I do not want any of them presented to my students as consensus. Equally, flag any point where your reading of the novel depends on a contested interpretation of Eco or on a detail of the plot you are not certain of, rather than smoothing over the gap. I need no quotations from the novel and no bibliography; I am interested in your reconstruction and your judgment, not in sourcing.
