My brother is getting married in September and I've somehow ended up doing the speech. Best woman, village hall in Devon, about a hundred people, four minutes tops because the caterers are strict about the timetable.

Here's the situation, which is why I need something specific rather than the generic thing you'd write for anybody. He's a moral philosopher. He did his DPhil on Bernard Williams and he's spent twenty-odd years arguing that consequentialism asks too much of ordinary people, because it makes them justify their own lives from nowhere. His party piece is that example Williams uses — the man who can only save one of two drowning people and saves his wife, and if he also has to think about whether saving her was the permitted or the better outcome, then he's had one thought too many. He's explained that to me atleast six times, always in someone else's kitchen.

His fiancée runs an outfit that gets cash straight into the hands of poor households in East Africa. Half the room gave away serious proportions of their income partly because he nagged them into it. The other half are her family, who are sheep farmers, plus one uncle who'll be asleep by the second course.

So I want the speech on his side without ever naming him or his side. No Williams, no philosophy, nothing that sounds like a seminar. And here's the crucial bit: I don't want it to justify the marriage. Not that they're perfect for each other, not that she's made him a better man, not a word about how much good the two of them will do together, not even as a wink. Every draft I've tried keeps sliding into reasons, and reasons are precisely the thing he thinks are one thought too many. It needs to sound like it's coming from inside being his sister, which is the only place I actually speak from.

Give me the whole speech, start to finish, in continuous prose the way you'd put it in an email — no headings, no bracketed stage directions, no bullet points, bold or tables, nothing that reads like a plan. British English. One properly funny bit early on, and the last thirty seconds can be sincere, just not wet.
