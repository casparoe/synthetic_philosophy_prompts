An airport-bookstore history of democratic decline I picked up opens with a long chapter on Rome, and its argument goes roughly like this. The Republic ran for centuries on unwritten custom — mos maiorum, deference to the Senate, magistrates who stepped down at the end of their year. There was no written constitution to appeal to, so the norms were the entire load-bearing structure. Then in 133 BC Tiberius Gracchus broke them: he pushed a land reform bill over the veto of a fellow tribune, had that tribune stripped of his office by popular vote, and stood for a second consecutive term, which was flatly unprecedented. Once that happened the norms were gone and there was nothing left to fall back on — the Senate answered by clubbing Gracchus and hundreds of his supporters to death, and political violence became the normal way to settle disputes. Meanwhile Marius had opened the legions to the landless poor, so soldiers now owed their land grants and their futures to their general rather than to the state, and generals like Sulla and Caesar could simply march those armies on Rome. The lesson the author draws: institutions don't save you. The Roman constitution was perfectly serviceable; what killed it was that ambitious men stopped deferring to it, and the same is true of any republic, including ours, which survives only for as long as its politicians choose to restrain themselves.

I found that genuinely persuasive and I already lean toward the norms-over-institutions view myself, but a friend who studied ancient history read over my shoulder, said it was "a just-so story," and wouldn't explain. I don't know enough to tell whether shes being snooty or whether something is actually wrong.
