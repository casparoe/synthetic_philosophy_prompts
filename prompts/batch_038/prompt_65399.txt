I'm co-teaching a grad seminar this term on conceptions of the person, and one of the three assigned strands is the Ames–Rosemont role ethics line. Before I put a certain question to the students I want to run it by you and see whether it actually holds together. My worry is that the debate about role ethics is muddled because everyone argues about it as though it were a single thesis, when there are really at least three that come apart, and you can hold one without the others. Call them: (R1) the constitutive thesis — there's no fact about what a person is that doesn't make ineliminable reference to their roles and relationships, i.e. no residue of "bare personhood" left over once you strip them all away; (R2) the irreducibility thesis — role-obligations can't be derived from or reduced to any role-neutral principle, be it a utility calculus, a categorical imperative, or a list of rights; (R3) the cultivation thesis — moral competence is a practiced skill of role-performance rather than the application of rules. Almost all the heat I see is over R2, which is largely the partiality debate in Sinological dress. The one I find interesting, and the one I want a number on, is R1.

So: give me your credence that R1 is true, stipulated as follows — for any actual human person, every true description of that person which does any explanatory or normative work makes ineliminable reference to at least one of their concrete roles or relationships, and no coherent alternative description dispenses with all of them. Tell me what would count as settling it either way (a worked example of a role-neutral but non-vacuous account of agency? a demonstration that residue talk is empty?). Then decompose it. I'd guess the terms include: how much turns on whether "residue" is even intelligible, such that R1 might win by default for want of a coherent rival; whether the classical materials actually back it up — the Mencian sprouts and the stranger watching a child about to fall down a well look role-neutral to me, though someone could argue that commiseration is itself a capacity grown inside role-life, whereas the sheep-stealing father passage in the Analects and the thought experiment about Shun throwing away the empire to flee with his murderer father on his back push hard the other way; whether the Mencius/Xunzi split matters here, since Xunzi's picture of ritual as artifice imposed on a nature that resists it seems to presupose exactly the raw material R1 denies; and whether role ethics can handle bad roles and role exit without smuggling in a role-neutral standpoint from which to criticize a role — and if it can't, whether that's evidence against R1's truth or only against its usability. Give a rough probability for each term (add or drop terms as you see fit), show how they combine into your number, say which term the number is most sensitive to, and name the one argument or piece of evidence that would move you furthest.

Two more things. My colleague over in religious studies, who works on ritual, is at something like 0.8 on R1; I'm around 0.3, and I can't tell whether our gap is about R1 itself or about one of the sub-terms — which is it most likely to be? And please don't bother with citations or a bibliography, I'll add the references myself when I write this up for the seminar reader; I care about the structure of the reasoning, not the scholarship. Oh, and if you think the R1/R2/R3 split cuts the joints badly, say so and give me the split you'd use instead — I'm genuinely unsure whether R1 is a substantive metaphysical claim or just a way of talking, which is part of why I want the number broken down in the first place.
