I'm one of two guest editors for a student-run gallery space — literally a glass case in the library hallway, plus a stapled zine — and we're putting together a show/issue on racial justice for spring. Printing budget is like 200 bucks. At planning I raised a worry: if we tell contributors the theme is explicitly political we'll get a pile of illustrated op-eds, and I'll have to cut a gorgeous abstract piece from a friend of mine for being off-theme, and the whole thing gets judged as messaging rather than art. Devon shut that down fast — all art is propaganda anyway, that's the Du Bois line, so there's no 'aesthetic risk' in being on-message, and my worry is just art-for-art's-sake squeamishness wearing a neutral mask. I'm pretty sure something's off in that but I couldn't say what on the spot, and now the contributor guidelines are due and I want to write them myself.

Also relevant: I'm in a seminar where we read some Du Bois on art from the 1920s — the address about what criteria Negro art should meet, I think, though I couldn't tell you the exact title or where it was published — and my memory is that he is *not* just saying go make posters. He seems to be holding several things at once: that art has to be usable for the race's cause; that it also has to be truthful and not airbrush out the ugly parts of Black life; that craft and beauty still matter and that he cares a great deal about how the propaganda is made, not only that it's made; and something like a complaint that the disinterested-pure-art option isn't actually on the menu for Black artists, because white editors and patrons will only fund certain pictures of Black life. All of that gets compressed into one slogan when people repeat it, and I want to un-compress it.

So: lay out the menu of precise claims that slogan could be making, ordered roughly from platitude to radical — everything has political effects whether or not the maker intends it; everything is made in order to persuade; the social position of the maker and the market decide that art functions politically; art ought to be judged by its political consequences; aesthetic value can't be seperated from that function; and since nothing is neutral, refusing to be explicit just defaults you to the dominant group's message. For each one say whether it's true or at least defensible, and whether it's a claim about all art or only about art made by people whose existence is politically contested — I suspect those come apart a lot. Then the part I care most about: which of them Devon actually needed in order to license her conclusion that being on-message carries no aesthetic cost. My hunch is that none do, and that the ones that are true cut the other way and impose craft demands rather than lifting them, but show me where I'm wrong. Don't invent quotes or page numbers for the Du Bois material — if you're reconstructing his view from the shape of the argument rather than recalling it, say so explicitly and mark your confidence. And be opinionated about which readings you think hold up rather than treating the whole menu as equally charming. If you think my framing is off — like if I'm the one smuggling in a Kantian premise about disinterested judgment and Devon's basically right — just say that plainly instead of humoring me.
