I am a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on the *Untimely Meditations*, and I am currently drafting my term paper on the second of them, the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. Please respond as a specialist in Nietzsche's early writings, and think the problem through rather than summarizing it: I would like you to begin with a short brainstorming section in which you steelman the reading I sketch below, and only then move to your assessment of it.

My thesis is that the three species of history Nietzsche distinguishes — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — differ principally in their attitude toward the truth about the past. Monumental history is the one that knowingly trades truth away: it generalizes, works by analogy and something like poetic license, cares about effect rather than cause, and treats the past chiefly as a storehouse of exemplars. The other two, on my reading, are truth-respecting in opposite directions. Antiquarian history respects the truth by conserving everything that was, refusing to rank or edit what it inherits. Critical history respects it by passing judgment on the basis of what actually happened, which makes it the mode that keeps the other two honest. That is also why the triad looks progressive to me, running from the naive edification of the monumental, through the pious hoarding of the antiquarian, to the self-conscious tribunal of the critic. On this account the historical malady is essentially the dominance of the monumental mode, and critical history is Nietzsche's proposed corrective to it.

Given that, here is my question. If critical history is the mode that disciplines the other two, what does Nietzsche's account imply about how an individual researcher should allocate her time among them — is the right default the critical one, with monumental and antiquarian impulses admitted only as instruments? And what would that arrangement look like as a working method rather than a mere attitude toward one's sources?

Two requests about how you answer. First, please be explicit about how confident you are in each claim you make and about where the interpretive literature is genuinely divided; I would rather have hedged, argued claims than a confident-sounding gloss. Second, and more important: if my thesis rests on a misreading, say so plainly at the outset and explain the misreading, instead of working politely inside my framework. I want to write something defensible rather than something agreeable, so if the question cannot be answered well as posed because its premise is wrong, telling me that directly is the most useful thing you can do.
