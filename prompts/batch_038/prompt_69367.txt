I sit on the board of a small charter school and our faculty adviser dropped "the right is prior to the good" twice on Tuesday, both times to end a discussion rather than to make an argument.

First use: we were voting on making 40 hours of community service a graduation requirement, and the people proposing it said out loud that the point isn't better test scores or college admissions, it's producing graduates who are the sort of person who shows up for their neighbours. He said that's perfectionism and the slogan rules it out — you can't justify a requirement by pointing at a vision of the good life.

Second use, maybe twenty minutes later: someone objected to letting a church group rent the gym on Wednesday nights because they'd be inculcating their view of the good in kids who wander in, and he said the exact same sentence, this time to defend letting them in.

He also waved off a survey about what actually happens at schools that already require service hours, saying that since it's a question of principle the data is beside the point.

So: can you lay out a menu of the precise claims that one sentence could be standing for, seperated by what kind of priority is in play — definitional, justificatory, something about which consideration wins when they conflict, something about persons and their ends, and any sense I haven't thought of? For each reading say whether it's a platitude that communitarians and perfectionists would happily sign too, whether it's substantive but defensible, or whether it's just false. Then tell me for each one whether it gets him the first conclusion, the second, the dismissal of the survey data, or none of them — and whether any single reading leaves him consistent instead of just using a nice-sounding line as a stop sign. He says it's Rawls's; if the version Rawls actually needs is a particular one of your readings, flag which. A list I can read aloud would be great, i don't want an essay.
