I'm a final year history student and my dissertation is on the Browning/Goldhagen argument about Reserve Police Battalion 101, so I've read both books plus alot of the review symposia and the follow-up literature. Can you take on the role of a philosopher of social science who has also spent proper time on historical method? Please think this through carefully rather than pattern-matching to the obvious answer — start by brainstorming a bunch of candidate names for what I'm about to describe, drawing from measurement theory, philosophy of science and historiography, and only then tell me which of them actually fit and which are red herrings. Be blunt if you think I've mis-described the thing.

Here's the pattern I keep running into and have no word for.

The single most load-bearing fact in the whole debate is that when Trapp told the men before the shooting at Józefów that they could step out if they didn't feel up to it, only a handful of several hundred did, and the ones who did weren't punished or sanctioned afterwards. Both camps accept that fact. But they read it in opposite directions. For Goldhagen, the absence of refusal is precisely the evidence that the men went along willingly, because if it had only been pressure you'd expect a fair number to take an exit that turned out to be free. For Browning, the absence of refusal tells you nothing about what was in their heads, because conformity, routinisation and careerism predict near-total compliance even from men who were privately appalled — which is what the distress and the drinking afterwards are meant to show.

And then that distress gets flipped the same way in the other direction: Browning reads it as evidence the men weren't eager, Goldhagen reads it as perfectly compatible with eagerness, since on his account the upset was about the manner of the killing rather than about the killing itself. Same datum, reversed reading, and no way to get between them.

Because here's the bit that actually bothers me: whenever I try to imagine a third piece of evidence that would settle it, I can't. Anything behavioural gets absorbed by both accounts, since each one says the behaviour is what its own story predicts. And anything about the men's beliefs comes to us through postwar testimony that both sides already agree is distorted for other reasons. So the shape of it is: two competing explanations of a mental state inferred from a single behavioural trace, where each side treats that trace as a clean indicator and the other treats it as a systematically corrupted indicator, and neither can be caught out because whatever turns up is equally predicted by both.

I'm aware philosophy of science has some very general thesis about theories and evidence that sounds adjacent, but that's far too big and too bland to be what I mean. I'm after the specific configuration, where the dispute is over whether the measuring instrument can be trusted and there is no second, independent instrument to check it against.

So: does it have a name? Please don't just hand me a label, that won't help. I want the carving done — which established concepts come nearest, what my case shares with each of them and where it would seperate out, and which one fits best on balance. And if you think nothing fits exactly, say so plainly and coin a term, with a real definition plus boundary cases telling me what would NOT count as an instance (I especially want to know whether ordinary scientific underdetermination and plain unfalsifiability are the same thing or not). No need for a reading list or citations, my bibliography is already enormous — I just want the conceptual work.
