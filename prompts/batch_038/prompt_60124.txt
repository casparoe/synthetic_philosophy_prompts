Spent 34 years building and debugging instrumentation, mostly feedback controllers and data acquisition for a synchrotron beamline, and I only got into philosophy properly since retiring. Which is how I ended up in a long argument about a donkey.

Here is what I want a number on. Not a summary of what people have said, an actual credence of yours, with the working shown.

Proposition: it is possible, metaphysically and not just as a story on paper, for a real embodied agent to be in a situation where (a) two available courses of action are exactly equally good all things considered, (b) the agent knows this and also knows that doing nothing is worse than doing either, and (c) nothing in the agent's own psychology or in the wider world picks out one of the two over the other, so that acting instead of starving is itself a kind of departure from what its reasons support.

Call the denial of that my deadlock thesis: every physically realizable agent is guaranteed a tiebreaker, because there is no such thing as a perfectly symmetric situation at the level of the hardware — thermal noise, stray fields, quantum events, a one-atom difference in the wiring — and whatever the tiebreaker is, it's part of the agent, so the choice is the agents own even when the agent can't give a reason for it. On that view the donkey cannot happen and the literature is about a set of situations with zero measure among real ones. I once had a scheduler deadlock between two identical-priority tasks and the fix was to add a deliberate one-bit asymmetry, which is probably why I find this so compelling. I sit at about 0.8 on the deadlock thesis, so 0.2 on the proposition. The woman in my reading group who actually has the training is around 0.75 on the proposition.

So give me your number, then break it apart: the sub-questions the credence genuinely rests on, a rough probability for each, and how you combine them — multiply, weighted average, minimum, whatever you think is right, and why that rule. Candidates I can think of, and please add or discard as you like:

- wether exact ties in value are possible at all, versus the suggestion I keep running into that two options can be neither better nor worse nor equally good but stand in some fourth relation. If that fourth relation is real, does it help me or hurt me here?
- whether the tie has to be exact to do the work, or whether a tie that is exact relative to the agent's own discrimination threshold generates the same problem
- whether a tiebreaker that is physical noise rather than a reason makes the resulting act less than fully rational, or rational-but-arbitrary, or whether that is a distinction with nothing on the other side of it
- whether consulting a randomizer on purpose — a coin, a number generator — settles the case or just moves it up a level, since you then have to ask why the agent chose to randomize
- whether the words rational and reason have a determinate enough sense here for the proposition to have a truth value at all

Then tell me, for someone deciding whether to change his mind: which single sub-question would move the overall number most and roughly by how much, and where you think the gap between my 0.2 and her 0.75 actually lives, because I suspect we are not even scoring the same term.

Last thing, and it's the part I care most about: say how confident you are in the number you just gave me — not in the topic in general, in that number — and single out the one step in your own reasoning you trust least. If you think my framing smuggles something in, tell me that instead of answering inside it.
