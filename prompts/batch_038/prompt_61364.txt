My brother Tom and his wife Ruth are having a humanist naming ceremony for their daughter Ivy in a village hall near Leeds on the 12th, and they've asked me to give the family address — the celebrant does the welcome, then me for about five minutes, then the actual naming bit where the parents explain the name. So I have to hand back to the celebrant rather than wrap the whole thing up myself.

The difficulty is that their marriage argument has been anthropic reasoning for about eight years. Ruth's a cosmologist, Tom does medical statistics. Ruth's the self-indication one — her line is that the fact you exist should make you more confident there's loads of observers out there. Tom's the self-sampling one — reason as if you'd been drawn at random out of the reference class, so your being you is the bit that needs explaining. As I've got it, he thinks Ivy is a lottery win and she thinks Ivy isn't surprising at all, and I can't see how they're both right about their own daughter, though I'm sure my version is a caricature and I don't want to repeat it back to a room containing two of Ruth's postdocs.

Which is the other problem: the work people are coming. Ruth's supervisor, her postdocs, and a philosopher of physics who once made Tom wear a paper cone at a dinner party for being a presumptuous philosopher about there being trillions of civilisations. The paper cone story has to be in the address because it's the only bit the non-philosophers will enjoy. The rest of the room is my parents, my mum's friends from the church she's left, and six children under ten. I teach year five, so I'm used to explaining things to people who haven't met the words before, but not to people who've written about them.

Constraints, and they matter more than they sound. Ruth has banned the usual stuff: no doomsday argument, no sleeping beauty, no many worlds, none of it. The celebrant has asked for nothing that reads as fate or a plan — no meant to be, no she was meant for us, no the universe arranging things. But I do want to say something true about why this particular child and not just any child is the reason we're all in a village hall on a Sunday, and I'd rather get there through Tom and Ruth's argument than step politely around it. I also think Tom has the better of it, and I don't want to pretend otherwise to keep the peace, but it can't turn into a seminar or a scoring of points — it's got to land as affection.

Can you write the address? Around 700 words, in British English. The cosmologists should be able to hear that I've actually got the distinction right, my mum's friends should be able to follow what's going on without feeling lectured at, the kids shouldn't be bored for more than about a minute, and the last thing I say should be something Ivy could be told in twenty years' time without it turning into sentimental rubbish.
