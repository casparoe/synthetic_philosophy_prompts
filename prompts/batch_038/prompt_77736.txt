I write novels — literary fiction, roughly one every four years — and each August I convene a residential craft workshop for twelve novelists, some published and some not, at a farmhouse in the Scottish Borders. We work for five days, and every evening there is a ninety-minute session that is meant to be doing something other than workshop. Next summer's Thursday evening is mine to design, and I want it to put into these twelve people's hands, rather than their notes, the force of a problem in logic that I have become convinced is the very problem they are arguing about whenever one of them says that a book is untrue.

Here is my understanding of the problem. Treat it as accurate and build on it; do not spend the session correcting me from a distance. There was a serious attempt to define closeness to the truth by comparing the true and the false consequences of two theories, in effect scoring how much truth a theory carries and subtracting how much falsehood it carries. That definition failed, and failed in a peculiar way: it entailed that no false theory can be closer to the truth than any other theory whatsoever, which is precisely the case the definition existed to serve, since the history of inquiry is a history of false theories that nonetheless look like improvements. Two families of repair followed. One keeps the ledger of consequences but restricts what may be entered in it, holding that only the relevant parts of a theory — its claims properly partitioned rather than allowed to be gerrymandered by logical trickery — contribute to closeness to the truth. The other abandons consequence-counting altogether and says that a claim is close to the truth in proportion to how similar the worlds in which it holds are to the actual world. There is a standing argument about whether the two families are even measuring the same quantity, and an impossibility result to the effect that no single measure can satisfy all the intuitive conditions on closeness to the truth at once.

My hypothesis — which the session should test rather than announce — is that readers' judgments about fiction behave like the second family while sounding like the first. When twelve novelists rank two books for truthfulness, they are ranking similarity between an imagined world and this one, under a subject matter nobody has named; and the ranking inverts the moment the subject matter is renamed, or the moment somebody bolsters a book with a true detail that does no work.

What I want is a runnable session, not an essay about one. Specifically:

Rules, materials, and a scoring scheme for a game or structured deliberation that twelve people can actually play in ninety minutes. Stakes should be in a writers' currency — whose line gets cut, who reads aloud, who takes the morning chore — not money.

The construction should be theirs. They should devise or discover the scoring rule and then watch it break, and you should design the break: tell me in advance which exploit will be found, what kind of participant finds it, and how I turn that discovery into the lesson rather than into a grievance.

The materials specification. I will write the prose myself, so tell me what each card or passage pair must be engineered to do — what each must get right, what it must get wrong, where the ranking must flip, and under which change of frame the flip occurs. If it is easier, draft two exemplar pairs so that I can hear the register, but the specification is what I need.

A minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps named moments of the play onto parts of the problem: content against similarity, the restriction to relevant consequences, relativity to a subject matter, and the question of whether the two families are commensurable at all. The name of the problem, and Popper's, arrive in the debrief and not before.

An account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, given that four of the twelve will want to convert everything into a discussion of taste and one will want to litigate the analogy from the first minute.

Finally, a frank statement of what the session cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply verbally — above all, whether the whole design trades on a pun between truth-in-a-fiction and closeness-to-the-truth, and if it does, whether that pun is doing genuine conceptual work or is merely my way of smuggling a metaphor past a room of professional metaphor-users.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, no assigned text before or after. Assume the room has never encountered any of this literature, that it is clever and quick, and that it is violently allergic to being lectured. Write the result as a facilitator's document complete enough that I could hand it to a colleague and she could run the evening in my absence without asking me a single question.
