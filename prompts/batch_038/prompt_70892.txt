I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm working on a piece about whether generations are real kinds or merely marketing categories. Please treat yourself as a philosopher of social science who has also done survey research, and push back on my framing where it is wrong rather than working within it.

Here is how I currently understand the cusp problem. The birth-year boundaries are settled in advance — Boomers 1946–1964, Generation X 1965–1980, Millennials 1981–1996, Generation Z 1997–2012 — so someone born in 1981 belongs to the Millennials and someone born in 1980 belongs to Generation X, full stop. Talk of 'micro-generations' like Xennials and Zillennials is pop-science fudging with no standing in the research. And because membership is determined before any data are collected, it seems to me the categories cannot be the weak link in a finding like 'Millennials are more likely than Boomers to support X': the real threats there are sampling error and question wording. So the cusp years matter for how I write — I shouldn't imply a late-1980 baby is secretly a Millennial — but not for whether a cohort comparison is trustworthy.

Is that right? Before answering, brainstorm the ways a group defined by a stipulated boundary could still go wrong: what the stipulation does and doesn't guarantee, what the boundary is supposed to be tracking, and what else varies alongside birth year when you compare two cohorts at a single point in time. Then tell me precisely where my reasoning breaks, what a defensible worry about cohort comparisons looks like, and what caveats I should carry into the piece.

End with a line beginning "Verdict:" giving, in one short sentence, whether the cusp problem is merely presentational, and naming the single most serious issue you identified.
