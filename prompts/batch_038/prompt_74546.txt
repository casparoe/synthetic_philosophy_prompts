Our decision theory reading group has been chewing on the Pasadena game for six weeks and we haven't moved an inch, and at this point I'm more interested in why we're stuck than in who's right. Quick setup so we're working from the same thing: a fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads; if heads first lands on toss n you get paid (-1)^(n-1) · 2^n/n dollars. So the probability-weighted contributions go +1, -1/2, +1/3, -1/4, ... , which in that order sums to ln 2, but the gains on their own add up to infinity and the losses to minus infinity, so it isn't absolutely convergent and by the rearrangement theorem you can reorder the terms to get whatever number you like, or no number at all. One half the group says this shows expected utility theory is genuinely incomplete and needs surgery — compare acts by the expectation of their difference, or drop completeness and let some pairs go unranked, or bound utility, or restrict the theory to absolutely convergent cases. The other half says it's a toy: no bookie would write that ticket, no agent will ever face it, and the theory declining to rank it is about as alarming as division by zero. A third faction, quieter, says fine — value is gappy, some gambles simply have no expected value, and that's a feature of the world rather than a defect in the theory.

Here's the bit that makes me think we're all answering different questions. A friend of mine who works as a quant came to one of the sessions and built a variant he calls the Antwerp game: same payoffs and same probabilities, except the rounds aren't indexed by our listing convention but by something physically canonical — each payoff is stapled to a successive one-second interval on a sealed tape recording decays from a radioactive source, so there's a determinate causal order to the branches. His argument: a conditionally convergent series with a fixed order does have a determinate sum, so the Antwerp game has an expected value, it's ln 2, and the whole paradox was an artifact of treating the index as arbitrary. The response he got is that expectation is an integral over outcomes, not a sum over an enumeration, and it exists only when the positive and negative parts are separately finite — the canonical order is a red herring, since you can always re-describe the very same physical gamble with the branch labels permuted, and a gamble's value can't depend on how we choose to count its outcomes. My working hypothesis, which I want you to stress-test rather than accept, is that the stalemate isn't about decision theory at all but about which of two mathematical objects the phrase "expected value" is being used to name: an order-sensitive sum over a given sequence, or an order-insensitive integral. If that's right, my friend and the objectors are talking past each other, the toy-case people are answering a third question entirely (what decision theory is for), and the gappy-value people a fourth. But I'm not sure that's the whole story — one person in the group insists something substantive survives the bookkeeping point, because whatever summation rule you adopt has to give verdicts that don't flip when you re-describe the same physical situation, and it's an open question whether any extension manages that while also respecting dominance. That's the part I can't get traction on.

So: diagnose this for me. Is it a verbal dispute over "expected value," a substantive normative disagreement, several different questions hiding under one heading, or a hidden formal question that nobody has stated cleanly? Say which pieces are which, and don't just split the difference — if you think my friend is simply wrong about the math, or that the toy-case objection and the incompleteness objection are actually the same objection in different clothes, say so. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction, a theorem about how EU can be conservatively extended, a new kind of case that cuts between the camps, a change of question — or an argument that no progress should be expected and why the impasse is structural. Two ground rules: no reading list and no citations, I can't check them and I don't want guesses; and where it helps, build your own little variant game to make a point rather than describing what people have said. Also flag anywhere you think I've mis-stated the setup or my friend's objection, since if the diagnosis turns on getting the details right I'd rather know now.
