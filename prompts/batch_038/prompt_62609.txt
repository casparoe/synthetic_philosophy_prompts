I build and maintain the ranking service behind a large consumer platform: the component that decides the order in which items are presented to each user. I have no training in philosophy, but I have been reading around the three-way distinction between freedom as the absence of interference by others, freedom as self-direction or self-mastery, and freedom as non-domination, and I want to know whether the third of these can say something about the system I work on that the other two cannot. I would like you to argue for a specific thesis about that system, under constraints I will state.

The system:

1. It removes nothing. The corpus is fully available; the ranker outputs a permutation, not a filter. Any item can be located by search or by following links, and the user can confirm this themselves.
2. The permutation is personalized. For each user it is selected to maximize a learned engagement objective.
3. That objective is fit on aggregate behavioral data, and the deployed policy is revised continuously by randomized experiments. Which ordering a user sees therefore also depends on the experimental cohort they were hashed into — an assignment that responds to nothing about them at all.
4. The user has read access to their own interest profile and may edit it, disable personalization (falling back to a non-personalized popularity ordering), export their data, or delete the account. None of these actions is gated, metered, charged for, or quietly penalized.
5. Close substitutes exist, and abstaining altogether is possible.
6. The service has nonetheless become load-bearing: professional contacts, local notices, and employment leads reach the user through it. These costs follow from the size of the network, not from anything the operator does to that particular user.

The thesis: a user of this system is dominated in the republican sense — Pettit's sense — and the domination consists not in any contraction of their options, since by hypothesis there is none, but in the fact that the process fixing the order of their choices is revisable at will by an agent other than them, and not revisable by them.

Constraints on the argument:

- Do not appeal to manipulation, addiction, outrage, polarization, or any harm the ordering might cause. I am not asking whether the system is bad for people. Locate the defect in the freedom relation itself, or concede that there is none.
- Do not smuggle in hidden filtering, shadowbanning, undisclosed experiments, dark patterns, or any other feature the specification excludes. If the argument needs one of those, it does not work on this system.
- Meet two objections directly. First: interference that tracks the affected person's own interests is usually said not to be arbitrary, and this system tracks the user's revealed preferences closely — so where is the arbitrariness? Second: a permutation is arguably not interference with anyone's choices at all, in which case the republican cannot even reach the question of arbitrariness. Be explicit about which route you take on the second objection — arguing that reordering is a species of interference, or arguing that domination does not require interference — and say what each route costs you elsewhere.
- Say briefly why the non-interference conception cannot register what you are describing, given clause 1, since that contrast is half of what I am after.
- End by naming the property that, if the system had it, would defeat your argument while leaving the personalization intact, and give me your honest assessment of whether that property is implementable in a system satisfying clauses 1 through 6.

I want the reasoning rather than the scholarship: no bibliography, no list of further reading, no quotations, and no need to tell me who said what. Roughly 900 to 1,200 words.
