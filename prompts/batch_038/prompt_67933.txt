I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on moral testimony, and I need a case built to order for a paper I'm revising. The thesis is that this literature runs together two questions that should be kept apart — whether you can get moral knowledge from someone else's say-so, and whether it's all right to act on what you get — and almost every example people use makes both come out the same way. I want the opposite. Please write the whole answer in British English, it's going to a UK journal.

Here's the spec. Build me one realistic scenario in which deferring to another person on a purely moral question is not merely permissible but the morally better option, and in which the deferring actually manifests a virtue — self-knowledge, say, or humility — rather than the moral cowardice or loss of integrity the pessimists always charge it with. And the mirror-image half, which is the bit I really need: trusting her own judgment in that same situation would have been the criticizable move.

The constraints are what make this non-trivial, so please respect all of them:

- Present-day, ordinary stakes, realistic detail. A workplace, a family, a volunteer organization, something like that. No trolleys, no courtrooms or judges, no hospital ethics committee, no institutional role-obligations quietly doing the work. The testifier is just a fallible but perceptive ordinary person — not a certified expert, not an oracle, not an AI.
- It has to be moral testimony proper. The testifier has no informational advantage about the non-moral facts: she read the same emails, she knows the same people, she wasn't in the room in a way the agent wasn't. If the case only goes through because she happens to know more about the world, it fails — that's ordinary empirical testimony in a moral costume, and it's the standard escape hatch I'm trying to close.
- The agent is a normal, competent adult. Not a child, not cognitively impaired, not exhausted or in shock, not new to the culture or the situation.
- Crucially: she ends up with understanding, not just belief. Once the reasons are put to her she can articulate them, endorse them, and apply them herself to a new case. So the case cannot be diagnosed as a mere understanding deficit. I want the epistemic side to be as healthy as possible so that whatever objection is left has to come from somewhere else.
- One or two paragraphs for the scenario itself. Then the analysis.

After the scenario, do the check, verdict by verdict, and say why each theory lands where it lands:

1. A Hopkins-style view on which deference in the moral case isn't really available — where not being able to work a moral question out yourself calls for suspending judgment rather than taking someone else's word. What does it say here, and does it have to concede the agent did the right thing?
2. A Hills-style view on which the trouble is that you need moral understanding rather than moral belief to act well and to have good character. Where does it land once understanding is present, and what's left of its diagnosis?
3. A knowledge-first optimist who thinks moral testimony transmits moral knowledge no differently than any other testimony, so there's no epistemic problem to begin with. Does the case vindicate that, or does the agent's deference still look somehow off on this view?
4. A virtue or moral-worth line on which acting from another's say-so can't have moral worth. Does the case beat it, or can it absorb the virtuous deference?

Then be honest with me about the mechanics. Name the single feature of the case that's doing all the work, and say what happens to each of the four verdicts if you delete it — I want to know whether the case collapses back into a bog-standard one. Then give me the strongest attack from whoever comes out worst. I expect at least two specific ones and I'd like to see you actually engage them rather then wave at them: the charge that the reason she defers — that her own judgment is compromised by a stake in the outcome — is a non-moral psychological fact about herself that she knows first-hand, so its not really a case about moral testimony at all; and the charge that once you know your judgment is compromised, the only defensible options are to suspend or to fix the bias, with deference sitting oddly between them.
