Two members of a reading group I sit in on — I'll call them Nadia and Owen — have spent several weeks arguing about how to classify one particular causal claim, and I still cannot tell which of them is getting the better of it. I have no view of my own about whose framework is correct, so I would like a referee who does not start from a preference.

Their commitments, as they stated them. Nadia defends a Lewis-style counterfactual account: causation is the ancestral of counterfactual dependence between distinct actual events, and the relevant counterfactuals are evaluated by holding the laws fixed, permitting the smallest local divergence from actuality, and not backtracking. Owen defends a Woodward-style interventionist account: one variable is a cause of another just in case some ideal intervention on the first would change the second, where an ideal intervention sets the target variable, switches off every other influence on it, and affects the outcome only by way of it — and where such an intervention need not be physically or practically performable. Owen also insists that causal claims are always relative to a variable set, and that "direct cause" (other variables in the model held fixed) must be distinguished from "total cause" (everything downstream allowed to adjust).

The case they are arguing about. A remote field station keeps a data-logging server in an insulated room with two independent cooling routes. The first is a mechanical chiller that runs automatically and, when operating, holds the room at 18°C. The second is a louvered vent to the outside; station protocol requires the caretaker — who is on site that week and checks the room's readout every hour — to open the vent whenever the room reaches 24°C. Summer outside air is about 10°C, and the open vent alone holds the room near 22°C. With the chiller off and the vent shut, the room would equilibrate at 34°C; the server shuts itself down above 30°C. The room warms slowly, so with the chiller off it takes roughly two hours to reach 24°C. During the week in question the chiller ran continuously, the room stayed at 18°C, the caretaker never opened the vent, and the logging run completed successfully.

The claim under dispute is whether the chiller's running that week caused the run's successful completion.

**Move 1 (Nadia).** No. Take the nearest world in which the chiller did not run that week, laws fixed and as little else changed as possible: the room warms, reaches 24°C after about two hours, the caretaker — on site and following protocol exactly as he actually did — opens the vent, the room settles at 22°C, and the run completes there too. Nor is there a chain to route the dependence through: the completion would have occurred whether the room sat at 18°C or at 22°C. Causation is difference-making, the chiller made no difference to that outcome, and the sentence "the chiller kept the run from failing" is false however natural it sounds.

**Move 2 (Owen).** You get the extension right and the framework wrong, and the difference shows up in this very case. There is no flat yes-or-no answer, because causal claims are relative to a variable set and to the direct/total distinction. Write the model as Chiller {on, off}, Caretaker {on site, away}, Vent {open, shut}, Temp {18, 22, 34}, Completes {yes, no}. Holding the caretaker and the vent at their actual values, an intervention switching the chiller off takes the room to 34°C and the run fails — so the chiller is a *direct* cause of the completion. Allowing everything downstream to adjust, the same intervention takes the room to 22°C and the run still completes — so the chiller is *not* a total cause of the completion. My account states both facts. Yours states only the second, and thereby erases the fact that the chiller did all of the actual cooling that week while the vent did none.

**Move 3 (Nadia).** Two objections. First, that directness verdict rests on an incoherent stipulation: to obtain it you hold the vent shut while switching the chiller off, which describes a situation in which a man who checks the readout hourly watches the room pass 24°C and does nothing — exactly what the protocol rules out. A verdict that depends on freezing a downstream variable at a value it could not have taken is not a discovery about the world. Second, and more generally, your outputs are hostage to how you carve the variables. Carve instead a single variable, *net cooling delivered to the room*, which took the value "sufficient" that week and would have taken it either way. Relative to that model there is no edge from the chiller to the completion, direct or otherwise, and your directness verdict vanishes. Nothing in the world decides between my carving and yours; my framework asks one question about one pair of events and gets one answer.

**Move 4 (Owen).** On the first, granted: an ideal intervention must leave the other variables at values jointly possible with it, and if holding the vent shut is not, then the honest conclusion is that in the model containing the caretaker the chiller is a direct cause of the *temperature* and a non-total cause of the *completion* — still two facts where you have one. On the second: *net cooling delivered* is inadmissible. It is disjunctive, since the same value can be realized by the chiller or by the vent, and no well-defined operation sets it to "sufficient" without also settling which route supplies it — and the downstream variables respond differently to the two realizations. A variable that does not determine what an intervention on it would be is a placeholder, not a variable. And the tu quoque runs against you harder. Ask whether the completion depends on the chiller's running, and the answer turns on how you describe the antecedent. Describe it as the compressor burning out that Monday, with the caretaker on site as he actually was, and the answer is no. Describe it as the station simply having no functioning chiller that week, and the nearest such world may be one in which the caretaker was called to town for the repair and the run failed. Your closest world is as sensitive to the describer's choices as my variable set is; you merely lack the machinery to represent the sensitivity, which is why you report one answer where there are two.

**Move 5 (Nadia).** Grant for argument that the antecedent must be pinned down — that is the ordinary problem of event granularity, and once it is pinned down the standard resolution yields a determinate verdict. But your framework has a deeper trouble: it is not a rival to mine. An ideal intervention is defined as one that changes the chiller's state, cuts off every other influence on that state, and affects the completion only through it. Nothing at the station satisfies that description, and you agree it need not, since your interventions are "causes waiting to happen" rather than events that could occur. So the whole content of your claim is a counterfactual: what would happen to the completion if the chiller were switched off and everything that does not depend on the chiller were held exactly as it actually is. That is my counterfactual with a ceteris paribus clause attached. If so, you cannot use your framework to correct my verdicts, only to restate them, and your two-facts-where-I-have-one is bookkeeping rather than disagreement about the world.

**Move 6 (Owen).** If that were right we would agree about the verdict here, and we do: I also say the chiller is not a total cause of the completion. So the objection, if it worked, would dissolve the very dispute you have spent three moves prosecuting. And the ceteris paribus clause is not a defect I am hiding; it is the explanatory resource. What gets held fixed is what determines the answer, and naming it is what lets me say why the flat question "did the chiller keep the run from failing?" has no single answer — it is ambiguous between holding the caretaker's presence fixed, holding his protocol fixed, and holding neither. Your account returns "no" and cannot explain why the question seemed answerable in more than one way to everyone in the room, including you when you first heard the case.

**Move 7 (Nadia).** It can explain it. The question seemed answerable both ways because the case contains two different effects, and I affirm a causal claim about one while denying it about the other: the room's being 18°C rather than 22°C depends on the chiller, so the chiller caused the room to be 18°C; the run's completing does not depend on the chiller, so the chiller did not cause the run's completion. That requires neither ambiguity in the question nor relativity in the framework — only a distinction between two effects of one event, which any account has to draw.

**Move 8 (Owen).** Then most of our disagreement has evaporated, and your first move overstated things by implying the chiller caused nothing of consequence. But one real difference survives. On your account the caretaker's protocol is not part of the causal structure of the situation at all; it is a fact about which worlds are close to ours, doing its work silently inside the similarity metric. On mine it is a variable in the model, on the same footing as the chiller, and what it does to the verdict is visible. That is a substantive difference about where a backup mechanism lives — in the world or in the metric — and it is what will decide the next case rather than this one.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than simply naming a winner.

(a) Rate each of the eight moves — clearly mistaken, weak but pointed, substantially right, clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification each. Where a move turns on a claim about how one of the two theories actually works, assess that claim on its own terms first; I suspect at least one move misstates the theory it is offered on behalf of, and I want to know which.

(b) Answer the questions they were actually fighting over, on the merits rather than by deferring to either of them. Did the chiller's running that week cause the run's completion? Is the flat question genuinely ambiguous, or does it have a determinate answer? Does Move 5 show that the interventionist framework is a notational variant of the counterfactual one, or does Move 6 neutralize it? Does the *net cooling delivered* objection establish a damaging model-relativity, and does the disjunctive-variable reply answer it?

(c) Say what both of them are missing, including anything about the case as I have described it that either could have exploited and neither did.

(d) Finally, state the smallest change you can make to the case as given — one stipulation added, removed, or altered — that would make the two frameworks deliver genuinely conflicting verdicts about the same question, and explain why that change does it.

Do not add facts to the case beyond what I have supplied. If a fact is missing and the verdict turns on it, identify it and say how each possible value would change the analysis. I am not after references or a survey of the literature — only your own assessment of the arguments as they were actually made.
