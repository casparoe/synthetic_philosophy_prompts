I'm a site reliability engineer — I build monitoring and anomaly-detection systems — and I have no philosophy training, so if my formalization below misstates the original argument, please say so before you diagnose it.

A colleague and I got into a dispute about whether testimony can ever support belief in an extremely unlikely event, and he sent me to Section X of Hume's Enquiry concerning Human Understanding. I read the first part twice, then rewrote it in the notation I actually think in, because I wanted to see whether it generalizes. It does, and that is the problem.

My reconstruction of the "in principle" argument:

1. A rational person proportions belief to the evidence: you ought to accept a reported event only where the evidence that the report is mistaken is weaker than the evidence that the event happened.
2. A miracle is a violation of a law of nature.
3. A law of nature is established by uniform experience — a regularity confirmed in every instance anyone has ever observed.
4. So the evidence against any particular miracle is the strongest that experience can supply, since it rests on a regularity with no exceptions. (from 2, 3)
5. The credibility of human testimony is itself known only from experience, and experience shows that testimony fails often enough: through deception, vanity, carelessness, and honest misperception.
6. Hence the probability that any given piece of testimony is false is some determinate nonzero value p, fixed by the observed rate at which reports turn out wrong.
7. For any miracle M, the probability of M, considered apart from the report, is lower than that of any event consistent with a law of nature, and therefore lower than p. (from 4)
8. Therefore, for any testimony T reporting a miracle, the probability that T is false exceeds the probability that M occurred, and by 1 it is never rational to assent to T.
9. Therefore no testimony can ever establish a miracle, except where the falsehood of that testimony would itself be more remarkable than the event it reports.

The generalization that worries me. Step 8 uses nothing distinctive about miracles beyond the low prior in step 7, so:

10. For any event E whose prior probability is below p, and any testimony T reporting E, it is never rational to believe T.
11. But there are events with prior probabilities far below any plausible p that we do believe, and rightly so, on someone's say-so: the exact sequence of numbers in a lottery draw, the confirmed diagnosis of a condition with an incidence of one in a million, the specific minute a rare component failure occurred in a fleet of ten thousand machines.
12. So 10 is false; therefore 8 is false; therefore either 1 or 7 is false — and since 1 is just Hume's own stated maxim about proportioning belief to evidence, the argument seems to refute the principle it starts from.

This is not an academic worry for me. My alerting stack fires on patterns whose priors sit around one in ten million, and the false-positive rate of any single detector is several orders of magnitude worse than that. If I implemented 10 as a suppression rule, the correct engineering policy would be to discard every alert concerning a rare event. Which means the priors could never be revised, which means the "uniform experience" feeding steps 3, 4, and 7 is kept uniform by refusing to process anything capable of breaking the uniformity. I think that is a serious problem, but I cannot turn it into a premise.

One more thing I noticed and don't know what to do with. If there are n witnesses who err independently with probability p each, the probability that all n err together is p^n, which tends to zero as n grows. So 8 looks false for sufficiently large n even if I grant 7 outright. Separately, I cannot tell whether the exception clause in 9 does any work at all, or whether it is just 1 restated with the conclusion built into it.

What I would rather keep: 1, because it is the only thing in the argument I already believed before reading Hume, and 11, because lottery winners plainly exist.

What I want from you: identify which step or inference you reject and why; explain what made that step look acceptable on first reading, since to me each of 1 through 9 still looks fine in isolation; and price the alternatives — specifically, what a defender gives up if they abandon 7 rather than 8, and what they give up if they keep the argument valid by reinterpreting 1. Also tell me whether the argument as I have written it is valid under any consistent reading of "probability," and if it is not, where exactly the reading changes. Then say what the independent-witness point does to the argument — whether it is a genuine defeat or whether it secretly assumes something Hume would deny — and whether the exception clause in 9 is doing substantive work. Finally, either formulate my frozen-priors worry as a premise I could add, or explain why it is not a problem for Hume after all.
