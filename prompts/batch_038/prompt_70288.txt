Over the last two years I've settled into a version of constitutive panpsychism that I want to state precisely, because which objections have force depends heavily on which details one holds onto. Four commitments are load-bearing for me. First, the fundamental physical properties are dispositional through and through — mass, charge, and spin specify only what a thing does under various conditions — and something must categorically ground that whole network; I take experiential properties to be the least bad candidate, and I regard this as an abductive claim about the best reading of current physics rather than an a priori truth. Second, the fundamental experiential properties are genuinely phenomenal, not merely protophenomenal: there is something it is like at the bottom, and I accept that this makes the combination problem mine to solve rather than someone else's. Third, I reject any expectation of a tidy mapping between the short list of fundamental physical properties and the rich diversity of human experience; the basic phenomenal inventory is very small, and everything recognisable as a smell or a shade of blue is a combinatorial product rather than a fundamental ingredient. Fourth, I do not think subjects have determinate boundaries. The intuition that there is exactly one bounded subject behind my eyes is, on my view, an artifact of how experience presents itself from the inside — the way a visual field seems to have a center without there being any such thing in the world — and once one stops treating that intuition as a datum, the demand for a fusion operator that turns many micro-subjects into one macro-subject simply loses its grip.

I've been defending this in a departmental reading group where the pushback has been repetitive and, I suspect, not the strongest available: mostly intuition-pumping (imagine two experiences merging, observe that nothing intelligible happens) plus a standing complaint that my fourth commitment is a surrender dressed up as a solution. I would rather have the hard version. So: what would count against this position? I want the defeaters — empirical findings, formal results, arguments, recalcitrant intuitions, developments in fundamental physics, results about the reliability of introspection itself — sorted from fatal, through seriously damaging, to merely awkward, with a sentence on why each sits where it sits.

For each defeater, tell me whether a committed defender of a view like mine would concede that it counts against them or dismiss it, and on what grounds; I am especially interested in the ones my side should concede, since those are the ones I cannot argue my way around. Then address these specific worries. Does the fourth commitment rescue the view or abandon the very thing the view was meant to explain, and if it collapses, which rival does it collapse into — cosmopsychism together with its own decomposition problem, neutral monism, eliminativism about subjects, something else? Treat separately the suspicion that my first and third commitments together leave the fundamental phenomenal properties doing no explanatory work whatsoever, so that the theory is at once unfalsifiable and inert; if that suspicion is correct, is it a genuine defect given that the position is offered as metaphysical interpretation rather than empirical hypothesis, and what would the position have to give up in order to risk anything? Be concrete on the empirical side too: which results concerning anaesthesia, the cerebellum, split-brain patients, the formal coherence of information-integration frameworks, or the demonstrable unreliability of introspective report would actually constrain or undermine a putative compositional law for subjects, as opposed to being irrelevant because the view operates at the micro level? Close with the single objection you expect would do me the most damage — the one I have probably been luckiest to have avoided so far.
