I produce a philosophy-of-medicine podcast whose listenership is split roughly evenly between practising clinicians and graduate students, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a view I have been calling reference-class indexicalism about disease. The episode is recorded in one sitting and edited to about forty minutes, so I cannot rely on retakes or on cutting around a stall.

Her position, as I read it: the statistical machinery she inherits from Boorse survives intact — disease is a departure from typical functional efficiency, where typicality is measured against a reference class of individuals at a comparable stage of life and of the same sex — but she denies that any single reference class is the one nature privileges. On her account a bare attribution of disease is not true or false simpliciter; it acquires a truth value only once a class is supplied. What makes diagnosis look objective is that institutions stabilize a default: the assay manufacturer's reference population, the guideline committee's cut-point, the age- and sex-banded table printed on the laboratory report. She maintains that this leaves her a naturalist in the full sense — no valuation enters the concept, only a parameter that context supplies. Her critics reply that the naturalism is a shell game: the default class is precisely where the values were relocated, and calling it a parameter instead of a judgment changes nothing about that.

She is articulate, generous, and exceptionally slippery. Her characteristic response to a counterexample is that the objector has mis-specified the reference class, and that once the class is identified correctly the verdict comes out exactly as her theory predicts. I want a question set that makes that response expensive for her without ever being unfair.

**The position as you understand it** — roughly 120 words. Restate her view in your own terms and say plainly where my summary above is misleading or oversimplified. If you think my label commits her to something she would disown (semantic relativism about disease talk, as against the weaker claim that reference classes are underdetermined by the biology), flag it here rather than burying it in a question.

**The questions** — eight to ten, numbered in the order I should ask them, about 1,000 to 1,300 words in total. For each: the question as I would read it aloud, in one or two sentences and with no preamble; a single line naming the pressure point it targets; and a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. The set must include at least one question on each item below, and should escalate as it goes:

- the mis-specification move itself — put her on record, before any case is presented, about what would settle which reference class is correct, by considerations independent of the diagnosis she wants;
- the sex-banded haemoglobin threshold and the age-banding of function, as cases where the default class looks chosen to deliver a verdict rather than found;
- a guideline committee lowering the blood-pressure cut-point, which converts a large share of the previously statistically normal into patients essentially overnight, and what her view says has changed: the disease, the class, or only our bookkeeping;
- traits far outside the class on the favourable side, where her apparatus appears to treat exceptional health as abnormality;
- whether two clinicians who disagree about a borderline patient are genuinely disagreeing on her view, or merely talking past one another, and what is left of diagnosis as a factual practice if the latter;
- the charge that she is a normativist in naturalist dress, framed so she has to locate the value judgment somewhere other than in her protest that there isn't one;
- psychiatry: a statistically typical reaction to an atypical environment, of which the dispute over whether ordinary grief should be diagnosable as depression is the obvious instance.

**The two questions she will handle best** — roughly 120 words. Tell me which of the above she has a genuinely good answer to, what that answer is, and consequently which one I should concede quickly in order to buy airtime for the rest.

**Do not ask** — roughly 80 words. Three questions that sound sharp and are not: softballs she can convert into a lecture, or traps that depend on a caricature of her view.

**The closer** — one question plus one conditional follow-up, roughly 80 words. It should ask what would count as evidence that her view is wrong, and it should be framed so that no single counterexample can refute a theory of this kind is not an available answer.
