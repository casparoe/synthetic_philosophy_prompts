I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a British broadsheet, and I am currently reporting a piece on the strange architecture by which civil aviation regulators decide that a pilot has stopped being a pilot. Once you look at it closely, the system sorts a person's capacities into at least three classes with wildly different shelf lives. In the United States, a pilot's certificate does not expire at all: it is held for life, and the written examinations on air law, meteorology, navigation and aircraft systems on which it rests are never sat again once the licence has been issued. European rules differ in detail but not in kind. What does expire is everything else. An instrument qualification lapses unless the holder has flown a specified number of approaches, holding procedures and tracking exercises within the preceding six months. To carry passengers at all, a pilot must have made three take-offs and three landings in the same category and class of aircraft within the preceding ninety days. And the type rating — the endorsement for a particular airframe — is revalidated by a proficiency check, usually in a full-flight simulator and usually every six to twelve months, in which an examiner scores the pilot against published tolerances: altitude held within a hundred feet, heading within ten degrees, and so forth. Alongside all of this sits the medical, which lapses every six or twelve months depending on the holder's age.

The detail that started me off is a proposal I have been shown by a national authority, which I will not name because it has not yet gone to consultation, under which a pilot returning after a long absence could regain the type rating by passing a demanding computer-based assessment of procedures and systems knowledge, supplemented by a fixed number of hours in a part-task trainer, rather than by a simulator check with an examiner. The pilots' association objected, but not on the grounds anyone expected. Their argument was not that the evidence base was thin, nor that the trainer was a poor proxy for the aircraft. It was that the scheme confuses two different things: a pilot who has been away has not lost the knowledge and could pass any written paper you set him tomorrow, whereas what he has lost is something no written paper could either detect or restore. The airline's head of safety, in reply, told me this sounded like mysticism dressed as expertise — that if the man can state correctly what the aircraft will do in each configuration and can execute the procedures on a screen, then whatever else the union believes is missing is not a matter of competence at all, and certainly not one a regulator should be codifying.

So I would like the implicit theory of the practice reconstructed, rather than the dispute settled on safety grounds. Does the bifurcation — a knowledge test that never expires, a performance test that expires constantly — only make sense against a view on which knowing how to fly is a genuinely distinct kind of state from knowing that such-and-such, or could a regulator who held that all knowledge is propositional, including knowledge of how to do things, run precisely the same schedule, treating the recurrent check merely as a cost-effective way of sampling a disposition that happens to decay? I want to be told where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, which rival accounts it rules out rather than merely sits awkwardly beside, and what the checklist-and-tolerances method of examining skill commits the regulator to about whether competence is an inner state or an observable performance. Then tell me what would actually have to change — the examinations, the intervals, the wording printed on the licence — if the authority adopted the safety manager's position and treated knowing how as a species of knowing that; and say whether the union's objection survives that adoption or is exposed as incoherent by it. Finally, since my editor will ask: does the expiring medical complicate the picture, or does the body sit outside it altogether?
