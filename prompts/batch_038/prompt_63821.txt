I'm setting the final for an upper-level Bayesian epistemology course — undergrads who've had a term of probability and have done conditionalization exercises, nothing measure-theoretic — and I want one essay-plus-calculation question on the problem of old evidence, worth 25 marks, answerable in about 75 minutes.

What I'm sick of reading is answers that recite "Bayesianism can't confirm a theory with evidence you already knew, because conditioning on E does nothing once P(E) = 1" and then stop. That's the label, not the analysis. So I want the question to force them into the arithmetic and then make them explain why the arithmetic doesn't settle the philosophical issue.

Here's the setup I want you to build the question around, keep it roughly this shape: Wegener proposes continental drift in the 1910s, and one strand of his case is the jigsaw fit of the South American and African coastlines — something mapmakers had noticed for centuries and nobody had ever taken to be evidence for anything. Decades later seafloor spreading and the magnetic striping of the ocean floor turn up as genuinely novel consequences.

Part of the question should just hand them numbers: P(fit) = 0.99, P(drift) = 0.05, P(fit | drift) = 0.98, and ask for the posterior and the confirmaton ratio, plus a couple of sentences in their own words on what the result shows. Deliberate trap here, and I want the model answer to walk straight into it: with those numbers the posterior comes out slightly *below* the prior, so the famous fit slightly disconfirms drift. I want students to spot that, do the implied P(fit | not-drift), and say what it means — that the likelihood ratio is doing almost no work when the agent is already near-certain of E, and that this is a feature of the stipulated priors rather than a refutation of Bayesianism. Marking scheme should reward the student who notices and punish the one who reports 0.0495 as if it were a satisfying answer.

Then a second part: tell them that for a careful geologist in 1915 P(fit) wasn't 0.99 but 0.9, since coastlines erode and a jigsaw-looking fit could be coincidence. Redo it, and say what changed and why it changed. Third part: bring in the Garber-style response, where what the scientist learns isn't the old evidence but the new proposition that her theory entails it — I want them to say what has to be true of the prior over that entailment claim for the move to deliver any confirmation whatsoever, and where it comes apart when the hypothesis space is itself growing, which is what's really going on when somebody invents drift in the first place. Fourth part: does the later success on magnetic striping retroactively license treating the coastline fit as confirming, and on what principled basis rather than as a special-pleading patch?

Deliverables: the question exactly as students would see it, a model answer of around 750 words that includes the arithmetic worked through, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. Two things I especially want flagged in the scheme. One is the logical-omniscience dodge: students who say the fix is to stop assuming agents know all the logical consequences of their credences, and treat that as dissolving the problem instead of relocating it onto the question of where the prior over the entailment claim comes from. The other is the "it's all about the priors" shrug, which is true and worth nothing unless they say which priors are objectionable and why. Also tell me plainly in the scheme what a B answer typically does that an A answer doesn't, besides the point of the arithmetic — that's the line I actually have to draw when I'm marking at 2am.
