I'm a respiratory therapist at a small community hospital, and because our ethics committee is supposed to have a "frontline staff" voice on it, they stuck me on it two years ago. Nobody on it is a philosopher — two docs, a chaplain, a risk-management lawyer, a nurse manager and me — so we mostly just go around the table and say what feels right, and whoever talks longest wins. I want you to write up the short opinion I can hand out before we meet Thursday, because this case has us split and I think half of us are arguing about something the other half isn't even disagreeing with.

The case. 81 year old man, advanced dementia, came in from his memory care unit with aspiration pneumonia, low grade fever, needing oxygen. Twenty-two years ago, when he was 59 and completely healthy, he signed a directive saying that if he ever lost his mind he wanted nothing done — no antibiotics, no feeding tube, no hospital, "just keep me comfortable and let it end." He signed it about two weeks after his own mother died in a nursing home. His daughter has the original in her glove box, it's signed and witnessed and dated, and she wants us to follow it to the letter. Legally I'm pretty sure it's binding. The attending wants to give him five days of IV antibiotics and see where he lands, and the memory care staff who've known him three years keep saying he isn't miserable — he sings along to hymns, he flirts with the lunch lady, he gets happy about popsicles, he holds your hand if you sit down. But he can't tell you what day it is or why he's in a hospital bed, and when you ask him if he wants to go home he says sure.

Here's the thing I keep turning over and I want you to actually work with it, because I've never heard anybody say it out loud. I think there are two patients in this room. There's the patient on the paper — the one the 59 year old pictured, lying there not knowing anybody's name, being dragged along by machines, humiliated. And there's the patient in the bed, who is not that guy, even though he has the same name and the same social security number and the same fingerprints. The directive is addressed to the paper patient. So what I can't figure out is whether a document written *to* somebody who doesn't exist can be an instruction *about* somebody who does, or whether that's just me being cute and dressing up "we'd rather treat him" as a principle.

And I know that doesn't automatically get you to treating him. Maybe the 59 year old knew exactly what he was afraid of and it's condescending for a bunch of us who met him once to say he'd actually be fine with this. He got to look at his own mother's ending and decide he wanted no part of it, and that's more than most people get. Then there's the daughter: she made her dad a promise and she's the one who has to live the rest of her life having broken it, not us, and we go home Thursday night either way. I don't want a memo that treats her as a villain or as someone who's "in denial," which is what the attending kept saying.

So please draft the committee's recommendation. Actually pick something — treat, don't treat, or some narrower thing like a time-limited trial with a stated review date — and give a timeframe we could hand to the floor nurses, like what happens by Sunday. Then give us a test somebody can apply at 2am with no lawyer present, for the next version of this case, not just this one. And a short separate section: should we start flagging any directive older than ten or fifteen years for a re-affirming conversation while the person can still have it, and what's honestly wrong with that idea, because I can feel the objection coming and I don't want to pretend it isn't there.
