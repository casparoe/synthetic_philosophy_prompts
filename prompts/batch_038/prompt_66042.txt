Long story short: my grandmother signed a directive about thirty years ago saying not to keep her going once she couldn't recognize any of us, and last month the family honored it, and the whole thing went about as well as you'd excpect. Since then I've been reading around the advance-directive literature, and in a comment thread that ran way too long I ended up formalizing the case *against* what we did — partly as a steelman, partly because I wanted to know whether I should feel bad. The problem is that the argument, if it works, works far too well. It seems to demolish every promise, contract, resolution, and pension plan anyone has ever made, and I can't locate the leak. Here's what I've got:

(1) A directive binds the patient only if the signer and the person it gets enforced on stand in the right relation — either they're the same person, or the second one authorized the first one's decision. (2) There's no third source of that authority; authority over a life is rooted in identity or in authorization, full stop. (3) In deep dementia the patient authorized nothing: she doesn't remember signing, can't now consent or refuse, and if you asked her she'd happily wave the directive off. (4) Nor are they the same person — no shared memories, no shared projects, no continuity of character or values — so on a psychological-continuity account the signer and the patient are at best two distinct stages of a life. (5) So by (2) the directive doesn't bind, and honoring it means ending the life of someone who never agreed to it, for the sake of someone who is gone. (6) But nothing in (1)–(5) is special to dementia. Every commitment assumes the future self is enough like the present self, and selves drift: the addict signing a Ulysses contract, the nineteen-year-old pledging herself to a faith, the thirty-year-old locking up retirement contributions, you at 4pm breaking a promise you made at 9am. (7) And there's no non-arbitrary line for how much drift is too much, so if we can't say where bindingness stops, it never starts. (8) Conclusion: precedent autonomy is a myth, and what actually decides these cases is just the interests of whoever is in the bed — who, in my grandmother's case, was pleasant, enjoyed her food and old records, and seemed to have no complaint about anything.

The thing I most want to keep is (4); I've read enough Parfit that I'm not going to pretend identity does the work. The thing I second-most want to keep is the intuition that a directive like hers *should* bind, which I know is me trying to have it both ways. (2) is the premise I suspect, but cutting it feels like just asserting the conclusion I want, and (6) also bites somewhere I can't place. There's also the awkward fact that the instrument she used was explicitly designed to keep operating after she lost capacity, which sounds like it has to matter and I can't say why or at which step. So: which step do you cut, why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it down, and what does cutting it cost you — including whatever you'd tell my friend who says (7) is doing an epistemic-vagueness trick? Lead with the step number, keep the whole answer under 400 words, and tell me what would flip your verdict: which consideration would make you say (8) is right after all, and where in your own diagnosis you think you're most likely to be wrong.
