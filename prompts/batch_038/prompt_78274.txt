I am in my second year at a sixth form college and I am writing an EPQ on whether human co-operation can be explained without assuming that people are anything other than self-interested. My supervisor has asked me to set out my own position in advance so that she can attack it properly, and I would rather hand in something that has already been stress-tested by someone other than her. So: could you go through the list below and tell me whether the commitments hang together as a package, or whether some of them pull against one another? I have deliberately not said which ones I am most attached to, and I would prefer you not to try to guess, since I do not want the verdict softened in my favour.

Here they are. First, I hold that rational choice is a matter of maximising expected utility given whatever preferences one happens to have; preferences are inputs to the theory rather than outputs of it, and I do not think there is any independent standard by which some preferences count as more rational than others. Second, in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma I take mutual defection to be the uniquely rational outcome, and I am unmoved both by the suggestion that one should co-operate because one's opponent is likely to reason much as one does, and by the suggestion that some universalising argument of a Kantian kind rescues co-operation. Third, in a repeated prisoner's dilemma with no commonly known final round I think tit-for-tat and its relatives are the strategies to play: Axelrod's tournaments, in which that strategy kept winning, together with the folk theorem, seem to me to show that a sufficiently long shadow of the future genuinely transforms the game rather than merely redescribing it. Fourth, I think human morality is to be explained by reciprocal altruism and kin selection, and I firmly reject group selection as an explanation of anything; it looks to me like the old for-the-good-of-the-species fallacy with new mathematics bolted on. Fifth, I think what makes co-operation possible among large numbers of unrelated people is the costly punishment of free riders: take the punishers away and co-operation collapses, which is why I think punishment does more work than any quantity of goodwill. Sixth, when I read about laboratory public goods experiments in which people hand over real money in one-shot, anonymous games, I take the contribution to be genuine rather than the product of participants having misunderstood the instructions, and my explanation is that we have socially shaped preferences which misfire outside the environment that formed them; but I also want to say that those contributors are behaving irrationally, because they are surrendering money for no return whatsoever. Seventh, as a methodological matter I want explanations to be individualistic and mechanism-based, and I am deeply suspicious of any account that explains a behaviour by pointing to its usefulness to some collectivity.

What I would like back is an audit rather than a lecture. Please tell me which of these commitments are in genuine conflict and which only look as though they are, and rank the real conflicts by severity instead of presenting them all as equally bad. For each genuine conflict, say what would have to be given up or rewritten in order to remove it, and what the cheapest such revision would cost me elsewhere on the list; if one single change would repair most of the damage, identify it. I should also mention a worry I keep getting from a friend in my reading group, which is that my third commitment cannot survive backward induction, since any finite game unravels to defection from the last round backwards; tell me whether he is right about that or whether he has misread what I am claiming. And where two of my commitments seem to clash but do not really, please say so explicitly and explain why, so that I do not spend the viva defending myself against a charge that was never properly there. One last thing about the form of the answer: my supervisor requires everything we submit to her in plain prose, so no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables, just continuous writing of the sort one would put in a letter.
