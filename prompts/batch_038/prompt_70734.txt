In the Letter Spirit project associated with Douglas Hofstadter and his Fluid Analogies Research Group, a "gridfont" is a complete alphabet drawn by a single imaginary hand on a fixed rectangular grid. The project's guiding intuition is that what makes a given glyph an instance of, say, the letter 'a' is not any local feature, template, or rule, but its participation in a coherent style that runs through the alphabet as a whole. I should like a thought experiment engineered to stress that intuition in a direction its proponents seldom make explicit: the possibility that style and category come apart in both directions.

Please construct a transformation, applied uniformly to every letter of a gridfont, that satisfies all three of the following:

1. **Category survival.** After the transformation, each glyph, presented on its own to a literate reader, remains an unambiguous instance of the letter it was before. Specify the transformation in enough detail — which strokes move, by how much, on what grid — that a reader could apply it themselves to an ordinary sans-serif alphabet.
2. **Style death.** The transformed alphabet, presented as a complete set, no longer reads as the work of one hand: it reads either as a miscellaneous assemblage or as the interleaved output of several hands.
3. **No smuggling.** The transformation must be expressible as a single systematic principle rather than as random perturbation, and it must not achieve its effect by degrading legibility, by making the letters ugly, or by exploiting any particular reader's unfamiliarity with a script tradition.

Then construct the converse case: a transformation under which stylistic coherence across the alphabet is preserved, yet at least three specified letters cease to be instances of the categories they occupied. Name the three and explain precisely why each fails.

Describe the glyphs in words or in a simple coordinate notation on a grid of your own choosing; assume no images are available.

After both constructions, run the check. For each direction, state what a template-and-rule account of letter identity predicts, what a prototype or best-example account predicts, and what the style-constituted account predicts, and say honestly whether your case was designed to satisfy those predictions or genuinely falls out of them. Identify which feature of each transformation is doing the work, state what happens to the dissociation if that feature is removed, and set out how a defender of the view that letter identity is a matter of local, context-independent form would attack your case rather than concede it.

Conclude with a short verdict: do the two directions together establish that style and category can genuinely come apart, or is one of the two constructions illicit in some way you have not yet disclosed?

Please write your answer in British English.
