I am a doctoral candidate in political philosophy working on a chapter about Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal (1970), and I have built an argument that I intended to show that the result is no artifact of the Prude-and-Lewd story but a general refutation of deontological liberalism. My adviser's objection is that it proves too much, and I cannot find the exit. Throughout, let L be Sen's minimal liberalism — for each individual there is at least one pair of social states over which she is decisive, her strict preference between the two fixing the social ranking of that pair — and let P be the Pareto condition.

(1) A right, for the purposes of social choice theory, is a protected pair: an ordered pair (x, y) over which its holder i is decisive, so that whenever x is strictly preferred to y by i, the social ranking places x above y.
(2) That decisiveness is unconditional. Were it to hold only on some profiles of the other individuals' preferences, the protected sphere would be revocable at their pleasure and would not be a right at all.
(3) P is likewise unconditional: for every pair and on every profile, if each individual strictly prefers x to y, the social ranking places x above y.
(4) The domain is unrestricted, so for any protected pair (x, y) of any individual there is an admissible profile on which the holder strictly prefers x to y while every other individual strictly prefers y to x.
(5) On such a profile, (1) and (2) require the social ranking to place x above y, and (3) requires it to place y above x. Hence L and P are jointly unsatisfiable.
(6) Nothing in (5) appealed to Sen's particular preferences, to the number of protected pairs, or to the number of right-holders; the construction goes through for any assignment of protected spheres whatsoever. So no system of rights — a single pair over which book a person reads, or an entire bill of rights — can be reconciled with unanimity.
(7) But a protected sphere that must be surrendered whenever everyone else happens to disapprove of its exercise is not a protected sphere. Rights therefore cannot operate as trumps in social choice; they can enter only as one weighted set of preferences among others, and deontological liberalism is incoherent.

I should say where my own sympathies lie, so you can judge whether they are survivable: I want to keep (7), since I think the genuine lesson of the paradox is that rights are weights rather than trumps, and if forced to choose I would abandon (2) before (3). What I cannot do is identify the defective step, since each of (1)–(6) reads to me as an unpacking of its predecessor. Tell me which step fails and why it looked acceptable, what the least costly repair of my argument would require giving up, and whether that repair leaves Sen's own theorem intact — I have not questioned the theorem itself, only my generalization of it. Please close with a line of the form \answer{n}, where n is the number of the earliest step at which the argument breaks.
