You are a poet with the training of a working textual critic: you have spent years inside critical editions of literary and documentary papyri, you know the Leiden system of editorial sigla cold, and you write plain, accomplished contemporary free verse. Think carefully before you draft, and treat philological accuracy as non-negotiable — a classicist who picks up the page should find nothing misused, while a reader who has never seen a critical edition should still find it a moving poem.

I am opening a graduate seminar on how we know anything about the distant past, and I want to hand out one page that performs that epistemology rather than describing it. The page is to be laid out as a page from a critical edition of a fragmentary ancient source. It is entirely invented. Nothing in it may refer to real texts, real cities, real scholars, or real editions; do not cite anything, do not supply parallels, and do not reach for outside sources.

The given material — keep all of it, and invent freely around it:

- The event is the surrender of a small inland city. Everything later writers argue about turns on a single clause: who opened a gate at night. A bribed guard? A delegation of citizens who decided to save the city? The general's own order, relayed?
- P: a carbonized roll, some forty lines legible, copied perhaps two centuries after the events it describes; even its own date is inferred rather than stated.
- B: a twelfth-century parchment copy of a ninth-century manuscript that no longer exists, corrected throughout by a later hand — so that where B agrees with P it may prove nothing at all.
- The indirect tradition: three quotations of the same passage embedded in later works. They are the only evidence for the stretch where P goes blank, and they survive only because one later author was collecting rare words and another was making an unrelated point about a proverb. What reaches us has been filtered by interests that have nothing to do with ours.
- Three editors (invent them: surname plus the year of their edition) have supplied three different words in the decisive lacuna. The printed text adopts one of the three.

Now the form. Satisfy every constraint:

1. Main text: 24–32 lines, unrhymed, lines of roughly equal length, in three to five stanzas.
2. Sigla: use the Leiden conventions correctly, and use at least five distinct kinds of editorial sign, keeping them properly distinguished — text restored by the editor in a gap, a gap left unrestored, text judged spurious, letters a scribe omitted that the editor restores, an abbreviation expanded, letters uncertain. Explain none of this anywhere on the page. If you cannot deploy a sign correctly, leave it out rather than approximate.
3. Gradient: the ratio of supplied to transmitted text must rise steadily across the page. The first stanza should be almost entirely what survives; the last line of the poem should consist wholly of restored text, with nothing transmitted in it at all. This curve is the argument — the closer the text comes to the question we actually care about, the more of it is ours.
4. The decisive lacuna must fall exactly where the clause assigns responsibility, and the space available must be estimated from physical evidence (the length of the gap, the spacing of the line), so that what may be supplied there is constrained by something other than an editor's preference.
5. Apparatus criticus beneath the poem: 8–12 entries in the standard format — line number, lemma, closing bracket, then readings and sigla, semicolon-separated — using the customary Latin abbreviations. Among them there must be: an entry showing that a reading everyone prints has no manuscript support and rests entirely on a conjecture; an entry in which B's agreement with P is discounted because of the later hand; an entry where the only evidence is the indirect tradition and the three quotations disagree; and an entry in which a line is deleted as an interpolation on the strength of a later author's silence.
6. Above the poem, a conspectus siglorum of three or four lines; after the apparatus, a closing note of three to five sentences in the editor's own voice.
7. In that closing note, allude — in your own words, without naming anyone and without quotation marks — to the two founding gestures of the genre: the compiler who says that it is his business to set down what he is told but none of his business to believe it, and the historian who admits that the exact words of speeches could not be recovered and so made each speaker say what the occasion seemed to require, adding that people present at the same events disagreed with one another according to their partiality and their memory.
8. Do not resolve who opened the gate. The page must leave that question exactly as undecided as the evidence leaves it. Keep the register plain: no exclamation, no address to a muse, no meta-commentary about the writing of poems, no use of the words truth or meaning.

Begin with a planning note of five sentences or fewer: which line carries the decisive lacuna, how the density of supplied text will curve across the page, and which single apparatus entry does the sharpest work. Then give the page itself — titled the way a fragment is titled in an edition — and nothing after it.
