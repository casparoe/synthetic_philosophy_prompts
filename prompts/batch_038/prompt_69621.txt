This week's session in my second-year course on strategic explanation in politics runs 75 minutes with 30 students, mostly economics and IR majors. The room has movable chairs, a whiteboard, and no projector, and I have roughly 250 poker chips in three colors plus a stack of index cards. By the time we meet, they will already have had the standard material: the chicken game, the fact that a player can improve her position by destroying her own options, and Schelling's remarks on the value of being seen to be unable to retreat. What I do not want is an activity that dramatizes that last point once more, because most of them have already filed it away as a piece of counterintuitive trivia they can recite. I want them to run into the structure of a commitment *race*: that once burning the bridges is publicly known to be the winning move, both sides scramble to do it first; that the prize goes to whoever is *seen* to have done it; and that the whole danger of the situation lies in not knowing whether the other side has already done it before you decided to.

Concretely, I would like the game to generate four realizations in roughly this order, and to generate them in the players rather than in my mouth: that being unable to choose is worth something; that it is worth nothing unless the other side observes it and believes it; that this produces a race to be first, in which arriving second is worse than never having entered; and that the winner of the race is systematically the player with the least to lose, or the least command over her own later actions. Two further constraints on the design. First, the commitment device should be social rather than mechanical — no sealed envelopes, no surrendering one's decision sheet to the facilitator, no pre-written move sequences — because what I most want them to leave holding is the thought that in actual bargaining almost nothing is literally irreversible, and what makes retreat impossible is a cost of being seen to retreat that somebody has manufactured. Observers, public announcements, and reputation stakes are all fair game; real money is not. Second, please arrange for each pair's collision to fall partly on players who had no part in causing it, in chips, so that the question of who pays for a lost race is felt rather than asserted.

What I need from you: complete rules; a materials list confined to what I have above; a chip-denominated payoff scheme with the numbers actually worked out, including what a player earns for winning the race, for conceding early, for conceding late, and for colliding, and what the uninvolved bystanders lose; a minute-by-minute timeline covering 15 pairs playing at once with one facilitator circulating; a second round in which the two seats are asymmetric in stakes rather than in information, together with advice on how to assign that asymmetry so the low-stakes seat does not become an obviously comfortable place to sit; and a debrief that maps identifiable moments of play onto the four realizations, phrased as questions I can ask rather than points I can make. Be blunt about what will go wrong the first time I run it. I expect that some pairs will simply refuse to escalate and settle into polite alternation, that parallel games will finish at wildly different speeds and leave half the room idle, and that at least one player will decline to pay a reputation cost I have no practical way of enforcing — tell me what to do about each, including what to cut if we are running fifteen minutes behind. Then tell me which feature of a real commitment race this activity cannot reproduce and that I will therefore have to supply myself; my guess is the fact that the people who make the commitments are rarely the people who bear the costs of the collisions, but argue the point rather than deferring to my guess, and tell me how much of it belongs in the debrief and how much should stay out. Finally, if you can build in a graded or deliberately ambiguous commitment — one that makes retreat very expensive but stops short of making it impossible — please do, and tell me whether that addition is too subtle for a group encountering the structure for the first time. My own suspicion is that the ambiguous commitment is the one that actually wins these races, and if your design shows that, the session will have taught them something I could not have taught them from the front of the room.
