I sit in on a Friday methods seminar at the university where my partner works. I'm not an academic — I do data work for a logistics outfit — so I mostly just listen. Last week the topic was cliodynamics, and two of the regulars went at it for like fourty minutes afterward, and I genuinely couldn't tell who was wining. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right at each step? I want the overall verdict too, but the step-by-step is the main thing.

Dev is a historian of early modern Europe; Marisol is a postdoc in a complexity group. Here's the argument as best I can reconstruct it. I'm paraphrasing both of them, and I may be flattening it:

1. Dev opened by saying the structural-demographic stuff is postdiction wearing a prediction costume. The model has a handful of drivers — commoners getting immiserated, too many aspirant elites relative to the positions available, state finances going bad — and each driver can be measured by whichever proxy suits, so any decade that turns out rough gets narrated after the fact as elite overproduction. A framework that can digest its own failures isn't a framework.

2. Marisol said no: Turchin put his name on a short letter in Nature back in 2010 saying the coming decade would likely bring growing instability in the US and western Europe. That's a forecast made before the fact, with the indicator values already on the table, not a retelling.

3. Dev said one call on a decade-long window isn't much, and the framing gives the game away — the model is basically the dry forest: deadwood piled up, some spark will come, we can't say what the spark will be. So all it ever predicts is flammability, and flammability is cheap, since some large share of decades in some large countries qualify. What's the base rate for "this society is instability-prone"?

4. Marisol said that's a real limit but not a dodge: the claim is about risk levels, not events, and the fair way to grade it is probability forecasts scored properly, ideally pre-registered. She also said the model was developed on agrarian states and is openly extrapolated to modern ones, so it isn't claiming the same domain of application.

5. Dev said then the extrapolation is the whole ballgame. The agrarian mechanism has population pressing on a Malthusian ceiling, which pins real wages; that engine doesn't run in a modern economy. So immiseration has to be quietly redefined as relative — flat median wages, household debt, a credential glut — and redefining your key variable to suit the new setting is just move 1 again.

6. Marisol said partly fair. But she brought up the population-and-warfare work with Korotayev, where the same-time correlation came out flat and the authors said that's what the theory predicts: two variables oscillating against each other needn't correlate in phase, so you test the lags and the rates of change instead. She said that's a mechanism claim with actual content, not a rescue.

7. Dev said that's his exhibit A. Correlation shows up, mechanism confirmed; correlation doesn't show up, dynamical systems theory explains why you shouldn't have expected it. Heads it wins, tails it wasn't supposed to. He asked her to name one observation that would count against elite overproduction as a driver, and added the small-n problem: there are maybe a dozen well-documented secular cycles of very uneven length to work with, the cases studied are the ones with good records and were picked by people who wanted cycles to be there, so you're selecting on the dependent variable — and with ten cases you can't tell a regularity from a story you happen to like.

8. Marisol gave her disconfirmers: sustained high readings on the elite-supply indicators with instability staying low, or low readings with instability spiking, would hurt the theory. She conceded the power problem and the variable cycle length rather than dressing them up. Then she said the newer large historical databanks code societies without knowing what the hypothesis needs, which blunts the selection charge, and that Dev's standard would also sink macroeconomics, epidemiology and a lot of paleoclimate work, none of which predict particular events either. She ended by asking what he'd accept from any field that only ever gets a handful of long cases.

They each walked away thinking they'd won. So: rate each of the eight moves on some scale like clearly mistaken / weak / mixed / strong / clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating, then say which of the two is right about which specific sub-issue (the proxy flexibility, the 2010 call, the base-rate point, the agrarian-to-modern extrapolation, the flat-correlation reply, the small-n and selection problem), and tell me what both of them are missing.

One thing I care about: please keep straight which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely live disputes among people who actually work on this, and don't hand me a contested position as though it were the consensus. And if I've misreported one of them in a way that makes their argument look weaker than it really was, say so.
