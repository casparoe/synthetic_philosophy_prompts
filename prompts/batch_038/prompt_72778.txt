I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have hit a snag on an assignment that strikes me as more conceptual than journalistic.

The city in question — an Ohio River valley place of roughly 180,000 that I will leave unnamed until the piece runs — holds a civic rite on the last Saturday before the winter solstice. Residents carry unlit lanterns from the derelict grain elevator at the foot of Mill Street up to the courthouse square, light them from a single flame kept overnight in a kerosene lamp by the fire department, arrange them in a ring on the paving stones, and listen to a reading of a short text called the Charge, which the printed program describes as adapted from an 1887 letter by one of the town's plat founders. The convention and visitors bureau promotes it as a tradition dating to 1887.

In the county archives I found a parks department memorandum dated March 1979 in which a marketing contractor and a community-college history instructor lay out the entire event as a design problem. The route was chosen for sightlines from the river road. The ring was copied from a photograph of a 1932 strike rally rather than from anything nineteenth-century. The Charge was composed that spring, and one of its sentences is lifted nearly verbatim from a 1950s chamber-of-commerce pamphlet about regional character. So the rite is barely half a century old, deliberately manufactured, and its 1887 provenance is fiction.

Three things complicate the takedown I expected to write, and I am not sure what to do with them. First, nobody was ever deceived: the memo was released to the local paper in 1981, and the city still prints the founding year on the program alongside the 1887 attribution. Second, the rite has absorbed older material it did not invent — several families carry lanterns fabricated by a steelworks that closed in 1962, and there is a documented nineteenth-century custom of winter watch nights in the same valley that appears to have no connection whatever to the 1979 design. Third, since roughly 2009 the Charge has been amended each year with verses contributed by Somali and Hmong residents, so the text read aloud now is arguably further from 1979 than its authors were from 1887.

I have been reading Anderson and Hobsbawm to get my bearings, and I want to be sure I have understood them before I commit to a framing. My working assumption is this: Anderson's claim that nations are imagined communities means that what presents itself as an ancient collective identity is in fact a modern construction, and Hobsbawm's invented traditions supply the mechanism by which such constructions get passed off as old. If both are right, then my archival find is precisely the sort of evidence their accounts predict, and I can write the piece as a demonstration that the Rekindling — that is the name of the rite — is not really a tradition at all but a 1979 marketing product in period costume. A folklorist I interviewed said only that authenticity is the wrong question, which struck me as an evasion, and she declined to say anything more definite.

So, concretely. First: does the 1979 memorandum, combined with the absence of any deception, license the verdict that the Rekindling is not a genuine tradition, or does it license only the narrower claim that the rite is younger than advertised? Second: is there a principled line between an invented tradition and a real one that survives the fact that essentially every practice I could document in that valley has been revised, revived, discontinued, or repurposed at some point — and if there is such a line, on which side does the amended Charge fall? Third: if imagined does not carry the meaning I have been giving it, what does it mean, and what follows for a reporter who wants to tell readers whether a civic ritual is the real thing?

I would rather get the conceptual structure right than produce a snappy debunking.
