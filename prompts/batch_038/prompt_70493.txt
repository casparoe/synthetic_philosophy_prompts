Hey — I'm a second-year history PhD student and I've spent the last eight months as the de facto project lead on a digitization grant at a county historical society, which is a lot of responsibilty for someone whose actual dissertation is about something else entirely. I have to submit a decision memo by Friday and I want to think it through with you before I write it. Please act as someone who is both a philosopher of history and archival ethics AND has actually processed collections and dealt with funders and advisory boards — I don't want pure theory and I don't want pure workflow pragmatism either. Think carefully. Before you get to a recommendation, spend real time brainstorming, including options I clearly haven't thought of.

Here's the situation. The records are 14 bound registers of people admitted to a county infirmary between 1878 and 1931 — about 3,900 entries total, and the registers call the people "inmates." Roughly a sixth of the entries, call it 620, have no surname at all. They're recorded as "Elias (negro)," or "Mary, from the Whitfield place," or just "colored woman, about 60, sent by Justice Hale." A run of entries in Volume 3 gives only initials. The clerk's hand changes a few times and the later clerks get sloppier. Almost none of those 620 people have any other trace anywhere: nothing in the cemetery index, nothing in the local papers we've checked, no discharge paperwork. The infirmary destroyed its own loose files in 1946, deliberately as far as we can tell — the board minutes just say "old papers destroyed, no further record."

The grant deliverable is a searchable public database, and the funder's entire pitch is descendants finding their people. The schema has a required surname field. The technical team's proposal, which everybody likes because it's simple: for those 620, infer a surname from context — the farm they came from, the justice who committed them, the household they last worked in — enter it in square brackets in the surname field, and normalize the first names (Saml to Samuel, Bett to Betsy/Betty, and so on) so the search actually works. Then attach a note to the record saying the surname is inferred.

The objection came from one member of our community advisory board, a woman whose great-grandmother is probably somewhere in Volume 3. Her point, roughly: you're handing these people the last name of whoever had authority over them, and then you're putting that name on the internet permanently where it will be the only thing anyone ever finds. Her alternative is to transcribe exactly what's on the page, leave the surname field empty or use a placeholder, and let people search the transcribed text and the notes instead.

The technical team says an empty surname field means the record won't come up in a surname search at all, so we've made those 620 people unfindable for precisely the audience the grant exists to serve. And the statewide index we're required to feed into rejects null surnames.

There's a third thing bugging me. While cross-checking I found maybe a dozen cases where the same person plausibly appears twice under two different renderings — "Elias Whitfield" in 1901 and "Elias (col.)" in 1904, similar ages, committed by the same justice. If I assign the inferred surname, those two records will auto-merge in the database. If I don't, they stay two separate people. I have no idea which is true and there's nobody alive to ask. So the naming choice is quietly settling a factual question about somebody's life — how many times they were locked up — and it's settling it by database default instead of by evidence.

So: is the inference defensible? And what do I actually write on Friday?

Please do this in order: (1) genuine brainstorming first — map the option space, including structural or technical fixes that dodge the dilemma entirely, anything involving the schema, how search and ranking behave, how a note could be surfaced to a user rather than buried, how the same problem could be handled differently for different subgroups of entries. Don't just polish the two proposals I handed you. (2) Then tell me what's really at stake in each option, and be precise about who bears the cost, when, and whether it's reversible — I have a nagging sense the two sides aren't disagreeing about the same thing and I can't articulate what the mismatch is. (3) Then give me a recommendation I could defend to the funder, the technical team, and that advisory board member, including draft wording for the note that would appear on the record. (4) Flag what you'd want to know about the registers that I haven't told you. (5) Tell me what would change your mind — what evidence or what argument would push you to the other option.

Two constraints. Don't hand me a reading list or citations, I know the literature already, that's not the help I need. If you think I'm framing this wrong, say so bluntly; I'm attached to my framing but I'd much rather be wrong on Friday than wrong in a public database that's up for twenty years.
