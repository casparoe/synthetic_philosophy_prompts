I cover science and ideas for a general-interest site — mostly neuroscience and AI, but there's alot of overlap with philosophy of mind, and once a quarter my editor lets me run something deliberately silly under our "hard problems" banner. This quarter it's the causal exclusion argument, and I've decided the funniest honest way into it is a corporate HR memo.

What I want: a departmental restructuring / redundancy memo, roughly 700 words, from the head of People and Causal Resources at a large organisation, announcing the findings of a Causal Closure Compliance Audit into the Department of Beliefs, Desires and Intentions. Deadpan corporate register the whole way — the kind of document that opens with a wellbeing paragraph and is full of "in scope," "redeployment," "no change to business as usual." No winking, no asides to the reader, no footnotes inside the memo, and please don't explain the joke anywhere in it.

The target of the satire is the argument's logic, not any person — invent your signatory and your company. The rule I want you to hold yourself to: every absurd thing in the memo has to be a faithful restatement of a move the argument actually makes, so a philosopher of mind reads it and thinks ugh, that's exactly right, rather than that's not the argument. So I'd like it to enact, without ever naming any of it:

- the causal closure premise, delivered as an audit finding to the effect that the physical plant department is already discharging every task on the audited department's job description, with the spreadsheet evidence
- the premise against systematic overdetermination, expressed as company policy — two independent sufficient causes turning up for the same shift, every shift, treated as either a scheduling error or a fraud, plus HR's official line on coincidence
- three options offered to the audited department: full merger/redeployment (which should sound like a promotion and be quietly identity), retention on the roster with a title, a badge, a newsletter and no remit whatsoever (this one should sound generous and read as devastating), and voluntary redundancy with a consultancy arrangement where nothing is ever consulted
- the leftover problem that survives the merger option: even after the department is told it just is the physical department, no one can say which of its outputs were produced in virtue of the job title rather than in virtue of being on the payroll at all. Put this in an appendix or a self-certification form the employees have to fill in, and let it stay genuinely unresolved
- something for the property dualists who declined the consultation and set up a rival firm next door; HR should be courteous about them and visibly unable to explain how they get anything in or out of the building

The comedy should come from the the register colliding with the metaphysics: performance improvement plans for properties that fail to exclude anything, a grievance procedure that has to be filed by the physical department on the mental department's behalf, severance denominated in something appropriately worthless, a farewell card signed by everyone.

Then, out of character and in a separate section, give me about 250 words of notes for me as the writer. Which section of the memo is carrying which premise; where you've taken liberties or leaned on a contested reading (is closure an empirical claim or a stipulation about what counts as a cause, is the no-overdetermination step a premise or something you're smuggling in as a conclusion, does the merger option actually fix the appendix problem or just relocate it). And on that last bit I don't want a both-sides shrug — commit. Tell me which premise you think actually fails and why, in your own voice, with your reasons on the table. If you think none of them fail and the audit is simply correct and my department should pack up its desk, say that and defend it. I need to know where the joke is doing real work and where it's cheating.

Typos welcome in my brief, none in your memo.
