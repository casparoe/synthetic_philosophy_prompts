I am the grader for an upper-level modal metaphysics seminar and want a second opinion on one submission before I enter marks. The assignment question and the student's answer are reproduced in full below.

**Question (1,200 words maximum).** Nadia is a concert violinist in the actual world. Consider two claims about her: (A) Nadia might have broken her left hand as a child; (B) Nadia might never have taken up the violin at all, and be a carpenter instead. Explain how a counterpart theorist and a theorist who accepts transworld identity would each account for the truth of (A) and (B). Then take a position: is the disagreement between these two accounts a disagreement about how things are, or only about how to describe a reality on which both sides already agree? Defend your position.

**Student's answer.** Both accounts grant that (A) and (B) can be true. They disagree about what the truth of these claims consists in.

The transworld identity theorist holds that one and the same individual, Nadia, exists in the actual world and in other possible worlds as well. In some of these worlds she has a broken left hand; in others she is a carpenter. (A) and (B) are therefore true in virtue of how Nadia herself is, in those worlds. The cost is a problem of cross-world identification: nothing about the carpenter's qualities can make her Nadia rather than a merely similar individual, so the theorist needs a non-qualitative ground of identity, a bare thisness that makes the carpenter her.

The counterpart theorist denies that Nadia exists anywhere except in her own world. Individuals are worldbound. (B) is true not because Nadia is a carpenter in some world, but because in some other world there is an individual who resembles Nadia closely in the respects that matter and who is a carpenter. The truth-makers for de re modal claims are thus never the subject herself but her counterparts.

Here the two accounts genuinely diverge. Which resemblances "matter" is not fixed once and for all: the counterpart relation shifts with which respects of similarity the context makes salient. When a childhood injury is salient, the violinist in another world with a broken hand is Nadia's counterpart; when careers are salient, some quite different individual may be. It follows that the counterpart theorist is not after all reporting facts about Nadia's modal properties, but only registering which way of carving up the resemblance facts we find convenient. This is why the dispute cannot be substantive. The two sides agree about what the worlds contain and about which individuals in them have which properties; they differ only in the notation they use to describe that agreement. Saying that Nadia herself is a carpenter in another world is just a more expensive way of saying that a sufficiently similar individual is one.

So the disagreement is verbal, and what would resolve it is not further argument but a decision about which idiom to adopt, the way we decide whether to treat a comparative like "tall" as tracking something real or as a matter of conversational convenience. A middle view, on which one counterpart relation is objectively privileged by nature, is not open to the counterpart theorist: to privilege a single relation would be to say that a given individual just is the same individual spread across worlds, which is the identity view.
