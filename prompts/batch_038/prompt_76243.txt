I work on long-lived infrastructure planning tools, and I've been trying to understand how an unending future breaks ordinary cost–benefit arithmetic. Please construct a thought experiment to the following specification.

Setting: a real decision an actual public body could face — a groundwater allocation, a fisheries quota, a sovereign wealth fund, a transmission corridor — with no deaths, no rights violations, no trolley-problem machinery and no Dyson spheres. Assume time runs on forever in discrete generations, and the choice fixes a welfare level per generation in perpetuity.

The two options must satisfy all of the following, and you must show that they do rather than assert it:
1. Each option's total welfare across all time is infinite, so plain "add it all up" comparison is undefined and neither option can win by having the larger infinite sum.
2. The eventually-ahead rule — A beats B if, from some date onward, A's cumulative welfare exceeds B's at every later date — strictly prefers option A.
3. Discounted utilitarianism, at any rate a real treasury would defend (say 2% to 7% per generation), strictly prefers option B.
4. For every horizon a planning office would actually use — the next two centuries, say — the finite totals favor B, so nothing an ordinary appraisal would catch forces A's victory.

Then the demonstration: give the per-generation numbers explicitly (a formula or a short table will do), the partial-sum arithmetic that produces verdict 2, the discounted arithmetic that produces verdict 3, and an account of what happens to the discounted ranking as the discount rate falls toward zero, including any crossover point. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work, explain what breaks if that feature is removed, and give the discounting defender's best attack on the case — for instance challenging the period length or the comparability of welfare across distant generations — together with your view of whether that attack succeeds.

Format: exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion. Item 1 is the scenario, item 2 the numbers, item 3 the eventually-ahead verdict, item 4 the discounted verdict including the zero-rate limit, and item 5 the feature doing the work plus the discounting defender's best reply. Keep each item to one or two sentences, except the scenario, which may run to three.
