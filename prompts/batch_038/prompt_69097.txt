Thirty-eight years of my working life went into low-temperature transport measurements, and I have come to philosophy only since retiring. I now record an hour of conversation each month for our university's alumni association, where the listeners are intelligent and patient but not professional. My guest next month is a philosopher of physics whose recent book argues from a familiar structuralist premise — that physics tells us what mass and charge do, but stays silent about what underlies the doing — to a conclusion I would describe as pessimist rather than merely panpsychist. She holds that the intrinsic character physics omits is phenomenal, and that much of it is affectively negative; her specific proposal is that negative valence arises wherever a disposition is systematically frustrated, so that suffering is not a late acquisition of nervous systems but a ground-floor feature of the world. From this she draws a moral consequence of considerable scale: that the hedonic ledger of the cosmos is overwhelmingly non-biological, and that human and animal welfare work, however admirable, addresses a rounding error.

Please write my questions rather than her answers: nine or ten of them, in the order you recommend, with a sentence or two justifying that order. Each should come with the single plain sentence of setup I must say first, so that a listener who has never met the structuralist premise knows what is at issue; a follow-up keyed to the answer I expect her to give; and a brief note on what a genuinely good reply would sound like, since I will be hearing her in real time and want to know whether I have made contact or merely been polite. I have no interest in biographical questions, in anything that invites her to restate the view, or in anything a well-read listener could answer on her behalf from memory. One caution about her style: she tends to dispose of demands for specifics by observing that the questioner has smuggled in the vocabulary of the very picture she disputes — mechanism, measurement, function. Two of your questions should therefore be built so that this reply has nothing to grip on: framed entirely out of materials she has already granted, namely her own criterion of frustrated dispositions and her own moral conclusion.

The pressure points I want worked, though you may merge or reorder them as you judge best: first, the step from silence about intrinsic natures to a claim about their hedonic tone — why suppose the ground floor is valenced at all rather than phenomenally neutral, and if valenced, why negative rather than positive; is that asymmetry an argument or an intuition carried over from our own case. Second, her criterion itself: the frustration of a disposition is already a structural and dispositional description, which looks like the return of exactly the functional vocabulary she says cannot capture experience; and does it not make suffering pervasive precisely where nothing appears to be going wrong — a lattice settling into equilibrium, say. Third, combination, but for value rather than for consciousness: if this suffering is owned by no one — no unified perspective, no memory of it, no aversion to it — in what sense is it bad, and bad for whom. Fourth, weighting: whether an enormous quantity of unowned micro-suffering can outweigh a small quantity of intensely owned suffering, and what she says if she answers that no single subject contains both, so no comparison is available to make. Fifth, a colleague who grants her structural premise entirely but insists that so far as we can tell the intrinsic natures are neither good nor bad: is her disagreement with him substantive or merely temperamental, and what settles it. Sixth, the practical consequence — we cannot act at the fundamental level, so does her view license quietism about the suffering we can reach, or the opposite verdict that the physical universe would be better without, which no one is in any position to act upon; I want both horns put to her in one question.

I should tell you what I find hardest about her position, in case it is usable. In my old trade a claim that could not be distinguished from its rivals by any measurement was not yet a hypothesis, and I confess that instinct still governs me. I also recognize that it may be crude here: a claim about intrinsic natures is arguably not the kind of claim a measurement settles, and I have no wish to embarrass myself by demanding a laboratory test of something never offered as an empirical theory. I therefore want one question that forces the distinction between those two readings — that her thesis is empirically idle, versus that it belongs to a category for which empirical idleness is no objection — rather than letting the conversation slide across it. Close with a plain question about what would count as evidence against her view, and mark which single question I should cut if we run long.
