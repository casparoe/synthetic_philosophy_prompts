I TA a first-year metaphysics section and next week we're doing states of affairs and the unity of the proposition, and honestly the readings left me unsatisfied in both directions — the regress gets waved away in a sentence or treated as a knockdown refutation, and nobody in the papers I assigned actually sits with it. I want to hand the keener students a short dialogue to pick apart in section, and I'd like you to write it for me.

Two people, both sharp, neither a strawman — I'd picture them in an empty seminar room with a whiteboard that still has "Romeo / loving / Juliet" scrawled on it as a list. Call them whatever you like. One holds that a fact really is a complex: particulars and universals plus a tie that binds them, and that the tie isn't itself a further relation on the list, so the regress either never starts or, when it does, is harmless in the way that any infinite regress can be. The other holds that this is precisely the dodge — that if you can't say what the tie is without making it another item, then the whole build-it-up-out-of-constituents picture is wrong, and that you have to start with an already-unified whole and treat the "constituents" as abstractions from it rather than parts that get assembled. I want the exchange built on two cases they both have to swollow. First, direction: on the first character's own ontolgy, Romeo loves Juliet and Juliet loves Romeo have exactly the same items, and they plainly don't say the same thing, so she owes an account of where the difference lives — and watch what happens to any ordering item she adds to fix it. Second, the bill on the other side: if unity is primitive, the second character loses the thing that made the compositional picture worth having, namely that sharing constituents is what explains why some propositions entail others and why inference gets a grip at all, and the first character should press that hard more than once rather than just noting it. At some point one of them has to ask the question I think decides the whole business — what makes a regress vicious rather than merely infinite, since there are obviously harmless ones running around — and then the second character needs a story about why this one differs while the first claims hers is benign in exactly the same way.

Neither should win outright, but by the end I want it clear what each is paying, and I'd like one of them to voice the worry that the two positions might have collapsed into the same claim said two ways — a tie you refuse to treat as a relation versus a unity you refuse to analyse — with the other one trying, and maybe failing, to show there's a real difference. Hard cap of 750 words for everything. Before the dialogue, give me two sentences, one per character, stating flatly what each is committed to, so I can check whether the students read it right. No bibliography, no suggested readings, and don't have them quote anyone — a name dropped in passing is fine, but the whole thing should run on argument, not authority.
