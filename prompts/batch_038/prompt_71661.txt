My mom moved in with us in March. Early-onset-ish, though nobody will commit to a label, and she's now at the stage where she still writes shopping lists and still says "let me put that down," but the loop is broken — she writes it, and then she can't use it, and three days later she finds the list and it doesn't mean anything to her. That detail has been rattling around in my head alongside something I've been reading for fun, and I've started an essay for a public-philosophy site that runs about 1,500 words. I've got maybe 1,100 written. Before I finish it I want someone to tell me whether the spine of it actually holds up, because I've gone back and forth enough that I can't tell conviction from attachment anymore.

The framework I'm using is Dennett's four kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures (flexibility built by selection), Skinnerian creatures (reshaped by their own history of reward and punishment), Popperian creatures (able to run the options internally so a bad plan gets killed before it's tried), and Gregorian creatures (importing design work from outside the skull — language, notation, writing, calendars, and the whole shared pool of tools). I'm not going to introduce it from scratch in the essay, the venue's readers mostly know it.

Here's the draft. Comments on it, please, after.

---

Dennett's taxonomy is usually presented as a ladder, and the picture that comes with it is ascent: matter that could only be shaped by genes learns to be shaped by consequences, then learns to shape itself in imagination before acting, and finally starts borrowing brains from its culture. I want to suggest the ladder runs down too, and that descent is not ascent reversed.

Read the four kinds as an inventory of competences — can it learn from outcomes, can it plan, can it use symbols — and decline looks like peeling layers off in order. What's left at the bottom should be a cleaner, more primitive mind: first the symbols go, then the planning, then the conditioning, then the reflexes. Watching my mother, I don't think that's what's happening, and I don't think the inventory reading can even describe what is happening.

My mother still writes lists. The notebook is on the fridge where it has always been, the pen is attached to it with a string, and she reaches for both without hesitation. The tool is present and the habit of reaching for the tool is intact. What's gone is the far side of the loop: the written line no longer functions as a prompt to her later self. So she is not, as people say, losing her tools. She's keeping them. She is what I want to call tool-widowed: the tools outlive the competence that made them tools, and the household keeps setting the table for a guest who can't read the invitation.

The claim I want to defend is that the Gregorian layer is not an addition stacked on top of the others but load-bearing for them. Her Skinnerian habits were never free-standing operant learning in a bare environment. They were habits organized around the kettle that clicks, the pill box with the days printed on it, the calendar in the hall with the granddaughter's name in a square, the chair by the window at four o'clock because that's when the light is good and also when the paper used to come. What looks like a preserved capacity for simple routine is really a capacity to be tuned by scaffolding that other people maintain. When the tools go dark — not removed, just no longer readable — the Skinnerian layer doesn't get revealed underneath in pristine form. It loses its inputs. That's why the decline comes out of order, and why it comes faster after a move than after a diagnosis: the move didn't take a competence away, it unmoored the environment the competence was running on.

The obvious objection is that this is just the extended-mind and scaffolding literature with an anecdote stapled to it. Cognition spills into notebooks; minds are scaffolded; we've known since the nineties. Two differences, I think. That literature mostly concerns intact agents who offload successfully — the case that does the work is the one where the agent reliably consults the notebook and trusts what's in it. The interesting pathology is coupling that persists physically and fails functionally. And the extended-mind argument is usually aimed at a metaphysical question about where the mind stops, whereas mine is aimed at a design question: which stratum supplies the flexibility, and what happens when that stratum goes. The second difference is the one I'm least sure of.

The other thing the descent case teaches is that build order isn't necessity. If the Gregorian layer were a natural capstone that presupposes a Popperian one, you shouldn't be able to have the top without the second-from-top. But we are currently building things that look like exactly that: systems trained on the sedimented products of a Gregorian culture and tuned by feedback into fluency with those products. They handle our symbols, our notation, our argument forms, our genres. And there's no reason to think they run the kind of internal model whose candidate actions get killed before they're tried — the tuning is Skinnerian in method and Gregorian in content, with the Popperian middle skipped. If that's right the ladder describes one historical route to a mind, not a dependency graph anyone has to obey. My mother's case is the mirror image: the top goes dark while the strata that were always parasitic on it fail out of sequence.

Which leaves an unflattering practical point. If the tools are constitutive rather than assistive, then clearing the clutter because she doesn't use it anymore is a category error, and leaving the calendar on the wall isn't a courtesy. But the harder half is that the scaffolding is now maintained by us, which means part of her mind is running on our labor and can be switched off by our exhaustion. The taxonomy has no slot for distributed upkeep. Neither, it turns out, does the care plan.
