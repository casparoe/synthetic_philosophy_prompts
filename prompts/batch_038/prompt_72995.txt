my dad died in March. For the last six years of his life he wasn't angry at god or in revolt or anything dramatic — he just couldn't find him. He'd have believed in a second if anything at all had shown up. He kept saying grace out of habit and told me once that it felt like talking into a disconnected phone. My mum and I have been going round the standard argument ever since: if there's a loving god there shouldn't be honest people in that position, and the answers people give — that a god who showed himself unmistakably would overwhelm us and wreck the freedom to love him, that a father who is always visibly in the room never finds out whether his children stay because they want to — those answers might be right, and they also seem to cost exactly the person they're supposed to save.

What I want is a short story, fifteen hundred words give or take, that takes the second answer seriously from the inside. Tell it from the far side: the one who is hiding. He's arranged the whole thing himself — small untraceable provisions, a life kept running from behind a wall, no messages, nothing that could be mistaken for a sign, because a sign is the one thing he can't afford — and after years of it he finally gets what he wanted: some overheard sentence, or one small unobserved act, that proves the love coming back to him isn't coerced, isn't gratitude aimed at a visible benefactor. And that's the moment the trap shuts, because returning now would make the whole thing conditional after the fact; its the absence that made it real, the only version of the love he could ever have wanted. The horror should be that the plan worked. I want the reader to feel the door lock rather than be told about it.

A few things I care about. Nobody gives a speech about theodicy or explains the setup — the situation has to be legible from what he does and what he notices. Don't use the word god anywhere in it and don't let the narrator theologise; call him whatever he'd call himself, or nothing. Keep it domestic and concrete, the kind of detail a man who has done this for twenty years would remember: the sound of one particular latch, a bill settled in someone else's handwriting, the weather on a specific afternoon. And please don't resolve it — no revelation, no reunion, and especially no irony where it turns out the other person knew all along, which is the ending I'm most afraid of. If there's any grace in the thing, let it be the grace of someone who keeps doing the work after it has stopped meaning what it meant.

Just the story, no title and no note afterwards about your choices. And no formatting of any kind in the response: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose only, the way you'd write a letter.
