Here's the passage, roughly the whole treatment he gives it:

'A team of AI-safety researchers finally put a price on mathematical uncertainty. Their trick is a market. Every sentence in a formal language gets its own stock, which pays out a dollar if the sentence is true and nothing if it's false, and each day the reasoner posts a price for every stock — that price just is its probability. Then they turn loose on this market every trading strategy you could write down in polynomial time, and they demand that none of them can earn unbounded profit while risking a bounded amount of money. Any sequence of prices that survives that test is what they call a logical inductor, and they proved one exists — more than that, they proved you can compute one. Everything else drops out of that single no-arbitrage condition: the reasoner learns to price "the nth digit of pi is a 7" at about a tenth, learns to trust its own future opinions more than its present ones, and ends up coherent in the limit. Notice what this does to Gödel. Proof cares about derivations; probability only cares about truth, so a logical inductor is perfectly free to price Peano arithmetic's own Gödel sentence at a dollar — the thing is true, after all, even though no derivation exists — and nothing in the framework complains. Structurally it's the old Dutch-book argument pointed the other way: Ramsey and de Finetti showed your degrees of belief are coherent if and only if no bookie can make you a guaranteed loser, and this team showed your beliefs about mathematics are rationally uncertain if and only if no fast algorithm can arbitrage you. And it isn't just a philosophy result. On any given day the prices are actual numbers a computer spits out, and those numbers are what a reasonable agent's confidence in the twin prime conjecture ought to be.'

His payoff line, a few pages later, is that this is why we should take an AI's stated confidence in a conjecture as a genuine probability rather than a vibe, and that a machine can be "appropriately unsure" about math in exactly the way we can't get a proof checker to be.
