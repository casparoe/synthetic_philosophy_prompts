So I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and I'm sitting on a piece that has me weirdly stuck on a question my editor thinks is a total nothing-burger. Here's the setup. A grad student cold-emailed me a reanalysis of one of those findings that everyone on my beat has cited for a decade — the one about a scheduling practice at work supposedly producing a big, measurable drop in burnout. I spent three weeks going thru their code and data with two people who don't know each other, and both said it's clean, that the original effect basically evaporates once you fix how the missing data was handled. As far as I can tell, they're right. But I don't actually believe it. And I don't mean I have some counter-evidence tucked away — I interviewed the original authors twice and their defenses were embarrassing — I mean I've been covering this area for eleven years, I've stood in a dozen labs, and there's just a part of me that cannot get to "the finding is false." If my wife asks me at dinner whether the thing is real, I find myself saying well, the reanalysis is solid, which is not an answer. Legal read the draft and cleared it, but they did say it's the sort of story that, if we're wrong, ends up in a defamation demand rather than a correction, so the stakes aren't nothing.

Two complications that make this feel less like a personal hangup and more like a real problem. First, our house style bans hedging in the nut graf — the copy desk will strike "suggests," "appears to," "raises questions about," all of it — so what's going to print is a flat declarative sentence under my byline saying the effect isn't there. Second, I tried to solve it by attributing rather than saying it myself, i.e. writing that the reanalysis shows the effect isn't there, and my editor killed that version on the grounds that it reads as cowardice and that if I'm going to run it I have to own it. Meanwhile I also do a podcast version of the column, and I know the host will ask me whether I personally buy it, and I'm fully planning to say no — the evidence points one way, my gut doesn't. That answer feels fine to me, honest even, almost virtuous. It's the print sentence that feels like I'm doing something I have no standing to do, even tho everything I have points exactly that way. A friend of mine who does philosophy said "oh, that's a case" and then changed the subject, which was useless.

So, help me out, because I keep going back and forth and I want to know whether there's an actual rule here that I'm breaking or just squeamishness dressed up as scruple. Is it any worse to print a flat claim I don't believe when my evidence is as good as evidence gets, than it would be to print one I do believe on that same evidence — does what's going on in my head enter into whether I'm allowed to say it, or does only the truth and the quality of my reasons matter? Is the attribution version I got overruled on actually a different kind of act, or am I just saying the same thing in a font that lets me feel better? And what about the podcast: if I go on record saying I don't believe the sentence I published, does that undercut the publication, or is it the one part of this whole thing that's clean? Finally — should I just kill the story, and would killing it be the bigger failure than running it? I'd rather have an answer that tells me what standard applies and where exactly I sit relative to it than a pep talk about my feelings.
