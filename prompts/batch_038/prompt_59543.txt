I've talked myself into a view of money that's basically Berkeley's in The Querist (1735–37), and now I want to know exactly how much trouble it's in.

The view, as I hold it. Money is not wealth and never was: the true idea of it is "altogether that of a ticket or counter" (Query 23), and its entire usefulness consists in the fact that it "stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour" (Query 5). From that I take four things to follow, and I want to defend all four:
(1) quantity on its own is nearly irrelevant — Berkeley's point that less money circulating swiftly is equivalent to more circulating slowly (Query 22) is the whole story, not a cute aside;
(2) a country with no gold or silver can be exactly as rich as one with vaults full of it, provided some token is actually circulating — the denominations can be retained "although the bullion were gone" (Query 26) — which is why he was pushing a national bank issuing paper;
(3) hoarding isn't thrift but a kind of economic pathology, since a token that isn't moving isn't performing money's function;
(4) there's no such thing as money's intrinsic value, and store-of-value isn't a separate function that trades off against medium of exchange — it's the same labor-motivating function viewed across time.

So: what would count against this? I don't want the "Berkeley ignored X" book report, I want the exposure map.

- Which actual monetary phenomena are hard or flatly impossible to describe on this view, as opposed to merely awkward for it? I have in mind things like what happens when people quietly stop using a token and start pricing in a different one — does (4) leave me any way to describe that other than as mismanagement?
- Which bits of later analytical machinery (the theory, not the historiography) would a defender say the view survives, and which would they concede it doesn't? Be specific about which of (1)–(4) the concession lands on.
- The self-defeat worry I keep coming back to: if money's only job is to prompt industry, then a planner who could coordinate industry directly wouldn't need money at all, so on my view money's existence is just a stand-in for bad coordination. Is that a bug, or is it the point? And what would have to be true for money to be doing something I can't reduce to labor-motivation?
- Where this overlaps with chartalism, credit theories of money, and the anti-bullionist side of those 18th c. debates: does the family resemblance shield the view, or expose it to objections aimed at those cousins?
- Does the position risk anything at all? If I retreat to "any collapse is a collapse of implmentation, the tokens were mismanaged," at what point does that make the view unfalsifiable — and given it's half a theory of value and half a policy program, is that fatal for a position of this kind or is it acceptable?

Rank the defeaters from "I'd give the view up" to "I'd absorb it with a qualification," and tell me which ones a committed Berkeleian would themselves concede as counting against them rather than explain away.
