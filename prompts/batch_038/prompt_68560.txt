so I'm about four months into a job at a company that builds land-registry software — the pitch is that the whole chain of title for a parcel goes on an append-only ledger, so a buyer can see every transfer back to the first recorded deed and a court cant quietly rewrite history afterwards. Half our early customers are in places where the paper records are genuinely patchy, and there's a running internal argument about what the system should do when it hits a gap. One faction wants the parcel flagged "unverified" permanently: if any link is missing or looks tainted — a forged signature, a transfer recorded by a regime that confiscated land from a minority community sixty years ago, whatever — the flag stays and every later buyer takes the parcel subject to whoever shows up with the older claim. The other faction wants a lookback window: after, say, twelve years of open, uncontested possession the system mints a clean title and stops surfacing older claims entirely. Our CTO backs the second one and keeps saying he's just "matching what the law already does," and when I actually went and read some property law, I think he's right, which is what's bugging me.

because the law does do that, and it does it in several different places at once. There's adverse possesion, where someone who occupies land openly for the statutory period just becomes the owner and the paper owner's title evaporates. There's the separate limitation period on actions to recover land. There's the treatment of cash and cheques — as I understand it, if a thief steals your banknotes and spends them at a shop that has no idea where they came from, you can't get the money back from the shop, the claim dies right there even though the shop did nothing to earn it. And there's bankruptcy discharge, where a debt that is plainly owed gets cancelled by collective decision and the creditor's claim is gone for good. But then the stolen-car case runs the other way: if I buy a car in total good faith from a thief who looked like a normal dealer, in a lot of common-law places I don't get title at all, and the true owner can take the car back however long afterwards, and apparently even from a fifth good-faith buyer down the line. So the same legal system says the history of a holding matters and travels with the object, and also that the history stops mattering after a while, or at an innocent purchaser, or when a court declares it dead. Which is it?

I've been reading Nozick on the side — entitlement theory, justice in acquisition, justice in transfer, rectification, and the Wilt Chamberlain business about how any patterned principle has to keep interfering with people's voluntary choices to stay patterned — and it looks to me like his is exactly the view that takes the history of a holding seriously, which would make these doctrines straight-up incompatible with it rather than merely awkward for it. Is that the right read, or can an entitlement theorist absorb adverse possession and the cash rule through the rectification principle without giving the game away? I want you to reconstruct what theory of property rights this bundle of doctrines actually presupposes, and to be careful to separate what it strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with — like, maybe it doesn't need any utilitarianism about title at all, maybe all it needs is the claim that the content of a property right is whatever a publicly knowable rule scheme says it is, and maybe even that's too much. Tell me where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, i.e. where two rival theories would both license the same doctrines, and tell me whether the cash rule and the car rule come out of one principle or whether that's a real inconsistency in the practice rather than in my description of it. Also relevant to the day job: our ledger is append-only, so it structurally cannot undo a transfer, only add a new one on top, and we market that as a feature because it means we never make moral judgements about who really owns what. Does that design choice quietly settle the question in one direction? And what would our system concretely have to start doing — what would it have to be able to represent that it currently can't — if the "unverified forever" faction is right that title is a matter of moral history rather than of institutional rules?
