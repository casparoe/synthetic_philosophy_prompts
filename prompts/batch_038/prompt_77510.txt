For four years I have been drafting a long essay about what happens to certain words once a legal duty is bolted to them, and I have hit a wall. I can describe a pattern I keep meeting, but I cannot tell whether it is one pattern or three, and I do not know whether the people who study this have already named it. I trained as a lawyer, not as an academic, so I may be missing something obvious, and I would rather be told that plainly than be handed a bibliography — I cannot chase references down at the moment, and I am not asking for any. What I want is the conceptual carving.

Stated flatly, the pattern is this: when a label is defined in law such that applying it triggers an action-forcing consequence, the argument about whether the label applies stops being an argument about the facts and becomes an argument about what applying the label would commit us to. The descriptive question is quietly exchanged for a question about the cost of the answer, and the exchange is not noticed as an exchange; both sides go on talking as though they were still disputing what happened.

Two instances I keep returning to, with the facts as I understand them so that you need not look anything up.

First, Rwanda. While the killing was underway, American officials were, by their own later accounts, careful not to say the word in public, and the reason given afterward was not evidential but practical: a public finding would create pressure to act, and possibly an obligation to act under the treaty, and there was no intention of acting. Spokespeople pressed on the point produced strained formulations — conceding the mass killing, then distinguishing what had occurred from the legal term. I know the word was coined precisely because no existing term covered the pattern, and that the treaty definition that followed was narrower than its author wanted; the narrowing is part of why the label now carries so much weight, and so much consequence, on its own.

Second, the interrogation debates of the early 2000s. What reached the public was framed almost entirely as a question about what would follow from the classification: if this technique counts, the option disappears, and with it intelligence we may need. Very little of the public argument engaged the prior question of whether the technique in fact produces the kind and degree of suffering the definition speaks of; and where it did engage, the question was often recast as one about what the person applying the technique meant to bring about, which conveniently moved the discussion away from what was being done to the person on the table.

Third, milder, and closer to my own material: whenever a body is deciding whether to attach consequences to a label, witnesses and drafters spend most of their time on the consequences rather than on the criteria, and afterward everyone says the label was "settled."

So: does this have a name? I am aware that people write about the reluctance to use the G-word, and that there is a general worry about atrocity language being stretched until it means nothing. I am also aware of a broad idea that how much you need in order to properly say something might depend on what turns on it. None of these is what I am after. I want the specific thing: the displacement of a classification question by a question about the consequences of classifying, in a debate where the displacement goes unremarked by everyone taking part.

I also need you to tell me whether it is one phenomenon or several. As best I can sort my own confusion, it might be (a) an epistemic matter — how much proof you need before you may say something goes up when a great deal rides on the answer; (b) a dialectical one — the topic of the argument has changed without anyone announcing it; (c) an institutional one — a predictable pathology of drafting definitions so that classification entails duties, which means the fault lies in the drafting and not in the speakers; or (d) something about the saying itself — that uttering the label is a kind of doing, an undertaking rather than a report, so that no amount of evidence settles it independently of what you thereby sign up to. My suspicion is that these come apart, and that my instances are not all instances of the same one.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order, and keep to the rough lengths.

1. **Short answer** (about 100 words). Is there an established name, yes or no, and your best single-sentence statement of what the phenomenon is.

2. **One phenomenon or several** (about 300 words). Sort my options (a) through (d). Say which of them each of my two main instances actually exhibits, and whether they are instances of the same thing at all.

3. **The nearest concepts** (about 400 words). Three to five candidates, each with a plain statement of what it claims and a precise account of where it stops short of what I have described. Do not pad this with background on atrocity politics or on the elements of the legal definition; assume I know both.

4. **Near misses** (about 300 words). Distinguish the phenomenon from each of the following, and say in one or two sentences per item why it is close and why it is not the thing: plain equivocation between a strict legal sense and a loose moral sense; the strategic over-application of a label until it loses its force; ordinary caution about asserting what you cannot yet prove; dismissing the person who uses the label on account of their incentives; and the lawyerly habit of construing a text narrowly to avoid a hard question it would otherwise raise.

5. **A name and a definition** (about 250 words). If nothing established fits, coin a term. Define it with something like conditions rather than a gesture, and give me one case that falls just outside the definition so I can see where the edges are.

6. **Defect or feature** (about 200 words). Is this displacement a bug in how we name atrocities, or is it what makes action-forcing labels usable at all? Commit to one, and say what would have to be true for the other answer to be right.

One further question, answered briefly at the end of section 6 if you have room: is there anything about my description that suggests I have simply failed to notice a distinction that everyone in the relevant literature treats as obvious? If so, name the distinction rather than sparing my feelings.
