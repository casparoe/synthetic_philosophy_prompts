Third year reading group on consciousness, and basically our whole argument about panpsychism keeps turning on one sentence my prof repeats constantly: "physics tells us what matter does, not what matter is." He gets it to do at least three jobs at once — first to show physics leaves something out (some intrinsic nature sitting behind all the causal roles), then to argue that the only candidate for that leftover is experience, since experience is the one case where we're acquainted with a property from the inside rather than from its effects, and finally, last week, to wave off the combination problem, on the grounds that combination only looks like a problem if you think of intrinsic natures as stuff that has to add up, which he insists the slogan doesn't commit you to.

I'm pretty sure one sentence can't carry all three. Can you lay out as a menu the distinct precise theses it could be expressing? I want the epistemic/methodological ones (claims about what physics as a discipline describes, or what we could know from it) cleanly separated from the ontological ones (that all physical properties are purely dispositional; that there is a categorical base under the dispositions; that properties are individuated by their causal roles so a property with no causal role isn't a physical property at all; the relationalist option one guy in the group keeps pushing, that "what it is when it isn't interacting" is just an empty phrase). For each reading: how strong is it, would mainstream philosophy of physics actually grant it, and does it license the second step from "physics is silent about X" to "X is phenomenal," or does that step collapse into an inference from ignorance? Then say which reading, if any, actually dissolves the combination problem instead of relocating it, and flag any pairs of readings that are mutually exclusive — I'm near certain he's sliding between two of them mid-argument and I want to name it.
