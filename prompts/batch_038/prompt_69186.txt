Last night my friend Dana and I got into a nearly three-hour arguement about humanitarian intervention that started because a coworker recommended a documentary about Libya in 2011 and we watched it over dinner. I'm not in poli sci — I do intake at a legal aid clinic — so I kept feeling like I was missing something, and by two in the morning I mostly just felt talked past. The phrase she kept reaching for was "illegal but legitimate," which she says is what everyone reaches for whenever Kosovo comes up. My problem is that she seemed to use it in three completely different senses in one evening, and when I told her so she said I was nitpicking.

Move one: Kosovo '99, NATO bombs without Security Council authorization because of the veto threat, and she says illegal but legitimate, full stop, clearly meaning it was the right thing to do. Move two: I bring up Iraq '03 and she says that one doesn't get either half of the phrase — not legal, and not legitimate either — and the difference, she says, is that nobody credible outside the coalition vouched for it. Move three: Libya, and she gets uneasy. Her line was that 1973 authorized protecting civilians, that the campaign slid into helping topple Gaddafi, and that the "legitimate" part was supplied by the same governments flying the sorties plus a handful of abstentions she refuses to count as endorsement. Then when I pushed on whether she'd just defined legitimacy as whatever the interveners say about themselves, she came out with: when a war is illegal, calling it legitimate is just a polite way of saying you're glad they did it. Pretty cynical thing for a person to say and then immediately go back to insisting Kosovo was legitimate.

So can you take that one phrase apart for me? I want the menu of precise claims it could be making, not a single gloss — the boring conceptual point that law and morality come apart all the time, the straight moral-verdict reading, whatever the difference is between saying an act was justified and saying it was excused-but-still-wrong (I have a feeling that distinction is doing work here and I don't know how to spell it out), the reading where legitimate just reports who accepted it, the reading where it's really an indictment of the Charter with an implied demand that the law change, and any others I'm not seeing. For each: is it a platitude everyone should accept, a substantive claim that has to be argued for, or actually false? Then tell me which reading Dana needed at each of her three moments — is there one reading that lets her keep Kosovo, dump Iraq, and be uneasy about Libya all at once, or does she have to switch senses mid-sentence to pull it off? And does her cynical jab survive her own use of the phrase, or does it undercut her Kosovo verdict? I dont need sources or a bibliography, I just want to be able to say something more precise than "you're using that word three ways" next time we pick this up.
