I have just been seated on the energy commission of a small low-lying state, and our opening session is devoted to a single choice: a French-style path — one standardized, state-backed, vendor-supplied reactor displacing most of our coal and diesel generation — or a German-style path — no nuclear, renewables and storage, with coal and gas carrying the interim decades. The commission is split evenly, but not along the lines anyone expected: the members invoking the precautionary principle are the ones pressing for the reactor, and the members reasoning in expected-value terms are the ones urging us to decline it. I want to circulate a framing paper that makes that inversion precise rather than rhetorical.

Please act as a philosopher of risk regulation with real policy experience, and think carefully before writing. First, in three or four sentences, sketch what structural features of a decision problem could drive the two frameworks apart in this direction; only then commit to a case.

Specifications for the thought experiment:

- One invented but entirely realistic small state. No science fiction, no exotic technology, no unusual geology, no invented physics of climate.
- The scenario itself runs to a single paragraph; the analysis follows separately.
- You choose the numbers — probabilities, costs, damages, share of global emissions — and both frameworks must be handed exactly the same set. The divergence may not come from differing estimates or differing discount rates smuggled into the description.
- Cost-benefit analysis must conclude: do not build. The precautionary principle must conclude: build. Neither verdict may be asserted in the scenario; each has to fall out of that framework's own logic applied to the shared numbers.
- The state's own abatement must be genuinely marginal at the global scale, and both the climate harm and the reactor's accident harm must be irreversible in some respectable sense. The case may not be won by pretending that only one of them is.

Then deliver the following:

1. Each framework's reasoning in full, showing explicitly where every number enters.
2. The single feature of the case that produces the reversal, named and defended as doing the work.
3. One perturbation that removes that feature and collapses the two verdicts into agreement, together with an account of which framework moves and why.
4. The strongest attack a cost-benefit theorist can mount on the precautionary verdict — no strawman — and an honest judgment on whether the case survives it.
