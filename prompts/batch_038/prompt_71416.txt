I've talked myself into a position on Aquinas and tyrannicide that I'd like properly stress-tested. Roughly: that there's no real contradiction in him, and the appearance of one comes from runing two separate questions together.

The reconciliation I've got in mind goes like this. In the Summa's treatment of sedition he argues that tyrannical rule isn't just, because it's directed at the ruler's own good rather than the common good, and so disturbing it isn't sedition — though I seem to remember he attaches a proviso about the disturbance not doing more harm than the tyranny itself. Meanwhile in the articles on killing he holds that a private person may not kill, since doing so for the safety of the community belongs to whoever has charge of the community, on that part-to-whole reasoning about cutting off a decayed member. So: the question of *who* may act is settled by public commission, not by the target's title; and where a ruler has no title at all — a plain usurper — there's no bond of obedience to disturb, so resisting him isn't the thing sedition names. I'm fairly sure that distinction between a defect in title and tyrannical exercise comes from the jurists rather than from Thomas, so I know I'm reconstructing. And the nervous bits in his little work on kingship about private people taking matters into their own hands I'd read as prudence about consequences — you tend to end up with something worse — rather than a claim of principle.

What would count against all that? I want the defeaters sorted, fatal versus merely awkward, and I want to know which ones a serious defender of the consistency reading would themselves concede and which they'd wave off.

Places I suspect it's exposed, tell me if i'm wrong:

- If the ban on private killing really does turn on the absence of commission rather than on the target's title, then the usurper move buys nothing at all: the lone assassin of a usurper is still uncommissioned. Does my view have any answer?
- Who exactly is the multitude that can act? A majority, the leading citizens, the whole body? And if the multitude can commission somebody, is that public authority or just a very large private one — does the distinction survive the question?
- The harm proviso looks like it could make the whole thing unactionable: you can always say the disturbance would have done more damage, and you only find out afterwards. Is that a genuine problem for a view of this kind, or is a checkable-in-hindsight proviso fine?
- What would actually be decisive here — a passage where he flatly denies any right against a usurper, say, or his treatment of the Church's power to depose a ruler, which might just relocate the authority instead of answering the question?
- Chronology: if the consistency reading needs the texts to be pulling the same way at the same time, how much weight should I give to evidence of a change of mind, or of the kingship discussion being early, unfinished, or possibly not his?

And then: what does the consistency reading predict that the rival — that he simply never settled it — doesn't? If it turns out the reading has been qualified to the point where nothing could count against it, say so plainly, and tell me whether that's a problem in this case or just what reading a medieval theologian charitably requires.

No reading lists or citations please, I can't look anything up right now. But if a step in your answer is leaning on your memory of what the text actually says, flag that as you go.
