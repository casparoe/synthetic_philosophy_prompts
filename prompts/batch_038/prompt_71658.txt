For thirty-one years I directed a laboratory that measured trace contaminants in marine organisms, and most of my working life consisted of establishing what a given measurement could and could not support. Since retiring I have turned to moral philosophy, where I have become absorbed by the dispute over whether an individual act can be wrong when it makes no discernible contribution to a collective harm — the single portion of fish, the single tank of fuel, the single ballot. I convene a reading group that contains two committed camps on this question, and after a year of circular argument I have asked them to undertake an adversarial collaboration in writing. Your task is to draft the document they ought jointly to sign.

The parties are as follows. The first, whom I shall call the inefficacy camp (A), holds that for a large class of individually negligible acts there is no individual causal contribution to be found: aggregate harm is generated through thresholds, quotas, standing contracts and forecasting systems, so the ordinary retail purchase neither brings about nor hastens any harm at all, and a moral theory that condemns such a purchase is condemning an event that does not occur. The second, the expected-contribution camp (B), holds that A collapses determinate contribution into expected contribution: even within a thresholded system the probability that a particular purchase is the one that tips the system across a boundary is small but non-zero, and a small probability multiplied by a large harm yields a real expected harm properly attributed to the act — which, B insists, is precisely what makes the aggregate of such acts the disaster that it is.

To prevent either side from retreating to the stock examples, I have supplied two cases from my own field, and I want the document written about them rather than about cases in the abstract.

Case 1 (quantised ordering). A supermarket chain stocks a frozen fish product. Store-level demand is aggregated by an algorithm that rounds each store's daily demand to the nearest five hundred portions before it reaches the distribution centre; the centre then replenishes in fixed pallets of three thousand portions against a standing quarterly contract with the processor; the processor's seasonal throughput is fixed in advance by quota and by plant capacity, so that a pallet delivered later in the quarter is shifted in time rather than added to the catch. Question: does one customer's purchase of one portion stand in any causal relation to any fish being caught, and does it alter the expected number caught?

Case 2 (continuous accumulation). The same fishery's processing vessels discharge a coolant that disperses through a semi-enclosed basin and degrades with a half-life of years, so that the standing concentration is effectively the time-integral of all discharges. There is no threshold, no quota and no quantisation: each kilogram discharged raises the concentration by a fixed tiny increment, and biological damage is a smooth increasing function of concentration. Question: does a single vessel's single discharge stand in a causal relation to any particular organism's being damaged?

I require the following components.

1. A numbered section of claims that both parties can sign. This should include, so far as they are available, claims about what is true in each case as a matter of physical and economic fact; claims about what any adequate moral theory must explain; and claims in which each side records what it concedes the other has genuinely shown.

2. A table of the residual disagreements. For each row, give the disagreement in two columns: A's own preferred formulation and B's own preferred formulation, each written so that its own side would accept it as accurate rather than tendentious. Where a row turns on the use of a term — cause, make a difference, harm, responsibility — mark the disagreement as verbal, identify which part of it is verbal and which part is not, and propose the shared vocabulary both would adopt thereafter.

3. The cruxes: those sub-questions whose settlement would actually move at least one party. I expect at least one crux to concern whether Cases 1 and 2 differ morally or only descriptively; at least one to concern whether a non-zero expected harm attributable to an act can ground a duty when no outcome whatever is affected; and at least one to concern whether the quantisation in Case 1 is a difference of degree from Case 2 or a difference in kind.

4. For each crux, the test, argument, or case that both parties accept as bearing on it. Here I insist on a distinction I spent my career enforcing: separate the cruxes that are empirically resolvable — where one could name a quantity to be measured or an observation to be made in a real supply chain or a real basin — from those that no measurement could resolve. For the empirical cruxes, name the quantity precisely and record each party's prediction of its value or sign in advance of any data. For the non-empirical cruxes, record what each party would accept as a decisive argument, and whether either is prepared to state in advance what would refute them.

5. A short closing section stating whether the agreed claims and cruxes transfer to the two other standard instances of the problem — an individual's greenhouse-gas emissions, and one ballot in a large election — and identifying exactly where the transfer fails.

Two constraints on execution. First, do not adjudicate: give no verdict on which camp is right, and do not smuggle one into the agreed-claims section by phrasing a contested point in a single party's vocabulary. Second, do not soften the disagreements into generality; a joint statement whose disagreement table could be signed by anyone would be worthless to me, and I would rather see one sharp, honest point of collision than five anodyne ones. Write it as a document — first person plural where the parties speak together, third person where they speak separately — and give me the reasoning rather than the scholarship: no citations, no reading list, no appeals to authority.
