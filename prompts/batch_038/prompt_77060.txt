I am grading the short-answer section of a final exam in an undergraduate philosophy-of-science course whose last three weeks covered causal inference. The question below was worth 20 points. Please grade the student response against the question as asked: a score for each part, and then a point-by-point set of comments that justify those scores. **Question as it appeared on the exam:**

A district randomly assigned 800 ninth-graders, by lottery in the first week of the fall term, to a new after-school numeracy tutoring program (400 students) or to business as usual (400). The tutoring meets 5:00–7:00 p.m. on Tuesdays and Thursdays. The district also runs an optional evening study workshop, open to all students, which meets 6:00–8:00 p.m. on those same evenings; attendance at the workshop was recorded over the course of the term. At the end of the term all students took a proficiency exam.

- Overall: tutoring 250/400 passed (62.5%); control 296/400 passed (74%).
- Among students who attended the evening workshop: tutoring 74/80 (92.5%); control 216/240 (90%).
- Among students who did not attend the workshop: tutoring 176/320 (55%); control 80/160 (50%).

(a) [8 points] Describe the pattern in these figures and explain why the overall comparison and the workshop-specific comparisons point in opposite directions.
(b) [8 points] Which figures should the district report as the effect of the tutoring program on pass rates? Justify your choice.
(c) [4 points] What further information, if any, would you want before committing to that answer?

**Student response:**

The overall figures and the figures within each workshop group disagree about which arm did better. Control students have the higher overall pass rate, 74% against 62.5%, but tutored students do better among workshop attendees, 92.5% against 90%, and also among non-attendees, 55% against 50%. This is Simpson's paradox.

It arises because the two arms are not distributed the same way across the workshop groups, and because pass rates differ sharply between those groups. In the control arm 240 of 400 students, 60%, attended the workshop, while in the tutoring arm only 80 of 400, 20%, did; workshop attendance goes with a much higher pass rate in both arms, roughly 90% against 50%. The control arm's overall rate is therefore pulled up by having most of its students in the high-passing group, and the tutoring arm's overall rate is pulled down by having most of its students in the low-passing group. The reversal is a weighting artifact rather than a sign that the data are inconsistent: given the four subgroup rates and the subgroup sizes, both overall rates follow by arithmetic.

The district should report the workshop-specific figures. Workshop attendance is associated both with assignment, 60% against 20%, and with the outcome, and that is the standard test for a confounder; a confounder has to be conditioned on before an association between program and passing can be read causally. Randomization is supposed to balance factors of this kind between the arms, so its failure to do so here means the overall comparison is not the comparison randomization was designed to deliver, and adjusting for workshop attendance is what restores it. On the adjusted numbers the program raises pass rates by 2.5 points among workshop attendees and 5 points among non-attendees, so it is effective in every subgroup we can examine, and therefore effective for the population as a whole. Reporting 74% against 62.5% would mislead, since a marginal association of that kind need not reflect any causal relation, and the district should not publicize it.

For (c), I would want to know how many students in each arm were assigned to tutoring but never attended, and whether pass rates were tracked separately for those students. I would also want the lottery records: a forty-point gap in workshop attendance between two randomly assigned arms is large enough that I would want to rule out a defect in the randomization itself.
