The paper I'm presenting at a population ethics workshop in three weeks defends a thresholded negative utilitarianism. On my account pleasure and pain are not commensurable on a single scale: below a threshold of intensity, pains trade off against pleasures in the familiar way, but above it an episode of suffering has lexical priority, in that no amount of pleasure and no number of delighted people can outweigh it. The threshold attaches to individual episodes rather than to aggregates, so I am prepared to say that a world containing one person above it is worse than a world holding countless sub-threshold sufferers and no delight whatever. My selling point is that this keeps the negative-utilitarian intuition while shedding the extinction implication, since a world whose inhabitants all suffer mildly is no worse than an empty world.

I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense. The room is mostly people who work on formal axiology and impossibility theorems and think in terms of continuity, additivity, and trade-off structure; there is one health economist who builds cardinal measures of wellbeing; and one deontologist who thinks the project misconstrues morality from the start. Give me the six to eight objections I'll actually face from this audience rather than from a general philosophy crowd, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased as they would phrase them, each with a reply I can deliver in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is softest. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
