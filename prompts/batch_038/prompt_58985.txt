I'm a veterinary anesthesiologist, and before that a resident in human pediatric anesthesia — I was in the field during the years when it became impossible to defend operating on neonates with muscle relaxant and minimal analgesic, a practice that had been rationalized in part by appeals to the immaturity of the infant nervous system and to the absence of any memory that could be formed. Almost all of my working life is now spent deciding, for patients who cannot tell me anything, whether they are merely reflexing or actually suffering. I titrate to multiples of MAC, I read autonomic surrogates — heart-rate variability, pleth variability, the nociception indices ported over from human operating rooms — I watch a limb withdraw under a regional block that has abolished sensation below the level of injection, I check catecholamines and cortisol when the monitors disagree with each other, and I think in terms of the triad of hypnosis, analgesia, and amnesia rather than in terms of consciousness as such. The discipline is deliberately operational: anesthetic depth is what the endpoints and the monitors say it is, and when two surrogates conflict we treat that as a question about which one is better calibrated, not as a metaphysical problem. I've been drafting a protocol for inferring nociception-driven suffering in non-verbal patients, and a philosopher colleague read it and remarked, sympathetically but firmly, that it was behaviorism with better instrumentation — that I had reinvented Descartes' account of animals and simply dropped the theology.

That remark has stuck with me, so I want to test it by having you do the translation properly. Take the Cartesian position on animal minds and restate it claim by claim in the vocabulary I actually use. The pieces I have in mind are: the automaton thesis itself, with the animal spirits and the machinery of reflex given as a purely mechanical causal story; the two behavioral criteria Descartes deploys in the Discourse — that beasts never arrange signs in order to declare their thoughts, and that even where they match us in particular performances they never match the open-ended variety of circumstances in which reason, rather than the disposition of the organs, would show itself; the metaphysical claim that animals lack an immaterial thinking substance, with the pineal gland as the interface where such a substance would have to act in us; and the softening in his late correspondence, where he grants animals something like sensation insofar as it depends on bodily organs while still withholding thought. For each of these, give me the closest honest rendering in my own terms: depth of anesthesia and MAC, nociception against antinociception, arousal, reflex arcs left intact below a spinal block, surrogates standing in for an unobservable state, and above all the responsiveness-versus-reportability distinction that governs how I think about awareness without recall. I want the translation to be faithful rather than flattering — do not quietly rebuild my operationalist assumptions into Descartes and then congratulate me on how well he fits, and flag every place where you had to choose between two candidate renderings because my vocabulary admits both.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Sort the claims into three piles — what maps cleanly onto my concepts, what maps only with real distortion, and what my vocabulary cannot express at all — and say why each lands where it does. Two specific questions sit inside that. First: does the Cartesian criterion of flexible, circumstance-independent performance amount to anything I could operationalize — a failure of generalization, in my language — or does it only look like an empirical test because it is doing metaphysical work at the point where it gets applied to a particular animal? Second: when I infer suffering from autonomic surrogates in a patient who cannot report, am I structurally a Cartesian, inferring an inner state from its outputs, or am I doing something Descartes explicitly declined to do for beasts? Tell me whether the inexpressible residue is a defect of my framework, which by design brackets exactly the questions he cared about, or evidence that his inference from behavioral criteria to the absence of thought was never licensed to begin with. Close with the uncomfortable version: if the criterion really is flexible use of a sign system to declare thoughts, then a modern large language model seems to clear a bar that a dog does not — say what that does to the argument, and whether his test was ever tracking a substance rather than a signal. Reasoning only, please; I'm not after a bibliography or a survey of the secondary literature.
