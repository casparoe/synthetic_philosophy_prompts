I am in my third year of a philosophy PhD, and my department runs a small internal summer fellowship: ten weeks of funding, no teaching, with the deliverable being a paper of roughly eight or nine thousand words plus a talk in the work-in-progress series in the autumn. I need the proposal, about fifteen hundred words, and I want to build it around a question I have been circling since teaching. Every spring I lead one section of the introductory metaphysics course, and we spend two sessions on time travel and the grandfather paradox. Every spring the same exchange happens. I explain the familiar response — that what the traveler can do is sensitive to which facts you hold fixed, so relative to a narrow, local set of facts about the situation he can kill his grandfather, while relative to the wide set that includes the settled history he cannot — and a student says, in effect, that this is not a solution but a contradiction wearing a hat. The reply never lands, and I have come to think it fails to land not because the students are confused about modality but because it answers a question they did not ask. What they are asking is not whether the killing is compossible with some body of facts, but whether the traveler can sensibly deliberate about doing it, and what his own evidence tells him about how the attempt will go. My suspicion is that the paradox leaves an epistemic and practical residue that survives the modal bookkeeping entirely, and that the literature's concentration on the modal question has left that residue unnamed. You should correct me if that impression of the state of the debate is wrong.

The half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal rests on a three-way distinction. There is modal "can" — compossibility with some specified set of held-fixed facts. There is epistemic "can" — compatibility with everything the agent knows or is entitled to believe. And there is what I want to call deliberative "can" — whether an option is genuinely available to the agent as something to settle by practical reasoning rather than to predict. My hunch is that the local/global response does real work on the first, is silent on the second and third, and that the second and third may turn out not to be two notions but one, in which case the interesting thesis is not that deliberation needs its own modality but that the grandfather paradox is partly an epistemological problem about self-knowledge in a fixed-history setting. I want the proposal to be organized around a test case I have constructed for this purpose. Two travelers, identical in every modal respect, each with the means and the motive and the opportunity. One has read the local histories and knows that in the relevant year his grandfather survived an assassination attempt by a man nobody ever identified; the other has no such information, and no bearing on the outcome beyond the brute fact of his own existence. If what a traveler can do is settled by the modal facts, the two stand in exactly the same position. Yet it looks as though only one of them can rationally take up the project, while the other is obliged either to abandon it or to explain away the one piece of evidence he has — his existence — as somehow not evidence at all. I also want the proposal to take up the analogy with logical fatalism, the claim that a settled truth about the future leaves nothing for the agent to settle: my instinct is that the two arguments are structurally much closer than the time-travel literature usually admits, that this closeness is an unexploited resource, and that the analogy nevertheless breaks at a specific point where the direction of the fixing relation starts to matter. Locating that breaking point is, I suspect, a substantial part of the work.

Write the proposal with the following in it. State the question precisely, formulated so that it has a determinate answer and is not merely an invitation to survey the terrain. Explain why the two or three standard positions leave it open, describing those positions by their content rather than by citation: do not include a bibliography or a literature review by reference, since I will add citations myself, and do not supply titles, dates, or page numbers you are not certain of. Give the method week by week across the ten weeks, saying concretely what I would be doing — which distinctions I would argue for, which cases I would construct, what the argumentative sequence is and what each stage depends on. Say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives (a new modal semantics for "can," a move to branching or many-worlds models, or dismissing the puzzle as a pseudo-problem), and what each of those would leave unanswered. Say what a result would look like and what would count as the project's failing, including the specific worry that deliberative "can" collapses into epistemic "can" with extra steps — and whether that collapse would be a failure or a finding. Wherever you rely on a claim about how the debate has gone that you are not confident of, mark it as uncertain rather than asserting it; I would rather have hedged claims I can check than confident ones I cannot.
