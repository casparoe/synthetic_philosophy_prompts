I'm not an academic, I fix industrial HVAC for a living, but a couple months ago I watched something about the American nuclear arsenal and there's one part of it I haven't been able to stop thinking about. Here's what I've pieced together, so correct me if I've got any of it wrong. At the bottom of the system, everything is built around the idea that no single person can be trusted with a weapon. Every warhead has a lock on it that needs a code. In a missile silo two officers have to turn their keys at the same instant and they drill that until it's muscle memory; on a submarine it takes several people at several stations. The design philosophy is basically: no one human being, acting alone, ever gets to make this happen. At the top it's the exact opposite. One person can order all of it and nobody else has a formal veto — not Congress, not the cabinet, not the joint chiefs. The secretary of defence transmits the order and confirms it really came from the president, but as far as I can tell he isn't supposed to be able to block it. And that person may have half an hour, sometimes less, between a radar warning and impact, so the whole thing is deliberately built to be fast rather than thought-through.

The part that actually broke my brain is this. Those same crews, the ones with the keys, are also taught that an illegal order has to be disobeyed, that they're personally responsible for what they do, that "I was only following orders" is no defence at all. So the system tells the operator both that he's a moral agent who has to evaluate the order and that he's a cog who has to execute it inside a few minutes while the phone is still crackling. And there's a deterrence reason for the top half, which is the bit I can't get past: an adversary has to believe the retaliation would genuinely come, so you don't want a committee, you don't want anyone who could be picked off or persuaded or slow-walk it, and you want the order to still go out even if the capital is gone. Which means the features that make the threat believable are exactly the features that make one exhausted person with bad judgement dangerous. My brother in law keeps saying "relax, obviously the guys in the silo would refuse a crazy order," and I can't articulate why that isn't the answer. I think it's because the system is arranged so that they can't, but I'd like to hear it argued properly rather than just felt.

So: what does this whole arrangement presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not a description of the hardware. What view of authority has to be true for "the office-holder's judgement just is the country's judgement" to make sense — and which rival views does it quietly rule out, like legitimacy coming from consultation, or moral responsibility not being handable up the chain, or a decision this size having to be somebody's decision rather than a machine's default? Then tell me where the contradiction I keep hitting actually lands: is the operator's role flatly incoherent, or is there a story on which his judgement is real but pre-shaped by years of training and time-boxed to a few seconds, so the thinking happens in advance instead of in the moment? I'd also like you to be honest about where the practice doesn't force any of this on you — maybe a plain "it's a credibility machine, don't read philosophy into the engineering" account licenses every single feature and I'm inventing the tension. And finally, if we swapped the presupposed view for the distrustful one and actually designed it around no-single-person-gets-this — a second signature, a standing panel, a rule that retaliation only follows a decision reached by survivors who have time to think — what would we lose? Would the deterrent stop being credible? And if it would, does that mean deterrence has been resting this whole time on a claim about what rational agents will do that nobody would accept in any other context?
