I'm in a student policy group that got asked to write a short brief for a state commission that's rethinking the "purposes" clause in our sentencing guidelines -- the bit that currently says punishment should be proportionate to the blameworthiness of the offense, with a proposal on the table to swap that for language about using no more restriction than necessary to protect public safety. Our group is split down the middle: half of us lean free-will-skeptic (the Caruso/Pereboom crowd), half think that's a philosophical temper tantrum dressed up as policy. The co-chair, a 3L who's normally pretty sharp, drafted the framing paragraph that's supposed to open the brief, and I want to know how much of it I should let stand:

"Both sides of this debate actually accept the same picture of the world and disagree mostly about vocabulary. Dennett is a compatibilist, so he thinks determinism is true but beside the point -- responsibility just comes down to whether you could have responded to reasons, and he's content to keep desert-talk around because it does useful social work. Caruso thinks the metaphysics is settled by neuroscience instead: experiments show your brain commits to an action before you're aware of choosing it, so the sense of being the author of your acts is a story you tell afterwards, which is why his position is best described as the free-will-illusion view. That's also why his position leads straight to emptying the prisons, since you can't punish anybody for bad luck -- either they're dangerous or they aren't, and if they are, you hold them for as long as it takes, which for a serious offense could mean forever. So the skeptics end up harsher than the retributivists they're attacking. And notice what's really at stake: nothing. Anyone who believes just deserts in the ordinary sense -- that people who do awful things end up badly off -- has no reason to care how this argument turns out."
