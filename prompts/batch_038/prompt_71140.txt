You are a philosopher of biology who has spent a career on the semantics of information talk in the life sciences, and who has also done a good deal of practical work coaching editors and science writers. Think carefully before drafting. Begin with a brainstorming pass that you show me: enumerate the distinct claims the sentence "DNA is a code" can be used to make, and for each note whether it is a claim about a mapping between two alphabets, a claim about copying, a claim about causal influence, or a claim about specification or meaning. Only then design the procedure.

I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine. Since our specialist science editor left, I have also become the de facto referee for the metaphors the section runs: I flag phrases in copy, writers argue back, and roughly a dozen such phrases a week come to me for a ruling. Each ruling has to be made in under five minutes, by a writer who is not a biologist and by me, who am not a philosopher of biology, with no specialist available to consult and no time to read around the question. The individual stakes are low; the cumulative stake is not, because our section publishes about genetics several times a month and a magazine that casually says the genome is an instruction manual is teaching its readers a picture of development that most people who study it would reject. Once or twice a year a working molecular biologist writes in to complain, and I need to be able to defend a decision in two or three sentences rather than in a footnote.

The trouble is concentrated in genetics. Our writers say, variously, that the genome is a code, a blueprint, a book, a script, an instruction manual, a program the cell executes, or a recipe. Some of these are licensed by a real and quite narrow fact: the mapping from nucleotide triplets to amino acids is arbitrary rather than chemically forced, it is rule-governed, it is digital in the sense of operating on discrete recombinable tokens, and it requires decoding machinery that is not itself part of the message. Others are licensed by a different fact, or by nothing at all.

Here are the distinctions I want the procedure to carry, stated as I understand them — correct me where I have got them wrong:

- The narrow coding relation holds between nucleic acid and protein, and only there. The same molecule stands in a different relation to its own copies and to its RNA transcripts: templating, where product and template correspond structurally within one alphabet family. A template resembles what it produces; a code need not resemble anything. Conflating the two is where most of the damage starts.
- Neither relation extends to the organism. No sequence is a structural model of a body, and no sequence is an executable procedure whose running is development.
- The decoding apparatus is constitutive, not incidental. The assignment table lives in the ribosome, the transfer RNAs, the synthetases; and the table is not universal — the same triplet is read one way in the cytoplasm and another way in a mitochondrion, and variant tables occur elsewhere. So "the code" is a property of a translation system, not of a molecule sitting in a nucleus.
- "Information" slides between a causal sense (this sequence makes a difference to the outcome) and a semantic or representational sense (this sequence is about the outcome, or specifies it). Writers routinely earn the causal sense from an experiment and then spend it on the semantic sense, which the experiment never delivered.
- If development requires the coordinated contribution of many resources inside and outside the genome, then nominating one of them as the carrier of the specification is a decision about emphasis, not a finding. I want my writers to be able to see when a sentence has smuggled that decision in as though it were data.

What I want from you is a decision procedure my desk can actually run, not an essay about the debate. It should consist of numbered questions in the order a non-specialist should ask them; for each question, what counts as a passing answer and what disqualifies the phrase outright; a small closed set of verdicts — run it as is, keep it with a mandatory gloss (and tell the writer what the gloss must say), strike and replace, escalate to me; the points where the procedure stops and hands over a judgment call rather than an answer; and a condensed one-page version at the end that a writer could pin above the desk.

Be explicit about the costs of the simplification. Tell me which of the distinctions above the procedure deliberately drops because a generalist cannot apply them in five minutes, and which cannot be dropped without the procedure becoming affirmatively wrong. Tell me where it will misfire: the phrases it will wrongly permit, the phrases it will wrongly ban, and how it should handle the asymmetry between headline and body — a phrase that survives in a paragraph where a gloss can follow may be indefensible in a six-word headline where no gloss fits. Say plainly which of your steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on these four, all of them composites from my desk rather than quotations from anything published:

1. "This gene codes for a receptor," spoken by a structural biologist whom I am quoting directly.
2. "Every cell in your body carries the same instruction manual," a sentence of my own in a piece about cell differentiation, which I like and intend to fight for.
3. The headline "Cracking the code of ageing," over a piece about epigenetic clocks.
4. "Junk DNA," used three times in a piece about genome size.

At least one verdict should go against my preference, and I expect it will be number two — tell me honestly if the procedure kills it, and whether it kills it for a reason I can put in a letter to a complainant. Also flag any case where the right verdict depends on the audience, because a specialist reader will accept an idiom that a general reader will misread.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: the classes of genetic talk it does not reach, the disputes it cannot settle even in principle, and the situations in which following it would make my section less accurate rather than more.

No citations, no bibliography, no reading list — I cannot check any of it and I do not want to. Describe positions by their content; if you name anyone, name only what you are confident of, and do not attempt to reconstruct anyone's exact wording. Plain language throughout, roughly 1,500 words, and written to be used rather than admired.
