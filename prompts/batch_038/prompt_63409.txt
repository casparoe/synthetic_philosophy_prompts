**My situation.** I am preparing a seminar on the possibility that the basic axioms of mathematics are inconsistent. As a point of entry I have been working through the second of the problems Hilbert posed in his 1900 Paris address, the one concerning the compatibility (consistency) of the arithmetical axioms, in an English translation. **The passage, as I have it.** What follows is *my own* paragraph-by-paragraph reconstruction of the stretch of the second problem that bears on consistency; it is deliberately not a quotation, since I cannot check wording offline, and I would rather give you an honest paraphrase than a fake verbatim text. I am aware that in compressing I may have flattened distinctions, merged two moves into one, or even mislocated a claim. Part of what I need from you is help spotting where that has happened.

- P1. In geometry, when one asks whether a set of axioms is compatible, there is a familiar method: exhibit a domain of objects, and assign the primitive notions to features of that domain, in such a way that the axioms come out true under the assignment. If that succeeds, and if the domain itself is not in doubt, then any contradiction derivable from the axioms would show up as a contradiction inside the domain. So the question is transferred to something already accepted.
- P2. This is the route for the axioms of the continuum: the relevant domain is supplied by the real numbers (or, equivalently, by a suitably arithmetised conception of points and segments), together with an Archimedean requirement and a further requirement that closes the domain off against any extension.
- P3. The analogous route is unavailable for arithmetic. If one tried to exhibit a domain satisfying the arithmetical axioms, that domain would have to be produced by arithmetical means; one would already be using the theory whose compatibility is in question. The reduction would therefore be circular, and arithmetic's compatibility cannot be secured by interpreting it into a domain that arithmetic itself provides.
- P4. Hence a direct method is required: one that operates on the axioms and on the admissible inferences themselves. The rough sketch is that the axioms generate a stock of statements, and the inferences generate more from those; the task is to show that this process never yields a statement together with its negation. What is being examined, in the first instance, is the derivational process, including the reasoning by which the desired result is established — that reasoning being itself something to be surveyed rather than presupposed.
- P5. Then comes the criterion: if the axioms, together with everything that follows from them, never lead to a contradiction, then the axioms are true and the entities they define exist. Compatibility is the mark of existence, and of truth, in mathematics; this is what makes any appeal outside the system — to intuition, say, or to a realm of objects independently given — unnecessary.
- P6. Establishing the compatibility of the arithmetical axioms is what finally disposes of the old anxieties about irrational magnitudes, about the continuum, and about infinite totalities: those anxieties dissolve once we know the axioms do not conflict. And in the other direction, where an assumption can be shown to generate a contradiction — as with the assumption that there is a totality of all cardinal numbers — the corresponding concept simply lacks mathematical significance.

**My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter.** I suspect that P1–P3 already trade on a distinction between two notions of compatibility that only later became clearly separated: a *semantic* notion, on which compatibility amounts to there being a domain in which the axioms hold, and a *proof-theoretic* one, on which it amounts to the non-derivability of a contradiction by the permitted rules. On this reading, the circularity complained of in P3 is precisely the unavailability of the semantic notion for arithmetic, and that unavailability is what forces the development of the proof-theoretic notion in P4. I also suspect that P5's criterion silently slides between the two: it is stated in proof-theoretic terms but its conclusion is semantic (existence). I would like you to assess whether this reading is textually plausible given the paraphrase, whether it is anachronistic, and whether there is a better account of what is going on.
