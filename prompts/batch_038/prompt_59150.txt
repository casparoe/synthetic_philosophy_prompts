I am six months into a novel, and I have begun to suspect that its form is doing something I never authorized. My subject is a physician who ran a clinic in a provincial city for forty years: indispensable, irreplaceable, and — on the testimony of nearly everyone who worked under him — cruel in ways that were not incidental to his usefulness but continuous with it. The book is narrated by four of his former assistants reconstructing him after his death, each correcting the others, none of them reliable. I did not set out to write a hagiography. But I now see that a reader who is moved by such a man, and the chapters in which he is at work on an impossible case are the ones that move me most, will carry away the shape of the life long after the qualifications have faded. Shape is my medium. Caveats are footnotes to it.

Before I decide how to end the book, I want the specification rather than another argument. What would a theory of moral learning from lives — running from Plutarch's remark in the preface to the Alexander that he is writing lives rather than histories, and that a trifling act or a casual phrase often discloses a man better than a battle with thousands slain, through the saints' lives, to the biographical novel — have to deliver in order to count as adequate? Give me the desiderata, with the case or consideration behind each one.

The list should cover, at minimum: the phenomena such a theory must account for, including admiration, emulation, and the sense that a life teaches something no argument states; the cases it must get right, among them the flawed exemplar whose virtues are inseparable from his vices, the saint's life that has quietly deleted everything inconvenient, the life whose unity is plainly imposed by the teller rather than found in the living, the reader who imitates the wrong feature because it was rendered charmingly, and whatever difference there is — if there is one — between being taught by a life that happened and by a life that was invented; the distinctions it must not collapse, such as that between knowing a life and knowing propositions about it, between admiration and emulation, between a trait that is legible in a person and one that is transferable to another, and between being moved and being convinced; and the findings about character it must remain consistent with, including the situationist material — the ordinary person made into someone else by a corridor and a clock, the divinity students who stepped over a man in distress because they were late — together with our habitual over-reading of disposition from a single vivid scene.

Then tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are the settled tastes of particular camps. I especially want to know whether treating the situationist results as constraints on a theory of exemplars is a genuine requirement or a prejudice wearing the clothes of data.

And tell me which subset cannot be jointly satisfied. I have a suspicion, and I want it tested rather than flattered: that a theory demanding the exemplar be given in full particularity — the bad mornings, the motives he could not have articulated, the accidents that made him what he was — thereby makes him unimitable, since what is imitable is a pattern and patterns are abstractions from particulars; whereas a theory that preserves imitability has already begun to simplify, which is to say, to hagiographize. If that is a real bind and not merely professional anxiety, say so and price the horns. If it is not real, show me where I have misdescribed it.

Finally, and briefly: which single requirement excludes the largest number of candidate theories, and how do three families fare against your list — an exemplarist virtue ethics on which admiration of the excellent is the ground of moral concepts; a view on which lives yield not knowledge but perception or moral imagination; and a deflationary view on which the narrated life contributes nothing that its best paraphrase into principles does not already contain.

A table is acceptable for the requirements, provided the motivating case stays attached to each row. I am not assembling a taxonomy. I am trying to learn what my book is permitted to do.
