**Nobody Can Be Told To Move: Why Hilbert's Hotel Refutes Itself**

Everyone knows the hotel. Infinitely many rooms, numbered 1, 2, 3 and so on, every room occupied. A guest turns up at reception. The manager rings every room and asks each guest to move up one: the person in 1 goes to 2, the person in 2 goes to 3, and so on. Room 1 is now free and the new guest moves in. Nothing was built, nobody was turned away, and yet a room appeared. That's the trick that's supposed to make actual infinity feel weird but legitimate.

My claim is that the trick fails, and fails for a reason that isn't usually noticed. The manager doesn't perform one action, he performs infinitely many, one per guest. Each of those is a person getting up, walking along a corridor, opening a door, going in. If each takes a minute then after an hour only sixty guests have moved and rooms 1 through 60 are a shambles. There's no moment at which the shifting is finished, so there's no moment at which room 1 is free and everyone else is settled. The hotel hasn't accommodated the new guest. It has described accommodating him.

The obvious reply is that this is being pedantic about implementation. Just don't have anyone move up one. Give every guest a rule instead: whoever is in room n goes to room 2n. Then the guests all move together, in one go, and rooms 1, 3, 5, ... are left empty, which is infinitely many empty rooms, so the new guest can have room 1 and there's no queue of actions to get through. Supertask avoided.

I don't think this reply is available, for two reasons. First, "all at once" is not something a hotel can do. If every guest leaves their room simultaneously then for a while everyone is in the corridor and every room is empty, which is fine, but if they don't leave simultaneously then someone in room n arrives at room 2n while its occupant is still in it, and we're back to needing an order, which is back to the sequence. Either way you either have a moment where nothing is where it should be or you have a sequence. Second, and more importantly, the rule version isn't a process at all. Saying "the guest from room n is now the guest in room 2n" is just a redescription of the collection. It tells you how the rooms are labelled, not that anything happened. And a redescription can't be an argument for the existence of the thing being redescribed, because the redescription only makes sense if the infinite collection is already there, complete, doing what it's told.

If I'm right about this then the same shape of problem shows up in the diagonal argument. To build the number that isn't on the list you have to look at the first digit of the first entry, the second digit of the second entry, and so on down the whole list. That's an inspection you never finish. So the diagonal number is never produced, and if it's never produced you haven't shown there's a real number missing from the list, only that the list has a rule attached to it.

Which is where I want to land: infinity works as a potential, as a direction of travel you can always take one more step along, but not as an actual, as a completed thing sitting there being operated on. Every operation on a completed infinite turns out to be either an unfinished sequence or a description pretending to be an operation. Aristotle was basically right and the hotel is a magic trick where the trick is the word "now".
