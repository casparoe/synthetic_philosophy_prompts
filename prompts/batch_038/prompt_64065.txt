I'm a staff software engineer at a payments company. I have no formal training in philosophy or economics; what I know I've picked up by reading on my own. For three years I've run an optional ten-week evening seminar for engineers here on mechanisms for collective decision-making, and week eight is always futarchy — the proposal to let a democratic vote settle the ends and let prediction markets settle the means. The seminar ends with a written assessment. I want to replace this year's question, because it can now be answered competently by anyone who has read a summary of the proposal online.

Here is the design I want the question built around. I sketched it myself; treat it as the spec the students are handed.

The Conditional Welfare Ledger (CWL). A public body faces a binary choice between policy A and policy B and must adopt one by a fixed date T. Two contracts are issued, each paying one unit of currency per point of a welfare index W that is pre-registered before trading opens and measured over a fixed window after T. Contract A pays only in the world where A is adopted; contract B pays only in the world where B is adopted. Trading runs continuously until T. Positions in the contract for the branch that is not adopted expire worthless; they are neither unwound nor refunded. At T the body is obliged to adopt whichever contract has the higher volume-weighted average price over the final trading week. To address thin liquidity, the treasury funds a standing market-maker that quotes both sides of both contracts at a fixed spread, must fill any order up to a stated size, and is prohibited from trading on any information bearing on which policy will be adopted.

It is a 48-hour take-home on which I expect roughly two hours of work. The question should force students to work out from the spec alone what the CWL price is actually an estimate of, what the adoption rule requires it to be an estimate of, and precisely why those come apart. It should require them to identify at least one actor for whom moving the adoption decision is cheap given the expiry convention I've specified, and to express that cost in the same units as the treasury's exposure — a candidate who says manipulation is possible but never works out what it costs should not pass. It should then require them to judge whether the market-maker patch addresses either of those two problems, and to say what a real patch would have to change about the payoff structure rather than about transparency, auditability, or disclosure.

Deliverables: the question exactly as students would see it, including the spec; a model answer of roughly 900 words; a marking scheme out of 25 built around must-hit points, each with its mark value and one line on how candidates typically botch it; and a short closing paragraph saying what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one.

Two constraints. First, no citations, bibliography, or named literature anywhere in the question or the model answer. The students have no reading list, and I don't want to imply that a good answer depends on having read someone; everything must be arguable from the spec. Second, the model answer should build up any concept it needs rather than naming it. If it requires a distinction from decision theory or social choice theory, state the distinction and say what turns on it — don't deploy a term of art and assume the reader already has it. I will be reading the model answer myself to check it, and I won't be able to tell whether an unexplained technical term is doing work or just decorating.

One more thing, and please do it first: if any part of the CWL spec as I've written it is incoherent, unimplementable, or accidentally settles the very issue the question is meant to leave open, say so separately before writing anything else. I would much rather hear that now than have thirty engineers writing careful answers about a broken mechanism.
