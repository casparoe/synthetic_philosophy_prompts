I have been reading about levels of explanation in biology and the philosophy of mind, and I keep circling back to a passage in a widely read critical book on the subject. Its summary of the cranes-versus-skyhooks argument runs as follows:

"Dennett divides any proposed explanation of design into two boxes. A skyhook is a factor that does its work from above the level being explained -- an intelligent designer, a purpose built into nature, or, on some readings, a mind that is not itself the product of design. A crane is anything that produces organized complexity while staying within the level being explained; natural selection is the paradigm case. Greedy reductionism is the error of denying that cranes exist and insisting that the base level is all there is. Dennett accuses his opponents of greed, but on inspection he cannot have it both ways. A crane is defined by what it is not -- not a skyhook, not reducible to the base level -- so it is not a mechanism at all but a promissory note. Either cranes are fully derivable from physics, in which case they are no threat to the reductive programme and the whole apparatus is rhetorical decoration, or they are not so derivable, in which case Dennett has conceded irreducible levels and become an emergentist while continuing to describe himself as a materialist. His own examples give him away: he insists that the design stance and the vocabulary of belief and desire must be retained because they cannot be dispensed with, which is the claim that some higher-level phenomena are not reducible. And because a crane, on this definition, is any factor that lifts design above the substrate without descending from it, natural selection is itself mind-first in the sense the argument forbids: it presupposes the very design it was summoned to explain. Searle's charge that the mind is irreducibly first-person, and Nagel's that materialists always end up smuggling mind back in through the side door, therefore apply to Dennett with full force."

Please audit this. Which sentences would survive contact with the actual argument, which depend on quietly redefining the author's terms, and which are simply false as descriptions of his position? Address in particular three things: how the summary characterizes what a crane is supposed to be, and whether that characterization licenses the charge that cranes are defined residually, as whatever fails to be reduced; whether the two-horns dilemma it constructs is one a defender could accept, or whether it trades on a distinction the argument actually draws; and whether the claims attributed to Searle and Nagel, taken as accurate paraphrases of their complaints, do the work the passage asks of them once the earlier errors are corrected. Finally, tell me what the passage would have to establish before the collapse charge became a live objection rather than a restatement of the disagreement, and what a reader who knows only this summary would most plausibly come away believing that is not true.
