I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background whatsoever, so forgive the sloppy framing. For about six years I've been in a group chat with three friends — one does data science, one's a nurse, one went to seminary and then didn't — and roughly every 18 months we have the exact same argument about where religion comes from, and it never moves. Not an inch. Same two camps, same examples, and as far as I can tell everyone agrees on basically all the facts.

Camp A says religion is a byproduct. Nobody evolved it; it falls out of hardware that got selected for other jobs: we over-detect agency (rustle in the grass = predator, not wind), we simulate other minds so cheaply that we can run the simulation on minds that aren't there, disgust and contamination intuitions generalize into purity rules, and slightly counterintuitive concepts are memorable and therefore spread. Religion is a spandrel — or maybe several spandrels in a trench coat.

Camp B says no, it's an adaptation, or at least the group-level machinery is. All that expensive stuff — fasting, tithing, sabbath, food taboos, painful initiation, celibate cadres — is costly signaling. It filters out free riders, which is precisely the problem any cooperative group has to solve, and groups with the filter out-cooperate and out-breed groups without it. Religion is what made large-scale cooperation possible at all.

What frustrates me is I can't figure out what they actually disagree about. When the nurse brings up that commune thing (something about 19th century American utopian communities, religious ones lasting longer than secular ones), camp A doesn't dispute the numbers — they say a group lasting longer isn't the same as the trait being an adaptation, and anyway the unit-of-selection business is contested. When camp A brings up agency detection and the memory advantage for weird concepts, camp B doesn't dispute that either — they say sure, that's the raw material, selection then co-opted it, which is still selection. Each side treats the other's best evidence as fully compatible with its own view. That should deffinitely mean something and I don't know what.

There's also a third thing that keeps surfacing. The ex-seminarian friend says both camps are arguing about a word: his claim is that there's no such thing as religion the way there's a such thing as a kidney — no natural kind, just a bunch of seperate practices and intuitions we lump together for historical reasons — so asking what religion is for is like asking what the weekend is an adaptation for. The other two think he's being defeatist, and that cross-culturally you find a recognizable cluster whether or not you like the label.

My hypothesis, which could be completely wrong: the empirical facts aren't in dispute, and the real fight is over (a) what the explanandum even is — one trait with a function vs. a loose bundle of byproducts vs. no kind at all — and (b) what would count as evidence that a trait has a function. If that's right, it's a verbal disagreement wearing an empirical costume. Tell me if it's wrong, and where.

So: diagnose it. Not a survey of the field and not a verdict on who's right about religion, I genuinely don't care about that part. Which kind of thing is it — a real empirical question with an answer we haven't found, a fight about evidential standards for function claims, two sides answering different questions under one word, or bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for? Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says when they argue, not in a generic description of the two positions. Then tell me what would count as progress from where I'm sitting: is there an experiment, a natural experiment, a modeling result, a definitional move, or a better question that could actually move this group chat? If the honest answer is that it can't move, argue for that rather than just asserting it. And if there's a framing all three of us are missing — something that would dissolve the argument instead of winning it — say so, even if it makes my question look dumb.
