I'm not an academic, but I've spent years reading in cliodynamics, and I've come to accept a specific version of the structural-demographic picture: that political instability in a large modern state is substantially predictable years in advance from a few slow-moving indicators — above all an oversupply of credentialed aspirants relative to the positions of power and prestige available to absorb them, together with stagnating wages for ordinary workers and a fiscally strained state. My own amateur version tracks degrees conferred per year against the number of senior public and corporate posts, and the series I get seems to lead political turbulence by roughly fifteen years. I also treat the well-known published forecast that the 2020s would be turbulent as a genuine out-of-sample success rather than a lucky hit.

What would count against this? I want a map of its exposure, not a defense or a demolition. Which findings would be decisive against it and which merely awkward — would a long stretch of heavy elite overproduction accompanied by durable political calm refute the theory, or does it have a principled reason to expect such cases? How much should the standing complaints worry me: too few genuinely independent cases, the freedom to fix the window and the indicator set after seeing the outcome, indicators that are only loosely operationalized, and a theory that forecasts propensity far better than form or timing? And does the whole thing lean on there being roughly regular multi-generational cycles, or can the indicator-based forecast stand without them?

My private worry is sharper. Stated as a claim about when instability arrives, the theory is exposed but often misses; restated as a claim that instability merely becomes likelier within a broad band, it is hard to contradict. Is that a legitimate retreat of the kind seismology makes, or has the view been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, is that fatal, or only deflationary? Say which of these defeaters the theory's own defenders would themselves concede and which they'd dismiss as a misreading, and order them by what they should cost me.
