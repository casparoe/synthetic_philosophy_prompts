Last night I argued with a friend from university until two in the morning. He went on to do a doctorate in philosophy; I went into process safety. I run HAZOPs, layers-of-protection analysis, bowties, SIL verification, and incident investigations for a living, and I have no metaphysics training whatever — everything I know about this debate he summarized to me over dinner. His claim was that my discipline is metaphysically indefensible: that an absence is not the sort of thing that can be a cause; that if the failure to close a valve genuinely caused the release, then so did the failure of every person on the planet who was not in that plant to close it; and that our fault trees are therefore not representations of causal structure but a bookkeeping convention dressed up as ontology. My reply was that this is either a discovery about how careless engineers are, or a sign that his literature has manufactured a deep puzzle out of something we settle as a matter of routine practice. I would like to know which.

So restate the debate to me in my own vocabulary, as precisely as you can manage. I mean the vocabulary of initiating events and top events, preventive and mitigative barriers, minimal cut sets, safety-instrumented functions and their probability of failure on demand, active and latent failures, HAZOP deviations built out of guide words such as NO and REVERSE, the system boundary that has to be drawn before a tree means anything at all, and the ALARP judgement that decides which branches get developed and which get closed off. Take the positions one at a time: counterfactual dependence and its promiscuity problem; the contrastive repair, on which causes are always causes-rather-than and effects always effects-rather-than; the process or conserved-quantity account that demotes omissions to some derivative, second-class status; and the view that omissions are never causes but can nonetheless carry genuine causal explanation. For each, say what a competent facilitator is committed to when she decides that the entry recording the operator's failure to isolate the line belongs in the tree — and which of these positions, if any, describes what she is actually doing.

Then give me the residue report, and treat it as the main event. For each position: what translated cleanly, what you had to bend to make it fit, and what has no expression at all in the vocabulary above. Flag the bending explicitly. If you find yourself reaching for something fault trees do not have — obligations, expectations, agency, a preferred contrast class, a norm about which barriers ought to exist — in order to make the philosophy come out right, say so rather than sliding it in as though it had been there all along. Do not translate by quietly assuming my framework already answers the question.

Finally, adjudicate. For every piece of residue, tell me whether it marks a genuine defect in the engineering vocabulary — something process safety cannot represent and arguably should — or whether it is evidence that my friend's puzzle evaporates once you notice that scoping a system boundary is the very move the philosophers are looking for and cannot perform without circularity. Be concrete about three places where I suspect the trouble sits: whether a probability of failure on demand, being a disposition of a real installed device, means my quantified omissions were never absences to begin with; whether removing a barrier and never having installed one are causally different, as my bowtie notation insists they are; and whether the promiscuity objection retains any bite against a tree whose cut sets are fixed by an approved safety requirements specification. End with your honest verdict on who won last night.
