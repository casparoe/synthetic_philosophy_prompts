Ok so I've been reading LW-adjacent stuff since the sequences and I thought I had the outside view properly internalised, and then I got into a comment-thread argument that used it to prove something I can't accept, and now I can't find where the wheels came off.

The concrete case: how much longer the post-1945 arrangement lasts — the great-power peace, the hegemon, the whole Bretton-Woods-descended order, whatever you want to call it. I said the usual inside-view things: nuclear weapons make great-power war unwinnable, the economies are entangled to the point where conquest doesn't pay, there are standing institutions that let states read each other's intentions, territorial annexation stopped being a legitimate thing to do. My opponent said all of that was exactly the noise the base rate exists to cancel, and then ran this:

(1) When you forecast X, you start from the base rate of the reference class X belongs to, not from a story about why X is different.
(2) For any X there is always a populated class to hand — hegemonic orders, long peaces, empires, large bureaucratic systems — at whatever level of description you like.
(3) Every past member of those classes that turned out to be business-as-usual also came with a detailed, sincere, expert-endorsed story about why it was permanently different. This is a constant across the record, not a coincidence.
(4) So having a detailed sincere story about why your case is different is not evidence that it's different. The failures had one too; it's already priced into the base rate.
(5) So no quantity of inside-view reasoning can move your credence off the base rate. The base rate is the answer, not a starting point you adjust from.
(6) Hence on the concrete case: lifespans of past orders are what they are, the surviving fraction drops steeply with age, and the correct credence that this one is permanent is roughly the fraction of past orders that turned out to be permanent — call it zero.
(7) And this also disposes of my other objection, that you can make the base rate say anything by picking the class. If I think empires and modern orders aren't the same kind of thing, that's an inside-view story about the details, and by (5) it can't move me either. Reference class choice isn't a loophole.

Then they added the bit I actually wrote down and now don't believe, becuse I think it bites them harder than it bites me but I can't show how:

(8) Whoever assembled the base rate in (2) did it by making precisely the case-by-case similarity judgements that (5) forbids — is Rome in, is Han in, is the EU a polity or a treaty, when did the Ottoman order end, does a religious order count. Every base rate anyone has ever quoted is a fossilised heap of inside-view reasoning.
(9) So the outside view either can't be run at all, or it runs only by permitting the thing it prohibits. Taken seriously it refutes itself, and (6) doesn't survive its own method.

I'm confident (9) is wrong, or at least wrong as a way of winning the actual argument. I do not want to give up (4) — "everyone always thought they were the exception" feels definately like one of the best-supported claims in the whole forecasting literature and it's the reason I believed in base rates to begin with. I'd also like to keep (1), at least as a default. Which step is broken, and why did each of the candidate breaks look fine when I read them? For every exit you think is live, tell me what it costs — including whether giving it up concedes something to my opponent that they can use again. And once the bad step is stripped out, say what the outside view does license about the concrete case, because "roughly zero" and "not permanent" are doing different work in (6) and I suspect one of them is a freebie.
