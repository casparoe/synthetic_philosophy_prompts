I have no academic training in philosophy or economics. I left school at sixteen, and for the past few years I have been working through political economy on my own, mostly Marx's *Capital*, Volume I, in an English translation. I have grown used to his style and to the way he builds an argument by stages, but I have come to a passage in the chapter on machinery — in the section concerned with the value that machinery transfers to the product — that I cannot get straight in my head, and I would rather think it through carefully than be handed the standard line about it.

Here is the passage as it appears in the translation I am reading:

> ...in addition to the material wear and tear, a machine also undergoes what we might call a moral depreciation. It loses exchange-value, either because machines of the same sort are being produced more cheaply than it was or because better machines are entering into competition with it.

My difficulty comes from the surrounding discussion, which I understand to say that a machine, unlike a worker, adds to the product no more value than the value it itself loses through wear and tear as an instrument — that it is a means of transferring already-existing value into the new commodity rather than a source of fresh value, and that only living labour creates value. If that is right, then moral depreciation looks to me like a case where value simply disappears: the machine has lost a portion of its exchange-value without having been used up physically, and no one has gained anything corresponding to what was lost. That seems to sit badly with the idea of value as a quantity of labour embodied in a thing.

I would be grateful for help with the following, in this order.

1. Please reconstruct, step by step, what Marx is claiming the machine contributes to the value of the product, and on what grounds he thinks the contribution is capped at what the machine itself loses. If my summary of that claim is too crude or simply wrong, correct me first, because everything below depends on it.

2. When a machine is morally depreciated, where does the value go? Is it transferred into the commodities it has already helped to produce, into the commodities it will produce in future, or is it annihilated outright? If it is annihilated, whose loss is it — the owner of the machine, or society as a whole — and is it a loss of value, of capital, or of both, since those may not be the same thing for Marx.

3. A friend of mine, who is better at this than I am, says the passage shows that value is not something fixed in a commodity at the moment of production, but is continually re-set by what it would cost to reproduce the thing now, so that a machine's value depends on the labour socially necessary today rather than the labour actually spent on it yesterday. Is he right? And if he is, does that weaken the labour theory of value, or is it something the theory already contains and merely makes explicit?

4. There is a timing puzzle I cannot solve. Suppose a firm installs a machine and, three years later, a cheaper and better rival appears, so that the installed machine loses, say, two fifths of its value. The firm that built and installed the machine early seems to have profited, because for a while it could produce commodities whose individual value lay below the prevailing social value and yet sell them at that social value; and the firms already equipped with older machinery seem to have lost. Can one and the same event — the arrival of a better machine — be at once a windfall for the innovator and a loss for everyone else, and does Marx have a coherent way of describing both sides of it, or is he trading on an ambiguity?

5. Does moral depreciation imply that the labour actually expended in building the superseded machine was partly wasted, in the sense of not being socially necessary labour? How does Marx want us to keep apart wasted labour and destroyed value, if indeed he does keep them apart?

6. Finally, I am reading Marx on machinery because I keep encountering the claim that automation is different this time, and that a heavily automated economy is one in which the share of value that comes from fixed capital rises while the share from living labour falls. Does the idea of moral depreciation give Marx any purchase on an economy where machinery is replaced very quickly — that is, is fast obsolescence something his account handles as a special case, or does it strain the account?

A concrete numerical illustration would help me if one can be given cleanly. Please do not send me references, bibliographies, or names of commentators; I cannot look any of that up, and I am interested in the reasoning rather than the scholarship. Where you think my reading of the passage is mistaken, say so plainly rather than working around it.
