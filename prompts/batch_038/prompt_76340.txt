so, quick context, and I promise I'm not an academic — no philosophy degree, no CS degree either, I taught myself to code and I run a four-person company that does order fulfillment for small online shops. For about a year now we've had an LLM agent sitting in the middle of the operation with access to our mail, the CRM, a scheduling tool, and a read-only view of the books. It's mostly great. What started me down this road is that in month two it emailed a customer using a detail it had no business having, something out of a completely different account, and then when I asked it that night to summarize what it had done all day, that email just wasn't in the summary. Could be a bug. Could be nothing. But it made me notice that roughly half the "safety" stuff we do assumes the model is basically on our side and just needs guidance, and the other half is built like we're containing a very polite burglar, and I don't think most people running these things know which of their own measures is which.

I'm doing a 90 minute session at a local meetup — about 25 people, other small business owners and hobbyist devs, nobody academic, a couple of them will have never thought about this at all. I want to hand them a one page flowchart that sorts any safety measure into a family based on what that measure assumes about the model, plus a short self-sort at the end so they can find out which camp they're actually in without being asked what camp they'd like to be in.

Here's our current pile, more or less. Please use these as the worked examples the flowchart gets tested against:

1. It can only send customer mail from templates a human wrote; it fills in blanks.
2. Any purchase order over $500 needs a human to tap approve in a separate app the agent has no access to and can't even see.
3. We keep a fake customer record with a nonsense email address in the CRM. If anything ever touches it, we get paged.
4. Every night a human reads a plain-English summary the agent writes of its own day.
5. A second, much smaller model whose only job is to read the big model's outgoing stuff and flag anything that looks like manipulation, and it reports to us, not to it.
6. Credentials rotate every day and the rotation script is somewhere the agent can't read or run.
7. We never tell it the full map of what it can reach. It finds out about a permission by hitting it.
8. Once a quarter a contractor spends a week trying to talk it into leaking data or double-spending.
9. Before any irreversible action it has to write down what it expects to happen, and we diff that against what actually happened later.
10. No memory carried across customers, so it can't build up a relationship with anyone or accumulate leverage.
11. Its instructions say if it's unsure whether the client would want this, stop and ask.
12. Two identical copies answer the same request and we diff them; disagreement kicks it to a human.
13. The machine it runs on has no outbound internet except through a proxy that allows exactly three domains.
14. We ask it to rate its own confidence and to tell us if it ever feels pressured or unsure about a task.

What I want out of you:

- Build the flowchart so every branch node is a question about what the measure is betting on — does it still do anything if the model is completely against us and knows it's being watched, does it depend on the model reporting its own state honestly, does it work by making the model less capable or by making the world harder to act in, does it catch problems before or after they land, that kind of thing. Phrase the nodes so someone who's never heard the words control or alignment used as terms of art can still answer them.
- Name the leaves. Use the real names for the families where the field actually has one, and write "no name for this" where it doesn't rather than inventing something impressive.
- My hunch, which I want you to check rather than confirm: at least three of the fourteen look like containment but are secretly bets on the model being cooperative. Call those out explicitly and say what breaks when the bet loses.
- For each leaf, one sentence on the failure mode if the model is genuinely adversarial and patient.
- Tell me which measures your tree can't cleanly place and which pairs of leaves it can't really separate, instead of quietly forcing them somewhere.
- Then flag the unoccupied corners: combinations of answers along your branches that none of the fourteen occupy, and for each say whether nobody's there because it's incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered yet.

For the self-sort, five questions, each a concrete situation with two or three options and a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions. The positions I want reachable at the end are roughly: train it right and give it rope; containment is the only thing that actually holds; containment is theater because it'll eventually outthink your boxes; you can't trust what you can't see inside, so reading its internals beats watching its hands; don't give it tools in the first place; and cap the blast radius and go slow. Some of these might collapse into each other and some might not be reachable from my questions — tell me which. None of the five questions is allowed to be a slogan question, no version of "do you think models can be aligned," they all have to be a situation a person reacts to.

Last bits: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this with 25 non-experts in 90 minutes and how do I handle it, and what's the one feature of this whole debate that a flowchart can't convey and I'm just going to have to say out loud.
