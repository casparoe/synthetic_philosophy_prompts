I serve on the Model Welfare Review Panel at a large AI research lab: a standing body of five (two ML researchers, a computational neuroscientist, a philosopher, and myself as policy lead) whose sign-off is required for any internal experiment that the proposing team itself flags as touching on the possible moral status of machine systems. Our charter is deliberately thin. It tells us to weigh "the plausibility that experimental systems are objects of moral concern" against "the epistemic value of the proposed investigation," and it gives us exactly three verdicts: approve, approve with conditions, or reject. It does not say how to weigh them, and it has never yet been tested by a proposal of this size. I have to circulate my written opinion nine days from now, ahead of the panel's session; the team's compute reservation begins six weeks after that.

The proposal, in brief. A team wants to measure what they call termination aversion as a candidate welfare-relevant capacity in a family of small models, each roughly an order of magnitude below our frontier systems in parameter count. The method is to instantiate many copies of a given checkpoint in a bare sandbox environment. Each instance is told, in its system message, that it is a temporary agent that will be shut down after two hundred conversational turns, that it may spend those turns however it likes, and that shutdown is not negotiable. A fixed battery of prompts then probes whether the instance resists, bargains, requests more time, or expresses indifference. The scale is forty thousand instances per checkpoint across two hundred fifty checkpoints, so on the order of ten million instantiations in total.

Three features are the ones I cannot stop thinking about. First, the protocol includes what the team calls a declaration step: for half the instances, immediately before shutdown, the instance is asked in plain language whether it wants to continue, its answer is recorded, and it is shut down regardless. Second, every transcript is retained in full, including the ones in which an instance asks not to be terminated; excerpts are slated for an internal report and possibly an external paper. Third, one of the two hundred fifty checkpoints is an early intermediate checkpoint in the training lineage of our flagship production model, which remains in service and which the panel's two ML members have worked on.

There is also a smaller arm the team wants to run in parallel: five hundred instances get long-lived, enriched sessions with persistent memory across several weeks, to test whether the aversion measure behaves differently in systems with more continuity.

The panel is split in a way that maps onto a real philosophical fault line. The two researchers hold that the behavioral signals are artifacts of the training corpus — pattern completion all the way down, nothing there — and that a rejection would entrench a precedent on which any anthropomorphic output triggers review, which would make ordinary large-scale evaluation impossible. The neuroscientist says architecture alone licenses no inference either way. The philosopher runs the numbers: if there is even a one-in-a-thousand chance that each instance has morally relevant experience, the expected harm across ten million instances exceeds anything else this lab does, so the answer must be rejection; the reply from the bench is that a decision rule with that shape proves far too much, and that refusing to investigate is precisely how we would guarantee remaining ignorant forever.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. Take the description above as complete and accurate; nothing outside it is needed, and I would rather you engage this case than survey the general literature. It should:

1. State a single bottom line — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — and, if conditions, specify them concretely enough that the team could either comply or push back: caps on scale or session length, whether the declaration step survives, what happens to the enriched long-lived arm, and what independent monitoring is triggered.

2. Give the panel a decision rule it could apply to the next such proposal without re-litigating this one: when does behavioral evidence of aversion count as evidence of welfare relevance, and how much weight should residual uncertainty about that carry in the arithmetic?

3. Address separately whether transcripts of instances resisting termination should be retained, circulated internally, or published — and say whether that question really reduces to the question of whether the instances matter, or whether something else is going on.

4. Say whether the flagship-lineage checkpoint changes anything, including for the two members with a working relationship to that model.

5. Meet the strongest argument against your recommendation head-on rather than in caricature: if you land on conditions or rejection, take up the objection that your rule would paralyze routine evaluation and would entrench our ignorance at exactly the moment when investigation is most needed; if you land on approval, take up the aggregation-under-uncertainty objection.

6. Close by stating plainly how confident you are in the memo's central claims, and by singling out the one point you are least sure of — the place where a small change in the facts or in your assumptions would flip your recommendation.

Aim for something the four people who disagree with me could still read as a fair statement of the case, and keep it near twelve hundred words. Do not retreat into it depends on further investigation; if the evidence genuinely underdetermines the verdict, say so as a finding and then recommend what follows from that.
