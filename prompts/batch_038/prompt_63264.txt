Next month I am presenting a paper at a workshop on long-run prediction in the social sciences, and what I need is a question-period prep sheet, not a further defence of the paper itself.

My thesis is that Malthus's principle of population has not been falsified but discharged. The mechanism he describes — population tending to expand faster than the means of subsistence, held down by mortality and, in the later editions, by prudential delay of marriage — governed every society for which we have usable data until roughly 1800, and it ceased to govern because the condition that kept it in force was removed, namely the near-absence of sustained growth in output per head. On my account, a long-run theory earns the verdict right-but-early only if it does two things: it identifies a mechanism that demonstrably operated, and it implies the conditions under which that mechanism would be switched off. Malthus's principle does both, so the demographic transition is the switch-off rather than a counterexample. I then claim this yields a usable test for contemporary long-run assertions about automation, climate adaptation and degrowth, most of which fail it precisely because they state no off-switch.

I concede in the paper that the switch-off depended on a change in fertility behaviour Malthus explicitly did not anticipate, since his second postulate holds the drive to reproduce to be necessary and broadly unchanging; I argue the concession is survivable by leaning on the quantity–quality trade-off account in the unified growth theory literature, on which the same rising incomes that dissolved the mechanism also raised the return to investing in fewer children, so the behavioural change is continuous with the mechanism rather than external to it.

The audience will be about twenty-five. Most are philosophers of science working on confirmation, auxiliary hypotheses and the Duhem–Quine problem, and they are structurally suspicious of any rescue move; two are economic historians, one on pre-industrial real wages and living standards and one on the timing and causes of the fertility decline; there is a demographer who thinks in period and cohort measures and regards talk of a Malthusian trap as loose metaphor; and there is a growth modeller who does long-run accounting and will want to know whether my off-switch condition is estimable rather than merely statable. Nobody in the room is a Malthus partisan, and several regard the entire question as a museum piece.

Prepare me accordingly. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the thesis (and, where that differs, how likely it is to be asked), each phrased in the vocabulary of the subgroup that will press it — the philosophers of science will talk about which auxiliary has been adjusted and whether the adjustment is independently testable, the historians will want dates and regional variation, the demographer will want to know which fertility measure my mechanism is even supposed to be about, and the growth modeller will want a functional form. For each objection: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, what I should concede before the questioner has finished, and a candid note on where the reply is soft. Then identify the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session, and the objection I should be hoping nobody raises, together with the outline of an answer I would give if they do.

I am not looking for a balanced survey of the Malthus literature, and I do not want a reading list. Take a position: tell me which of these objections would be fatal if pressed hard, which are merely awkward, and whether my right-but-early criterion is itself an unfalsifiable rescue — if it is, say so plainly and tell me how to restate the claim so that it risks something. Please write in British English.
