I'm co-teaching a weird little seminar this term called "How Things Fall Down" — half archaeology grad students, half philosophy people, and the two groups have been politely not understanding each other since week two. The last session is on whether "collapse" names a real kind of event at all, or is just a word we reach for when a society loses the features we happen to like. The obvious move is to sit everyone down with Easter Island and the Classic Maya and let them argue, but we've tried that twice now and it collapses (ha) into a fight about whether Diamond is a bad historian and whether the whole vocabulary erases the people who survived and kept making pots, and nobody actually thinks about the concept itself. So instead I want a designed pair of fictional-but-plausible cases, built to specification, that force the disagreement into the open using invented data rather than real contested sites.

Here's what I need. Both cases come from the same archipelago — call it whatever you like, something Pacific-ish or Mediterranean-ish, your call — and both run through the same fifty-year drought episode caused by the same volcanic event, so the exogenous shock is held constant and the only thing that varies is what each polity did and what happened to its people. No Europeans, no invaders, no migrants as the trigger, and nothing in the description may appeal to what anyone believed, intended, or decided: every claim has to be legible from an excavation and survey record — settlement counts, house-floor areas, rates of monument construction, storage capacity, isotopes, pollen and charcoal, burial demographics, whether the local script is still being used. Write each case as a site-report abstract, 200 words max, not as a story. Case A: sociopolitical complexity drops off a cliff (no more monuments, script stops, elite compounds abandoned, dispersed farmsteads instead of a capital) while per-capita well-being visibly improves — wider diets, more storage per household, better dental health, population flat or slightly up — and there's unbroken continuity in pottery style and burial practice. Case B: complexity is maintained or even grows — the palaces keep being repaired, the administrative machinery keeps producing records — while the human picture goes the other way: shrinking stature, infant mortality up, population down by a third, resource base visibly degraded. I'd like you to include at least one piece of evidence in each case that cuts against the verdict the case is engineered to produce, so there's something for a skeptic to grab; feel free to invent proxies for this, e.g. a count of administrative sealings per household as a bureaucracy measure, which in Case B keeps rising while monument volume per capita falls.

Then do the analysis, and this is the part I actually care about. Run both cases past four positions: the Tainter-style account (collapse as a rapid loss of sociopolitical complexity, possibly a rational economizing move once the marginal returns on complexity go negative), the Diamond-style account (society damages its resource base and pays for it demographically), the deflationist view in the lineage of that 2010 essay collection answering Diamond (there are no collapses, only reorganizations, and the word does political work), and a fourth view I want you to just call the well-being-first account: the only thing that can make an event a collapse is what happens to the people, per capita, as read off the osteological and dietary record. The target pattern is a criss-cross — in Case A only the Tainterian says "collapse," and says it approvingly, while the other three say nothing of the kind happened; in Case B the well-being theorist and the Diamondian both say collapse and say it while the palaces are still standing, the Tainterian says "not yet, this is pre-collapse," and the deflationist refuses the word again. Those verdicts have to fall out of what each view actually says about the evidence, not be asserted — a case that stipulates its verdicts is useless to me, since the whole point of the exercise is to show the students that four smart people can agree on every fact in the record and still disagree about whether an event occurred. After the table: name the single evidence item doing the most work in each case, then remove it (make the isotopes undatable, make the sealings unprovenienced) and say what happens to the verdicts; give me the best attack each badly-treated theory can mount, especially the Tainterian on Case B and the deflationist on both; and finish with a short paragraph on which cell of the space you deliberately left empty — complexity up, well-being up, and then a hard stop — and whether anyone could coherently occupy it. If at the end of all that "collapse" still picks out something, say what; if it's just the name of a place where four metrics disagree, say that instead, but make the students earn it.
