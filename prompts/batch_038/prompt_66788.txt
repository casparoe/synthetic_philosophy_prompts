I serve on the clinical ethics committee of a regional hospital, and we take up the following case on Thursday. A 46-year-old man who sustained bilateral insular damage in a stroke four years ago requires a series of bedside debridements for a chronic leg ulcer. He reports feeling the sharp, well-localized sensations of the procedure but says they do not bother him; he has declined both sedation and topical anesthetic, explaining that the drugs cloud his head and that the sensations are, in his words, only information about what is being done to his leg. The nursing notes conflict: one nurse documents grimacing and two episodes of his jerking the leg away mid-procedure, while another reports that he lies still, converses normally, and asks to finish early only because the sessions are tedious. His brother has asked that anesthesia be made a condition of proceeding, and the attending surgeon maintains that there is nothing to relieve and that overriding a consenting adult would be plain paternalism. Our capacity policy assumes that a patient's own account of his distress settles whether he is being harmed, and it offers no guidance when that account and his observed behavior come apart. Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the committee before the meeting: recommend a concrete course of action for the next debridement, give reasons the surgeon and the nursing staff could each respond to, engage the strongest case for the position you reject, and address whether the consideration that normally obliges us to provide analgesia applies to a patient in this condition.
