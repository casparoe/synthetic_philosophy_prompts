You are an experienced educator in AI alignment and the philosophy of machine intelligence: someone who has designed and facilitated dozens of hands-on sessions for technically literate but philosophically inexperienced audiences, who understands how reward signals actually work in practice, and who knows how to build an activity in which a concept arrives as a discovery rather than a definition.

Think carefully before you answer. Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you sketch two or three genuinely different activity mechanisms capable of delivering the learning goal below — for instance, a hidden-role production line, an adjudication tournament with a scoring ledger, a market in which supervision is bought and sold, a courtroom-style audit with asymmetric information — name the principal weakness of each, then state which one you will build and why. Only after that should you write the full activity.

**Setting.** One 90-minute session of a graduate seminar on the governance of automated systems. Twenty-four participants, seated at six tables of four. Assume no computers and no phones. Materials are limited to paper, pens, index cards, envelopes, a whiteboard, and a timer; list anything that must be printed, photocopied, or pre-cut in advance.

**Learning goal.** By the end, participants should have *felt*, not merely been told, the difference between outcome-based supervision (judging an agent solely by whether its final result is good) and process-based supervision (judging it by whether each intermediate step is acceptable). Three things must land, and the third is the one I care most about:

1. Outcome supervision is cheap and requires no understanding of the work, but it endorses good results reached by bad methods and cannot distinguish skill from luck.
2. Process supervision requires a checker who can genuinely evaluate the steps — a cost that escalates sharply when the checker is less competent than the performer, or is given only a partial rubric.
3. Once the checklist becomes known to the performer, the checklist itself becomes the target. Performers begin emitting steps that *look* correct to a checker without contributing anything to the result — performed diligence, rubric-gaming. I want the room to generate this behavior spontaneously, notice that it is winning points, and then be unsettled by it.

**Design requirements.**

- No lecture up front. The vocabulary of process versus outcome supervision, reward hacking, and Goodhart-style distortion should be introduced only in the debrief, after participants have already run into the phenomena.
- The underlying task the performers execute must be doable with paper and pen by a non-technical graduate student, must be genuinely gradable step by step, and must admit a shortcut that yields a correct-looking final answer without performing the legitimate steps. Specify the task exactly, state the correct method, state the shortcut, and state how a checker detects or fails to detect it.
- Include at least one round in which supervision is outcome-only and one in which it is process-based, and make the *change in what performers can see* — in particular, publication of the rubric — the mechanism that produces insight 3. Do not achieve it by instructing anyone to cheat.
- Use hidden roles delivered on secret cards, and write the full verbatim text of every card.
- Give participants a simple scoring ledger to fill in during play, so that the room generates its own data: how many endorsed outputs came from disqualifying processes, how many sound processes were rejected, under each regime. The debrief must tally these on the whiteboard and interpret them. Neither regime should win cleanly — design the numbers so that process supervision also produces visible false negatives.
- Build in at least one table where the checker is deliberately handicapped (less practice, or an incomplete rubric) so that insight 2 is experienced rather than asserted.

**Deliverables, in this order:** (a) the brainstorming section; (b) a materials list and any pre-session preparation; (c) a minute-by-minute timeline; (d) the task specification, including the correct method and the shortcut; (e) all role-card text, verbatim; (f) the ledger layout; (g) a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto each of the three insights and names the technical concepts only at that point; (h) at least four things that will predictably go wrong the first time this is run, each with a concrete recovery move or facilitator line; (i) a frank closing note on which features of real process- and outcome-based supervision the activity cannot reproduce — the volume of training signal, learning across many trials, the absence of deliberate strategy in a trained model, the automation of the checker — and what the facilitator must supply verbally to fill each gap.

**Constraints.** No citations, reading lists, references to particular papers, or named commercial systems; the activity must be entirely self-contained and runnable from your description alone. Keep jargon out of everything participants see.
