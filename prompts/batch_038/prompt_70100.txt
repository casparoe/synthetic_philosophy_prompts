I've been on my college's student hardship fund committee for three years now — six of us, two faculty, two admin staff, two student reps, and we meet every couple of weeks to divide up a pot that's roughly forty grand a term. The remit we were handed when the fund was set up says we exist "to make sure no student is held back at this university by their financial situation," and I promise you that basically every argument we have is secretly an argument about what that sentence is doing. Nobody on the committee has any philosophy training, including me (I do a lot of googling at 11pm), but we've built up a body of decisions over the last few years and I want to work out what theory we're actually running, because we're supposed to write a public guidance doc at the end of term and I don't want us to publish something we'd be embarrassed by.

Our track record, as best I can reconstruct it: (a) a student whose family business collapsed mid-semester, we covered his tuition and living costs for the full year, no debate; (b) last year we turned down an almost identical request from a guy whose own income was just as low but whose parents are both well-paid and had simply decided to stop supporting him — we said household resources count, not just his personal bank balance, and he was furious and said we were means-testing his family rather than him; (c) we fund a standard laptop for anyone whose coursework needs one and we do not means-test that at all, and on the same logic we funded a note-taker, a specialist screen-reader setup and a bunch of extra transport costs for a Deaf student from a comfortably-off family — that whole package cost maybe five times our biggest hardship grant; (d) a student asked us to upgrade his standard-issue machine to a high-spec one because the base model "can't do what I need," and when we pushed, what he needed was 4K video editing and gaming, so we said no — but the very same term we did pay for a beefier machine for a music production student whose assignments required rendering, and for a dentistry student who needed specific CAD software; (e) a student who took a £1,500 award from us, put it into crypto and lost all of it came back for a second award and we refused him, while that same term a student whose landlord ran off with her deposit and two months of rent got a second award from us without a moment's hesitation; (f) twice now we've funded things purely on the grounds that someone looked likely to not finish their degree — a carer's grant for respite hours and bus fares, and a payment to cover the costs of a mandatory unpaid placement — even though neither of those applicants scored badly on the wellbeing question we ask on the form.

Now this week's round, and we have money to do one of these properly, not both. Applicant A is a mature student, 34, first in her family to go to uni, works nights in a care home, no dependants, and she is miles below everyone else on our financial-need ranking — bottom decile easily, and that's counting her as a household of one like we do in (b). Her grades are fine, her attendance is fine. She's asked only for the standard laptop, and in the optional box on the form where we ask how she's doing she wrote that she's genuinely happy, that things are good, and that she doesn't want to take money away from anyone worse off than her. Applicant B is 20, second year, finances mid-range — above the median of our applicants, below the university average. She's miserable and anxious, says so plainly, has missed two deadlines, is on an informal academic warning, and wants a rent top-up so she can drop from twenty hours of work a week to ten. The two student reps have already said out loud that they'll vote for B "because she's the one actually suffering," one of the admin staff has said that A is objectively the poorest person we'll see all year and it would be indefensible to skip her, and the faculty chair keeps asking what "held back" means in our own remit, which is unhelpful at 4pm on a Thursday. Two of the reps have also started throwing around the phrase "adaptive preferences" about A, and one of them pushed back with what I thought was a decent line — that this is just a polite way of telling a 34-year-old woman that she's wrong about her own life. I keep wanting to say both that A's contentment shouldn't count because she's been taught to want little, and that B's distress should count because it's real and present, and I'm aware those two intuitions can't both be as obviously right as they feel.

So: what rule, or what mixture of rules, is our committee actually operating? I'd like the candidate metrics spelled out properly — I know there's a whole debate about what the equalisandum should be, welfare versus resources versus capabilities, plus the responsibility-sensitive versions of each — and I want to know which of those survive all six of our precedents, which get killed by which precedent, and whether any of our past decisions have to be written off as one-off mistakes rather than evidence. Then tell me what each surviving rule says about A versus B, give me your best guess at how we'll actually vote and why, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, say plainly what principle we'd be committing ourselves to with each choice — because that's the thing that'll end up in the guidance doc. Finally, design me one further hypothetical application, cheap to describe, that would split the surviving rules cleanly apart, so we can use it as a test case next term.
