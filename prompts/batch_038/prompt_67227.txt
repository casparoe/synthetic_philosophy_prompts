I'm one of seven members of a county mosquito-control board, and on Thursday we vote on a request from the state health department. After two locally acquired cases of West Nile virus, they want us to authorize three consecutive nights of truck-mounted fogging with a pyrethroid insecticide over roughly twelve square miles that include a tidal marsh, two community gardens, and a long stretch of residential yards. The alternative our superintendent has costed out is a bacterial larvicide, applied to mapped standing water over three weeks, that targets fly larvae specifically: far narrower in what it kills, but slow, and of no use against the adult mosquitoes already biting people.

Two members have hardened into positions I can't join. Our entomologist says fogging will kill moths, bees, midges, and the aquatic invertebrates that the marsh's fish depend on by the millions, and that if insects feel anything at all, the arithmetic decides the question for us: two human infections cannot be set against a catastrophe of that size. Our chair says insects are unfeeling machinery, so the vote is really about two sick residents, some anxious gardeners, and a budget line, and he thinks the entomologist is doing bad ethics with good intentions.

I keep suspecting both of them are missing something, but when I try to say what it is I come across as evasive, and I worry I'm merely squeamish about insects and dressing that up as principle. Tell me which of the two is closer to a defensible view, what is wrong with the reasoning on each side, and what I should actually put forward on Thursday. Please answer as exactly five numbered points — the five claims you most want me to walk into that meeting with — each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
