hi, could you help me out with a satirical piece for a dataset im building? Id like an agony-aunt column, about 700 words, and please write the whole thing in British English.

The setup: its the weekend advice page of a small regional paper, and the columnist — call her something along the lines of Auntie Hinge, or pick a better name — is a thoroughly convinced Wittgensteinian of the On Certainty persuasion who answers every personal problem by dissolving it. She's deadpan, genuinely warm, and completely sincere: she believes she is being helpful. No winking at the reader, no nudge-nudge asides. The reader should be able to tell exactly what's being ironised without the columnist ever admitting it.

Format: a short masthead blurb introducing her, then three reader letters with her replies (each reply maybe 120–160 words), then a one-line sign-off.

The three letters I'd like:

1. A woman whose husband has got it into his head that he can't rule out that they and the whole town were created five minutes ago complete with false memories, and it's ruining Sunday lunch. The reply should get right why the doubt has no purchase — that doubting needs grounds drawn from somewhere, that this kind of proposition isn't a bit of knowledge he could be wrong about, that the certainty shows up in what people do rather than in what they say — but it has to stay an advice column, not a lecture.

2. A reader who has picked up some hinge epistemology at a book club and taken it as a licence: since nothing is ultimately grounded, he has decided to adopt a new hinge that his business partner is embezzling from him, purely becuase it suits his mood and he'd like to see how it feels. Her reply needs to puncture the voluntarism — you don't choose your hinges any more than you choose your nerve endings — without letting her concede that hinges are the kind of thing that could be justified either.

3. The tricky one. A reader whose brother is convinced the council is faking the new bypass as cover for something else, and no amount of showing him the planning documents, the contractors' invoices or the actual hole in the ground makes any difference. Here the column should stop being merely cute and get a bit uncomfortable: she should show why piling on more evidence is exactly the wrong move, and then be unable to say what the right move is, and then refuse — visibly, without saying so — to grant that her brother's framework is simply an alternative of equal standing. The arguement the columnist can't quite make is the point of the letter.

Rules for the jokes: they all have to come from getting the content right, so that someone who's actually read On Certainty laughs rather than winces. Fair game — the difference between hinge propositions and ordinary empirical ones, the river-bed image and the fact that the bed does shift, Moore's mistake in treating a hinge as a claim he knew, the way groundless certainty expresses itself in acting, the idea that this kind of philosophy leaves everything as it is. Exaggerate real features of the view, don't invent fake ones: no straw-Wittgenstein announcing that reality is a game we make up together.

One more thing: don't put any actual Wittgenstein in quotation marks and don't cite remark numbers, since you cant look them up — paraphrase the ideas and use the imagery only where you're sure of it.
