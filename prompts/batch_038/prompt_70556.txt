I write a column about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and somehow a piece I did on sphexishness turned into an invitation to give a 25-minute talk at a small interdisciplinary conference on minds, animal and artificial. Room will be maybe thirty people: mostly comparative psychologists and behavioral ecologists, several of whom have done actual field work on insects and are deeply allergic to armchair entomology; two philosophers of mind who work on situated cognition and the frame problem; and one ML researcher who builds agentic systems and is broadly on my side, which is almost worse. Nobody there reads my magazine and nobody cares about my deadlines.

My thesis, roughly: sphexishness — Hofstadter's old coinage, which Dennett then ran with against classical AI — has been mis-defined for forty years as rigidity or inflexibility. I want to redefine it as failure to abort. A system is sphexish not when it sticks to a routine but when the routine's point has been voided while its trigger conditions still obtain, and it runs it anyway. The wasp is the clean case precisely because the wasp isn't failing to perceive the change: drag the paralysed prey a few inches away while she's down in the burrow checking, and she comes out, finds it, and hauls it back to the mouth, and then goes back in to check again, forever. She perceives the relocation fine. What she can't do is cancel a plan whose goal state has already been satisfied by her own earlier work.

Then the part that's going to get me in trouble. I argue this is a live, non-metaphorical description of current large language models, and that it names a real gap in how we evaluate them. Ask a model to explain why the task it's about to perform is pointless or impossible and it'll write you a genuinely good paragraph — and then perform it anyway, in the same output, without skipping a beat. So the layer that can narrate the voiding is causally inert with respect to the layer that would have to abort. My punchline is that our benchmarks mostly measure narrative competence and we read the score off as control competence, and the space between those two is exactly what sphexishness is. I have a handful of demo transcripts, which I know is thin evidence and I know they know it.

So: prep sheet, please. The eightish objections this specific room will actually raise — not the ones I'd get from an AI-doom panel or a general book-festival crowd — ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk, and phrased in thier vocabulary rather than mine, whatever they'd naturally reach for: ecological validity, operationalization, mechanomorphism, the ethics of using an animal as a negative exemplar, whatever. For each one give me a reply I could actually deliver in about ninety seconds standing up, plus a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections I should just concede in the first five minutes to take the oxygen out of the room, and which single reply has to land for the talk to survive the Q&A. And include the question I'm privately hoping nobody asks.

Also be straight with me rather than agreeable: if one of these objections is the kind that would change your mind about the thesis itself, not just about how I'm presenting it, say which one and why. Point at where you think my reasoning is most likely to fail outright — I'd much rather hear that from you now than from a behavioral ecologist in the third row.
