So my PhD advisor retired in June and the department asked me — unanimously, apparently, and without consulting me — to give the toast at the dinner in october. I've been puting it off for a month and now it's three weeks away.

He's a cosmologist, and for the last twenty-odd years he worked on basically one problem: the Boltzmann brain objection. In case you're rusty on it: if your theory says the universe goes on long enough and ends up in equilibrium, then random thermal fluctuations should produce vastly more momentary brains-with-false-memories than actual evolved observers like us — so anyone who sincerely believes that theory ought to conclude she's probably one of those flakes, briefly hallucinating an entire life, and therefore can't trust any of the observations that led her to the theory in the first place. He treated that as a reductio and spent his career killing models that failed it. It wasn't a hobby, it was his whole professional identity.

Which creates the problem. Nearly every warm thing you'd normally say at one of these dinners is exactly the move his life's work says is worthless. "We'll all remember this." "Your students will carry it forward." "Thirty years of evidence that..." — memory, testimony, other observers reporting on things, all of it, on his own account, is the least trustworthy material in the multiverse. I don't want to sand that down into generic gratitude, and I also don't want to turn a dinner into a lecture on the measure problem.

So: write the toast. About four minutes, meant to be said out loud. I want the argument doing real structural work — it should determine what I'm permitted to say and what I have to leave out, not just turn up as a nerdy aside for the people who'll get it. The room is maybe a third cosmologists who will catch everything, and the rest family: his wife (a pediatric nurse who has said, out loud, that she finds the whole subject morbid), his three kids, and his younger sister, who's a Methodist minister. Please don't be cute about souls and don't make the wife the butt of anything.

It should get exactly one real laugh — one that the cosmologists get as a substantive point and the family gets as affection. The "you spent your career trying to prove we aren't figments" territory is available, but try not to use the most obvious version of it. And the ending has to actually honor him without saying anything he'd count as a bad argument; if you have to pick between moving and rigorous, pick rigorous — he'll respect it more and it'll land harder.

Reason I care this much, for what it's worth: reading his papers as a first-year gave me a genuinely bad six weeks. I could not shake the feeling that my memories of my own childhood were the least likely available explanation for having them, and he was the only person who didn't tell me that was silly. He asked what my priors were. So if the toast can nod at that without becoming about me, good.

Two last things. Don't put words in anyone's mouth — no invented quotations from famous cosmologists, he'll be sitting three feet away and he will know. And here's a detail you can build on: his office whiteboard has carried the same sentence since 2011, erased and rewritten at the start of every semester in slightly different words. I'm deliberately not telling you what it says. Work out what it would have to be.
