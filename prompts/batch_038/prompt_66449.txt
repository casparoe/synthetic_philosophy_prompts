I sit on a small advisory panel attached to a city government that has been flirting with publishing its own wellbeing dashboard, and last week's meeting turned into an argument I have not managed to settle in my own head since. One of the other members — an economist, an unabashed fan of the annual international happiness league tables — defended the whole enterprise with an analogy he says he picked up at a methods seminar. Asking people to place their own life on the familiar zero-to-ten ladder, where the top is the best possible life for them and the bottom the worst, is exactly like putting a thermometer in a room. The instrument is crude, any individual reading is noisy, and the number means nothing unless you know what it is an instrument for — but nobody concludes from that noise that temperature is not real, and nobody says a reading taken in Oslo cannot be compared with one taken in Lagos merely because the rooms differ. On his account the ladder measures something, namely how people are doing, cross-country differences in the averages are differences in that thing, and so a government that wants to do well should simply aim to move its number up. He even offered the finding that people in some middle-income countries report doing better than their incomes would predict as evidence that the instrument is picking up something the income statistics miss, rather than as a symptom of the instrument misfiring. When a younger colleague objected that thermometers are calibrated against an independent standard while self-reports are calibrated against nothing, he replied that the ladder is just a thermometer that has not been calibrated yet, and that anchoring vignettes, translation protocols, and repeated cross-national panels are precisely the calibration programme, still in progress.

Could you audit that analogy for me, properly rather than rhetorically? I would like the correspondence laid out explicitly — what plays the role of the temperature, the mercury or thermistor, the graduated scale, the room, the person reading the instrument off, the calibration bath, the ambient conditions — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that look merely contingent, where better instruments or better methods could in principle close the gap, and the ones that look structural, where no methodological improvement would help. I am especially interested in whether the causal arrow runs the same way in the two cases; in what changes when the thing being measured can itself be moved by the act of measuring it, or by what the respondent takes the scale to be asking about; in whether holding the scale constant really buys comparability once the wording is translated and people bring different reference groups, different habits of self-presentation, and different ideas about what a seven is; and in whether an evaluative judgement about one's life as a whole is even the same kind of quantity as a momentary physical state, given that the ladder and the affect questions seem to rank different countries differently. His fallback position matters here, so please treat the claim that the analogy fails only because calibration is incomplete as a live option to be argued with, not dismissed.

Then tell me whether his conclusion survives. He wants three separate things from the analogy: that the ladder tracks something real; that the numbers are comparable enough across countries to support a ranking at all; and that a government should therefore treat a higher national average as a target to be maximised. Say which of those the analogy can carry, which it cannot, and whether the third is really a different question from the first two — one about targets, incentives, and the possibility of gaming the number rather than about measurement. If you think there is a better analogy for what national wellbeing statistics actually are — a barometer, a symptom, a self-portrait, an election result, a price index, something else entirely — say what it is, what work it does that the thermometer cannot, and be candid about what the replacement smuggles in of its own. Finally, please close with what would change your mind: which argument, finding, or consideration would lead you to a different verdict on the analogy, and at which step in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong. I have read enough of these debates to know that partisans on both sides settle the conclusion first and then go looking for the disanalogy that supports it, and I would rather know up front whether you have done the same.
