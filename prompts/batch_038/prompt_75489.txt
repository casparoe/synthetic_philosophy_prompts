I am a junior, and over winter break our school's poker club ran a hundred-round coin game as a practice exercise: a fair coin, and before each round you choose what fraction of your bankroll to stake — heads pays back fifty percent of the stake, tails costs forty percent of it. Two of my friends played opposite strategies. Dana staked her entire bankroll every round; Sam staked a fixed quarter every round, which he said was the growth-optimal fraction. I recorded everything in a spreadsheet, and the results were strange: Dana's expected final bankroll worked out to roughly a hundred and thirty times her starting money, but her median result was about half of one percent of it, whereas Sam's median was close to double his stake. In my simulations Dana still finished above where she began roughly one time in nine. Neither of them would concede anything afterward, and I could not tell which one was making the mistake.

(1) Sam: staking everything is irrational even though each round has a positive expected value, because the long-run growth rate is negative, so over many rounds you are very likely to end up far behind someone staking less.

(2) Dana: expected value is the standard of rational choice, and your quarter-stake rule is the Kelly criterion, which just means maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth — you have picked one arbitrary utility function out of infinitely many and dressed it up as arithmetic.

(3) Sam: it is not a utility function. It concerns what actually happens to one person over time, not an average over imaginary simultaneous copies of her; the mean across parallel universes is not a fact about my bankroll.

(4) Dana: then explain why we only played a hundred rounds. Over any finite horizon, expected final wealth is maximized by staking everything, since expectation is linear in the stake. You abandon expected value precisely when it stops favoring you.

(5) Sam: the median outcome and the probability of finishing below your starting money both favor a quarter; your higher average is carried entirely by lucky paths that almost never occur.

(6) Dana: except that they occur about one time in nine, by my own count. So the choice of statistic decides which of us is irrational, and you owe me a reason to prefer the median to the mean that is not simply a restatement of your preference.

Please adjudicate move by move: say which of the six steps is right, partly right, or mistaken, and why, then say who is more right overall and what both of them may be missing — including whether anything would change if Dana could replenish her bankroll from a part-time job, or if neither of them knew for certain that the coin was fair. Finally, please distinguish what here is settled mathematics from what remains genuinely disputed among people who work on these questions, and do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus.
