ok so i'm prepping a handout for a grad decision theory seminar and I need a thought experiment built to spec. Half the room is convinced that risk-weighted expected utility (Buchak's REU) is immune to money pumps because it evaluates globally individuated acts rather than outcomes one at a time, and I want a case that settles it using the theory's own arithmetic instead of assertion. So the case has to actually *generate* the verdict — if you stipulate that the trade "looks tempting" without running the numbers at each node, it fails.

constraints on the setup:

- the agent's risk function is r(p) = p^2 and her utility is linear in dollars, u(x) = x. REU computed the standard way: order the outcomes worst to best, and the decision weight on each outcome is r(P(at least this good)) − r(P(strictly better)). Same r at every node — no preference change, no ambiguity, no uncertainty about her own future behavior.
- all probabilities objective and known. one ordinary chance device (a fair coin, or a coin plus some stated random draw). no exotic physics, no predictors, no state-dependent utility.
- stakes a real person might face, a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, in a plausible everyday setting — a job, a medical decision, a small business, a company's randomized bonus draw. not a casino.
- at most two decision nodes, with at least one chance event resolved before the second one, so that conditioning on the branch she actually landed in is something she can really do.

what it has to do:

1. at node 1 she chooses between act A and act B and takes A, because REU(A) > REU(B).
2. the draw resolves and she's offered a $50 trade that swaps what she holds in that branch for something else. Evaluated with the same r applied to the *conditional* distribution given the branch she's in, the trade is REU-improving, so she takes it. She must not look stupid — it has to be the locally right call by her own lights. the trade can be any shape you like (gamble for a sure amount, buying down the downside, whatever) as long as the rest holds.
3. the act she ends up holding, net of the $50, is globally REU-worse than plain A.
4. and this is the part that makes it a pump rather than a mistake: if the exact same trade had been offered at node 1 as a single package before the draw, she'd have turned it down. She refuses the package and then buys it in pieces.
5. hand the identical tree to an agent with the same dollar-linear utility but r(p) = p — plain expected utility — and she must not be pumpable: either she declines the $50 trade in that branch, or taking it leaves her globally better off. I want the risk function isolated as the culprit.
6. bonus points if the branch she *didn't* land in would have offered her a symmetric trade she'd also accept, so the pump works whichever way the draw goes.

then show the work:

- give me the tree and the full arithmetic at every node: outcome distribution, cumulative probabilities, decision weights, REU figures. I want to be able to check it on a calculator.
- name in one sentence which structural feature of the case does the work, then run the removal tests. what happens if r is linear. what happens if she's allowed to commit at node 1 (resolute). what happens if she's sophisticated — at node 1 she correctly anticipates the trade she'll accept at node 2 and folds that into her choice between A and B. Does sophistication dissolve the pump or just relocate it?
- give the REU defender's best attack on the case, not a strawman. the real line is that REU only ever ranks globally individuated acts, so a conditional evaluation of one branch isn't a verdict the theory issues at all. Then say what that reply costs her.

finally, commit — I don't want a menu. Tell me which of these you actually think the case supports: (a) REU isn't a theory of rational choice for agents who decide over time; (b) REU is fine as a theory of one-shot choice but incomplete until its given an account of dynamic choice; or (c) the case doesn't touch REU at all because it illicitly imports a local evaluation the theory never endorsed. Pick one and argue for it. And flag explicitly, rather than burying it, the place in your construction where you had to make a choice that a partisan of one of the other answers would dispute — starting with whether applying the risk function to the conditional distribution of a resolved branch is even legitimate.

Keep the scenario itself tight, a paragraph or two; let the verification take the space.
