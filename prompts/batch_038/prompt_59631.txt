My friend and I argued for most of last night about whether the climate-and-geography school of history deserves the reflexive dismissal it gets, and we both invoked Henry Thomas Buckle without having actually read him. I have since gone to the source: the opening of Chapter II of Volume I of his *History of Civilization in England* (1857), "Influence Exercised by Physical Laws over the Organization of Society and over the Character of Individuals."

> If we inquire what those physical agents are by which the human race is most powerfully influenced, we shall find that they may be classed under four heads: namely, Climate, Food, Soil, and the General Aspect of Nature; by which last, I mean those appearances which, though presented chiefly to the sight, have, through the medium of that or other senses, directed the association of ideas, and hence in different countries have given rise to different habits of national thought. To one of these four classes, may be referred all the external phenomena by which Man has been permanently affected. The last of these classes, or what I call the General Aspect of Nature, produces its principal results by exciting the imagination, and by suggesting those innumerable superstitions which are the great obstacles to advancing knowledge. And as, in the infancy of a people, the power of such superstitions is supreme, it has happened that the various Aspects of Nature have caused corresponding varieties in the popular character, and have imparted to the national religion peculiarities which, under certain circumstances, it is impossible to efface. The other three agents, namely, Climate, Food, and Soil, have, so far as we are aware, had no direct influence of this sort; but they have, as I am about to prove, originated the most important consequences in regard to the general organization of society, and from them there have followed many of those large and conspicuous differences between nations, which are often ascribed to some fundamental difference in the various races into which mankind is divided. But while such original distinctions of race are altogether hypothetical, the discrepancies which are caused by difference of climate, food, and soil, are capable of a satisfactory explanation, and, when understood, will be found to clear up many of the difficulties which still obscure the study of history.

Three things in this paragraph are defeating me, and I want help reading it rather than being handed Buckle's reputation.

1. The split. The aspect of nature works "by exciting the imagination," while climate, food, and soil "have, so far as we are aware, had no direct influence of this sort," working instead on the organization of society. So there are two causal routes with different mechanisms and apparently different domains. Does that architecture do real work for him — why does one class of environment require the imagination as intermediary and the other not — and what is "directed the association of ideas" doing there as a mechanism? Relatedly, does "physical laws" in his chapter heading mean the laws of physics, or laws concerning physical things?

2. The limiting clauses. My friend's charge is that "so far as we are aware," "under certain circumstances," and "in the infancy of a people" are the usual unfalsifiable padding, absorbing any counterexample. But the last of them looks like a commitment rather than an escape: it implies that the aspect of nature loses its grip as knowledge accumulates, so the theory predicts its own weakening in exactly the places where it has been most successful. Is that the right reading? If so, does Buckle end up with a two-track account — environment sovereign in early and non-European societies, subordinated in modern Europe — and is that a resource for him or a fudge?

3. The race sentence. He calls "original distinctions of race ... altogether hypothetical" and offers climate, food, and soil as what actually explains the differences between nations that people ordinarily explain by race, which reads like a substantive anti-racial move for 1857. My friend says it is cosmetic: one fixed inheritance has been traded for another, and peoples are still made rather than making. Which of us has it right? Is there anything in the paragraph itself — the emphasis on the accumulation of wealth, on knowledge, on the imagination as the site of effect — that makes the substitution more than a change of label?

Please stay close to the wording; what I want is an account of what he is claiming, not a verdict on whether the claims are true. And where the reading of Buckle is a matter of dispute rather than fact, mark it as such: I want to be able to tell my friend which parts of your account are settled among people who work on this material and which are live disagreements, and I do not want a contested interpretation served to me as though it were the consensus.
