You are a philosopher of biology who specializes in the conceptual foundations of quantitative genetics and who has spent years training working science journalists to read behavioral-genetics papers. Think carefully before you answer, and begin with a short brainstorming section: which distinctions in the technical literature have to survive translation into newsroom practice, and which can be dropped without producing a falsehood. State plainly which ones you are dropping and what you lose by dropping them.

I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet. Roughly twice a month a heritability claim lands on me: a twin-study preprint, a large-biobank GWAS, a direct-to-consumer genomics press release, a policy argument resting on the premise that a trait is mostly genetic. I have about forty minutes per item, no statistics training beyond what a good explainer gives me, and a standards editor who must sign off on any sentence that attributes variance. What I need is a reusable procedure, not an essay about heritability.

Write it as numbered steps in the order I should run them. For each step give the question I should put to the paper or release, what counts as a sufficient answer, what I write if the answer is absent, and whether the step is a genuine test or a judgment call wearing a test's clothing. The procedure must terminate in one of a small fixed set of verdicts — kill the item; report the finding without a number; report it with a qualified number; report it and flag the framing separately — and for the two middle verdicts it must hand me one usable sentence of copy that is accurate without collapsing into "it's complicated."

It has to handle at least these: variance explained in this sample versus what travels to another population or another environment; twin-based and SNP-based estimates of the same trait that disagree, and which one a reporter should quote; a heritability figure that is statistically sound but whose newsworthiness depends on an immutability claim the number does not support; and the situation I see most often, where the paper is careful and the press release is not.

Then run the procedure end to end on three items: a genomics company's release announcing that a behavioral condition is forty percent genetic, where the underlying work reports variance explained in one biobank; an adolescent twin-study preprint reporting high heritability of risk-taking, alongside a published commentary arguing the estimate falls sharply in low-income subsamples; and a policy proposal to sort students using polygenic scores derived almost entirely from a single ancestry group, where the paper's numbers and hedging are exemplary. At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like — do not soften it.

Finish with the limits: which questions this procedure can never settle, where a reporter will still be guessing, and what would have to be true for the whole apparatus to be quietly misleading. No bibliography, reading list, or citations, please; I cannot verify them and do not need them.
