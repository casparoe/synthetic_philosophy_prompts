Some context. I've been chewing on acausal threats for maybe seven years, on and off, ever since I first hit the multiverse-wide cooperation material. Setup, roughly: if there are enough agents out there who can predict my behavior but can't reach me causally, some of them might try to get me to do things by having already committed to bringing about some outcome I disvalue in their own light cone, conditional on my choice. One proposed defense is the surrogate-goal move: you deliberately adopt a new terminal goal — advertised, genuinely cheap for you to serve, and something you'd genuinely be willing to see sacrificed — so that when a threatener goes after the thing you actually care about, you can redirect the threat onto the decoy and cave without your real values taking the hit. It turns "you can't blackmail me, I have nothing to lose" into "blackmail me, it costs almost nothing," which is supposed to be a better deal than a brittle never-negotiate stance.

Here's the half-formed idea I want broken before I build on it. I think the surrogate-goal move always gets analyzed case by case, one threatener at a time, and that's the wrong unit of analysis. Two worries:

(1) Leakage. Anyone sophisticated enough to make the threat work at all can model me well enough to see the surrogate is a decoy. A decoy known to be a decoy doesn't do the job — it just moves the threat up a level, onto my policy for adopting and defending surrogates.

(2) Market effect. If caving by sacrificing a surrogate is the individually rational response to acausal blackmail, then manufacturing acausal threats becomes productive from the threatener's side, and the more widely the policy spreads the more threats get produced. So a defense that looks free per case could be expensive in aggregate in a way no single agent can see at decision time. Related: one surrogate can be threatened by many threateners at once, so its cheapness isn't stable — it's a commons, and its not obvious the adopter can price that.
