I have no academic background in this subject. I spent several years reading game theory on my own while running a weekly strategy-games group, and I am now applying for a place on a part-time MA, which requires a proposal for an independent piece of research of roughly 10,000 words to be completed over twelve months.

The half-formed idea I want to develop concerns the assumption that rationality is common knowledge among the players. It seems to me that this assumption is made to do two quite different jobs in the standard arguments. Sometimes it operates as a claim about what each player knows of the others' reasoning, and is used to rule out possibilities in advance. At other times it operates as a licence to keep drawing conclusions at points in the game that the assumption itself has already declared unreachable, which is precisely where the centipede game and other backward-induction arguments get their bite. My suspicion is that much of the familiar puzzlement here comes from sliding between these two jobs without notice, and that pulling them apart would let one say what a player is entitled to believe about an opponent after an unexpected move.

Please turn this into a proper proposal. I need a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer rather than being a restatement of an old disagreement; an account of why the existing treatments leave the question open instead of settling it; the method I would actually follow, month by month, together with a defence of that method against the obvious alternatives, such as rebuilding the analysis on the idea of small probabilities of error, or exploiting the resemblance to the surprise-examination paradox; a description of what a successful result would look like and what would show that the project had failed; and an honest judgement on whether the whole thing is too ambitious for someone with a year, no formal training and no supervisor. I will supply the references myself, so please do not include a bibliography.
