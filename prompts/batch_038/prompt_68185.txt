I work as a risk analyst. I price insurance portfolios, I do outside-view forecasting for infrastructure clients, and from time to time I give expert evidence in civil proceedings. Over the years I have accumulated a set of firm judgements about when a base rate may legitimately be applied to a single case and when it may not. What I have never managed is to state a principle that survives contact with the whole list. I should like to know whether one exists.

Here are the verdicts.

1. A clinician asks me for the probability that a particular patient — 58, male, smoker, two previous cardiac events — survives five years. I am entirely happy to take the frequency in the class of statistically similar patients and report it as the probability for him. No unease at all; I would think it evasive if the clinician refused the number.

2. The same clinician asks me for the probability that this patient gives up smoking within the year. I refuse to hand over the class figure. I insist on facts about him: whether he has booked a cessation clinic, whether his partner has just quit, whether this admission was the frightening one. The base rate here feels as though it answers a different question from the one asked. I cannot explain the difference from case 1, since there too I have facts about him and use them cheerfully.

3. A client asks for the probability that a proposed light-rail line comes in on budget. I begin from the recorded outcomes of comparable rail projects and treat the client's bottom-up schedule as an adjustment to that distribution, never as a substitute for it. If a project team presents an elegant detailed plan and no comparison class, I discount their number almost to zero.

4. A client asks the same question about a first-of-a-kind demonstration plant with no genuine precedent. I refuse to widen the class to "large public projects in general" in order to produce a figure. I would rather report that no defensible number exists than manufacture one from a class that shares nothing relevant. I am aware that in case 3 I positively demanded a class spanning projects that differ from this one in many respects.

5. In a civil case, the claimant was struck by a bus whose operator is unknown; the evidence is that the defendant runs 70 per cent of the buses in the town. I think that evidence, standing alone, should not support a finding for the claimant, even though on any threshold view of the standard of proof it puts the probability at 0.7.

6. A numerically equivalent case in which the 0.7 comes instead from an eyewitness — the witness says the bus wore the defendant's livery, and the court is told the witness identifies liveries correctly 70 per cent of the time in those conditions — I do think suffices. Same number, opposite verdict. I hold both, and I accept that any account on which the standard of proof is simply a probability threshold must pronounce one of them wrong.

7. That same 0.7 statistic, used to justify screening everyone in the group or to set a premium, strikes me as perfectly good evidence. My resistance in case 5 is specific to the courtroom, and all I can offer by way of explanation is the phrase "that case is about this person".

8. On how narrow to go: I accept narrowing the cardiac class by a genetic marker whose mechanism nobody can explain to me, and I reject narrowing it by month of birth, for which I likewise have no mechanism. Pressed, I say the marker is a real property of the patient and the birthday is an artefact of how the records happen to be sorted. I recognise that this is a shrug dressed as a rule.

9. On my own account: I forecast political events on a prediction platform and essentially never consult base rates. I read the reporting, produce a number, and over several years have beaten people who do consult them. Nevertheless I tell clients, in case 3, that the base rate must govern. I do not know whether the difference is that political forecasting is a skill and project forecasting is not, or that I am flattering myself.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of candidates, that generates these verdicts. State each clause precisely and identify the case that motivates it, so that I can see what work each clause is doing. Then tell me what it delivers on cases I have not listed — specifically: whether an insurer may use a postcode-level class that correlates strongly with ethnicity; whether a parole board may rely on a recidivism score derived from people who differ from the prisoner precisely in the respects that matter to him; and what your principle says about a genuinely unrepeatable event such as the outcome of the next general election.

Most importantly, give me the cost. Say which of my nine verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which of them I must therefore give up, and say whether the ones that survive do so for reasons that ought to embarrass me — because I would rather be told that my practice rests on two incompatible principles applied in different rooms than be handed a scheme that quietly rewrites my cases until they fit.

I want a committed answer. Do not survey the options and leave the choice to me. Name the principle you think is correct, defend it against the obvious rivals — a pure probability-threshold account, accounts that turn on causal or explanatory relevance, accounts that turn on the sensitivity or normalcy of the inference, and any account you prefer to these — and state plainly which of my verdicts you are overturning and why. If your conclusion is that no single principle can hold cases 1, 2, 5 and 6 together, argue for that conclusion rather than merely noting it; and if you think one of my verdicts is simply mistaken, say so and say which. I also know the classical advice for this problem — take the narrowest reference class for which reliable statistics exist — and I want you to tell me whether that rule is any use to me at all, since it seems silent on cases 2, 5 and 8, where the question is not how narrow to go but whether the class applies in the first place.
