I retired three years ago from a career in reactor-systems reliability, and I have spent most of the retirement reading political philosophy badly but enthusiastically — the fair-play account of obligation, the republican argument about civic arms, the literature on when a state may compel a person to risk being killed. That reading has now acquired consequences: the legislature of my country has appointed me as one of three non-parliamentary members of its Standing Commission on National Service, and the Commission reports in six weeks. We are a mid-sized European state of some 6.4 million people, constitutionally neutral, with no alliance membership, no foreign bases on our territory, and a statute barring our forces from collective-defence operations abroad (peacekeeping under a United Nations mandate is permitted). The Commission is split five to four, and because the members have taken to trading arguments past one another, the chair has asked each of us to circulate a short written opinion before the closing session. Mine should be a decision memo: a clear recommendation among the options on the table, with the reasoning laid out so that a member who disagrees can say exactly where they disagree.

The parameters, since a memo that ignores them is worthless. Each year roughly 72,000 citizens reach eighteen. Our present force is all-volunteer: 9,500 full-time personnel and a contractual reserve of 6,000, with an unfilled establishment of about 2,100 posts for the third consecutive year and a rising average age. The general staff's annex, released to us, states a war establishment of 41,000 trained personnel within thirty days of mobilisation and a further 25,000 for territorial-hold tasks within ninety days. The three options formally before us are: (A) continue the volunteer force and close the gap with pay and conditions, supplementing it with a compulsory civic year that may be discharged in the military, in civil protection, or in unpaid care work; (B) reinstate conscription on the Nordic pattern I have been reading about — universal liability on paper for both sexes, but with only a selected fraction of each cohort actually called up, the rest passing straight into a reserve roll; or (C) a genuine militia system on the Alpine model, in which a large majority of each cohort completes basic training and returns to civilian life with periodic refresher service, and in which a civilian alternative of deliberately longer duration remains available. Four of my colleagues have planted flags I must address. The chair favours A, arguing that the civic year gives him universality at a tenth of the cost. A retired brigadier favours C and says that anything less is a standing army by another name, and that a standing army is incompatible both with neutrality and with the diffusion of force that keeps the state answerable to its citizens. A historian on the Commission opposes B and C outright and holds that no state in our century has ever discharged its justificatory burden for compelling a person to be available for killing, and that the burden of proof sits permanently with the conscriptionist. Our youngest member, a student representative, says she will support B only if selection among the liable is by lottery rather than by aptitude screening, on the ground that a merit screen converts a shared liability into a tax on the willing and the employable. Two further questions have been referred to us separately and must be ruled on in the memo: whether a liable person may pay a fee or find a substitute, as nineteenth-century drafts permitted, and whether we recognise objection to a particular operation as distinct from objection to service as such.

Please draft the memo for me, roughly fourteen hundred words, in headed sections, with no citations or reading list — I want arguments, not authorities. It should recommend one option, or a specified hybrid, and give a phased implementation schedule anchored to the date of our report; identify the two or three indicators that, if observed within a stated number of years, would tell the legislature the recommendation was wrong; rule explicitly on paid substitution and on selective objection; and say, for each recommendation, whether the reason doing the work is an engineering consideration about capacity, a distributive consideration about who bears the burden, or a claim about the state's authority to compel, since I suspect the Commission keeps sliding between the three. I would also like it to test a thought of my own rather than flatter it: I have been arguing that a militia system's real advantage is not capacity but feedback, that it closes a loop which a professional force leaves open, since the cost of a decision to fight is then registered in a large fraction of households rather than confined to a small and largely invisible one. I want the memo to say honestly whether that loop operates in practice, given that only bereavement closes it rather than deliberation, and given how few households are touched even under option C. Finally, devote a section of real substance to the strongest case against my recommendation, stated as its own proponent would state it and not as a strawman, and explain why I nonetheless vote as I recommend.
