So: on November 18, 1883 the American railroads basically just decided to invent time zones, and I want to know what Lewis makes of it. Facts first, since I dont want you guessing at these. Before that day every town kept its own local mean solar time — noon in Chicago was 12:31 in Pittsburgh, 12:24 in Cleveland — and there were well over a hundred local times in use, so schedules and train orders were written in whatever the nearest city's clock said. A private association of railroad managers, meeting in Chicago in October 1883, adopted a plan drawn up by the editor of the official railway guide, splitting the continent into five zones, one of them up in the Canadian Maritimes. They picked a Sunday for the switch because traffic was light, and station clocks got reset off the Naval Observatory's telegraph signal. Newspapers called it the day of two noons. Most towns followed within a year or so. Detroit didn't: it held onto local time for decades, Henry Ford supposedly carried a watch with two dials, and when the city council finally ordered the clocks set back 28 minutes, roughly half the city obeyed and half refused, the mess got worse, the council rescinded its own order, and Detroit eventually voted its way into standard time in 1905. Meanwhile the U.S. Attorney General is said to have ordered federal departments not to run on railroad time until Congress authorized it, and Congress didn't get around to that until 1918 — 35 years of a nationwide private convention with no legal standing whatsoever.

Now the analysis I'm after:

- The mapping. Who is Lewis's population here — the railroads, the towns, both? Is this one coordination game or two nested ones, where the railroads play a symmetric game among themselves and everyone else plays follow-the-leader against the timetable? Give me the regularity, the recurring situation, and which of his conditions actually hold in 1883 and which don't. I'm suspicious about common knowledge, and I think Lewis himself later backed off requiring it.
- Whether the arrangement is arbitrary in his sense. Any other meridian and any other set of zone lines would have done the job — but not equally well for everybody, and towns on the wrong side of a line still lobby to get moved. Can arbitrariness survive that kind of case, where picking the equilibrium is itself distributive?
- What the framework explains and what it goes quiet on: the Attorney General's refusal, the 1918 ratification, the fact that changing a town's zone today means petitioning a federal agency. Is a convention that gets absorbed into statute still a convention?
- Detroit 1900 as the control. A decree that failed where 1883's decree succeeded. Which of Lewis's conditions broke, and does that show his account needs something about who's inside the population?
- And whether this was ever really a coordination problem. Read it through E.P. Thompson's stuff about clock time and work discipline in industrial capitalism and it looks more like a power move wearing a coordination costume. Can Lewis's apparatus even distinguish those two, or does it lack the vocabulary?

Finally, tell me what the theory predicts about the current fight over locking the country into permanent daylight saving — noting that states can opt out of the seasonal change but can't unilaterally make it permanent. Lewisian convention, or something else entirely? And where he's genuinely silent, say so instead of squeezing an answer out of him.
