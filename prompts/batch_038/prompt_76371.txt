Forty years in a physiology lab, and only now in retirement am I getting into the philosophy end of what we used to do for a living. So here's one I keep chewing on.

Libet died in 2007, which means he never got to see the accumulator reply to his famous experiment — the one where a leaky stochastic integrator running on ongoing subthreshold noise reproduces the whole shape of the readiness potential just as an artifact of averaging backwards from movement onset, and where the same slow negative drift also shows up before fast reactions to an unpredictable interruption, in trials where the subject wasn't preparing to move at all.

So put yourself in his chair. Would he have granted that the 1983 result says nothing about free will after all, or would he have fought back — and on what grounds? And what happens to his fallback, the idea that conscious will might still act as a veto in the short window between the reported urge and the movement? If the urge report is itself just another readout of that same noise, is there any window left to veto in? Give me your best guess at the argument he'd actually have made, plus where you think he would of been right and where wrong. Don't turn him into a strawman, the man was careful with his data.
