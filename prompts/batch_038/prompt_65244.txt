Build me a case of underdetermination that's meant to be cured by indirect evidence — the sort of thing you reach for when you want to insist that two rivals are never really empirically equivalent, only equivalent-so-far.

The spec: two theories, T1 and T2, about some concrete invented phenomenon — your pick, something with lots of measurements attached to it, like why a certain alloy goes brittle below a threshold temperature. They have to agree on every possible observation in that domain: same predictions, same curve fits, everything. Then you bring in evidence from a completely separate line of investigation that lets you tell them apart after all.

Constraints:
- write both theories out explicitly, and say what the total evidence in the first domain consists in — no hand-waving;
- the discrimination has to run through a third claim that's independently well supported by its own evidence, not through one rival just being ad hoc or internally inconsistent;
- if T1 and T2 end up disagreeing about anything observable in the first domain, the case fails;
- keep the scenario itself to about two paragraphs.

Then run the check: show the reasoning from the outside evidence to T1 over T2, and name which feature of the case is doing the work. And one last thing — suppose I grant the whole case. Explain why someone who was worried about underdetermination in general shouldn't be impressed, i.e. what's left standing once you've got this. That's really what the exercise is for: I want to see whether indirect evidence dissolves the problem or just relocates it.
