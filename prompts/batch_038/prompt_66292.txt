For the past three years I have convened a residential working group on the ethics of selection and reward, supported by a small foundation whose trustees have now asked for a single circulate-able deliverable: a joint statement from the two members of the group who have spent that time disagreeing most productively. Both are critics of meritocracy as it is currently practised, but critics for reasons each regards as incompatible with the other's, and their exchanges have reliably ended with one accusing the other of changing the subject. Rather than have them write it themselves, I should like you to draft the statement that a genuine adversarial collaboration between them would have produced. It must read as a document both could sign without reservation about the wording — not a report about a debate, not a summary of the literature, and not an adjudication of who is right.

The two positions, stated so that neither is defined by a label its own holder would reject:

- **Camp A — pre-institutional desert realism.** There are facts about what a person deserves which obtain independently of any scheme of social cooperation, resting on desert bases such as effort actually expended, contribution made to a joint endeavour, and options forgone for the sake of others. Institutions are criticisable precisely because they can fail to track those facts. Meritocracy's defect, on this view, is that it tracks credentials and marketable talent rather than desert properly understood, so the remedy is better tracking, not the abandonment of the concept. The familiar objection that diligence and character are themselves products of a fortunate upbringing does not show that desert claims are empty; it shows only that they are graded — that a person's desert can be diminished without being eliminated.

- **Camp B — institutionalism about distributive shares.** The question of what someone deserves *by way of a distributive share* has no determinate answer apart from the public rules of a just basic structure (the broad line associated with Rawls), since natural endowment and the cultivated disposition to deploy it are morally arbitrary from the standpoint of justice. Inside just institutions the operative notions are legitimate expectation and entitlement, not desert. Meritocracy's deeper defect is that it invites people to read a distributive outcome as a verdict on their worth, so the remedy is to stop distributing by desert altogether rather than to distribute by it more accurately.

Both camps accept, and the statement should say so explicitly, that measured merit in existing selection systems correlates strongly with the circumstances of one's birth, and that this is unjust. They disagree about whether that shared condemnation rests on the same grounds, and the statement is to confront that disagreement rather than settle into the overlap.

Please produce the following, in British English throughout, running to roughly 1,600–2,000 words:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — at least nine, and none of them so thin that a partisan of either camp could not have written it. Where the two camps accept the same claim for different reasons, mark that in a bracketed note, since several of the agreements will be unstable in just this way.
2. A disagreements table with these columns: the disagreement stated in deliberately neutral terms; the formulation Camp A would itself prefer; the formulation Camp B would itself prefer; the crux, that is, the narrower sub-question such that settling it would actually move at least one side; the case, argument, or piece of evidence both accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction about that crux, recorded in advance. Every formulation of a side's own view must be one that side would sign unamended.
3. Two worked test cases that the table's cruxes are to be run through. First, an abolition case: a contribution-tracking scheme is dissolved overnight, and the question is what, if anything, was true about desert in the moment before dissolution, and whether that claim has the same content in a community which never had such a scheme. Second, a live policy: a publicly funded care sector proposes to replace seniority-based pay scales with wages tied to contribution units recorded by peers, and each camp is to say what it recommends, why, and what it expects the scheme's moral and practical standing to turn out to be.
4. A short section stating which parts of the dispute the collaboration has come to regard as verbal rather than substantive — I suspect the two uses of desert talk in assessments of a person's character and in claims to a share of output may be doing different work — together with the reasoning, and not merely the verdict.
5. A residual dissensus register: the questions deliberately left unsettled, with one line each on why settling them would require resources the collaboration does not have.

Finally, for each crux, state honestly what either side would count as evidence or argument against its own view, including where a side thinks no such evidence exists and is therefore not falsifiable on that point. The document must contain no bibliography, no footnotes, no references to particular works, and no quotations from any text; it should be self-contained and argue from cases and analysis, so that a reader who has never encountered the debate can follow and assess every claim on its merits.
