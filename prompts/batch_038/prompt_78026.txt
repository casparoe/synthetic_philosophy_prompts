For most of my working life I have been a forensic economist: plaintiffs' counsel hire me to put a defensible dollar figure on a harm, and my reports have to survive a Daubert challenge. The tools of that trade are narrow and specific. Duty, breach, causation-in-fact, proximate cause, and foreseeability. The reasonable-certainty standard, which bars recovery for harms that are speculative rather than merely large. The distinction between a compensatory measure (what the plaintiff lost) and a restitutionary one (what the defendant gained), and the fact that these two numbers can diverge wildly. Apportionment among multiple tortfeasors, and the difference between joint and several liability and several-only. The collateral source rule. Limitations periods, the discovery rule, and continuing-violation tolling. Class ascertainability, and the practical requirement that a damages model be reducible to a formula applied to a defined population. The loss-of-a-chance doctrine, where a plaintiff recovers a fraction of a full harm proportional to the probability of the better outcome that was destroyed. And the recurring fight inside my own profession over whether to adjust earnings projections and work-life-expectancy tables by the claimant's race or sex — defended as accuracy, attacked as the entrenchment of past discrimination in a present-tense number.

I have also been reading the rationalist blogosphere for close to twenty years, including its periodic eruptions over reparations for historical injustice, and I have followed those arguments back into the philosophical material they draw on: rectification of past wrongs, the choice of counterfactual baseline, Parfit's non-identity problem, whether what is inherited is benefit or obligation, whether claims can run to groups rather than to identifiable persons, and what discount rate applies to a harm two centuries old.

I want a translation, not a summary. Restate the reparations debate in the vocabulary above, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. Specifically:

1. Where the argument holds that the injury is not a discrete historical act but a persisting structure, does that map onto continuing-violation tolling, onto proximate cause, or onto nothing at all — and does the mapping preserve the argument's force or quietly reduce it?
2. When the baseline is a but-for world in which the original wrong never occurred, and the non-identity problem bites, which doctrinal slot does that difficulty fall into in my framework: causation, certainty, or standing? Can any of those slots actually bear the weight being placed on it?
3. Do advocates move between the compensatory and the restitutionary measure without marking the transition, and is that equivocation only visible from inside my vocabulary?
4. What does my requirement of an ascertainable class and a formulaic damages model do to a claim whose beneficiaries are defined by descent and whose defendants include the dead, the dissolved, and the general taxpayer?
5. My profession says adjust projections for the discrimination that actually operated in the claimant's labor market; the reparations argument says the baseline should ask what the claimant would have had absent that discrimination. Is this a genuine contradiction between the two frameworks, or the same quantity computed against two different counterfactuals that have simply been confused for one another?

Then answer the question I actually care about. Is the residue a defect in tort damages as a way of thinking about historical injustice — a framework built for two-party, discrete-in-time, individually ascertainable wrongs that therefore cannot express certain true claims — or is it evidence that some of those claims were never determinate to begin with, and that the philosophy was doing work that looked like rigor and was not?

Finally, attack rather than confirm my working hypothesis: that the entire dispute is a measurement problem. On this view everyone concedes breach and causation in the abstract, and the real disagreement is about the discount rate, the choice of counterfactual, and who has standing — so the argument is about econometrics wearing philosophy's clothes. If that is wrong, tell me exactly where it is wrong and what the disagreement would look like if the measurement were perfect.

Please do not give me case citations, statutes, or a reading list; I want the concepts and the structure of the disagreement, not a bibliography.
