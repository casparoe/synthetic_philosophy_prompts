Prepping a session on interpersonal utility comparisons for a reading group and I want to use Flowers for Algernon without turning it into a poster for a view I already hold. The wrinkle I keep hitting: nearly all the evidence about whether Charlie's life goes better or worse comes from the progress reports, and those are written by the very mind whose capacities are what's changing — the instrument covaries with the thing its measuring. So when pre-op Charlie says he's happy at the bakery and post-op Charlie says intelligence has cut him off from everyone, are those two verdicts on a common scale at all, or is it closer to getting reports from two different people, each calibrated against their own history of experiences? Read the novel against the debate about whether welfare comparisons need a shared basis — Robbins's line that they're value judgments rather than science, versus the reply that ordinary empathy plus a common human nature is enough — and tell me which Charlie, if either, is in a position to judge, and whether anyone else in the book does better: Alice, his sister, the researchers, the coworkers who insist he was happier back when he was one of them.

Two things I want you to push on rather than skate past. First, Charlie at his peak says he's borrowed the old Charlie's life and the old one is waiting around to get it back — does that two-persons reading actually help the comparison, or does it fight the book, which seems to insist on one continuous life? Second, where does the novel resist the whole framing? I half suspect the final report and the request about Algernon's grave is doing something the utility talk can't cash out, and I want to hear it argued rather than gestured at. And please don't just hand me the sceptic winning by default — point to the places where the book makes the comparison look not merely possible but easy, and say what's doing the work there.
