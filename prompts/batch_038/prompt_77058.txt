I work in software — infrastructure, mostly — and I have no formal training in philosophy. The one text I have read closely is the Zhuangzi, in translation, and mainly the parts about death: the passage in which his wife dies and a friend, arriving to offer condolences, finds him sitting with his legs sprawled out, drumming on a basin and singing; the rebuke the friend directs at him for it; and the reply he gives about the sequence of change that runs through the seasons, and about his own first impulse to grieve before he thought the matter through. I want to put him in a situation he could not have foreseen and see how much of that position survives contact with it.

Suppose a company where I work has built a service with three components. First, a continuity-preserving upload: over roughly eighteen months a client's neural tissue is replaced incrementally with synthetic substrate that reproduces its function, in increments small enough that the client never loses consciousness and never wakes up discontinuous from the night before; at the end of the schedule the biological body is permitted to fail and what remains continues. Second, a pause: at any point the client may have a snapshot taken and held in reserve, so that if the continuation goes badly there is something to revert to. Third, a residue service for the bereaved: a conversational system trained on the decedent's messages and recordings, sold to survivors explicitly as a way to finish conversations they never got to have.

Please grant the company everything it claims. The technology works exactly as advertised, there is no failure of memory or character, and nobody is deceived about what it is, so he cannot dismiss it as a confidence trick or as a delusion about what survives. I want to know what he would say under those conditions.

Would he regard the upload as a refusal of death, and so as precisely the clinging to living and aversion to dying that he faults in others — or as one more transformation, no different in kind from the turning of the seasons and therefore a matter of indifference? Does his verdict turn on whether the claim in the company's marketing, 'you will not die', is true, or only on whether the client believes it and wants it? Second, take two clients: one who wants the procedure because he is terrified of ceasing to exist, and one who is entirely at ease with dying but wants it because his family cannot let him go. Would Zhuangzi bless one and refuse the other, bless both, or refuse both, and on what grounds — that is, is the standing of the procedure determined by the inner state of the person undergoing it? Third, the residue service. His conduct at his wife's death looks like a refusal of exactly this: he did not want to keep her, and he objected to the mourner who wanted him to. Does that give him grounds to object to selling a simulation of the dead to the living, or does his admission of an initial grief he had to reason himself out of weaken his position here? And what would he make of the client who, some years after the upload, asks to be ended, and of a company that treats the request as a scheduling matter?

Please write to me in continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want your reasoning. Close by telling me how confident you are in the verdicts you have given, and single out the point where you think you are most likely to have got Zhuangzi wrong — including whether that uncertainty comes from the texts being genuinely indeterminate on the question or from your having to extrapolate well past anything he addresses.
