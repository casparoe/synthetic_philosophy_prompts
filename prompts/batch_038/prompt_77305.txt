My cousin keeps the minutes for a small town museum in central Europe, a place that was occupied twice last century. The national archive has finally released the occupation-era police files, and in them are the names of some forty locals who informed on their neighbours. The board has to decide whether the names go into the permanent exhibition, and two members had a row about it that she forwarded to me. Neither would budge.

H said there's a duty to remember, owed to the people who were denounced — roughly Margalit's point that memory is an ethical obligation to those you share a thick past with, not a general moral nicety. Burying the names, she said, tells the victims' families their suffering is too awkward to display, and lets the town keep a flattering story about itself that the files flatly contradict.

M said this is exactly the memory-as-secular-religion thing Rieff goes on about: remembering has become an obligation nobody is allowed to question and a machine for manufacturing grievence. None of the informers is alive; the exhibition would be publishing the guilt of grandchildren, one of whom sits on the board. Peace, he said, is sometimes bought with forgetting, and the towns that got on best afterwards were the ones that let it go.

H said the grandchildren aren't being punished, the records just being kept, and that "letting it go" was never neutral — it was a decision taken by people with something to hide. M said that's his whole point: memory is always selective and always run by whoever's in charge, so there's no duty to remember, only choices about what to keep. H said if that's all remembering is, then shredding the files is as defensible as framing them.

Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right at each step, and then who's more right overall? I especially want to know whether M's selectivity point actually defeats H or just changes the subject, and what the two of them are both missing. British English please.
