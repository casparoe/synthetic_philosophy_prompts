I work in television, not in a university: I'm the script editor and senior researcher on a six-part documentary series about the man behind the gospels, and my own training is in current-affairs journalism rather than anything ancient. We have a problem I don't know how to solve. Every draft script arrives with two kinds of error baked in. The first is narration that quietly promotes a gospel scene into plain fact, so that a story told once, decades later, by a writer with a theological purpose becomes something our narrator states to twenty million people as history. The second is the opposite failure: a script so covered in hedges that it never says anything, which the network will not broadcast and which I think is dishonest in its own way, since some things really are close to settled while others really are not, and treating them alike is a kind of lie too. What I need is a repeatable procedure my two junior researchers can run on each individual claim before it goes into a script, because we have roughly three hundred of them across the series and about twenty minutes per claim.

I keep reading that historians in this field use a handful of named tests for whether a piece of material is likelier to have originated early — something about a claim appearing in several sources that don't derive from one another, about material that would have been awkward for the early movement to invent, about material that fits neither the Judaism of the time nor the later church's interests, about consistency with what else passes, and about traces of an Aramaic-speaking Palestinian setting. I don't want a lecture on the debates; I want those ideas, plus whatever else you think belongs, converted into an ordered set of questions with a plain-English answer standard for each: what response counts as passing, what counts as failing, and what makes a question undecidable for us. The procedure must output one of four dispositions — assert in the narrator's voice, attribute on screen to a named source or to a character, present explicitly as contested with both sides, or cut — and it must say what combination of test results produces each one, including the two claims we treat as bedrock (that he was executed under the Roman governor and that he was baptized by John the Baptist) which I suspect will not come out of your tests as the strongest possible cases and which I need explained to my producers. It must also handle our practical constraints: nobody on my team reads Greek or Aramaic, so the procedure needs an explicit rule for what to do the moment the answer turns on the meaning of a single word; every on-screen assertion has to be backed by something we can name in the graphics; and I need a clear marker for the point where the tests run out and the claim goes up to our consulting academic, who reads each script for one day only, so I cannot send him everything.

Please also be blunt with me about the machinery rather than just delivering it cheerfully. I want the known weaknesses of these tests stated where they actually bite in the workflow — that a test keyed to awkwardness depends on guessing what a first-century movement would have found awkward, that a test keyed to dissimilarity from both sides can leave you with a figure who belongs to no world at all, that the tests tend to favour sayings over deeds and to say nothing whatever about chronology, about how often something happened, or about what a man thought he was doing — and I want a short list of the steps that are really judgment calls wearing a procedure's clothes. Then run the whole thing on three cases, showing the reasoning at each step and the disposition at the end: that he caused a disturbance in the Temple in Jerusalem during his final week; that he expected the world as it stood to end within the lifetime of his hearers; and, as the one where your answer will annoy me, the claim our marketing department has been pushing for the trailer, that he was married — for which the only documentary support my researchers can point to is a small papyrus scrap that surfaced in the press some years ago and whose authenticity most of the experts who examined it dispute, plus the argument that everyone around him in that society was married. If the procedure says cut, say cut, and tell me what I would have to be able to show before it would say otherwise.
