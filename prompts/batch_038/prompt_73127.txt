I spent thirty-four years as a field entomologist, most of them doing mark-recapture and occupancy work on temperate Lepidoptera, and I retired two years ago. Since then I have taken up philosophy with the same habits I brought to fieldwork: I read slowly, I keep notes, and I want to know how a claim was measured. I have no formal training in the subject, but I am thoroughly fluent in the statistical machinery of population biology — detection probability, occupancy models, life tables, sampling design, power analysis, and the propagation of uncertainty through an estimate — and that is the vocabulary in which I actually think.

The material I have been reading concerns insect sentience, and in particular a family of arguments that runs roughly as follows. The number of insects is astronomically large. The probability that any given insect is sentient may be very small. But a small probability multiplied by an enormous number still yields an enormous expected quantity of suffering, and therefore the expected value of our conduct toward insects remains large even under heavy skepticism about their inner lives. I follow the arithmetic perfectly well. What I do not understand is what the individual terms are doing.

My request is that you translate this argument — together with the most serious objections to it — into my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage, term by term. Where the philosophers write probability of sentience, what is the counterpart in a discipline where probability attaches to detection, to occupancy, or to a parameter estimate, and never to a subject's capacity to feel? Where they write expected suffering, what is the counterpart, given that my expected values are always expectations over a sampling distribution generated by a stated design? Where they write number of individuals, what is the counterpart, given that my N is never a census but an estimate whose plausible range may span two orders of magnitude? Please do not smooth over the joints; the joints are what I am interested in.

I also have a conjecture of my own, which I would like you to test rather than to flatter. In an occupancy study, a site at which I never detect a species is not thereby an unoccupied site: non-detection becomes informative only once I have an estimate of detection probability, and that estimate comes from replicate visits to the same site. It seems to me that much of this discussion treats a low probability of sentience as though it were a measured quantity that licenses a discount, when in fact nobody has estimated the analogue of p at all. If I am right, the reasoning has the same defect as counting non-detections as absences. But tell me whether the analogy actually holds. What would replicate visits to a site be, in the case of an experience? Is there any procedure that could play that role, or does the analogy collapse precisely at the point where it seemed most promising?

One further wrinkle from my own tables. In a life table for almost any insect I have studied, the overwhelming majority of individuals are concentrated in the earliest instars, and most of them die there. The vast numbers invoked by the argument are therefore mostly numbers of very brief lives. My discipline keeps duration explicit; the argument I am reading does not appear to. Whether the quantity of interest is abundance, number of deaths, or total time exposed to harm changes the result by a large factor, and I cannot tell from the philosophical literature whether that choice has been made deliberately or has simply never been noticed.

Then give me the residue report. Divide your findings three ways: claims that translate into population biology cleanly and without remainder; claims that translate only with distortion, stating exactly what you had to bend to make them fit; and claims that have no expression in my vocabulary whatsoever. For the third category especially, I want your judgment — is the residue a genuine blind spot in a discipline that has never needed to ask whether its subjects feel anything, or is it a sign that the original argument was less determinate than its arithmetic suggested? And I would like a direct answer on whether any of the argument's motivational force survives the translation, or whether the translation itself dissolves it.

Two ground rules. Do not offer me references, reading lists, or citations; I am not hunting for sources, and I have no intention of chasing them. And do not retreat to the observation that consciousness is difficult to define. I have read that, and it is not an answer. Wherever you suspect that a term in the philosophical argument quietly presupposes something my vocabulary cannot supply, say so plainly rather than translating it as though the two operations were equivalent.
