I cover science and ideas for a magazine — mostly not law — but I've got a long assignment on how the postwar tribunals built the machinery everybody now reaches for when they talk about war crimes, and literally every person I interview says "victors' justice," either as an accusation or as a shrug meaning *well, that's how founding works*. I want to get past the slogan before I write anything. So: give me the spec. The list of what any account of when it's legitimate to punish someone under a law that didn't exist when they acted has to deliver, aimed at the Nuremberg and Tokyo cases rather than in the abstract.

For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it — the concrete thing one of those tribunals did or failed to do that makes you think a theory has to handle it. And sort them three ways: what's non-negotiable for anything calling itself an account of criminal justice, what's only a demand of one side of this particular argument (the critics' purity tests versus the defenders' minimum bar), and what's empirical rather than conceptual, i.e. turns on what the record shows rather than on the concepts. I need that third bucket because half my interviewees are doing history and calling it philosophy.

Three things I can't get traction on from the commentary, and I want them built in as requirements rather than answered as trivia:

1. Retroactivity looks to me like it's doing at least three different jobs here and people slide between them: retroactive as to what counted as an offence, retroactive as to who gets to try you at all, and retroactive as to the defences and standards available to you once you're there. Is that a real decomposition or am I inventing it to sound clever? Give me the requirement attached to each, and say which one does the actual work in the argument — my guess is the middle one, since the offence-definition complaint is mostly about the aggressive-war charge and the tribunal had treaties to point at, whereas nobody had ever agreed in advance on who convenes the thing.

2. The bootstrapping problem. It looks like you cannot simultaneously demand of the first tribunal in any system that it be (a) constituted under law that already existed, (b) staffed by adjudicators with no stake in and no complicity with the conduct charged, and (c) capable of actually reaching the people who did the worst things — because at the founding moment the only actors with the power to constitute it are the ones who won. If that trilemma is real, say so plainly and say which leg has to give. That's my lede and I want to know whether it survives scrutiny or is a nice-sounding nothing.

3. A counterfactual-variation requirement I want stated explicitly, not left as my complaint: a theory that condemns these tribunals owes me the small change that would have made them legitimate, and a theory that defends them owes me the small change that would have made them illegitimate. Anything that can't answer either is unfalsifiable sloganeering wearing a black robe. Include it in the list and show me it biting on at least one actual feature of the trials.

Two more cases your list has to handle, and I want to see whether it does:

- Suppose the tribunal had been constituted not by the winners but by a coalition of neutrals, and one member of that coalition was at that moment doing the very thing on the indictment. Does the objection turn into hypocrisy rather than partisanship? Are those the same defect under different names or two different ones, and if two, which is worse for the legitimacy of the verdicts? I suspect two and I suspect hypocrisy is worse, because partisanship at least predicts the outcome and hypocrisy corrupts the rule you're leaving behind.

- The attempt after the First World War to arraign the Kaiser under the penalties part of the peace treaty, framed as an offence against international morality rather than as a defined crime, which went nowhere because he was never handed over. Whatever requirement in your list explains why that collapsed and these charges stuck — put it in, and tell me if the explanation is about legality, about custody, or about the intervening twenty years changing what states had already signed up to.

Skip the history lesson, skip the scorecard, and don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I've got my own stack and you'd just be guessing. Keep the weight on the requirements and the cases motivating them. And if two of your own requirements genuinely can't both be met, that's the thing I most want; don't bury it in a paragraph, pull it out and name it.

Last line, on its own: Verdict: followed by the single requirement that eliminates the most candidate accounts (or the word none, if you think they can all be satisfied together).
