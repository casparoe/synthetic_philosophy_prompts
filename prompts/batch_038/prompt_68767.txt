You are a comparative neuroethicist with a foot in both worlds: you read the primary cell-counting literature on brain composition, and you have actually sat on committees that had to turn that literature into a written allocation protocol. You are scrupulous about not letting a tidy normative story override awkward data, and equally scrupulous about not letting a data dump stand in for an argument. Please think carefully and work in stages rather than going straight to a finished scenario.

Some background, because it fixes what the case is for and therefore what would count as cheating. I spent three hours last night arguing with a friend about whether brain size is morally relevant. Her position is instrumental and, I have to admit, not obviously wrong: no committee can count the neurons of a live animal, whereas brain mass and cranial volume are cheap, measurable, and published for hundreds of species, and they track behavioral capacity well enough across the animal kingdom that an institution rationing welfare resources has to give them some weight. My position is that mass is a proxy that fails exactly where decisions are hardest, because most of the tissue in the largest brains is doing sensorimotor work with no plausible bearing on welfare, and because a measure that is systematically wrong in favor of the biggest animals is not a usable measure at all. Neither of us could produce a case that settled anything, which is why I want one built to order. A scenario that simply announces the rankings I want, or that quietly rigs the empirical inputs to produce them, fails; the rankings have to fall out of real comparative data plus criteria the committee actually publishes.

To keep you from having to guess at the data, here are the anchors I am confident about from last night. The African elephant brain weighs roughly 4.6 kilograms, about three times the human brain, and contains on the order of 257 billion neurons — but about 97.5 percent of them are in the cerebellum, leaving only some 5.6 billion in the cerebral cortex, which is roughly a third of the approximately 16.3 billion in the human cortex despite the elephant cortex having more than twice the mass. On encephalization quotient, the African elephant sits around 1.3, cetaceans around 3, humans around 7 to 8; and there is a well-known inversion in which capuchin monkeys score higher on encephalization than great apes even though the great apes are the more behaviorally flexible. Everything beyond these anchors is your responsibility, and for each empirical value you use I want it labeled: published and solid, order-of-magnitude only, or contested — including cases where a figure rests on a single post-mortem specimen or on an extrapolation from an allometric equation.

Now the design task.

First, brainstorm briefly on the page. List five or six candidate individuals or species you could plausibly use, note for each which pairs of criteria you could get to disagree, and only then commit to a trio. If you cannot find a real trio that generates what I am asking for, say so plainly and give me the closest approximation with the gap identified. Do not fudge numbers to manufacture the result.

Second, construct a single realistic allocation problem: one indivisible resource at a real kind of institution — a single place in a permanent palliative-care program after a funding cut, one course of an expensive treatment, one transport slot, your choice — and three candidates for it. Constraints: all three must be extant real species; they must be cases that could plausibly present to the same institution at the same time, so the tie-break is not an artifact of my having chosen animals that never meet; no uplifted animals, no artificial substrates, no hypothetical species, and no abilities attributed to a species that it does not actually have. Hold fixed, so far as the biology allows, everything else the protocol might care about — comparable remaining life expectancy, comparable documented suffering from the presenting condition, no differing legal or donor pressure — and explicitly note any residual difference that could be doing work instead of the variables I care about. Keep the scenario itself to three paragraphs.

Third, the mechanism. The institution's written protocol ranks candidates by three published criteria: absolute brain mass, encephalization quotient, and a standardized behavioral battery. Each criterion must yield a strict ordering, and the three orderings must form a preference cycle, so that under a simple majority-of-criteria rule no candidate beats both others and the protocol as written gives no answer. State each ordering, then state the pairwise majorities that produce the cycle.

Fourth, add a fourth criterion — estimated number of cerebral cortical or pallial neurons — and report whether it breaks the cycle and in whose favor. Then answer a harder question: does this fourth criterion deserve to be treated as independent evidence, or is it best read as the behavioral hypothesis measured a second time, given that neuronal counts were promoted as the replacement for encephalization precisely because encephalization failed?

Fifth, tell me which single feature of the case generates the cycle, as precisely as you can, and then run the subtraction: if that feature is removed or matched across candidates, does the case collapse into an ordinary dispute between absolute size and size-relative measures, or does the cycle survive?

Sixth, steelman my friend. She will insist that the committee never claimed mass tracks moral status, only that mass is the best cheap estimator available under uncertainty, and that incoherence inside a written protocol is an administrative defect rather than a moral one. Make that reply as strong as you can and tell me whether the case survives it. In particular, work through the distinction I kept reaching for last night: between brain size being morally relevant in itself and brain size being evidence of whatever is morally relevant. My suspicion is that the cycle is decisive against the first claim and nearly silent on the second — tell me whether that is right, or whether an estimator that produces cyclic verdicts is thereby impeached as an estimator too.

Finally, a short section on transfer to humans. Would the same three criteria generate a cycle among individual human beings? Take into account the ordinary range of adult human brain volumes, including the average difference between male and female brains without a corresponding difference in cognitive performance; the reported cases of severe hydrocephalus in which people functioned far better than the amount of cortical tissue would predict, while noting that what those cases show is disputed; and the small-brained extinct hominins of Flores who nonetheless made tools. Close by saying whether the historical use of brain-size rankings to justify hierarchies among human groups is itself a reason to distrust a size-based moral weight independently of its predictive accuracy, or whether that is a separate political argument that should be kept out of the science.

Two housekeeping points. Do not give me a reference list, a bibliography, or citations — I cannot check them and they would only add noise; just report the values you are relying on with the confidence labels above, and flag anything you suspect you may be misremembering. And if at any stage you find that one of my specifications cannot be jointly satisfied with real data, stop and say which one, rather than quietly relaxing it.
