I'm a retired electronics engineer — thirty-odd years designing analog-to-digital converters — and since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy and some history of computing, mostly badly and out of order. Here's something I keep circling. In the lectures published postumously as The Computer and the Brain, von Neumann describes the nervous system as a mixed machine (all-or-none digital impulses riding on analog membrane processes) and then says the language the brain actually uses is lower in precision and more statistical/arithmetic in character than ours, to the point that we'd need a genuinely new notation to describe it rather than the mathematics we already have. My trouble: he was writing well after Shannon's 1948 work, so I can't dismiss it as ignorance of channel capacity, redundancy and error-correcting codes — which look to me like they say the same thing rigorously, namely that a noisy link still carries information reliably, you just pay for it in bits per symbol.

So compare the two as accounts, not as anecdotes. What is each one actually a theory OF — is von Neumann's claim about notation, about precision, or about something like a meaning-carrying structure that Shannon deliberately brackets off at the start? Where does his redundancy-in-the-brain point overlap with Shannon's redundancy, and where does it genuinely not? And then tell me where you'd plant your flag: does the need-a-new-mathematics claim survive as an interesting thesis once you grant Shannon everything, or is it just an un-quantified premonition of information theory? Please be concrete about which features of the nervous system (spike all-or-noneness, noise, energy cost, whatever else you think matters) are doing the work in each account.
