I'm giving the principal comment on a colleague's book at a symposium in April and I want to work out in advance what she's going to say to the case I plan to put to her, rather than improvising from the floor. Her book is about transformative choices — roughly, choices you can't make well by imagining the outcome or by asking people who've been through it. She's in the same neighbourhood as the well known recent work on transformative experience, but the view is her own, so please don't just hand me that literature back.

Her verdicts on examples, from the book and from about a dozen talks of hers Ive sat in:

- Having your first child: transformative.
- Having a fourth child when you already have three: not.
- Moving permanently to a country whose language you don't speak, after two fortnight visits: transformative.
- Moving back to the village you left at eighteen and have visited every summer since: not.
- A first ten-day silent retreat: transformative. The twentieth, same teacher, same centre: not.
- Electing an operation you need that will leave you permanently deaf: transformative.
- Being twenty and reading a book that overhauls your political convictions in a week: not.
- Signing up as a paid volunteer for a trial of a drug that permanently dulls your sense of taste: transformative.
- Marrying someone you've lived with for twelve years: not. Marrying someone after a six week courtship: transformative.

Three commitments she repeats constantly: transformation is a property of choices, not of events; a choice isn't transformative if the person could reasonably have found out what it's like by asking around; and the real mark of a transformative choice is that the person who makes it isn't the person who has to live with the result.

Here's my case. Fran is fifty, and a family mediator of twenty-five years who has talked several hundred people through reconciliations with the relative who harmed them. She has also done it twice in her own life — with her father, and later with her sister — so she knows from the inside both what a reconciled relationship feels like and what the change in herself felt like while it was happening. She's now deciding whether to forgive the brother who did her a very serious wrong thirty years ago. She tells me there is nothing left to find out: no description anyone could give her, and no experience she hasn't had or watched closely. She also tells me she can no longer rank her two futures. The Fran who forgives and the Fran who doesn't care about different things, don't agree about which of those lives is better, and she can't say which of the two she'd endorse from where she's standing. And she's clear that nothing anyone says to her will settle it — she thinks only doing it will.

So: is she going to call Fran's choice transformative? Be careful here, because I think at least three different rules fit every verdict in that list and they do not give the same answer on Fran. Lay out the candidate rules, what each one predicts, your best guess at her actual verdict and the reasoning behind it, and whether her three stated commitments pull together on this case or against each other — if they conflict, tell me which one you expect her to soften and what that costs her elsewhere in the list. Then give me one further case I could put to her in the discussion that would show which rule she's really running, and say why that case is decisive rather than just more of the same. If you think Fran is a bad case to raise publicly — rigged, or vulnerable to an obvious deflation — say so plainly and tell me what to raise instead.

British English throughout, please.
