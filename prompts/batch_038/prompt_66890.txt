I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been assigned a piece on how government puts a number on a human life. The reporting keeps colliding with a problem my editors regard as settled and my sources regard as live. The concrete puzzle is this: when regulators, international appraisal bodies, and welfare economists move a value-of-a-statistical-life estimate from one population to another — from the United States to a lower-income country, or from a rule affecting affluent commuters to one affecting low-wage workers — they routinely scale it by income, typically using an elasticity somewhere on the order of one-half to one. The stated justification is that willingness to pay is income-dependent, so a fixed dollar figure would misrepresent what a given risk reduction is worth to people at different incomes. Please do not concern yourself with the precise elasticity or with the current status of any particular agency's guidance; what interests me is the conceptual structure of the move.

That structure looks like an interpersonal comparison of utility, which was supposed to have been expelled from economics by Lionel Robbins and the ordinalist consensus that followed him. So: compare Robbins's prohibition with the reasoning embedded in income-scaled VSL. Where are the two genuinely in conflict, and where is the conflict only apparent? Two questions in particular I would like answered. First, is the elasticity claim best read as an interpersonal assertion — that a dollar buys less well-being for a rich person than for a poor one — or as a purely intra-personal, behavioral prediction about how each income group will in fact trade money against risk, a prediction Robbins himself could swallow without embarrassment? Second, does the familiar charge against Robbins, that he slid from the impossibility of verifying comparisons between persons to the claim that such comparisons are meaningless, get any traction on this case, or is it beside the point here? I would also like you to bring in one of the standard resources available to the defender of comparability — Harsanyi's aggregation result, or the capability-style objection that money-based measures track command over commodities rather than what people can actually do and be — and to say which of them does real work for the VSL practice, as opposed to merely restating that comparisons are possible in principle. Finally, tell me whether the practice is vulnerable in the opposite direction too: income scaling appears to price the statistical lives of poor people lower, yet abandoning scaling would itself seem to require an interpersonal judgment about what a dollar of risk reduction is worth to whom. Does that symmetry of embarrassment survive scrutiny, or is one horn of it much weaker than the other?

Please close by telling me how confident you are in the verdict you reach, and by singling out the one point in your answer where you are least sure of yourself — where you are extrapolating from how risk valuation is actually done rather than reporting anything settled. Keep the whole thing under six hundred words, in running prose rather than a set of headed sections, and do not send me a reading list: I have my own, and I would rather spend the space on your reasoning.
