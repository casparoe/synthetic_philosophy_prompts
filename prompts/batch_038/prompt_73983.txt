I am a third-year philosophy PhD student (committee in philosophy of science, with a secondary reader in economic history) preparing an application for our department's dissertation-writing fellowship, which funds four months of full-time work on a single chapter. Please draft the proposal for me — roughly 1,800 words, in numbered sections. I will supply all references myself: no bibliography, no invented citations, no quotation from sources. Naming positions and arguments is enough.

The half-formed idea I want turned into a proposal. The Needham question — why modern science, and in its economic cousin why industrialization, emerged in early modern Europe rather than China — is normally treated as an empirical question awaiting better evidence. My suspicion is that it is at least partly a question of specification. Contrastive why-questions are answerable only relative to a determinate contrast class, and on the standard pragmatic account the fact and the foil must be values of a single variable. "Britain rather than China" appears to fail that condition in three distinct ways:

(i) **Unit mismatch.** The relata are not comparable individuals — a small, internally integrated kingdom and a continental-scale, multi-ethnic empire, with different temporal boundaries and different internal heterogeneity — so the putative variable ("industrialized: yes/no") may not be well defined for them at all.

(ii) **Explanandum slippage.** The question as inherited concerns science and natural knowledge; the question as currently debated concerns sustained growth and factory industry. The literature moves between the two without marking the transition, and answers to one get offered as answers to the other.

(iii) **Foil drift within an argument.** A candidate explanation — accessible coal and geology, factor prices and the high-level equilibrium, property rights and institutions, fiscal-military state capacity, demography and real wages — does its work only for some foils: England versus the Yangzi Delta, Europe versus China, 1800 versus 1400, anywhere before 1750 versus anywhere after. The same author may shift foils mid-argument, so that two positions can each be correct and still look contradictory.

I want the chapter to turn this diagnosis into something that can fail. Two deliverables: a taxonomy of well-posed formulations of the divergence question, each with its variable, unit, time index, and threshold; and a "foil audit" of several prominent explanations, showing which formulation each actually addresses and which it leaves untouched. I also want to defend a further claim: where the outcome is plausibly overdetermined — many independently sufficient causal routes to the same divergence — no unique difference-maker may exist, in which case a formulation stays ill-posed even after its units and time indices are repaired.

Please give me:

1. A precise statement of the research question, determinate enough that a reader could say what would settle it, explicitly listing which formulations are in scope and which I set aside and why.
2. Why the existing answers leave it open, and specifically why the gap is not evidential: why more data, another regional case study, or a better quantitative reconstruction would not close it.
3. The method, staged month by month across the four months, with the analytical apparatus named (contrast classes and relevance relations; difference-maker and interventionist accounts of causation; overdetermination and robustness). Justify this apparatus against the obvious alternatives — an original empirical synthesis, a formal or model-based treatment, a further case study, or simply declaring the question meaningless.
4. One fully worked row of the audit, as a proof of concept that the deliverable is achievable and not merely a promissory table.
5. What success looks like, and — this is what the committee will press — what counts as failure. Make the failure conditions specific rather than generic, and include at least these three: the audit showing that prominent explanations already fix their foils explicitly and transparently; every well-posed formulation collapsing into one that has already been answered; and the objection that the diagnosis is a redescription that discriminates no hypotheses and no evidence.
6. A short paragraph meeting the historian's objection that practitioners know perfectly well what they mean and that this is a verbal quibble, followed by a candid scope assessment: what a four-month chapter cannot do, and what I would cut.

Keep it in the register of a proposal rather than an essay — I am arguing that the work is worth doing and can be done, not defending a verdict. Assume the committee already knows the divergence debate; do not spend words on background.
