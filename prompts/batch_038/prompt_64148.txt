**Question 2 (800 words).** Explain Strawson's distinction between the reactive and the objective attitudes. What does he think would follow, for that distinction and for the free will debate, if determinism came to be generally and universally accepted? In your answer, say what work naturalness does in his argument.

**Student answer:**

Strawson's move is to change the question. Instead of asking whether the agent was free, he asks what we are actually doing when we hold someone responsible. His answer: not endorsing a claim about causation, but participating in a set of attitudes he calls the reactive attitudes — resentment, indignation, gratitude, forgiveness, hurt feelings, guilt. These are responses to the quality of someone's will. I resent you not because your action caused me pain but because it displayed ill will, or indifference, or a plain absence of the ordinary goodwill I take myself to be entitled to from other people. Trip me by accident and there is no resentment; trip me because I'm funny when I fall over and there is. Same bodily movement, different quality of will. Strawson thinks both the pessimist and the optimist miss this, since both treat the practice of holding responsible as something that needs backing from elsewhere — either from the falsity of determinism or from the efficacy of punishment.

He sorts the considerations that turn resentment off into two groups. The first is marked by phrases like "He didn't mean to" and "He couldn't help it": accident, ignorance, being pushed, having no acceptable alternative. The second is marked by things like "He's only a child," and more generally by psychological abnormality or moral underdevelopment — the warped, the deranged, the compulsive — plus the temporary cases of great strain or post-hypnotic suggestion. Strawson calls the second sort exemptions and the first sort excuses, and the important thing about them is that in both cases the reactive attitude is withdrawn and we stop treating the agent as a proper object of the demand for goodwill. Excuses and exemptions are two routes out of the moral community, one local and temporary, the other general. That is also why he thinks the ordinary compatibilist analysis of freedom — he would have done otherwise if he had chosen otherwise — is beside the point: it names a condition without explaining why that condition is the one that matters. What makes it matter is that its absence shows ill will.

The counterpart stance is the objective attitude. Here the other person is not a participant in a relationship with you but an object of social policy: something to be treated, trained, managed, cured, or simply avoided. Strawson is careful to say that we already adopt it, partially and locally, and for good reasons. But he is equally clear that it is affectively hollow. Resentment, indignation, gratitude, forgiveness — none of these survive into it; what is left is at most a clinical interest, or repulsion, or pity. Love does not survive it either, since love is itself a form of mutual reactive engagement. So the objective attitude is not a more rational or more adult way of seeing people; it is a thinner one, and that is why nobody should be in a hurry to universalise it.

Which brings us to determinism. Suppose everyone came to accept it. Strawson thinks essentially nothing would follow, and I think the reason he gives is empirical. The reactive attitudes are natural in the way that being startled by a bang is natural: understanding the acoustics does not stop you jumping. Believing that everyone's actions were caused will not stop you resenting your brother. So the argument for determinism's irrelevance rests on a psychological incapacity, which makes it a prediction about us rather than a claim about responsibility, and makes it contingent. You can imagine a community — a monastic order, a very heavily therapised culture, a differently evolved species — that actually did what the pessimist says we could do, and stopped resenting and being grateful across the board. If such a community is possible, then Strawson's conclusion is only about us: we happen to be built so that the free will problem does not bite. He has given up looking for a validation of the practice from outside, so all he can say to that community is that they are missing something. But "you're missing something" is not a refutation, and that seems to me the weak point in an argument his admirers treat as decisive.

Where I would push instead is a different place. If the reactive attitudes simply are the framework, there seems to be no standpoint from which to criticise a whole community's demands for goodwill — and some communities' demands have been monstrous.
