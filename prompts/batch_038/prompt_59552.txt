I serve on the grants committee of a private family foundation, and next month we vote on a single large proposal: $45 million now to acquire and permanently restore a coastal wetland that will buffer a city against storm surge. The engineers' models say the protective value is small for roughly the first forty years, rises steeply thereafter as sea levels climb, and then persists indefinitely. Our grant-management system requires one real number per proposal, and staff has been entering 5% — the figure our investment advisor applies to illiquid, long-horizon assets. At 5%, nearly all of the project's value falls beyond year 100 and the proposal fails on cost-effectiveness grounds. At the last meeting a trustee objected that the 5% contains a pure time-preference component that is nothing but our own impatience dressed up as arithmetic, and that removing it leaves a rate near 1.5%, at which this is the strongest $45 million opportunity we have seen in a decade. Our consultant then floated a third option: a schedule in which the rate declines the further out one looks, so that the far future is valued at the lowest plausible rate rather than an average — though he conceded the system cannot accept a schedule, only a single equivalent number, and that which number counts as "equivalent" depends on the shape of the benefit stream. A further wrinkle the committee has not confronted: our endowment is managed on the assumption of roughly 4% real returns in perpetuity, so whatever we enter for grants is also implicitly what we say about how this year's foundation values later ones.

Draft the memo I will circulate to the committee before the vote. It should recommend one of the three paths (5%, something near 1.5%, or a declining schedule recast as a single equivalent rate), give reasons members could actually argue with rather than defer to, and state plainly how much of the recommended rate is impatience and how much is a response to expected growth in future people's consumption. It must also take seriously the best argument for the losing side — that capital is productive, so $45 million invested today could be delivered to future people as a far larger sum, which may make a low rate worse for them than a high one — and say what survives of that argument after scrutiny. Close with the conditions under which the number should be revisited and a concrete timetable for doing so.

After the memo, provide a JSON object with exactly these fields: `recommendation` (string, one of "market rate", "low rate", "declining schedule recast"); `single_rate_entered` (number, annual percent, the value to type into the system); `impatience_component` (number, annual percent); `growth_and_inequality_component` (number, annual percent); `assumptions` (array of short strings, including any elasticity of marginal utility and consumption-growth figures you relied on); `why_the_equivalence_problem_resolves_this_way` (string, one or two sentences on how you converted a schedule or a benefit-stream shape into one number); `decisive_reason` (string, one sentence); `strongest_counterargument` (string, one sentence); `what_survives_of_it` (string, one sentence); `review_trigger` (string); `review_date` (string, ISO 8601).
