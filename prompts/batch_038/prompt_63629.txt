So here's my situation. I help run a reading group that's about half philosophy grad students and half people who actually work in local government, and for going on two years we've hit the same wall every single time Chesterton's fence comes up. One of us — call him the Burkean — really does hold the Burke/Chesterton line: inherited practices encode more than any of their current defenders could articulate, the presumption sits with the status quo, and you don't get to tear down a fence until you can explain what it was doing there. The other — call her the reformer — thinks that's just a bias wearing a wisdom costume: the presumption should sit with whoever currently benefits from the arrangement, and historically most fences got built by somebody who wanted something for themselves. Neither of them is being dumb about it, they just never end up answering the same question, and I'm pretty sure that's the actual problem.

The concrete thing we've been fighting about is a proposal to repeal our city's parking-minimums ordinance — the 1950s rule that every new residential unit has to come with at least one off-street space. And there's a wrinkle that's scrambled everyone's brains. In the council minutes the origin is fully documented: a downtown merchants' association lobbied for it because they were afraid cheap parking at new apartment buildings would pull shoppers away from their storefronts, and one councilman said at the time it was a stopgap to be revisited within a decade. Nobody revisited it. Meanwhile in the seventy years since, a bunch of other machinery has grown around it — impact fees keyed to parking ratios, a variance process, a density-bonus formula that references the required count — so we know precisely who built the fence and why, and we also know it now does a lot of things its builders never intended. One of our grad students writes his dissertation on teleosemantics, which is how the function stuff crept in, but I think he's onto something: I want you to use the etiology-versus-causal-role split in theories of function as the scaffolding for the central crux, i.e. Cummins-style "what does the thing do now" functions against the selected-effect/etiological "why is the thing there at all" functions of Wright and Millikan. My hunch — test it rather than just ratify it — is that Chesterton's fence is really an *etiological* demand, so the documented origin actually discharges it and hands the reformer a win, while the Burkean intuition is about something else entirely (the web of stuff that has since come to depend on the rule), and that once you pry those two apart a real chunk of the fight turns out to have been about words. If you think I'm wrong about that, say so and say why.

Deliverable: the joint statement these two could both put their names on. Start with a numbered agreed-claims section — the things both genuinely accept about how the ordinance arose, what it currently does, what the city could realistically measure, and where each side's argument has to be an argument rather than a demand. Then a disagreements table with these columns: the disagreement; the wording the Burkean would sign for his own side; the wording the reformer would sign for hers; whether it's a crux (meaning: settling it would actually move at least one of them) or not; the case, argument, or kind of evidence both would accept as bearing on it; and each side's prediction written down in advance, before anyone looks. Two of the items I want explicitly in there are (a) who bears the burden of proof and whether that burden shifts once the origin is documented, and (b) whether the Burkean's "presumption in favor of the status quo" is meant as a probabilistic claim about consequences or as a procedural rule about who has to argue first — I suspect that second one is the verbal part. And finally a short closing section flagging any residual disagreement that's genuinely substantive and any that was only ever semantic, with the reason for each call.

Don't give me a bibliography or cite studies, and don't name specific cities that have or haven't repealed this stuff — just describe the shape of the data or the structure of the argument that would count for both sides. Its fine to be somewhat long, but every agreed claim should be one a real Burkean and a real reformer would actually sign, not a strawman dressed up as common ground.
