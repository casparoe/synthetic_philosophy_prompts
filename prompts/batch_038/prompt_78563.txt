For thirty-eight years I worked in X-ray crystallography, latterly directing a structure-determination facility; I retired two years ago and have since been reading philosophy and the older classics of cognitive science with something like a graduate student's seriousness. I have come upon a recurring pattern that I cannot name, and I should like to know whether it has one already.

The pattern concerns what is lost, undetectably, when a document is copied faithfully. Three instances, all encountered within the past year:

First, a colleague lent me photocopies of pages from a physicist's laboratory notebook of the 1930s, with one entry transcribed onto a clean sheet. In the original, that entry had been squeezed into the bottom margin of a page otherwise occupied by a routine calibration; its marginal position marked it as an afterthought, a hunch scribbled for later. On the photocopy, standing alone on a fresh page with a date typed above it, the identical words now read as a considered result. Nobody had altered the wording or any framing statement.

Second, an email thread forwarded to me as a plain-text attachment in which the nested indentation distinguishing who was answering whom had been stripped by the client. The quoted sentences and their attributions were all intact; only the geometry was gone. Two readers of the attachment assigned the harshest sentence in the thread to the wrong person, and did so confidently.

Third, the converse case: a modern typeset edition of a seventeenth-century natural-history work in which the editor imposed regular paragraph indents and section breaks on what had originally been printed as a continuous block. Readers now attribute to the author a taxonomy of topics that exists only in the layout, and cite his divisions in argument as though they were his claims.

What troubles me is not simply that arrangement carries information. It is that the loss leaves no trace in the copy. Every word survives, the frame survives, the copy presents itself as faithful, and it offers the reader no handle by which to notice the absence; in the third instance the extra layer is not merely lost but manufactured, again without warning.

I have been reading Hofstadter's account in *Gödel, Escher, Bach* of where meaning is located, and his three layers of a message: the frame message, the outer message, and the inner message. I take these to be, roughly, a message about the message as a whole; the implicit instruction carried by its form as to how the content is to be decoded; and the content itself. Some of my instances look like damage to the second layer, since in the email case the indentation functions as a decoding rule. But the first and third do not fit, because marginal position and imposed indentation are not instructions for reading anything. They assert something about the status, the deliberateness, and the authorship of what is being read, and they do so in a manner no reader mistakes for content and yet every reader absorbs.

From my own field I reach for an analogy: the phase problem. A diffraction experiment yields intensities but not phases, and nothing in the intensities announces that the phases are missing, so the reconstruction is ill-posed and must be closed by assumptions imported from outside. I suspect the analogy is serviceable but not exact, and I should like to be told where it breaks.

So: is there an established concept for this — a layer of a document's meaning borne by the contingent physical arrangement of its carrier, which copying silently destroys or silently fabricates, and whose destruction cannot be detected from the copy itself? I do not want a label alone. I should like the nearest established concepts, wherever they live — in bibliography and textual scholarship, semiotics, the philosophy of language, media theory, archival practice, linguistics — and for each, precisely how the thing I have described differs from it and why the difference matters. If nothing fits closely, please say so plainly and then define a term for it: the conditions something must meet to count as an instance, the conditions that disqualify a case, and one or two borderline cases you expect me to dispute. And tell me whether the phase-problem analogy should be kept or discarded.
