I sit in on a metaethics reading group at my university even though I'm in linguistics, mostly because they let me have the leftover biscuits. Last Thursday the topic was expressivism and two of the regulars, Mira and Dev, got into a long argument about the Frege-Geach problem that I followed better than I expected to, but I still have no idea who won. The marker was dying so I only got down the gist, but the exchange went roughly like this.

Mira started by saying the whole problem is compositionality in a costume. A moral sentence shows up both on its own and embedded — someone had put a conditional on the board to the effect that if lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong too — and when you assert the conditional you aren't asserting that lying is wrong. So if the meaning of the embedded sentence is supposed to be the attitude of disapproval itself, there's nothing for that occurrence to contribute, and the semantics just stops. Dev said that's half a problem at best. Grant the expressivist a compositional semantics — he had Schroeder in mind, where every normative judgment shares one relational element and differs only in what it's directed at, so compositionality of states falls out of compositionality of contents — and you still haven't answered the harder question, which is why accepting the conditional plus the antecedent while denying the consequent is a *mistake* rather than just an eccentric combination of attitudes. Blackburn's answer, a higher-order attitude of disapproval aimed at precisely that combination, doesn't explain the mistake, Dev said; it just records that we dislike it, and since on this view there are no truths for the inference to preserve, the disapproval can't be about truth either, so it isn't about validity.

Mira's response was that Dev was inflating a local difficulty into a global one. Realists owe an account of modus ponens too, and theirs is that the premises can't be true while the conclusion is false; the expressivist's parallel is that you can't hold that trio of attitudes without incurring the higher-order condemnation. Demand more than that and you're asking for a theory of logical validity as such, which isn't anybody's special burden. Dev said there's a real asymmetry: the realist borrows a notion everyone already understands — truth-preservation, the same one governing every other declarative — while the expressivist's attitudes are sui generis and "these don't fit together" gets stipulated case by case. Then Dev went after the quasi-realist escape route: fine, moral sentences are truth-apt, the disquotational schema applies to them exactly as it does to anything else, so we inherit all the logic for free. But once you've conceded a truth predicate that behaves indistinguishably from the realist's, along with truth, knowledge and facts, the only thing left standing is the claim that judging is a conative state — and that's philosophy of mind, not semantics. So Frege-Geach is either unsolved or dissolved, and dissolving it means quietly changing what the view is about. Mira said changing the subject is a perfectly good outcome if it turns out the two sides never disagreed about semantics in the first place: both accept the same disquotational schema, so what's actually contested is what judging consists in, and the realist owes an answer there as well — what is believing that lying is wrong, if not some disposition to assent and react? Dev's last move was that the higher-order strategy doesn't generalize anyway: you need a fresh disapproval for every embedding site, the conditional, negation, a disjunction with a descriptive disjunct like "either lying is wrong or the bus is late", attitude reports. If the rule is "construct whatever higher-order attitude condemns the offending combination", that's a licence, not an explanation, and the realist doesn't need one licence per case.

At that point they both turned to look at me, which was awkard. Could you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step? I'd like a rating for each of the six moves on a scale from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each — please don't give me a survey of the problem itself, I've read the overview material and that isn't what I'm missing. Then separately: (a) is Dev right that there are two distinct problems being run together, one about what an embedded occurrence contributes and one about the normativity of the inference, and does it matter which one gets called "the" Frege-Geach problem; (b) is the deflationary gambit a genuine solution, a surrender, or neither — I couldn't tell wether Mira and Dev were actually disagreeing there or just describing the same move approvingly and disapprovingly; (c) if there was a turning point, which move was it. And finally, tell me what neither of them put on the table that should have been there; my vague sense is that they were arguing over a narrower set of options than really exist, but I can't say what the missing options are.
