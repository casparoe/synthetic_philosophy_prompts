You're going to have to do double duty here, so before you start: think like a philosopher of action and write like a poet, and don't phone it in. Take a beat to actually think about the structure before you produce anything.

I'm teaching a unit on weakness of will and I want a sestina — full form, six six-line stanzas plus the three-line envoi — about akrasia and hyperbolic discounting. Not about "procrastination" in the greeting-card sense, and definitely not a productivity poem. The content I care about is this: on the hyperbolic picture the agent isn't overruled by a passion, his ranking just changes as the sooner reward gets closer. There's an extra steep drop applied to whatever is available *now*, and that's what makes the preferences dynamically inconsistent rather than merely impatient — so there's no single stable ordering for him to be weak against. Which is why the old Socratic line has some force: if you can't act against your own best judgment, then the akratic agent has to be redescribed rather than condemned, and the redescription usually goes toward the self as a bundle of temporal stages bargaining with each other instead of one person failing. I want the poem to hold that question open — is the later self being overruled, or is he just a different person with different weights?

Two specific pieces I want rendered rather than named. Don't cite anything, don't name-drop papers or economists, no bibliography at the end — I want the ideas in the poem's mouth, not in its footnotes.

1. The naive/sophisticated asymetry. The agent who thinks his future selves will discount the same way he does keeps believing tomorrow-him will finally be in the mood, and so never starts. The agent who knows his future selves won't be in the mood does the awful thing immediately, precisely because he knows it never gets easier.
2. The twist on that: self-knowledge cures the putting-off but creates the mirror-image vice — rushing to do the thing whose rewards are immediate even though it would have been better done later. Knowing you're weak-willed doesn't make you strong, it just moves the failure.

Now, the form should do the argument, not decorate it. Here's the standard end-word rotation, numbers standing for the six end-words of stanza one: 123456 / 615243 / 364125 / 532614 / 451362 / 246531, with the envoi pairing them (6,2) (1,4) (5,3), two per line, mid-line and at the end. The permutation has a name, retrogradatio cruciata, backward crossing. Two things fall out of it and I want you to use both. First, the same six words come back every stanza in a different rank order — that is literally what a preference reversal looks like from the inside, so at least one word should mean something like "later" in the first stanza and "now" by the fifth, without changing its letters. Second, the sixth stanza lands on 2 4 6 5 3 1, which is one rotation short of the original order: a seventh stanza would take him home, but the form never gets there on the page. That non-arrival is the relapse, and I want the reader to feel it land without being told. Use the envoi as the moment the six fixations get paired off and settled — or the moment they visibly don't.

Before writing a single line, brainstorm out loud for a bit: pick your six end-words and say in one line each what the word carries and how its job shifts as it rotates; say who the speaker is and what hour it is; say what the immediate-cost task is and what the immediate-reward thing is that he's already gone and done too early. Keep the six words plain and repeatable — the classic sestina failure is choosing six fancy words that can't survive thirty-nine appearances.

Then write it. Lines can be any length but keep them roughly even; no rhyme needed. Tone: funny and a bit bleak, not inspirational, and the ending should be that there isn't one. A few lines about your choices beforehand is fine, but don't gloss the poem afterward.
