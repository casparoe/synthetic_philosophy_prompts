I'm not a philosopher and I've never taken a class, so bear with me on the setup. Eleven years on the retention desk at a regional internet company — I'm the guy who talks to people right before they cancel. Our training has exactly one rule about honesty, and it's presented like it's a virtue: you may never say a false sentence. Everything else is fair game. So when a caller asks "did you guys raise my price," the approved answer is "your plan is one we don't offer to new customers anymore." True. And every single caller hears it as "no we didn't." There's a second rule sitting next to it, which is that you never correct a customer's conclusion unless they ask you straight out and there's no way to answer honestly. I've been chewing on this for months and I built an argument that's supposed to show the honesty rule is worthless, and it does show that, but it also proves something I can't swallow and I can't find the seam.

Here it is. (1) Start with a test I made up driving home, no idea if its a real thing anybody has a name for — call it the readback test. Imagine the person says back to you what they took you to mean, and you nod and say "yeah, that's right." If you'd say that, you lied. If you'd have to say "well, that's not what I said," you didn't. (2) On our script, if the caller says "so it wasn't you guys who raised my price," I say "that's right" — or I do as good as say it, since the rule means I can't correct them and everybody on the desk knows the impression is the point. (3) So the scripted answer is a lie, and the company's "we never lie here" line is empty. (4) But the same test convicts my whole life. A friend asked if I wanted to come to his party and I said "I've got an early morning." I did have one. I could have gone and slept four hours and I knew that when I said it. If he'd read back "so you can't make it, no hard feelings," I'd have said "exactly." (5) And basically any answer that isn't total disclosure works that way, because if somebody reads back their impression and it's roughly what you meant them to walk away with, you nod. (6) So on my own test almost everything anyone ever says is a lie, the word stops picking out anything, and the argument against the script loses its teeth — the script can't be worse than lying if it is lying and so is the party answer and so is good morning.

Which step do I give up, and where does this go wrong, if it goes wrong anywhere? I'd rather lose (5) and (6) than let the script off the hook, because there's something about the script that I still think is worse than a flat-out lie, and that was the thought I started with and the argument dropped it on the floor somewhere. One more thing that might matter and I don't know where to put it. Last spring my daughter asked me in the parking lot whether her dog was going to come home from the vet, and I said "the people in there are really good with dogs," and he didn't come home. Readback test says I'd have nodded. And I don't feel like a liar about that one, not the way I feel like one at work, and I can't tell you why, except that one was love and the other one is a quota. I want to know if that's a real difference or just me arranging my feelings so I can keep both.
