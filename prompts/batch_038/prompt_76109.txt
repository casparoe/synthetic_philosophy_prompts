You are a political philosopher who has worked both on Simone Weil's critique of political parties and on the design of deliberative bodies. Please think carefully before answering.

I am a junior and one of fifteen members of my school's Student Senate, which is deciding whether to keep caucuses — standing blocs that meet in private, settle on a line, and then vote together in public. I read Weil's wartime essay arguing that political parties should be abolished altogether, and I want to know what standard any replacement arrangement has to meet before I can say her critique has been answered rather than dodged. Please write the desiderata for a design for collective decision-making that is meant to be Weil-proof: what it has to secure in the individual member, which failure modes it has to rule out (the bloc treating its own growth as the criterion of success; collective passion displacing the search for truth and justice; a member voting against her own judgment to stay in good standing; a platform that commits people before the argument has been heard), and which familiar demands — accountability to a constituency, coalitions stable enough to actually pass things, guaranteed minority representation — you would count as genuine requirements rather than prejudices carried over from party systems. For each item give the consideration or case behind it and mark it negotiable or non-negotiable, then say which single requirement eliminates the most existing proposals, including sortition with free votes, liquid democracy with delegable mandates, and caucuses with a conscience-vote exemption.

Finally, test a suspicion of mine: spend a few sentences brainstorming whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, on the grounds that holding a representative answerable to the people who chose her demands commitment in advance, while keeping her judgment intact demands that she be free to reverse that commitment when a better argument arrives. Give a verdict, and if the set really is unsatisfiable, say which requirement has to be given up.
