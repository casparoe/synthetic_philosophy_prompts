I sit on the flight-operations standards panel of a mid-sized national aviation regulator, and on Thursday we vote on a variance application from a cargo carrier that wants to opt out of our prescriptive flight- and duty-time limits in favour of a fatigue risk management system of its own design. The carrier's argument, which several of our own inspectors partly accept, is that prescriptive limits are quietly gamed: crews under-report fatigue because standing down costs them a trip's pay and a reputation with the scheduling desk, so the rule that looks hardest-edged actually generates the least truthful data. Their proposal accordingly routes the fitness decision through the crew. Before each duty period a pilot completes a short self-assessment; anyone scoring at or above a fixed threshold stands down on full pay under an explicit no-discipline guarantee; and the scores, together with a brief reaction-time test administered at the same station, are transmitted to us monthly in aggregate.

Two clauses have split the panel, and they are the ones I need thought through. The first is a positive attestation: the form asks the pilot to confirm not merely a number but that the number is accurate "to the best of my knowledge." One member reads this as requiring the pilot to certify that he knows he is fit, and objects that no one is entitled to that; another reads it as an empty signature line with no operational content whatever, and wants it kept for that reason. The second is a reverse-onus clause: once a pilot has self-certified as fit and cleared the reaction-time test, the carrier may roster them to the legal maximum duty period regardless of how much duty has accumulated that week, the objective test being treated as authoritative and the cumulative record as merely informational. The pilots' federation backs the attestation and detests the reverse onus; the carrier's safety department says the reverse onus is what makes the scheme affordable and that the attestation is paperwork. When I raised the deeper worry at last month's meeting — that the faculty doing the reporting is itself among the things the reported state degrades — our in-house counsel said we had wandered into a philosophy seminar and asked to be returned to the checklist.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote: grant, refuse, or grant with conditions, with a specific disposition of each of the two clauses and, if you think the attestation wording can be saved, a proposed redraft of it. The reasons must be ones the carrier's safety department could actually answer rather than regulatory boilerplate, and the memo must face the underlying question head-on: whether a self-report on an incipient, continuously shading state like drowsiness can bear the weight this scheme places on it, and what follows for where the cut-point should sit on a scale that is being applied by precisely the people closest to it. Our consultant's submission asserts that subjective sleepiness and objective impairment diverge most sharply after several nights of modest cumulative restriction rather than after one bad night, and that a self-assessment tool therefore has to be deliberately biased; but it argues this from data tables alone and never says why the divergence should have been expected in advance. I want the memo to supply that missing reasoning, and to say whether it tells against the reverse-onus clause, in favour of it, or against both clauses equally. Close with the strongest case for granting the variance exactly as filed, argued as persuasively you can manage, followed by an account of why you are nonetheless unpersuaded — or of what evidence would have to arrive to persuade you, and by when.
