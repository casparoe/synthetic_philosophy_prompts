I'm a novelist — I also teach a Saturday fiction workshop, 11 adults, two hours, in a borrowed library room — and I'm stuck on a scene where my narrator gets his sight back at 43, is handed a coffee mug, and I can't work out what he'd actually be able to tell me about it. So: design a workshop activity that walks my writers into the old question of whether a man born blind who learned a sphere and a cube by touch would spot them by sight the second he's given it, without naming it up front or lecturing on it. They should hit the wall themselves and end up arguing. Two constraints. No blindfolds and no simulating blindness — one of my regulars is blind and I'm not staging that, so whatever the exercise is has to be about seeing, not about being deprived of it. And it has to run on ordinary room stuff: mugs, keys, a cardboard box, phones, a lamp, paper. Then give me the debrief that maps what just happened onto the question and onto what people who got their sight restored late in life actually report, plus a craft payoff — I want them leaving with something useable about writing perception from inside a mind that doesnt have the concepts yet. Tell me what will predictably fall apart on the day, and which part of the problem this activity just can't reach and I'd have to supply by hand.
