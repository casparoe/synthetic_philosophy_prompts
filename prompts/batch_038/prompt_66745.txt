i'm TA-ing the discussion section for an intro aesthetics course and the last week is on Hume's Of the Standard of Taste (the 1757 one from the Four Dissertations), and instead of handing out another summary i want to hand out a sestina — full 39 lines plus envoi, but keep the lines short so it fits on one page. the class is half art students who think any appeal to expertise is just snobbery with better vocabulary, and half philosophy students who think Hume defined the problem away, so the poem should be aimed right down the middle of that argument.

the conceit: the six end-words are the five marks Hume gives for a true judge — good sense, delicacy, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice — plus a sixth that he never lists as a mark but leans on constantly, which is time, i.e. the fact that Homer still gets admired in London and Paris after all the changes of climate, government, religion and language. use the words in their bare form as the end-words (so prejudice, not freedom from prejudice) and let the permutation do the arguing: because the form keeps recycling the same six items in new orders, the poem should make you feel that the standard isn't a rule anybody could write down but just these six things shuffled around, and that the same word does different work in different stanzas. i especially want prejudice to flip — in one stanza it's the film the true judge has to scrub off his own eye, in another it's the thing Hume tells us to forgive in the poets of other ages, their manners and their religious opinions, since a young man will be moved by love and tenderness and a riper one by wise reflections and neither is going to be argued out of it. put the wine business in somewhere too, the Cervantes anecdote he borrows where one taster gets a hint of leather and the other a hint of iron, and the hogshead is emptied and there's the old key on its leather thong at the bottom — that's delicacy, but its also the whole epistemology in miniature, the confirmation turning up after the verdict.

then the envoi has to close the circle instead of breaking it: all six words across the three lines, and the last line should land the thing my philosophy students would pounce on anyway, which is that the standard turns out to be the joint verdict of the true judges, and the true judges turn out to be the ones who agree about which poems lasted, so the canon certifies the judges who certify the canon. don't announce that as a gotcha and don't resolve it either — Hume himself winds up conceding a lot of ground to differences of humour and age and country, and i'd rather the poem end in that same shrug-and-keep-both posture than with a clean answer. also please don't name Hume or say standard of taste anywhere inside the poem, no title along the lines of Sestina for the True Judge, and no note at the bottom explaining the form. if it needs explaining to the art students it hasn't worked.
