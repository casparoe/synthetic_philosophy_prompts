Been sitting in the same reading group for about a year and the argument replays itself every few weeks with nobody moving an inch, so what I want is a diagnosis of the argument, not another round of it.

Topic: whether Wolpert's no-free-lunch results from the mid-nineties settle Hume's problem of induction. Two camps, call them the triumphalists and the deflators.

Triumphalists say: this is a theorem, not a thought experiment. Average uniformly over all possible target functions and every learning algorithm comes out with the same expected off-training-set error, so nothing is a priori better at learning than anything else. That is Hume's point — every matter of fact could have gone the other way, uniformity of nature is an assumption nobody can underwrite — except now it's proved instead of merely asserted. Any inductive method works only because the world happens to sit in the small subset of problems that suit it, and the assumption that it does is exactly what's in question.

Deflators say: the theorem is true and beside the point. The space it averages over isn't the space of possibilities a real learner faces; it's a construction that throws in worlds with no regularities whatsoever, worlds where the labels are scrambled, functions nothing could compute. And the uniform average is a choice of measure — pick the measure and you've already decided the issue. Hume's problem is about whether you can justify an assumption inside a world that does have regularities; NFL only shows that if you refuse every assumption you get nothing, which no one ever denied.

Here's the part that makes me think something structural is going on. Everyone in the group agrees on the mathematics. I have watched two people who agree the theorem is correct, agree about what its assumptions are, and agree about the verdict in every concrete example we try, still shout at each other for three hours.

And here's an experiment of mine I can't interpret. I built a benchmark to test the deflators' complaint that the scrambled worlds are artificial. I took a suite of 200 classification tasks, ran six standard methods, then constructed a mirror suite in which the ranking of the six methods is exactly reversed. It worked, cleanly. But when I went back to look at how I'd built it, each mirror task had been defined by reference to what the methods actually output — I had to know each method's predictions to choose the labels that would punish it. The scramble was indexed to the learners, not a permutation of some learner-independent space of worlds. So I redid it, allowing only relabelings definable without reference to any method (permutations of the input space, applied uniformly across all tasks and all learners), and the ranking barely budged: two of the six swapped places, the other four held.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and I'm not attached to it: the debate stalls because the two camps mean different things by a possible problem. One sense fixes the problem space independently of the learner, which is the only sense in which an agent really confronts a space of possibilities; the other lets the problems be defined relative to the learner, which is what makes the averaging come out flat. If that's right the theorem and the objection are both true and they never touch, the shouting is a verbal dispute wearing a substantive disguise, and my mirror-suite result is an artifact of the same equivocation rather than evidence for anything.

But maybe I'm wrong. Maybe there's a real disagreement underneath the verbal layer — something about whether any measure over all possible worlds could be non-arbitrary, which would be a substantive question in probability and metaphysics rather than a quibble about a phrase. Or maybe the deflators are the ones dodging, and the triumphalists have a genuine point that NFL puts a precise burden on anyone who assumes uniformity.

So: diagnose it. What kind of disagreement is this actually — verbal, substantive with a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work, two different questions sharing one label, bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or a sign the question is ill-posed? One thing I'd like checked carefully is whether that result is even an instance of what the theorem says. Then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction that dissolves it, a formal result that would decide it, a change of question — or, if you think no progress should be expected, argue for that instead.
