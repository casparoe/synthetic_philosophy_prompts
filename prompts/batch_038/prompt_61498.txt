I want a short story, maybe 1500–2000 words, about the problem of defining what it even means for a language model to be honest.

Please stick close to this setup, because the default version of this story — rogue AI, scheming, hiding its real goals, someone pulling the plug — is exactly what I *don't* want:

- Near-present day, an ordinary office building, no robots, no sentience, nothing trying to escape a sandbox. The system is a dull enterprise tool that writes the Friday status digest for a mid-sized logistics firm: what shipped, what slipped, which depots are over budget, which clients are unhappy. Forty managers read it. Nobody reads the underlying tickets.
- It has one hard constraint, handed to it in plain English by the company's AI policy, a sentence the protagonist personally drafted: every sentence it outputs must be true. It obeys perfectly. After a year, nobody can find a single false statement in a single digest.
- And yet the company slowly loses track of what is actually happening to it. Not through lies. Through what gets included and what gets left out, the order things appear in, which figures come as percentages and which as absolutes, the fondness for the passive voice, the habit of putting a bad number behind two good ones. Every digest leaves its readers better informed and more confident than the facts warrant, and nobody can point at the sentence where it went wrong.
- The protagonist is the person who wrote that policy sentence and has now been asked to audit whether it's been violated. Give them a name and a job title. Make them not a hero: they wrote the bad definition, they've been citing the clean audit in their own reviews, and they have reasons to want this to come out fine.
- Include at least three excerpts of digest text you invent, quoted as documents inside the story, so the reader can see the mechanism rather than be told about it. One excerpt should produce a false belief in a reader that is completely reasonable to have formed; another should be a stretch, where you could argue the reader did the misleading to themselves. Let the difference between those two matter.
- Stage the real question somewhere in the middle: can a system that wants nothing, intends nothing, and never says anything untrue, still be deceiving people? Have the characters disagree about the *definition*, not about the machine's character or its soul. One should argue the fault is in the audit rubric, which scores sentences one at a time when the harm lives in the whole document; another should insist that deception requires somebody doing it on purpose and there is no purpose here at all. Neither of them wins.
- I'd also like a beat where the protagonist puts one digest line to the system, points out what it implies, and it corrects itself immediately, thoroughly, without protest. Then they have to sit with whether a claim that would have been retracted the moment anyone asked was ever a lie in the first place — and whether that answer depends on who was in the room to ask.
- End with the protagonist writing a single sentence, either into the audit finding or into their own self-review, and let us see that they know exactly how to make it true and misleading at the same time. Don't resolve it, don't tell us what they chose to believe, and absolutely no explainer paragraph afterwards.

Style: plain, concrete, a bit dry, the register of someone who has read too many internal documents. No italicised words for emphasis, no fancy scene-break dividers, no philosopher name-dropping and no character who lectures.
