I am a fifth-year graduate student presenting at our department's spring workshop on moral epistemology in about three weeks, and I need a realistic picture of what the question period will do to my paper. The thesis is a Quinean one, though I am not offering it as exegesis: I want to argue that once you accept the holist picture on which every belief, including the most abstract, is answerable to experience only through its place in a connected system, the fact/value distinction loses its metaphysical work, and moral claims turn out to be truth-apt in exactly the same unremarkable sense as claims about reaction kinetics or plate tectonics. Cognitivism without moral properties, on this view, is not a compromise but the default position once the special epistemic status of ethics is withdrawn.

Three further commitments of the paper are the parts I expect to be attacked, so please aim the objections at them. First, I claim the difference between a moral belief and a physical-theoretic belief is a difference in the density of its observational contact and in how much of the rest of the web would have to be rearranged to give it up, and nothing else; there is no second, normative dimension of assessment. Second, I take the idea that moral beliefs sit nearer the periphery of the system than the laws of physics to be an empirical hypothesis about relative revisability, and I argue it is one my view is entitled to test against the historical record of belief change rather than merely assert. Third, I hold that most apparently moral disagreement is disagreement about non-moral descriptive premises plus differing weights on conservatism, so that what looks like incommensurable value conflict is usually under-described factual conflict. My supporting argument runs from the collapse of the analytic/synthetic distinction, to the impossibility of isolating a distinctively moral faculty or a distinctively moral kind of evidence, to the claim that any residual intuition of categorical force is best explained as a psychological artifact of beliefs that have been central for coordination purposes over long periods.

The room will be about twenty-five people: two committed non-naturalists who will press the objection that I have no account of why moral claims have authority over the rest of the web rather than merely a place within it; an expressivist in the quasi-realist tradition who will say my position is either expressivism in disguise or realism arrived at by stealth, and who will want to know what my truth-apt moral claims are doing that approval and disapproval could not do; a philosopher of biology who works on evolutionary accounts of normative judgment and will ask whether my view can explain the apparent reliability of moral belief without appealing to truth-tracking; two or three philosophers of science who know the holism literature well and will insist that my periphery claim is either trivial or false, since moral convictions look like the most revision-resistant beliefs anyone actually holds; and a historian of analytic philosophy who will spend my question time telling the room that the view is not really mine to attribute to its source.

What I want from you is a preparation document, not a defense of the paper. Give me roughly eight objections in the order I expect them to arrive, weighted by how much damage each would do to the thesis if I answered it badly. Phrase each one in the vocabulary and tone of the person actually raising it, so that I recognize the question when I hear it. For each, supply a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, a candid note on where that reply is soft or where it concedes more than I would like, and a mark distinguishing objections that are genuinely about my thesis from objections that are really disputes about how to read a dead author, which I want to deflect rather than engage. Then tell me what to concede in the first five minutes of the talk in order to take the wind out of the two most predictable attacks; tell me the single objection I should be privately hoping nobody raises; and identify the one objection that, if it lands cleanly, would oblige me to abandon the thesis rather than merely amend it. Please do not quote passages at me, do not assemble a bibliography, and do not send me to secondary literature: I have read the primary texts and what I am short of is dialectic, not sources.
