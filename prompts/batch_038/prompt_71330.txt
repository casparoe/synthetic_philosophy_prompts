The application for an eighteen-month postdoctoral fellowship at a centre that funds work straddling philosophy and welfare economics requires a research proposal of roughly 1,300 words. Please write it for me from the half-formed idea below, which I have not been able to state crisply.

The received debate over interpersonal utility comparisons is organised around a binary question: either such comparisons are in principle available — through an extended-sympathy or impartial-observer device, together with a normalisation rule fixing each person's zero point and unit — or they are meaningless, since no one can observe another's utility and the apparatus is unfalsifiable. My suspicion is that the binary framing is itself what produces the stalemate, and that the more promising question concerns degree and domain: for which ordered pairs of persons, and to what precision, are comparisons possible? The specific proposal I want developed is that intrapersonal comparison across time is the right model rather than a separate and easier case. We treat a person's own past and future welfare as commensurable, but that treatment plausibly depends on the psychological connections — memory, anticipation, overlapping intentions — that make earlier and later stages the same person. If so, comparability should vary continuously with degree of connectedness, and the interpersonal case should fall out as a limiting case of the intrapersonal one rather than as a mystery of a different kind.

The proposal should contain:

1. A precise statement of the research question, formulated so that it admits of a determinate answer rather than a stand-off.

2. An account of why the existing positions do not settle it — the normalisation-based optimists, the sceptics about unobservable mental quantities, and the ordinalists who decline to ask. Do not include citations; refer to positions and arguments, and I will supply the references myself.

3. The method: what the formal machinery would actually look like, what a comparability index would be a function of, how the index would be constrained by observable or at least arguable facts about connectedness, and what I would do in each of the six quarters.

4. At least two test cases in which the graded account delivers a verdict that differs from both the sceptic's and the optimist's. One of these must be a pair with no psychological overlap whatever — an adult and their own infancy, say — where the account appears to predict incomparability while ordinary moral, clinical, and legal practice insists on comparison; say explicitly whether that is a bug, a reductio of the account, or evidence that the practice is confused.

5. Three explicit conditions under which I would regard the project as having failed. One of these must be the possibility that the grading does no independent work and merely redescribes the binary question in vaguer language.

6. An honest assessment of scope: what would have to be cut for the project to be feasible in eighteen months, and why the alternatives I am rejecting — a purely neuroscientific measurement programme, a revealed-preference reconstruction, or a retreat to ordinal social choice theory — would be worse uses of the time.

Maintain the register of a serious funding document throughout: no throat-clearing, no survey of the literature, and no promises about implications that the project could not plausibly deliver.
