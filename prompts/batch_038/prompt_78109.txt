Write me a short story, max 800 words, about a QA inspector at a glass-and-composites plant whose shipping form has one binary field per crate: "will this thing shatter if you hit it — yes or no." Three crates come through in one shift and each one breaks that question in a different way:

2. a pane that is chemically and microscopically just the same float-line glass, untouched, but it's been soaked in a resin that sits in the surface flaws and holds them closed; a warm solvent tank strips the resin out in about an hour and you're left with glass that shatters like anything else, but until somebody strips it, hitting it does nothing;
3. a laminate that is genuinely tough as it sits — you could lean on it with a mallet — but the impact itself triggers a phase change in the interlayer that makes the whole thing fly apart partway through the blow, so it only becomes the shattering kind of thing once you've started hitting it.

I want the inspector to slowly realize that one yes/no box cant be answering the same question in all three cases, but I don't want a lecture or a voiceover explaining the moral. Give it a coworker who insists the box just means "what happens when you hit it" and another who insists it means "what kind of stuff is in the crate," and let them actually argue at the loading dock. Keep it concrete: forklifts, clipboards, the sound a pane makes, some plausible materials-science detail about crack tips and interlayers, a supervisor who wants the form finished by 4.

Hard rule: the words "disposition," "fink," "mask," "conditional," and "intrinsic" don't appear anywhere in the story, and you don't name any philosophers, papers, or thought experiments. The reader has to reconstruct the three cases from what happens on the floor.

End with the three entries exactly as the inspector writes them on the forms, then one sentence of 25 words or less saying what you now think that yes/no field is actually tracking. No citations, no afterword beyond that sentence.
