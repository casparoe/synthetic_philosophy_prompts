I am recording a short segment for a statistics-and-philosophy podcast in which the frequentist, subjectivist and propensity interpretations of probability appear as three characters ordering drinks in a pub. Please write me the joke that opens the segment.

Requirements:

- Deliver the joke alone — no prefatory framing, no gloss afterwards explaining it, no title.
- Hard limit of 120 words in total, spread over no more than six sentences, with the punchline occupying the final sentence.
- The humour must arise from what the three interpretations actually claim about the world, rather than from the fact that their names sound vaguely alike or from any stock statistician material. In particular, the joke should only land for someone who can keep the positions distinct: probability as the limiting relative frequency over an indefinite sequence of trials under identical conditions, with the well-known embarrassment that no finite run ever quite reaches the limit; probability as a coherent betting disposition of an agent, disciplined by exchangeability and liable to be exposed as incoherent by a Dutch book; and probability as an irreducible single-case tendency of a physical set-up, which must somehow be distinguishable from the frequencies it is meant to explain.
- You may, if you wish, have one of the characters object that the others' favoured notion cannot be instantiated at all — that a genuinely single case has no reference class, that a perfectly calibrated agent can still be wrong about everything, or that a propensity defined by what it produces is no analysis at all. That kind of objection is welcome provided it is compressed into the joke rather than argued in it.
- Aim for something a working philosopher of statistics would find funny, but which does not presuppose recall of any particular paper, theorem or quotation; do not put words in quotation marks.
