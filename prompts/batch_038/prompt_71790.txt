I've got the midterms to mark this weekend and one of them I want a second pair of eyes on before I commit to a number, partly because the department now wants actual written feedback rather than a tick and a grade, and partly because I've read so many of these by now that I've loose track of what counts as a real error versus an undergrad just phrasing something clumsily. Nothing weird about this one — no extension, no plagiarism flag, the student was in most weeks — I just don't want to over- or under-mark it.

The question was the second of two essay questions, worth 20 marks, and the paper told students to aim for around 400 words:

"Economists and historians disagree about the 'fundamental cause' of differences in income across countries, with geography, institutions and culture as the three leading candidates. (a) Explain what the so-called reversal of fortune is and what it is supposed to show. (b) Explain how settler mortality is used as an instrument for present-day institutions, and what has to be true for that instrument to do its job. (c) Give one reason for scepticism that either exercise settles the disagreement."

And here's the answer, verbatim except that I fixed two spelling things:

"(a) The reversal of fortune is the finding that the parts of the world that were richest around 1500 — the Aztec and Inca empires, India, China — are mostly not the richest today, while places that were comparatively empty and poor in 1500, like North America, Australia and New Zealand, are now among the richest. Acemoglu, Johnson and Robinson use this to argue against the geography view. Their point is that geography obviously did not change between 1500 and now: the climate, the soils and the disease environment of Mexico are the same today as they were then, so geography cannot explain why the ranking flipped. What changed was the sort of institutions Europeans set up. Where Europeans could settle in large numbers they rebuilt their own societies, with property rights, constraints on the state and markets open to new entrants; where they could not settle they built extractive states designed to pull resources and labour out, and those institutions outlived colonialism. And since institutions are made by people and could have been made otherwise, this basically settles the matter: the geography hypothesis is wrong, and the fundamental cause is institutions.

(b) You cannot test this by just regressing income on some measure of property rights, because rich countries can afford good institutions at least as much as good institutions make countries rich. The trick is to find something that affects institutions but has no other effect on income today. They use the mortality rates of European soldiers, sailors and settlers in the colonies. Where mortality was high Europeans did not settle, and where they did not settle you got extractive institutions, so mortality predicts institutions; but nineteenth-century European mortality obviously cannot have any effect at all on a modern economy's GDP, so any correlation between the instrument and income must run through institutions. That is what makes it a clean instrument, and it is why the regressions are not vulnerable to the reverse-causation complaint from the first paragraph.

(c) Honestly the best evidence for the institutions view is not a regression at all. Korea was one country with one language, one culture and one geography until 1945; then the north got one set of institutions and the south another, and the income gap today is enormous. Because geography and culture are held constant across the two, the difference has to be institutions. That is why I think the scepticism asked for in (c) is overdone. The culture explanation, which is mostly a restatement of Max Weber, cannot handle Korea, and neither can Diamond's guns, germs and steel story, since what Diamond is really saying is that Europeans had a better culture of innovation. Between the reversal of fortune and Korea, what the evidence shows is that history matters, and the lesson for policy is that poor countries need to build better institutions rather than wait for their geography to improve."
