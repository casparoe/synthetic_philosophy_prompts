I am chairing a departmental seminar series in which each session is devoted to pressing a single, precisely stated thesis, and next term's guest defends a specific position on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory. I need a question set for the discussion period. Her position, as she has stated it to me in advance, consists of three commitments:

1. Two questions are routinely run together: a diachronic question about how an agent's credences ought to move, and a synchronic question about how much support a hypothesis receives from a body of evidence. The problem of old evidence, she argues, is an artifact of demanding that a single quantity answer both.

2. The synchronic question is answered by a counterfactual measure: the degree to which evidence E supports theory T is the change in credence in T that an agent would undergo on learning E, where the agent's starting point is a baseline obtained by erasing E from their total evidence and renormalizing. She insists the erasure must be total — not only E itself but every proposition the agent's remaining evidence owes to E must be removed, so that the baseline is built exclusively from propositions evidentially independent of E — and that, carried out this way, the measure is uniquely determined and does not vary from agent to agent.

3. Nothing need happen diachronically. An agent who already believed E does not, on coming to see that T entails E, undergo any rational change of credence in T; what changes is only her grasp of a logical relation that held all along. She adds that the philosophical examples are unrealistically stark, because in actual science nobody assigns credence exactly one to their evidence.

She illustrates the view with two cases and maintains that her measure ranks them differently: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, known for decades and accounted for only afterwards, and the eclipse measurements of the deflection of starlight, predicted in advance. On her view the second supports general relativity more strongly than the first.

Please write nine questions for the discussion period, subject to the following. Assume she will not cite texts, authors, or dates and will not engage with named rivals; every question must therefore press on the internal logic of the position exactly as I have set it out, and must be one she can answer from the armchair in two or three sentences of speech. Phrase each question as it would actually be spoken aloud, not as an essay prompt. Before each question, give a single sentence naming the tension it exploits. After each question, give the reply she is most likely to make in one sentence, and a one-sentence follow-up that grants that reply and presses further rather than repeating the question.

At least one question each must address:

- the case in which the agent's credence in E is one, and whether commitment 3 collapses if she is forced to deny that any scientist is ever certain of any piece of evidence — together with what is actually gained if certainty is replaced by something like 0.999;
- the claim that total erasure yields a unique baseline, tested against the Mercury case, where the perihelion anomaly was known only as part of a dense web of astronomical observation, and where the background needed even to state the anomaly appears to depend on data that would have to be erased;
- the relation between commitments 2 and 3: whether an agent who now knows that T entails E, but whose credence in T sits far below the counterfactual measure, is rationally criticizable — and, if the answer is no, in what sense the measure is normative at all;
- a theory constructed after the fact to entail E, and whether the counterfactual measure can distinguish it from a theory that entailed E all along, given that the measure is defined only over an evidence set from which E has already been removed;
- the ranking claim about her two illustrations, and specifically what in the measure — as opposed to something operating outside it — does the work of placing novel prediction above accommodation;
- what would count as a refutation: ask her to name a case in which her measure comes out near zero yet a scientific community rationally treated the theory as strongly confirmed, or the reverse, and to say what she would surrender if such a case were documented.

Two further structural requirements. At least one question must grant the synchronic/diachronic distinction outright and press her within it, so that she cannot dissolve the difficulty by invoking the distinction a second time. And no question may be one she can answer by restating any of the three commitments, by explaining how conditionalization works in general, or by observing that her critics have misunderstood the framework.
