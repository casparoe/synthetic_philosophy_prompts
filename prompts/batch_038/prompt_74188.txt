A colleague is putting together a 3-year, 4-person program on the formal side of disagreement — Bayesian epistemology plus a bit of social choice — and basically every piece of it leans on the common prior assumption, whether it's the Aumann-style "rational agents can't agree to disagree" line, the Harsanyi impartial observer route to utilitarianism, or the conciliationist claim that a peer's contrary opinion is itself evidence. I think that's the load-bearing wall and they should stress-test it before building anything else on top; they think it's just background plumbing nobody actually disputes in practice. Help me argue it, or convince me I'm wrong.

So: rank the open questions in this area by how much a small team could actually move them in three years toward something that changes how philosophers argue, not just toward another technical result. Candidates I can think of: whether there's any empirical or behavioral way to tell "different priors" apart from "one of them is updating badly" in a pair of disagreeing agents; whether the agreement-type theorems survive with heterogeneous priors in a way that's still interesting; whether the impartial observer's unanimity turns out to be spurious once you drop the shared prior; whether the common prior is even the right idealization compared to imprecise credences or ambiguity-averse models. Say which of these are upsteam of which, which need tools that already exist versus new ones, and what finding would make you reorder the list.

One constraint, just to keep it honest: assume nobody ever settles whether the common prior assumption is true. Only questions that stay live under both verdicts belong on the agenda.
