For several decades the ethics of testimony has hosted a debate in which both sides grant one another every case and nobody moves. I would like the joint statement that a genuine adversarial collaboration between the two camps could produce — not a survey of the literature, not a verdict on who is right, and no citations or reading lists.

The two positions:

**Camp A** holds that the difference between lying and misleading is morally load-bearing in its own right. To assert what one believes false is to violate a norm attached to the act of assertion itself — a warranty or commitment extended to the hearer — whereas to say what one believes true while foreseeing that the hearer will draw a false inference is to exercise a different and weaker kind of agency, one in which the hearer's own reasoning does much of the causal work. The difference is not merely one of degree.

**Camp B** holds that what matters is the speaker's aim: to bring it about that the hearer believes something the speaker takes to be false. Whether that belief is produced by the asserted content or by an implicature the speaker cultivated is, morally speaking, an implementation detail. Wherever the distinction seems to matter, Camp B attributes the appearance to other variables — probability of success, the hearer's entitlement to an answer, independent duties to clarify — rather than to the assertoric form of the utterance.

The collaboration should treat the following four cases as its shared data. For each, record what the two accounts respectively imply and where they genuinely part company.

**Case I — Same words, different selection procedure.** An auditor asks a contractor whether a project finished on schedule. Contractor One has privately surveyed the true answers available to her and picked the one with the greatest misleading yield; Contractor Two utters the identical sentence having given its inferential consequences no thought whatever, and is astonished to learn later that the auditor took away a false belief.

**Case II — The lie is not on the menu.** A witness testifies under oath in a jurisdiction that follows *Bronston v. United States* (1973) in prosecuting only literally false answers, and where a refusal to answer will be read by everyone present as an admission. She says something she knows to be literally true, expecting it to produce a particular false belief. Does the unavailability of perjury subtract anything from the badness of her answer, or is her conduct exactly as bad as the lie would have been?

**Case III — The impression that outlives the sentence.** A seller, a physician, or a fund manager makes a literally true statement, sees that it has landed wrongly, and adds nothing. Some bodies of law impose an affirmative duty to correct a false impression created by one's own true statement. Is the failure to correct a second, distinct wrong that leaves the original utterance unobjectionable, or does it render the original utterance itself an act of deception?

**Case IV — No examiner in the room.** The same content is delivered where no follow-up question is possible: a label, a prospectus, a disclosure page, an intake form. The remedy the *Bronston* court pointed to — the alertness of the questioner's next question — is structurally unavailable.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign, including claims about Cases I–IV, about the familiar situation in which a misleading truth is worse than an outright lie and the converse situation in which the lie is worse, and about what the legal treatment of literally true testimony does and does not show about the moral question.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: each residual disagreement stated in wording Camp A would sign, the same disagreement stated in wording Camp B would sign, and a neutral gloss. Language only one side could accept defeats the purpose; language so neutral that it states no disagreement defeats it too.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one camp — and for each, the case, argument, or kind of evidence both camps accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction written down in advance. Where a camp predicts that it would not move even if its prediction failed, say so plainly instead of papering over the fact.

4. An untangling of at least three contrasts that the single heading of this debate collapses: what is said versus what is implicated; whether the speaker believes the content false; and whether the hearer's false belief is intended as a means or merely foreseen. State which of the three each camp actually needs in order to survive, and whether the two camps have been disagreeing about the same one all along.

5. An explicit account of which part of the dispute, if any, is verbal — a disagreement about the extension of the word lie rather than about wrongness — together with a formulation of the residual substantive disagreement that does not use that word at all.

6. A test of any truce the two reach. If they can sign a weaker joint claim — for instance, that the morally significant variable is how thoroughly the speaker has disabled the hearer's ordinary means of correcting a false impression, with assertion versus implicature functioning only as a proxy for it — say whether that claim survives contact with a case in which proxy and variable come apart, and construct such a case: a literal falsehood the hearer can trivially check, and a literally true statement that leaves the hearer with no way of knowing which question to ask.

7. A closing section listing what each camp concedes it cannot explain.

Write in a formal register, in American English, with the agreed-claims section numbered and the disagreements set out as a table.
