Consider the apparatus by which professional history certifies its own claims. A monograph or doctoral dissertation carries a full set of notes in which every substantive assertion about what happened is traced to a source, cited down to repository, box or folio, and date; quotation is governed by typographic conventions that mark the historian's own contributions as intrusions on the document — square brackets for insertions, ellipses for omissions, a flag for the source's own errors; originality is certified partly by archival novelty, so that a candidate who has worked only in printed sources is at a disadvantage however strong the argument; the fabrication of documents or the invention of dialogue ends a career; and a mandatory survey of prior accounts frames the whole undertaking as a contribution to a debate about a common object.

Set beside that apparatus what the very same texts routinely do with no note anywhere: they open on a scene and supply the weather, attribute hesitation and motive to people whose inner lives no surviving document reports, join events with causal connectives, choose a beginning and an ending that fix who the protagonist is, and settle into a shape — tragic, comic, ironic, satiric — that no source licenses. Peer review polices the first list exhaustively and the second not at all.

Please address the following.

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. What account of the relations among trace, text, and past must the apparatus presuppose in order for a note to function as a warrant at all? Be precise about what a footnote is supposed to certify, and about what is happening when it certifies one sentence on a page whose remaining thirty sentences carry no certification.

2. Suppose two historians work from an identical body of documents, produce identical notes, misreport nothing, and yet one emplots the material as a tragedy of miscalculation and the other as a farce of vanity. Both pass review. What does the discipline's lack of any procedure for adjudicating between them reveal about what its warrant-system actually tracks? Is that lack a defect, a deliberate division of labour, or a confusion about what kind of thing an interpretation is?

3. Test the practice for internal consistency. (a) If new knowledge is produced by new documents, why does the same discipline also prize revisionist rereadings of documents everyone has long had? Say what each of those two criteria of merit presupposes about where the past is stored and how it is accessed. (b) The practice makes causal claims while treating counterfactual speculation as unserious, a stance Carr endorsed in dismissing might-have-beens; yet causal claims appear to require some comparison with what did not occur. What conception of causation lets the practice keep both? (c) The bracket conventions treat the historian's voice inside a quotation as contamination of the source, while the narrative treats that same voice as a transparent window onto the past. Can a single practice coherently hold both attitudes to mediation?

4. Distinguish what the apparatus strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and identify where it is philosophically underdetermined — where a Carr-style account, on which facts become historical facts only through the historian's present questions, or a White-style account, on which emplotment is a literary operation carrying content no document supplies, would generate exactly the same notes and exactly the same review reports. Does the apparatus decide anything between Ranke's aspiration — to show the past *wie es eigentlich gewesen*, usually rendered in English as the demand to show how it really happened — and the sceptical reply, or is it neutral ground on which both can stand without embarrassment? Relatedly: does it matter, for what the practice is trying to do, whether *eigentlich* is best given by "really," "actually," or "essentially," or is that a merely philological worry?

5. The sceptics write footnoted prose too. Does the fact that the critique has left the practice essentially untouched show that the critique was never about the practice, that the practice is capacious enough to absorb its critics, or that the argument from participation begs the question by assuming the apparatus's own account of what a note is for?

6. Finally, describe what would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced by one that treats narrative form as content-bearing rather than as a neutral medium. Give it concretely: submission guidelines, dissertation requirements, and the page itself — where notes would have to attach, what would have to be declared up front, what would become impermissible, and what would happen to the authority the apparatus currently lends to the unsourced portions of the text. Say what the discipline would lose by the change, and whether anything already present in current practice (for instance, reflexivity or positionality conventions borrowed from ethnography) gestures toward it.
