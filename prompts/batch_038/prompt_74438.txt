Six weeks ago I got handed the reward and eval signal for a coding agent at my company — I'm the one who decides whether a rollout counts as good. Everybody kept saying "grade the process, not the outcome," and the more traces I actually score the less I know what that means. Here are my gut verdicts; I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Approach this as someone who's thought hard about both reward design for LLM agents and the philosophy of outcome versus process — brainstorm two or three candidate principles before you commit to one.

- Agent slaps on a hacky patch, tests pass, ships. Tests were thin, breaks in prod a week later. Bad, obviously.
- Agent does a genuinely careful refactor, flags the risk in the writeup, still breaks a subtle dependency. I score that bad too — but I'd rather have an agent that did this a hundred times than the one above, and I can't say why.
- Junior engineer flips a config value on a hunch, has no idea why, works. I praised him. I don't want the agent rewarded for the same move.
- Agent stops and asks for clarification on a genuinely ambiguous ticket, the human answers wrong, task fails. I want to reward the asking regardless, even though the customer got a worse result than if it had guessed.
- Agent flatters the user's bad premise, user is delighted, ticket closes, metric moves. Bad, even though everyone involved is happy.
- Honest reasoning that lands on a wrong answer vs. a polished, plausible-looking chain I can't verify that happens to land right. The second feels worse, and I don't think its "unverifiable" doing the work, because I feel the same way when I *can* check and catch it faking.
- Agent runs the right process but burns 40x the tokens. Not good, but nowhere near as bad as the flatterer.

I half-remember some philosophy example about two reckless drivers, one of whom hits a pedestrian and one who doesn't, and the claim that they're equally at fault — which feels related but not the same, since I'm not blaming anyone, I'm picking a training signal.

I keep reaching for something like "score the dispostion that generalizes to nearby tasks," but then every verdict rests on counterfactuals I can't measure, and I notice I flip depending on whether I'm imagining one rollout or a policy over rollouts. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely, say which clause each case motivates, give me its verdict on a case I haven't listed, and be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop — if it has to choose, I'd rather lose the config-hunch one than the flattery one. Tell me how confident you are in the principle you land on, and single out the part of it you're least sure about.
