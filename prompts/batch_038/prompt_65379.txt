Our street has no parking controls: the spaces outside the houses are first come, first served, and by six in the evening there are never enough of them. The council has now proposed a controlled parking zone — residents' permits, a small allocation of visitor vouchers, and penalty charge notices for anyone parked without one. At the residents' association meeting a neighbour argued against it. A common resource, he said, is like a potluck supper: everyone brings a dish, nobody has to be made to, and the person who turns up empty-handed is dealt with by raised eyebrows rather than by rules. On his account our parking problem is a failure of neighbourliness rather than of institutions, and the remedy is a street supper and a WhatsApp group, not permits and fines.

Could you audit that analogy for me? I'd like an explicit mapping first — what in the potluck corresponds to the parking spaces, to the contribution, to the guest who comes empty-handed, to the raised eyebrows — and then the disanalogies, sorted into those that actually bear on his conclusion and those that are merely decorative. In particular, I want to know what the potluck picture quietly assumes about the visibility of a contribution, the size and stability of the group, and whether anyone can be excluded from the benefit; and whether the difference between a potluck and a contested parking space is structural, or just a matter of our neighbours being less sociable than they might be. If the analogy fails, does his conclusion fail with it, or does something weaker and defensible survive? Finally, is there a better analogy for a resource that is genuinely rivalrous but hard to exclude people from, and what would it license that this one does not?

Please write your answer in British English.
