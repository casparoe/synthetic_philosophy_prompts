You are a political philosopher with a specialty in the theory of secession and democratic legitimacy, and you are used to explaining technical work to intelligent readers who have never taken a course in the subject. Please think carefully before answering, and treat what follows as a real argument to be diagnosed rather than as a request for reassurance.

I have no academic training in any of this. I run a small civil-engineering firm and I got pulled into the topic by following the news over the years — Scotland in 2014, which London agreed to hold, and Catalonia in 2017, which Madrid did not, and the Canadian Supreme Court's 1998 opinion on Quebec, which as I understand it said something like: there is no unilateral right to leave, but a clear majority voting yes on a clear question would oblige the rest of the country to negotiate. Reading around that, I built the argument below to convince myself that secession is either easy to justify or impossible to justify, and I cannot tell which. Every step looked obviously true to me when I wrote it down. The conclusion is one I am certain is false.

1. A state's authority over me is legitimate only if I am subject to it in a way I could have declined. Being born inside a line drawn on a map is not something anyone did, and it cannot by itself generate obligations.
2. Declining a state's authority cannot mean simply voting against the government of the day, since that leaves the state's authority over me exactly as it was. The only refusal that actually ends authority is exit.
3. For an individual, exit means emigrating, which is expensive, often impossible, and in any event only transfers me to a different authority. For a majority concentrated in one region, exit means leaving with the territory, which is the only form of exit that ends anything.
4. So a regionally concentrated majority that declines the parent state's authority has a right to secede, and the parent state's refusal to permit it is just coercion dressed up as law.
5. Nothing in the reasoning so far referred to the size of the group, how long it has been there, whether it has its own language or history, whether its grievances are serious, or whether anyone would recognize it. None of those facts did any work.
6. Therefore the same right belongs to any regionally concentrated majority whatsoever: a province, a county, a village, a single street, and — since one person standing on one parcel of ground is a majority of one — an individual.
7. And it belongs equally to any majority inside the new state the seceders set up, so the new state is born with exactly the hole in its legitimacy that the old one had.
8. If 7 is right, then no territorially defined political unit has authority over its own residents that survives this argument. A state is at most a truce between people who have not yet organized themselves into a smaller majority, all the way down.
9. That cannot be correct. It would render every actual state illegitimate, including ones I regard as plainly legitimate, and it would make the secession of a place like Quebec or Scotland not a special case requiring justification but an arbitrary point at which we stopped counting.
