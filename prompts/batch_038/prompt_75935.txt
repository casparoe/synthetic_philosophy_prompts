I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about the moral-perfection episode in Franklin's Autobiography, and I have come away unsure whether the essay I am drafting survives her objection. She is a historian of early American literature and I am not, so she has the advantage on the text, but I think the conceptual ground is mine to defend, and I would like an impartial view before I commit to it.

Her position, briefly: the whole project is vanity dressed as method, and Franklin knows it. On her reading, the little book of ruled pages and the dot for every fault is a substitution of bookkeeping for character, and the story he tells about the man who buys an ax, is asked to turn the wheel while the smith grinds the face bright, grows weary, and at last declares that on reflection he prefers a speckled ax — that story is Franklin confessing, with a wink, that the bright edge was never really his aim. She also leans on his remark about pride, that even if he could suppose he had conquered it entirely he would no doubt be proud of the resulting humility, as evidence that he saw the scheme was self-undermining. My position is that this collapses a distinction, and that once the distinction is drawn the episode looks less like a confession and more like the description of an instrument. Here is the section as it stands, which is meant to be the spine of the whole piece:

Franklin's project is usually assessed by asking whether he succeeded. He did not, on his own account, and the criticism has taken that failure as the interesting datum: the ledger is read as a monument to self-deception, and the ax at the grindstone as the moment the deception is cheerfully admitted. I want to suggest that this framing rests on a conflation, and that once three things are pulled apart the passage stops being a confession and becomes the specification of an instrument.

Call them the state reading, the limit reading, and the register reading. On the state reading, moral perfection names a condition of the agent — a settled disposition from which fault has been eliminated — and the project is an attempt to reach and then inhabit that condition. On the limit reading, perfection names an asymptote: what defines the agent is the direction and rate of approach, and the exclusion of arrival is no more an objection here than it is an objection to a convergent series that its limit is never one of its terms. On the register reading, the perfection aimed at is not chiefly a condition of the soul at all but a property of a record; the project's product is a legible, dated, comparable account of one's own faults, and the ideal serves as the standard against which such an account can be generated at all.

The distinction matters because the familiar criticism has force only against the state reading. If perfection is a state, then the ledger is a device of self-deception, since it lets the agent mistake the keeping of accounts for the mending of character, and the ax story is the confession. But if perfection is doing the work of a register, the ledger is not a proxy for virtue; it is the project's output, and its function is the manufacture of attention. Franklin's method — a week of strict attention to each virtue in turn, thirteen weeks to a course, four courses to a year, a dot entered under the letter of the virtue in focus for each fault noticed — is not obviously a technique for acquiring dispositions. It is very obviously a technique for distributing attention across a list too long to be held at once. His difficulty with Order is a difficulty of attention rather than of will: the discovery is not that he is disordered but that he cannot attend to all of it simultaneously.

This is where unattainability changes sign. On the state reading an unreachable target is a defect, and the scheme is arranged to fail. On the register reading unreachability is a requirement. A standard that could be satisfied would, once satisfied, stop generating entries: the dot is produced by the gap between conduct and standard, and a closed gap means an empty page and a silent instrument. The ideal is load-bearing precisely because it cannot be discharged. Franklin's remark about pride is then not a shrug but a structural claim — there is no position from which the register exhausts its material, and he is aware of it.

The awkward evidence is the medium. Franklin gave up paper because the dots had to be scraped out and the leaves wore through, and transferred his ruled lines to the ivory pages of a memorandum book, where a mark could be wiped off with a wet sponge. That is the deliberate erasure of the record for the sake of reusing it, which looks less like the production of a durable self-account than like a reset between cycles. If the register were the product, why make it washable?

My answer, which does not entirely satisfy me, is that the sponge shows what the register is for: not posterity but the next course. The point is not that the record be kept but that it be read, that at any moment the agent have before him a comparison of this week with last; a page that accumulated dots indefinitely would become unreadable, defeating the purpose as surely as a page never marked. On this account Franklin is maintaining a control loop rather than writing a memoir: the ideal is a setpoint placed out of reach so that the error signal never vanishes, the dot is the error, and the weekly course is the sampling interval.

Which brings me to the objection she pressed and I could not answer, and which I think is the strongest thing that can be said against the essay: the reading is unfalsifiable. Every outcome — success, failure, abandonment, erasure — can be redescribed as the instrument working as designed, and a theory that accommodates any result explains nothing. The only reply I have is that the register reading makes predictions about the text which the state reading would not make: that failures will be reported as dated and counted rather than excused, that the precepts will be short and behavioral rather than inward, that the virtue Franklin conspicuously fails at will be the one governing attention itself, and that the ax story will be told with the smith urging him onward rather than with the buyer simply walking away. Whether those predictions hold is a question about the book and not about my theory, which is as it should be.

That is the draft. I would be grateful for comments on the following, in roughly this order of importance. First, tell me plainly whether you think she is right and I am rationalizing. Second, is the three-way distinction a genuine distinction, or is the register reading just the limit reading with bookkeeping bolted on? If the latter, the essay has one idea where it claims three, and I should say so. Third, does my treatment of the washable ivory pages work, or does it look like special pleading, and is there a better use of that detail? Fourth, is the reply to the unfalsifiability charge adequate — and specifically, are those predictions discriminative, meaning would the state reading really predict the opposite in each case? Fifth, the control-loop vocabulary: is it earning its place, or is it decoration that will make a philosophically minded reader wince? I want to know whether to cut it to a clause or to develop it properly. And sixth, please flag anything in the passage where you think I have misdescribed Franklin's actual method or the details of the episode, or where the prose is doing unearned work. I am not asking for references or a bibliography; I will supply those myself once I know what I am arguing.
