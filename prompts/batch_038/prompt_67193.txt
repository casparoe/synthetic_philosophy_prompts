I put the following argument together for the philosophy club at my school, and its conclusion is one I cannot accept, so I assume something is wrong with it — I simply cannot find the step.

(1) Updateless decision theory says that when you act, you should rank policies by the expected utility they would have had from your prior — the beliefs you held before learning which situation you are actually in — rather than by your updated beliefs.
(2) This is because your present evidence is evidence you already expected to receive, so updating only tells you which branch of your prior you happen to be in, and the branch-neutral standpoint is the right place from which to choose.
(3) But a prior is itself a branch: it is the product of a process — schooling, reading, temperament — that could easily have given you a different one.
(4) So by the reasoning in (2), the right standpoint for choosing a policy lies before your prior was fixed, not at the prior.
(5) Yet that earlier standpoint either has no beliefs at all, in which case there is nothing to compute an expected utility from, or it has beliefs, in which case it is just another prior and (3) applies to it again.
(6) Either way, there is no standpoint at which the demand to step back stops.
(7) Therefore updatelessness is either undefined or unsatisfiable, and cannot be the correct account of rational choice.

Which step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I would rather keep (1): in the coin-flip case, where handing over a hundred dollars in one branch is what would have earned you ten thousand in the other, the policy you would have picked beforehand still looks to me like the one you should pick now.
