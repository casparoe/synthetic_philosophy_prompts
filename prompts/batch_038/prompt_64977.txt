Retired geochemist, 72, three years of reading philosophy in the evenings since I hung it up. I've been chewing on the demandingness business — Singer's pond, the whole line about having to prevent bad things whenever you can do so without giving up anything of comparable importance — and instead of arguing with it in the abstract I did what I've always done, which is write down the cases, record my own verdict on each, and then look for the curve that fits the data.

The cases, with my verdicts. These are real numbers off my own bank statements, more or less:

1. Ten percent of my pension to the most cost-effective global health charities. Obligatory. I'd be ashamed to do less and I'd think badly of a friend in my exact position who did less.
2. Fifty percent. Admirable, not required. I don't think worse of someone who stops at ten.
3. Everything above what I need to stay fed, housed, and mobile — the strong version. I don't accept it, and I can't say why without sounding like I'm just protecting my wine habit.
4. Nothing at all. Wrong, obviously.
5. $4000 to redo the kitchen counters, which I did last spring. Fine. Except $4000 given away would have bought a lot more good, so "fine" is doing a lot of work there and I can't say what work.
6. Flying out for my grandaughter's graduation, about $900. Fine, and not merely excused — positively good, part of what a life is for. $900 toward a stranger's cataract surgery is also fine and clearly better on the ledger. Why do those two feel different in kind rather than just in amount?
7. A kidney to a stranger. Beyond the call of duty. And I think I'd mildly disapprove of someone who donated a kidney and then gave no money at all, which I realize makes no sense on any view that only counts totals.
8. My grandson's tuition, $150k over four years, which I'm going to pay. Permissible, maybe even required of me as a grandfather. The same $150k to charity saves several lives. I hold both of those. I know that's a problem.
9. A billionaire who gives away five percent: behaving badly. A grad student on a $30k stipend who gives away five percent: not behaving badly, maybe even praiseworthy. Same fraction, opposite verdicts. So fraction of income is the wrong variable, or at least not the only one.
10. The same grad student giving twenty percent, eating rice and beans, and dropping out of the program: I think that's actually worse than not giving, because she'd have earned and given far more later. But the billionaire at twenty percent is still not doing enough. So the demand seems to track your trajectory and not your snapshot.
11. Timing. Giving ten percent a year for the rest of my life feels right. Giving the identical total as one lump now and nothing afterward feels wrong, even though the money and the outcomes are the same. A total-only view shouldn't care. Mine does.
12. The local hospital foundation where my wife was treated: I give to it and feel good about it, even though per dollar it's much less effective than the global stuff. I also think the larger share ought to go where it does more good. Both feel right and I don't know how.

The tentative principle I've been trying on comes from a habit of thinking about instruments: you're obliged to hand over up to the point where giving more would start degrading the instrument — where it would damage your future capacity to earn, to give, and to be any use to the specific people who depend on you. A maintenance threshold rather than a maximizing rule. Keep the machine in working order, give away the surplus above maintenance.

Where I can see it going wrong: it's blatantly self-serving (how do I tell a genuine maintenance requirement from a rationalized wine habit?), it doesn't say what counts as degradation, and I'm pretty sure it doesn't handle 9 or 11 at all.

What I want from you:

- Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely, that fits most of this? For each one, tell me which of my twelve cases each clause of it is there to handle — I want to see the fit, not just the statement.
- Which of my verdicts have to go? I'd rather be told than flattered. I suspect 7 and 8 can't both survive, but I can't see which one is the loser.
- What does each candidate say about cases I haven't listed? Specifically: (a) is there a sharp number, or a gradient, and if it's a gradient, where does failing to do the extra thing turn from merely not-praiseworthy into blameworthy; (b) does the requirement cover time and not just cash — I have maybe 1500 healthy uncommitted hours a year and I spend most of them reading philosophy books that do nobody any good; (c) what happens at my death — does an obligation to leave money to charity compete with the tuition one, and does it matter that I won't exist to be affected either way?
- And where does my maintenance idea sit relative to the fair-share sort of argument I half-remember reading, the one that says your duty shouldn't grow just because other people's compliance shrinks? Is mine a special case of that, a rival to it, or does it only look respectable because I dressed it up in lab language?

One request: please don't tell me the data set is biased in my own favour. I know. That's rather the point of asking.
