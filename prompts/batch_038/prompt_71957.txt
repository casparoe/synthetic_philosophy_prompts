I hold a chair in a department that co-runs an interdisciplinary working group on explanation in the historical sciences. At last month's session, a graduate student asked a senior colleague from the history faculty whether his model of state collapse could be put to a predictive test — whether it could say, of several currently strained polities, which would actually fail. The colleague waved the question aside with a proverb. He recited the familiar chain: for want of a nail the shoe was lost, for want of a shoe the horse, for want of a horse the rider, for want of a rider the battle, for want of a battle the kingdom; and he concluded that the whole enterprise was misconceived, since everything of consequence in history turns on details of that order, which no model could ever capture. A seventeenth-century collection of aphorisms gives the opening of the chain as: "For want of a naile the shoe is lost, for want of a shoe the horse is lost, for want of a horse the rider is lost." I have no interest in the proverb's provenance and do not want it traced; nor do I want a bibliography or a reading list. I want the sentence taken apart. I am drafting my reply for the next session and would like your analysis to work from.

Three things, in order.

First, give me the menu of distinct precise theses that this sentence — and its compressed everyday form, the one my colleague actually reached for — could be expressing, ordered from the near-trivial to the most radical. I expect somewhere between five and eight entries. For each, state the thesis so that its quantifier structure is visible: is the claim that some small causes have very large effects, that most do, that every large effect traces back to a small cause, that nearby possibilities diverge, that the divergence is epistemically unrecoverable, or something else again? Classify each entry as a platitude, as substantive but defensible, or as false, and say briefly what would have to hold for it to be true. Lay the readings out rather than selecting one and expounding it.

Second, in the draft I am circulating I have introduced three labels, purely to keep the discussion honest. They are my own coinage and you will not find them in the literature, so please do not go looking for them: amplification, meaning that within a single actual course of events a cause small by any natural measure of magnitude brings about an effect large by the measure that matters; divergence, meaning that two courses of events which agree closely at one time come to differ greatly later — a claim about pairs of nearby possibilities, and by itself silent on whether the difference shows up at a coarse level of description; and macro-indeterminacy, meaning that a coarse-grained description of a system at a time does not fix, or does not narrowly fix, its coarse-grained description later, so that the large-scale outcome depends on details the large-scale description leaves open. Tag each reading in the menu with whichever of these it involves. Then tell me whether the three come apart: I should like at least one worked example of amplification without divergence, and one of divergence without macro-indeterminacy, if such cases exist, together with a judgement about which of my three labels the proverb, on its most natural reading, is expressing.

Third, and this is the point of the exercise, say which reading — if any — licenses my colleague's inference from the proverb to the conclusion that no model of state collapse can be predictively tested. Attend to the fact that the inference seems to require a further premise about our epistemic access to the relevant details, and to the objection that a premise about divergence alone cannot supply it, since a system may be wildly divergent at fine grain while remaining highly regular in its coarse-grained statistics. Deal with that objection head on rather than noting it.

Keep the prose lean and self-contained. End with a line in exactly this form: Verdict: followed by the number and label of the single reading on the menu that would carry his conclusion, or Verdict: none if no reading carries it.
