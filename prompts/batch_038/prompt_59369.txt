I am in Year 13 and writing an Extended Project on the question of why sustained industrial growth began in Britain rather than in China. My supervisor has asked me to open the project by setting out the commitments I am bringing to the question, precisely so that they can be challenged before I have built anything on top of them. This is what I have drafted, and I should like to know whether it holds together.

1. The question is well posed as it stands, and it is a fair one: we know perfectly well what it would be to explain why Britain industrialised and China did not, and treating the question as unanswerable in principle looks to me like evasion dressed up as sophistication.
2. An explanation of why one thing happened rather than another has to cite something that differed between the two. Features the cases shared can serve as background, but they cannot carry the explanatory weight.
3. I accept the comparative work suggesting that in the middle of the eighteenth century the most advanced regions of China and the most advanced parts of Europe were broadly similar — comparable living standards, comparable commercialisation and market development, comparable life expectancy. On this view there was no large pre-existing gap.
4. I also accept that the decisive difference was economic in a fairly specific sense: British labour was dear and British coal was cheap, so machinery that spent energy to save labour paid for itself there in a way it would not have elsewhere. More generally, I think the explanation must appeal to something structural and durable — prices, institutions, a practical and experimental culture of knowledge — rather than to accidents or to particular individuals.
5. A good account should leave the outcome contingent. I want to be able to say that it could plausibly have gone otherwise, and I am suspicious of any explanation on which the Industrial Revolution looks like something that was always going to happen.
6. Explanations earn their credibility by being the sort of thing the evidence could have contradicted; I distrust accounts that stay comfortable whatever turns up.

I am not asking for a reading list or for references, and please do not send me off to consult anything in particular — the exercise is to test the set as it stands, not to defer to authorities. What I want is an audit. Tell me which of these genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do; rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally damaging; and for each one say which commitment would have to be weakened or abandoned and what the resulting account would lose. Then tell me which single revision would resolve the most, and whether the revised set is one a historian could consistently hold while still claiming to answer the question in (1).

Finally, I should like you to be explicit about how confident you are in these verdicts, and to single out the claim in your answer that you are least sure of, together with what would make you change your mind about it.
