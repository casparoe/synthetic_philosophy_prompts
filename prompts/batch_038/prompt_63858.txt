Our association has 912 members — mostly small firms and individual practitioners — and an amendment article that has just been used against itself. I am one of seven members of the Governance Panel that has to decide what happened, and I am drafting the written opinion we will circulate before Wednesday's vote.

The amendment article of our Charter reads as follows; I set out all three of its provisions because everything turns on the wording, and I want you working from this text and nothing else:

§14.1 — Any provision of this Charter may be amended by a two-thirds majority of members voting at a general meeting of which notice has been given.

§14.2 — This Section 14 may be amended only by the unanimous consent of all members.

§14.3 (added by unanimous consent in 2019) — No amendment to this Section 14 takes effect unless it is adopted under the procedure for amending this Section that was in force at the time notice of the motion was given.

At a special general meeting last month, a motion to amend §14.2 — replacing "unanimous consent of all members" with "an 85 percent majority of members voting" — carried with 94 percent of the votes cast, and the chair declared it adopted. Immediately afterwards, the same meeting voted to delete §14.3 outright; that motion carried with 88 percent, and was declared adopted on the strength of the new §14.2 standard.

Two features of the meeting matter. First, unanimity is not achievable on our current register: eleven members are in long-standing arrears and have not voted in nine years, one individual member has died and the seat passes to a firm that has not designated a representative, and two members under common ownership abstain as a matter of standing policy. Second, this was the third attempt in four years to change the amendment article; the previous two failed for want of unanimity, and the association has been unable in the meantime to introduce a postal referendum procedure that most members want.

A petition signed by 63 members asks the Panel to declare both votes void. The petitioners say that an amendment adopted in breach of the very rule it purports to change cannot be valid, that §14.3 was added unanimously precisely to foreclose this manoeuvre, and that if 94 percent of those voting can rewrite the amendment article then no provision of the Charter is entrenched at all and §14.2 was idle from the start. The respondents say that §14.2 was drafted for a forty-member association and is now a deadlock clause; that a rule which the body it governs can never change is not a rule but a trap; that §14.3, read strictly, makes the 2019 unanimous vote the last amendment to Section 14 that will ever be possible, which cannot have been intended; and that the members' power to alter their own ground rules is not a gift conferred by §14.1 but something the Charter presupposes.

My own instinct is with the respondents. I do not want that instinct driving the opinion, and I expect you may conclude it is wrong.

For context on the ideas in play, I am aware of the theoretical apparatus behind this dispute: Ross's puzzle about an amendment rule that appears to authorize its own destruction; the distinction between a sovereign power that continues to be bound by its limits and one that can consume them; the regress that seems to arise if every change to the rules of change requires a prior change to those rules. Draw on these only insofar as they help decide this case. Do not survey them, do not cite authorities, textbooks, or decisions from other jurisdictions, and do not quote or invent Charter provisions beyond the three above. Where the text admits two readings, state which you adopt and why.

Please write the opinion using exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Holding — two or three sentences disposing of both votes.
2. The question presented — about 120 words. State precisely what must be decided, including whether the §14.2 amendment and the deletion of §14.3 stand or fall together, and whether §14.3 governs the vote that was taken under the notice already given.
3. Two readings of Section 14 — about 350 words. The strict-procedural reading and the self-embracing reading, each stated as its own advocate would state it, with the textual hook each relies on and what each implies about the permanence of Section 14.
4. What turns on the choice — about 250 words. Concrete consequences for this association over the next decade: the unreachable members, the referendum proposal, and what either holding does to the security of any minority protection the Charter contains.
5. The strongest objection — about 250 words. The best argument against the holding in section 1, pressed as hard as you can, including any way in which that holding lets a determined supermajority strip entrenchments permanently or leaves the Panel unable to say when, if ever, such a vote would be valid.
6. Disposition and timetable — about 150 words. What the Panel orders, what becomes of the second vote, what the membership may rely on in the meantime, and dates. Note that our authority comes from a separate article untouched by these votes and that our decisions stand unless reversed by two-thirds of members voting.
7. Drafting note — about 150 words. Replacement language for Section 14 that a future meeting could adopt to prevent recurrence, with one sentence on what protection the new language gives up.

Aim for roughly 1,300 to 1,500 words in total, written for members who have never encountered this puzzle in a law or philosophy book.
