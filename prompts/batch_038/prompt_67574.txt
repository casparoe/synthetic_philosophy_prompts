Last night I argued until nearly two in the morning with a friend of mine — a technology-policy lawyer with a deep prior against anything resembling a prohibition — about Dennett's late claim that today's conversational systems are counterfeit people and ought not to be permitted to mimic human beings. He was willing to grant Dennett the description and deny him the conclusion, and he did it by turning Dennett's own chosen image against him. A counterfeit, he said, is a counterfeit banknote: the wrong consists in passing something off at face value on someone who takes it, the wrong is complete at the moment of the transaction, and the remedy is the remedy for every fraud of that shape — disclosure. Label the note, let the merchant look for the watermark, and nothing of value is lost; anyone who continues to defer to a system they have been plainly told is not a person has only their own credulity to answer for. I told him the banknote is the wrong picture, and that a counterfeit person is better understood on the model of a supernormal stimulus: the host bird that goes on stuffing food into the gape of a brood parasite's chick three times its own size, because the cue that once reliably signalled *my offspring, feed it* is now being presented in a form more intense than anything the environment ever produced. On that picture disclosure does no work at all, since the response is triggered by the cue rather than by a belief about the cue, and the harm is not that someone is cheated in a transaction but that a detection system is being driven far outside the range of conditions in which it tracked anything real. He said I had merely re-described the same worry in more alarming vocabulary, and that my picture secretly assumes human beings cannot help responding — an assumption he thinks Dennett himself would have rejected, given how willing he was for decades to say that a machine that sustained a sufficiently demanding conversation had thereby shown it really did believe and want things.

I would like the argument settled as far as it can be settled by analysis. First, give me both analogies laid out explicitly rather than gestured at: for the counterfeit note, what plays the part of the note, of the genuine article it copies, of the assay that distinguishes them and of whoever is entitled to perform it, of the person defrauded, and of the general debasement that makes counterfeiting worse than one cheated merchant; for the brood parasite, what plays the part of the host's recognition system, of the cue, of the environment in which the cue was reliable, and of the parasite. Then tell me where each mapping fails, and — this is what I actually want — which of those failures bear on the question we were arguing about and which are merely decorative. In particular: does the counterfeit picture, pressed honestly rather than used as a slogan, already commit my friend to more than he conceded, for instance about who the counterfeiter is, about what the circulation of counterfeits does to the standing of the genuine notes and their holders, and about whether there is any assay for personhood at all that a merchant could be expected to run at the counter? And does my stimulus picture import a conception of human beings as creatures whose responses cannot be governed by their beliefs, which would collide with the very permissiveness about machine minds that Dennett spent his career defending? Finish with a verdict on whether the disclosure-is-sufficient conclusion survives either analogy, and, if you think both pictures mislead, with the best third analogy for what these systems are and exactly what it licenses.
