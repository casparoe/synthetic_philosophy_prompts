I am a novelist, and the book I am writing is an endless nest of embedded narratives: a character in the outermost story reads a novel whose protagonist reads a novel whose protagonist reads a novel, and so on downward, with no innermost book. Each level's story seems to be what it is in virtue of the reading and writing going on at the level beneath it, so the chain of in-virtue-of relations never terminates.

Write an essay on whether grounding — the non-causal determination relation in which one fact obtains in virtue of another — must bottom out in something fundamental, or whether an infinitely descending chain of grounds, with no ungrounded base, is genuinely possible. Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides: the thought that a fact whose entire explanation lies in still further facts is never explained at all; the objection that well-foundedness is a prohibition imported from proof theory and set theory that has no business in metaphysics; and the question whether an infinite chain can pass along a determination it never itself receives. Then tell me what my novel amounts to on each answer — the description of a real metaphysical possibility, a notational trick, or a fiction whose facts are true in the story without being grounded in anything at all — and commit to one of these.

Argue the case through on its merits rather than by appeal to authority, and end with a verdict, not a survey.
