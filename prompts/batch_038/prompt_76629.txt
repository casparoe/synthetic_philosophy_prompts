Last night I had a long and unresolved argument with a friend about Aristotle's doctrine of natural slavery, and I now think it turns on a passage I would like help reading closely.

My friend's defence is that the doctrine is not Athenian prejudice dressed up as theory but a functional claim with a genuine criterion: the natural slave, on this reading, is not the unintelligent person but the person who cannot originate a plan of action by deliberation and can only execute one laid down by another's reasoning. It follows, my friend says, that pointing to slaves who were clever, literate, and perfectly capable of managing large estates simply misses the point, since competence is not the capacity in question. My objection was that this renders the criterion invisible — nothing a slave actually does could ever distinguish the capacity for practical authority from mere skill at obeying well — so that Aristotle is not stating a theory but ratifying an institution in theoretical language.

Here is the passage I keep coming back to, from *Politics* 1254b, in the translation I am using:

"the soul rules the body with the sway of a master, the intelligence rules the appetites with that of a statesman or a king and in these examples it is manifest that it is natural and expedient for the body to be governed by the soul and for the emotional part to be governed by the intellect, the part possessing reason, whereas for the two parties to be on an equal footing or in the contrary positions is harmful in all cases. Again, the same holds good between man and the other animals: tame animals are superior in their nature to wild animals, yet for all the former it is advantageous to be ruled by man, since this gives them security. Again, as between the sexes, the male is by nature superior and the female inferior, the male ruler and the female subject. And the same must also necessarily apply in the case of mankind as a whole; therefore all men that differ as widely as the soul does from the body and the human being from the lower animal (and this is the condition of those whose function is the use of the body and from whom this is the best that is forthcoming) these are by nature slaves, for whom to be governed by this kind of authority is advantageous, inasmuch as it is advantageous to the subject things already mentioned. For he is by nature a slave who is capable of belonging to another (and that is why he does so belong), and who participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it; for the animals other than man are subservient not to reason, by apprehending it, but to feelings."

And this, a little later in Book I, at 1255b:

"the slave is a part of the master—he is, as it were, a part of the body, alive but yet separated from it; hence there is a certain community of interest and friendship between slave and master in cases when they have been qualified by nature for those positions, although when they do not hold them in that way but by law and by constraint of force the opposite is the case"

Between the two, Aristotle concedes that nature often fails to mark the distinction on the body: some people have the bodies of free men and the souls of slaves, and sometimes the reverse.

Could you work through the following with me?

1. What is "apprehend but not possess" reason supposed to be? Is a coherent faculty distinction actually available here — following another's reasoning versus deliberating for oneself — or does it reduce to a capacity for obedience? Note that, on the passage's own terms, apprehending reason is precisely what separates the slave from the domestic animal, so the slave cannot be treated as simply an animate tool.

2. Which of the analogies is doing the work? Aristotle begins by separating despotic rule (soul over body) from statesmanly or kingly rule (intellect over appetite), and then places the master–slave relation in the first category. But at 1255b the slave is a living part of the master, with a community of interest and even friendship with him. Can a part of a body be a friend of the whole? Does that formulation support my friend's functional reading, or undercut the despotic analogy that the argument needs?

3. In what sense is the relation "advantageous" to the natural slave, and does Aristotle require it to be good for the slave in the slave's own interest, rather than merely expedient for the household?

4. What does the body/soul mismatch concession do to the criterion? I read it as fatal to its testability; my friend reads it as Aristotle admitting that actual slaves mostly are not natural slaves, which would turn the doctrine into a criticism of Athenian practice rather than a defence of it. Is either reading genuinely available, and which does the surrounding argument support?

Then tell me plainly whether the reading my friend proposes — a capacity for practical authority rather than intelligence — survives the text, or whether it is a rescue operation. Please answer in British English, and do not trouble with references or further reading; I want close reading and argument, not a bibliography.
