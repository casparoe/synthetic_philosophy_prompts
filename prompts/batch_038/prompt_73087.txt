So — talk in about three weeks, joint session, and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than another defense of my view, because the view is already written.

The room: roughly half experimental and behavioral economists, the standard escalation-of-commitment crowd who run incentivized continue/quit tasks and treat the sunk cost effect as a well-replicated bias. The other half is a public policy school — program evaluation, cost-benefit analysis, people who get funded to tell agencies whether to keep or kill a program. Basically no philosophers; one person runs a lab that has published several of the canonical sunk-cost paradigms and will not enjoy being told the paradigm is blind by construction.

What I'm arguing, roughly: the "fallacy" is misdiagnosed. Ignoring past expenditure is only mandatory if that expenditure is causally and informationally inert going forward, and in a lot of real cases it isn't, for three reasons I want to keep strictly seperate from each other:

1. Information. My earlier investment was made under uncertainty and partly reflects evidence I've since lost track of or can no longer reconstruct. Quitting now overwrites a judgment made by a version of me who knew things I don't currently know. Continuing is a crude way of deferring to him.
2. Commitment. If I expect my future self to be present-biased and to want out of every long project the first time it gets unpleasant, then adopting a standing policy of "I don't abandon things I've paid heavily into" is a second-best way of binding myself. From outside it looks like a bias; from inside its a device.
3. Signaling. People observe whether I quit. Sticking with things I've sunk real money into changes how partners, funders, and employees predict I'll treat their own stakes. That's a genuine payoff, not a confusion about accounting.

And then the bite: at least one of these three makes "irrational" the wrong label for a big chunk of what the bias literature counts as fallacies, and the lab paradigm strips all three out by design — one-shot, anonymous, no future interactions, no private information the subject can't fully articulate, no self-control problem because everything is paid out at the end. The task is engineered so the sunk cost really is inert, and then we generalize from it to the Concorde.
