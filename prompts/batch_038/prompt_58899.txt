I am putting together a handout for the closing session of a bioethics seminar I am auditing, and the session is on the therapy/enhancement distinction. The trouble is that every example in circulation has been argued over so many times that my fellow participants pattern-match rather than reason: as soon as growth hormone, modafinil, cosmetic psychopharmacology, or beta-blockers for nervous musicians come up, people recite positions they already hold and nothing actually gets tested. I want to replace that with a case built to order, one that isolates a single structural feature of the debate instead of trading on the usual intuitions about competitive advantage. Please construct that thought experiment for me, and please do not reach for any of the examples I just listed or for anything else that is already standard in this literature.

The specification is as follows. I want two patients receiving the same intervention, at the same dose, for the same diagnosed condition, with the same measured shortfall on the same functional task, and with no difference in cost, availability, or personal ambition between them. The only respect in which their situations differ is the statistical distribution of the relevant trait in the population they belong to: in one, the shortfall is uncommon and pulls the individual well below the local average; in the other, the shortfall is close to the modal condition, so that the individual sits at or just above the local average despite being identically impaired relative to the functional task. Against that setup, a statistical account of health in the tradition of Boorse, on which health is statistically typical part-functioning relative to an age- and sex-matched reference class, must classify the identical intervention as therapy for one patient and as enhancement for the other; while an account in the tradition of Norman Daniels, on which the boundary tracks whether an intervention restores the range of opportunities and life plans a person could reasonably expect to pursue, must deliver the same verdict in both cases. I also want a temporal dimension: if the intervention becomes routine in the second population over a decade, the statistical account's verdict about the original patient in the first population should flip, even though nothing whatever has changed about that patient, her impairment, or her treatment. Further constraints, all of which I ask you to respect: the setting must be ordinary present-day clinical medicine, with a drug or procedure that is genuinely prescribed now, and no genetic modification, no implants, no speculative technology; the divergence between the two accounts must not be manufactured by fiddling with where a diagnostic cutoff sits on a bell curve, since I want the disagreement to be about which baseline the account appeals to rather than about a disputed number; and no scarcity, rationing, or fairness-of-access considerations may be doing any work in generating the verdicts, because the treatment must in principle be available to anyone who wants it. The scenario itself should run to no more than about two paragraphs, and should read like a case a physician might actually bring to an ethics consultation.

After the scenario, demonstrate that it satisfies every clause of the specification rather than merely asserting that it does. State the verdict each of the two accounts delivers in each of the three situations, and give the reason each account has for delivering it, so that a reader can see the verdicts falling out of the accounts themselves instead of out of stipulation. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and say what happens to the case if that feature is removed, including whether what remains collapses back into one of the familiar examples I asked you to avoid. Then present the strongest available reply on behalf of the statistical account, the objection you think has the best chance of dissolving the case outright, argued as its proponent would argue it and not as a strawman. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about your own case: which consideration, if I raised it, would lead you to concede that the scenario fails to meet one of my specifications or that the two accounts are not really in disagreement here, and at which step in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no boldface, and no tables.
