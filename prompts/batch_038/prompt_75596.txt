Our reading group's convenor has a line he keeps coming back to: Keynes's 1930 essay on his grandchildren's economic possibilities is really just an argument for early retirement on a public pension. The good life is the retiree's life, so the entire policy job is to hand the pension over and let everyone potter — which he uses to shut down any discussion of redesigning work, or of what people would actually do with the time, on the grounds that Keynes already settled it and turned out to be a UBI man before the term existed.

How far does that analogy actually hold? Give me the correspondence table first — whos the retiree, what plays the pension, what's the career that's been finished with, where do the forty-odd years of free time come from, and what in Keynes's picture is doing the work of the state pension authority. Then the places it snaps, and what it quietly imports from retirement that his picture lacks. Then tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it doesn't, whether there's a better analogy for the life he's describing.

Please seperate out what's settled among people who work on Keynes from what's genuinely contested — I don't want a disputed reading of the essay sold to me as the consensus.
