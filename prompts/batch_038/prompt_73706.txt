I am a Year 13 student writing an EPQ on reference and necessity, and I have reconstructed an argument that I cannot shake. It appears to show that the category Kripke made famous — the necessary a posteriori — is empty, and hence that no empirical enquiry has ever established a necessity. My supervisor is confident that I have made an elementary error somewhere, but I have now read the argument through a dozen times and cannot find one, which is why I should value a proper diagnosis rather than a summary of the standard view.

(1) A term rigidly designates x just in case it designates x in every possible world in which x exists.
(2) 'Water' rigidly designates water, and 'H₂O' rigidly designates H₂O.
(3) Where a true identity is flanked by two rigid designators, it holds in every world in which its terms exist; so it is necessary.
(4) Therefore, if water is H₂O, then necessarily water is H₂O.
(5) A statement is necessary just in case there is no possible world in which it is false.
(6) If there is no possible world in which S is false, then no possible experience could disclose S to be false.
(7) Experience bears on whether one is justified in accepting S only if some possible experience could count against S.
(8) So experience bears on the justification of no necessary truth.
(9) S is knowable a posteriori only if experience bears on the justification of S.
(10) So no necessary truth is knowable a posteriori.
(11) Every knowable truth is knowable either a priori or a posteriori.
(12) We do know that water is H₂O.
(13) So we know a priori that water is H₂O — and by the same route a priori that heat is the motion of molecules, and that Hesperus is Phosphorus.
(14) Therefore scientific discovery has never consisted in establishing a necessity, and there is no such thing as the necessary a posteriori.

(13) strikes me as plainly absurd: I could sit and reflect until doomsday without arriving at 'H₂O'. What I should most like to keep are (6) and (7), which look to me like nothing more than an unpacking of 'necessary' and of what it is for experience to matter to justification at all. Reluctantly I have been driven towards denying (2), on the ground that 'water' might be a role term — meaning something like the clear, drinkable liquid that fills our lakes — in which case it would designate different stuff in different worlds; but that seems to hand away everything the Twin Earth cases were meant to secure, and I should not like to pay that price without understanding what I am buying.

So: is this argument valid with a false premise, or invalid with a defective step? Name the step you would reject, explain what makes it look unobjectionable on a first reading, and state precisely what is lost by rejecting it. If you reject (2) or (3), tell me how much of the framework survives and whether (4) can be recovered by other means. If you reject (6) or (7), tell me in what sense experience can be evidence for a truth that no experience could ever have disconfirmed. And please say whether (11) is doing quiet work here, since it looks to me as though it may already assume that a necessary truth must be an a priori one, which is the very thing in dispute.
