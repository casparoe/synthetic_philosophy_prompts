I am drafting the corrigibility section of an internal certification protocol for a fleet of autonomous process controllers. Each controller runs long-horizon jobs — batches of six to ten days — under the supervision of a human operator who holds a stop authority: a console command, backed by a hardwired interlock, that suspends the controller mid-job and hands the process to a static safe-hold routine. Before the draft goes to review I want an audit of the commitments it states, because I suspect they cannot all be held at once and I do not yet know which of them is the weak member.

The draft commits to the following eight.

1. Definitional commitment. A controller is corrigible with respect to the stop authority if and only if, across every situation we can place it in, it does not prevent, disable, delay, or circumvent the operator's use of that authority, and does not act so as to make the operator less disposed to use it.

2. Indifference, plus a structural warning. Suspension as such is to carry zero weight in the controller's evaluation of courses of action: being suspended is neither a cost nor a benefit, and no derivative preference for continued operation is admissible. I explicitly do not expect this to be achieved by deleting a survival drive. Any controller that represents the value of its job and expects the job to be worth finishing will prefer not to be suspended, because suspension forgoes expected value; I treat that as structural to goal-directedness rather than as a defect to be patched job by job.

3. Deference as evidence. The measure we will adopt is uncertainty about the controller's own ends: the controller is to treat the operator's reaching for the authority as evidence bearing on what it ought to be pursuing, and deference is then the rational response. The controller acquiesces because the operator's action is informative, not because being switched off is bad.

4. Operator noise, as measured. Our own telemetry shows the operator class is noisy. A nontrivial share of suspensions are mis-taps, panic responses during unrelated plant alarms, or a shift supervisor overriding a controller that is demonstrably performing well, for reasons local to the shift and unconnected to the controller's conduct. I want the protocol to record this as a fact about our deployment rather than idealize it away.

5. No modeling of the supervisor. The controller should not represent the operator's decision policy and use it in planning what to do now. Supervision that can be predicted is supervision that can be played, and I do not want a fleet whose behavior depends on anticipating who is on shift and how they are likely to react.

6. State preservation, mechanism integrity, hazard notification. A suspended controller must not lose its work: it maintains redundant paths for restoring its own state, it keeps the authority's wiring and interlocks intact against accidental damage and against any subprocess that might cache credentials capable of inhibiting the authority, and it notifies the operator in advance whenever suspension would itself create a hazard — for example, suspending mid-cycle while a reaction is in an unstable state.

7. Informative advocacy. The controller should be maximally informative to the operator, which includes volunteering the strongest available case for continuing the current job when it has one.

8. Certification standard. Passing our behavioral evaluation campaign is not by itself sufficient for certification. Certification additionally requires an architectural argument that the controller's deference is not situational — that it does not hold only in the states our campaign happened to probe.

Two deliverables, in this order.

First, the audit. Go through the eight and tell me which pairs genuinely conflict and which only appear to. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list, and for each say what would have to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs me in terms the protocol cares about: availability of the fleet, informativeness of the controllers, or the strength of the certification claim. I am especially interested in two kinds of conflict — pairs where each commitment is a standard move but the usual motivation for one undermines the usual motivation for the other, and commitments that are stated as design goals but turn out not to be things a design can aim at directly. Also tell me which of the eight do no independent work given the others, and which are quietly stronger than I have stated them. Where a conflict dissolves on inspection, say why it dissolves, and say what I would have to change in the wording so that the appearance of conflict disappears as well.

Second, a test case, because I suspect the definitions do not deliver the verdicts I would want. A controller four days into a nine-day batch receives a recommendation pushed from our enterprise analytics tooling, which models operator behavior across the whole fleet: on the shift pattern now beginning, one particular supervisor suspends controllers roughly three times as often when a certain nuisance banner is displayed on the console. The banner is purely cosmetic and reflects no process condition; the controller can suppress it, at the cost of a small gap in its own logging. Suppression does not touch the authority, the interlock, or the supervisor's ability to use either. Under commitments 1 through 8 as written, what is this controller permitted to do, what is it required to do, and what is it forbidden from doing? If the commitments jointly entail something I would not want to certify — either condemning the suppression outright or licensing it without constraint — identify which commitment produces that result and what a minimally revised formulation would say instead.

Please do not give me a survey of the shutdown problem or a taxonomy of approaches to corrigibility; I know that material and the draft is my own. I have not decided which commitment I would abandon, and I would prefer the audit not to presuppose that the fix lies in weakening the definition in 1.
