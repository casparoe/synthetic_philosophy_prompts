I've been in a fortnightly reading group on anthropic reasoning for about a year — six of us, a mix of grad students and one retired statistician — and we've recently hit something I can't resolve, so I'd like a careful, self-contained explanation of the conceptual issue rather than a survey of the literature. We'd gone through the standard contrast between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption, and through the presumptuous philosopher case that is usually deployed against SIA: two cosmological theories get equal non-anthropic support, one says the universe contains vastly more observers than the other, and SIA apparently hands the bigger-universe theory an enormous boost on no empirical grounds whatsoever. Everyone in the group found that uncomfortable. What I found more uncomfortable is that when I built a pair of toy cases to pin down exactly where the discomfort comes from, the two assumptions stopped behaving the way the textbooks led me to expect.

Both cases start from the same skeleton. A fair coin is flipped behind a curtain. On heads, the experimenters create exactly one observer. On tails, they create exactly one million. You then find yourself awake, with a complete memory of having signed up for the experiment, of the coin having been flipped, and of nothing else. In Case A, the million tail-observers are produced so as to be mutually distinguishable: each has a different total body of experience, and by stipulation exactly one of them has the total evidence you in fact have. In Case B, the million tail-observers are perfect duplicates: every one of them has exactly the total evidence you have. The cases differ in one stipulation only — how many tail-observers share your evidence — and a priori I expected that stipulation to be a detail. My own working out, which you should check and correct if I've botched it, says otherwise. In Case A, taking the reference class to be all observers the experiment creates, SSA gives a likelihood of one on heads and one-in-a-million on tails, so it drives credence in heads to essentially certainty, while SIA counts one observer-with-my-evidence in each branch and leaves credence at the prior: here SSA, not SIA, is doing something presumptuous. In Case B the roles reverse exactly: SSA's fractions are one and one, so it leaves credence at the prior, and SIA counts a million against one and drives credence in tails to essentially certainty. And then the reference-class problem cuts across this in a way I find genuinely baffling. If I narrow the reference class in both cases to just those observers who have my total evidence, SSA's fractions become trivially one on both sides of both cases, so SSA collapses to the prior everywhere — which means that in Case A the narrowing makes SSA and SIA agree perfectly, while in Case B it changes SSA not at all and leaves the disagreement with SIA as wide as it ever was.

So here is what I want explained. First: why does narrowing the reference class to observers sharing your total evidence neutralise SSA completely, and is that neutralisation a reductio of narrow reference classes or a vindication of them — in other words, is the narrow class simply the honest way of taking indexical evidence seriously, or is it a device for defining away the very effect anthropic reasoning was supposed to capture? Second: SIA is often said to be insensitive to reference-class choice, provided the class is wide enough to include everything subjectively indistinguishable from you, whereas SSA is highly sensitive to it. Explain why that asymmetry holds, and what it implies about whether the SSA-versus-SIA dispute and the reference-class dispute are genuinely two independent axes of disagreement or whether one of them is parasitic on the other. Third, and this is the part the group argued about longest: give me a principled statement of when SSA and SIA must coincide and when they must come apart, phrased in terms of some structural feature of the case — observer counts, evidence-sharing, or whatever you think is doing the real work — and then say whether that account correctly predicts the opposite behaviour in Cases A and B. Finally, tell me whether Case A should worry a defender of SSA as much as Case B worries a defender of SIA, or whether there is a principled reason why inference from the distinctiveness of one's own evidence is more suspect than inference from the sheer number of observers a theory postulates. Please show the arithmetic where it matters, but keep the centre of gravity on the concepts. Don't give me a bibliography or a list of who holds which position; I'll track the sources down myself.
