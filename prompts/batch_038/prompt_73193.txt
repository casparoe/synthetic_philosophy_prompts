I keep hearing "the banality of evil" thrown around at work. Last week a manager used it to push back on a coworker who said the exec who signed off on the data-sharing deal "must know what he's doing is wrong" — his point was that since the harm came out of an org chart, nobody in the chain really deserves blame, and to say otherwise is just to be in the grip of a monster-mythology. Arendt's phrase from her book on the Eichmann trial, right?

Can you lay out the different precise claims that phrase could be making — something about how unremarkable the perpetrator looks, something about his motives, something about whether he's thinking at all, something about where responsibility sits once things get bureaucratic — and sort them into the near-trivial, the substantive-but-defensible, and the ones that are false or that later historians' findings about Eichmann undercut? Then tell me which reading, if any, actually licenses my manager's move from "the harm was distributed across a system" to "so no individual in it is blameworthy." I want a menu I can argue from, not a plot summary of the book.
