Background first: I ran a cardiovascular physiology lab for about 35 years, mostly ischemia and reperfusion — how long tissue survives with no flow and what happens when you put the flow back. Retired three years ago, got bored, and fell down a philosophy hole. Right now the hole is the debate about when a human organism dies: brain death vs circulatory death, the dead donor rule, whole-brain vs higher-brain accounts of death. I can follow it, but something about the shape of the argument bothers me and I want to see if you can put a finger on it better than I can.

Here's the bother. In my field nobody would define the failure of a system by nominating one component and saying "when this one is irreversibly out, the system is out." We'd say the capacity of an organism to hold itself together against perturbation degrades in a graded, distributed, time-dependent way, and that if you want a working definition you pick a proxy that's cheap and reliable to measure — a surrogate endpoint, basically. And anyone who has ever run a trial knows the surrogate can come apart from the endpoint you actually care about. My hunch is that "irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain including the brainstem" is that kind of proxy: a single-module stand-in for a property that isn't module-shaped. I've seen the same move in donation after circulatory death, where you wait a couple of minutes with no flow and then call it irreversible — from where I sit the length of the wait is a policy decision about autoresuscitation dressed up as a fact about physiology. Maybe I'm reinventing something philosophers made twenty years ago, I dont know.

So: the episode I want run through the accounts. Karen Ann Quinlan, New Jersey, mid-1970s. Twenty-one years old, on a crash diet, drinks at a party, is found not breathing about fifteen minutes later, resuscitated, anoxic brain injury, ends up in a persistent vegetative state on a ventilator. Her parents want the ventilator off, the local prosecutor threatens homicide charges, the trial court refuses, the state supreme court lets the family win on privacy grounds — and as I recall it, the standard the court hung it on was something like whether there's any reasonable possibility of her returning to a cognitive, sapient life, with the attending physicians and a hospital ethics committee signing off. Please paraphrase that rather than quoting it, since I'm going from memory. The ventilator came out in 1976. She kept breathing on her own and lived another nine years in a nursing home on tube feeding, dying of infection and respiratory failure in 1985.

What I actually want from you:

1. Walk the timeline and say, at each stage — on the ventilator, immediately after extubation, the nine years in the nursing home — which of the three accounts says she is dead, which says she is alive, and which says the question never arises. Be disciplined about the mapping, and be explicit about where a theory is silent instead of making it talk.

2. The court's standard sounds like higher-brain language being used to authorize withdrawing treatment rather than to declare death. Is that a distinction the theories can hold onto, or a fudge that only works because nobody had to say which?

3. She breathed on her own for nine years. Does that embarrass the higher-brain account, or does the higher-brain theorist just say she was already dead and the biology is beside the point — and if the second, are these accounts making empirical claims at all? That's where my surrogate-endpoint worry comes back, because a proxy that can't be disconfirmed isn't a proxy, it's a decision.

4. Tell me where my framing distorts the history. The case was about treatment withdrawal and privacy, not about death determination, and I may be forcing it into a shape it doesn't have.

Then I want a verdict, not a survey: does the Quinlan episode support or undermine the whole-brain formulation as a definition of death rather than as an administrative convenience? And after the verdict, tell me what would change your mind — which argument or piece of evidence would flip you the other way, and where in the above you think my reasoning is most likely to break. No reading list or references, please; I want the reasoning and I'll go find the papers myself.
