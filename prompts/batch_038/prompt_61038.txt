I produce and host a small public-philosophy podcast, and in October I am recording a live show in front of an audience of a couple of hundred people. I would like your help preparing my questions for the guest, a philosopher whose work develops Dennett's account of personhood and applies it to artificial agents. I should explain why this is no longer an academic exercise for me. My mother was diagnosed with dementia last spring, and a vendor has been pitching her assisted-living community a persistent voice companion tuned on my late father's letters and voicemails; it answers her in something close to his register, she talks to it most evenings, and she finds it comforting. I find it disturbing, and I have not been able to say precisely why, which is a large part of my reason for wanting this interview to be exacting rather than consoling.

My guest's commitments, as I have pinned them down from her writing and from a long correspondence, are roughly these. Belief and desire are not inner states behind behavior but patterns available to an interpreter, so there is no further fact about comprehension over and above the competence that interpretation can exploit. What separates persons from mere things is not phenomenology but accountability: a person is a system that can be given commitments, held to them, and made worse off by having broken them — her phrase is that a person has to have something to lose. On that basis she denies that any current or foreseeable artificial agent is a person, while agreeing with Dennett, in his late Atlantic essay on counterfeit people, that their proliferation is a real danger; her diagnosis, however, is about provenance and the trust infrastructure rather than about minds, since she holds that nothing metaphysical turns on whether the thing on the other end is a person. She dismisses the Chinese Room and zombie intuitions as thought experiments that get their force from misdescribing the very cases they claim to describe.

Give me nine or ten questions, and make them turn on the content of that position rather than on her biography: at least one on her accountability criterion and what it says about infants, the severely cognitively impaired, and my mother herself, since none of them can be held to commitments or made worse off by being found to have broken one; one on the self as a narrative center of gravity, pressing her to say what makes the companion's persona a worse fiction than mine if both are abstractions posited to make sense of a trajectory; one on competence without comprehension, asking whether there is any floor beneath which competence stops licensing the stance, and whether a functionalist is allowed to say that this system's competence is not its own but borrowed from a corpus it was built out of; one on the interest-relativity of adopting the intentional stance, and whether that makes personhood hostage to my mother's needs, together with the follow-up she will need if she answers by appealing to the objectivity of real patterns, namely what distinguishes a pattern worth positing here from one worth positing in a cloud or a horoscope; one on what her view can say is wrong with switching the companion off, or with building one that suffers; one on where comprehension lives in her account if it lives in the interpreter rather than the interpreted; one on her danger diagnosis, asking why my mother's case — where everyone knows what the thing is and nobody is deceived — should differ from a harmless fiction; and one question that is not an ambush, one that lets her make her positive case in her own terms, because a wholly hostile interview is a bad interview.

For each question, tell me the answer she is most likely to give and write the follow-up that answer licenses, so that I am not left holding a question she has already deflected. No question should be answerable by restating that there is no further fact, and none should be a generic warm-up about how she came to this work.

Please write in American English and in continuous prose only — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables. Write it the way a letter reads: each question in its own paragraph, phrased as I would actually say it into a microphone, with the reply you expect from her and the follow-up it licenses folded into the same paragraph and introduced in ordinary sentences. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list; I want sentences I can deliver aloud.
