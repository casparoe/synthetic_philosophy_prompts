So — five weeks from now I'm giving a department colloquium to a general metaphysics group, and what I need is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Let me set up the thesis first and then the room, because the objections I care about are very specific to who's sitting in the chairs.

The view. I'm arguing that al-Ghazālī's occasionalism is usually misfiled. The two standard readings are (a) he's a skeptic about causation, an eleventh-century proto-Hume running a "but how do you know the cotton will burn again?" thought experiment, or (b) he's a theologian whose real targets are the philosophers' denial of miracles and bodily resurrection, with the causation stuff purely instrumental. My claim is both get the shape of it wrong. On my reading his position is two-level, and deflationary about the causal relation itself:

- Read as claims that things necessitate their effects in virtue of their own natures — that fire, being what it is, makes cotton's burning inevitable — causal generalizations are false. That's what the fire-and-cotton case in the seventeenth discussion is actually doing. It isn't raising a skeptical worry about induction; it's a challenge to the idea that observed constant conjunction has to be *explained by* necessitating natures.
- Read as claims about a stable divine habit — his word is ʿāda, custom/habit — the very same generalizations are true, and they support prediction and inference perfectly well.

So the dispute with the Avicennans isn't over the extension of causal claims; everyone in the room agrees fire burns cotton. It's over what makes them true. I've been calling the resulting view "theological nomic positivism": laws are brute, ungrounded regularities, and the necessity we feel in them is something we project onto natures that were never going to supply it. Divine will does the job the powers realist wants natures to do, and does it by just being what it is, with no further explanation available.

Two things I'll argue in the talk that I know are exposed:
1. That the reading preserves the asymmetries we care about — fire burns cotton and not vice versa, the habit is ordered rather than random — without needing any intrinsic necessitation, because the ordering lives in the habit, not in the relata.
2. That the metaphysics is seperate from the theology: strip the deity and you're left with a bare-regularity view plus a modal error theory. I want to say that's a feature — that he's isolating a genuine philosophical mistake about necessity — but I know that's exactly where I'll get hit with either changing the subject or smuggling the deity back in through the back door.

The room. About 25 people, mostly metaphysics faculty and grad students. Specifically: two committed dispositional/powers realists who think in terms of powers, mutual manifestation, and what grounds a regularity; one philosopher of physics who is a Humean about laws but is deeply suspicious of anyone making the best-system analysis do metaphysical work; one interventionist about causation, Woodward-ish, who cares about invariance and explanation and will be interested in the fact that on my reading God can break the habit whenever; one historian of early modern philosophy who works on Hume and Reid and will not let the proto-Hume comparison slide; and a handful of grad students who've read only the handout. Nobody reads Arabic and nobody works on kalām, so they'll argue about the view rather than the philology — which is what I want, but it also means nobody is going to rescue me on exegetical grounds. The one thing several of them half-remember is that Ibn Rushd answered him, and that the answer was something like "without real causes there's no knowledge at all," so expect that to get waved at me at some point. As a group they're also allergic to two moves: being told they have to read him in his theological context (they hear conversation-ender), and being told a philosopher "isn't really denying" the thing he looks like he's denying (they hear defining the target away).
