I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working on agency and decision theory, and my committee has asked me to show up to next week's meeting with a proposed two-year agenda rather than a topic sentence — not "I'll work on Kavka's toxin puzzle" but a ranked list of the specific questions worth spending the years on, with the reasoning visible. Help me think it through. For context, since I don't want the answer to hinge on anyone remembering the details: in Kavka's 1983 setup an eccentric billionaire puts a vial of toxin in front of you that will make you painfully ill for a day but is otherwise harmless and non-lethal, and offers you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink it tomorrow afternoon. The money lands in your account hours before the drinking deadline, you're explicitly free to change your mind, and the billionaire can tell whether you have the intention. So at the moment of drinking you have every reason not to and no reason to. Kavka's own diagnosis is roughly that you can't intend what you know you won't do; Gauthier pushes back that once the intention is sincerely formed you're no longer free to reopen the question from scratch, so drinking is the rational continuation. The setup grew out of deterrence thinking — a state threatening a retaliatory strike it would have no reason to carry out once provoked.

Here's the candidate list I've been scribbling, some of which I made up myself, and I'm sure I'm missing ones that matter more than anything here: (1) which constraint on intention-formation is actually doing the work — "you can intend to A only if you believe you will A", or "only if you have, or believe you have, some reason to A when the time comes", or something about intentions having to be *formed on the basis of* reasons rather than merely accompanied by them? These give different verdicts on variants, and my suspicion is that most of the disagreement is downstream of people picking one without arguing for it. (2) Self-binding: if I can arrange a real penalty for not drinking — an enforceable promise, a hired enforcer, a reputation stake — do I solve the puzzle or just relocate it, since I now need a reason to enter the binding arrangement and I'd know in advance that entering it costs me the day of illness anyway? (3) A probabilistic version: the billionaire's scanner is only 80% reliable, or the payment is proportional to your credence that you'd drink, so intending becomes a gamble rather than a sure thing — does that dissolve the puzzle, sharpen it, or just add noise? (4) A two-agent version: I get paid if my partner forms the intention and they get paid if I form mine, so neither intention is self-supporting but the pair's is — does distributed bootstrapping actually work, and if not, what breaks? (5) Awareness: does the puzzle depend on the payoff already being secured, or on the agent *knowing* it's secured? A forgetful, self-deceived, or badly informed agent seems able to form the intention, which suggests the constraint might be epistemic rather than practical — and if so, a lot of the debate is misfiled. (6) The machine version: a deployed artificial agent whose internal states are fully readable by its counterparties and which recomputes expected value afresh at every decision point — can it be in the toxin situation at all, and what architectural or training features (myopia, dispositions installed rather than decisions made, no self-modification, opaque commitment stores) would let it form a toxin-type intention? I care about this last one beyond my dissertation because I'm loosely attached to a group with two engineers and 18 months of funding that wants to know whether "credible commitment between AI agents" is a real engineering target or a category error, and they've asked me what the conceptual work would even consist of.

So: give me the ordering, for a small group of philosophers with occasional help from those engineers, and defend it rather than just listing. I want the criteria made explicit and traded off against each other — how much of the rest of the list depends on settling the question first, how tractable it is with the tools we'd actually have in two years, and how much the answer would change what people build or do. Tell me which one looks profound but where settling it wouldn't move anything downstream, and which one I should drop or merge. Then name the single crux that I think most of the list implicitly rests on but haven't been able to articulate, and say what kind of result or argument over the next 18 months would promote or demote each item. Please don't give me a reading list or a survey of who said what — I can find that myself, and comittee members hate it when the agenda reads like a lit review. What I want is the dependency structure and the argument for it.
