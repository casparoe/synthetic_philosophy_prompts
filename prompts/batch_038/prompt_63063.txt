I'm putting together a session for a small reading group on the alignment-tax argument, and the whole thing hinges on the strategy-stealing assumption: the idea, borrowed from combinatorial game theory, that whoever comes out on top of a race can, once they're in control, carry out whatever plan the loser would have carried out — so grabbing a decisive advantage is never a loss, and you can always pay the safety tax afterwards, from a position of strength. I want a thought experiment in which that assumption fails cleanly, built to these specs:

- No AI in the scenario. No superintelligence, no uploads, no "decisive advantage within six months" business. Ordinary human organizations only — the point is to isolate the structural logic so nobody in the room can wave it away as sci-fi handwaving.
- The scenario itself is one paragraph. Concrete: name the organization, say what's being raced for, and say what the winning move actually is.
- The winning side must sincerely want to do the thing it ends up unable to do. No value drift, no corruption, nobody being stupid, no outside coercion. The failure has to come from what winning cost, not from who won.
- Stipulating the verdict counts as cheating. The impossibility has to be visible in the plain facts of the case, so that someone who'd never heard the term could reconstruct it from the story alone.

Then, after the paragraph, do the demonstration:

1. State the plan the loser would have pursued, and show it's not merely available to the winner but genuinely attractive to them — say why they'd prefer it.
2. Name exactly what the winning move consumed or foreclosed, and show that plan is no longer in the winner's option set even though they now hold all the power.
3. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict if you delete just that feature and leave everything else alone.
4. Give me the best counterattack from a defender of the assumption — something in the neighborhood of "you could have seized control first and then deliberated at leisure," or "your case only shows you should have won differently" — and say honestly whether the case survives it.
5. One short paragraph on whether that same feature is plausibly present in the real AGI-race version of the argument, or whether it's an artifact of the human-scale setup.

Last line of your answer, on its own, should be exactly: Verdict: FAILS or Verdict: HOLDS — meaning whether strategy-stealing fails or survives in the case you built.
