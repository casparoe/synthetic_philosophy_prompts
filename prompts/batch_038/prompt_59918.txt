**Question (25 points).** A radiocarbon laboratory reports that a single charcoal sample from a hearth has a 0.9 probability of dating to before 1200 BCE. The figure was derived from a calibration built out of measurements on very many other samples; this sample was measured once and will not be measured again. Explain how (a) a von Mises-style limiting-frequency interpretation, (b) a subjective Bayesian interpretation, and (c) a single-case propensity interpretation would each account for the reported 0.9, saying in each case what the probability is a property of. Then state which of the three, if any, is best placed to answer the objection that the hearth's date is a single, non-repeatable matter of fact, and explain why.

**Answer submitted.** *Frequency interpretation.* On von Mises's view a probability statement is a statement about a collective: an infinite sequence of outcomes of the same kind of trial whose limiting relative frequencies are invariant under any place selection. So the 0.9 is a property of the series of radiocarbon measurements on comparable material, not of this hearth; the reported figure is shorthand for how often samples of this description turn out to be older than 1200 BCE. Since the existence of the limit and its invariance are built into the definition of a collective rather than read off the data at hand, frequency claims are not ordinary empirical generalizations about the samples actually measured. This also means the frequency view changes the subject: it tells us about the laboratory's procedure, not about when the hearth was used.

*Subjective interpretation.* For the Bayesian the 0.9 is a degree of belief of whoever reported it, a property of an agent's doxastic state rather than of anything in the world. The constraint on such states comes from the Dutch book argument: an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability axioms is exposed to a package of bets that together guarantee a loss. Because conformity to the axioms is exactly what makes an agent's beliefs track the long-run frequencies of the events she has beliefs about, the Bayesian's 0.9 is answerable to the calibration data in the same way the frequentist's is; the advantage is simply that the Bayesian can also assign figures to one-off matters such as this hearth, for which there is no collective.

*Propensity interpretation.* Popper introduced propensities precisely to make single-case probabilities intelligible. On this view 0.9 is a dispositional property of the physical situation — the sample together with the processes that fixed its carbon content — and it belongs to that individual situation as fully described, not to any class of situations. Propensity theory therefore escapes the reference-class problem that besets the frequency account: there is no need to select a class of similar cases, since the propensity attaches to the case itself. It also makes the single-case figure testable, because a propensity is manifested in its outcome; finding out what the sample's date actually is amounts to measuring the propensity directly.

*Conclusion.* Since the three interpretations make probability a property of three different sorts of thing — an infinite series, an agent's belief state, a physical disposition — they cannot genuinely contradict one another; each describes a different aspect of the same situation. The dispute is verbal, and the single-case objection rests on a confusion, since no interpretation ever claimed that probability attaches to a bare particular.
