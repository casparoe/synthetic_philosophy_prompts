I'm in my second year of a philosophy PhD, and this term I'm in a formal epistemology seminar that has been working through the standard possible-worlds treatment of belief. Everyone there agrees that the treatment implies logical omniscience and that this is a tolerable idealization: an agent's belief state is a set of worlds, believing is a subset relation, real people are not closed under consequence, but the idealization earns its keep because it gives us a clean handle on entailment, contradiction, and consistency. For a short paper I set out to make precise why the idealization is supposed to be *tolerable* rather than merely convenient, and instead I built an argument whose conclusion is that a certain ordinary person believes everything. My seminarmates are confident the argument is broken, but after two weeks nobody has been able to say where.

Here it is. Let B_S be the set of worlds left open by everything S believes — by construction, the intersection of all the propositions S believes — and let propositions be identified with the sets of worlds at which they are true.

1. S believes p exactly when every world in B_S is a world at which p is true; that is, B_S is a subset of p.
2. Hence, if the propositions S believes have no world in common, B_S is empty.
3. Some agents believe propositions that have no world in common. Kripke's Pierre is the clean case: as a boy in Paris he learned from postcards and pictures that London is pretty, and after emigrating and picking up English in a drab part of the city he has become disposed to deny, in English, that London is pretty. The reading of the puzzle my seminar accepts — and the one I need — is that Pierre thereby has contradictory beliefs about a single city.
4. So B_Pierre is empty.
5. The empty set is a subset of every set of worlds, so by step 1 Pierre believes every proposition: that two plus two equals five, that the Riemann hypothesis is false, that Pierre does not believe two plus two equals five.
6. But Pierre is a competent arithmetician who would deny that two plus two equals five, and he certainly does not believe that he fails to believe it.
7. So step 1 is false.
8. And the damage is not local. If propositions are not sets of worlds, then entailment is not inclusion and a contradiction is not the empty intersection, so the framework that was supposed to explain what believing consists in no longer explains anything — for every agent, not merely for the inconsistent ones.

The escape route I expected to take is blocked, or so my advisor says. I wanted to reject step 3 in the familiar way: Pierre's two beliefs are not about the same object of belief, they are held under different guises or modes of presentation, so there is no contradiction to begin with. His response was that if belief content is guise-relative then contents are fine-grained, and fine-grained contents are precisely what step 1 excludes — so the step I chose in order to rescue the semantics is a step that destroys it. Nor does step 3 need Pierre. The preface gives me unsatisfiability without any puzzles about names: an agent who believes each of a large body of claims and also believes that at least one of them is false has a belief set with no world in common, and her B is empty just the same. So I appear to be choosing among an impossible-worlds semantics I cannot state coherently, a fragmentation account that leaves me with no single doxastic state to talk about, and a hyperintensional semantics that faces the granularity problem.

Tell me which step to reject and what rejecting it costs. I would like to keep step 1 if I possibly can, since everything else in the seminar's toolkit depends on it, but I want to know whether keeping it is even coherent, and whether steps 5 through 8 form a genuine reductio or merely take an idealizing model literally — and if the latter, what exactly separates a model that idealizes from one that misdescribes, given that the model here delivers a theorem, not an approximation. Then run through the repairs one by one: constraining B_S to be non-empty, so that the semantics simply declines to describe Pierre; adding impossible worlds where logical laws fail; inserting an awareness operator; Stalnaker's fragmented or compartmentalized belief states; structured and otherwise hyperintensional propositions; and the dispositionalist line on which implicit belief really is closed under consequence, making step 6 the false premise rather than step 1. For each, say what it buys, what it gives up, and whether my argument can be re-run one level up against it — against the impossible worlds, the awareness operator, the fragments, or the structures themselves. If you think the dispositionalist is right that step 6 is what has to go, say so plainly, but explain what remains of belief as an explanatory notion once everyone implicitly believes everything, including every contradiction.
