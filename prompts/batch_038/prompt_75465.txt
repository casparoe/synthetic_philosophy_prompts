Ok so I'm a staff engineer at a mid-size SaaS company, no philosophy background whatsover, and I've been volunteered to give the opening ~10 minutes at our company-wide "learning review" next Thursday about a 9-hour outage we had in March. I want you to write the actual speech. Not an outline, not notes — the thing I read out.

The room: about 120 people. The four of us who were in the war room that night. Our VP Eng, who owes the board a one-line explanation and keeps asking me for "the root cause" like it's a missing sock. Roughly 30 people who joined after March and know this incident only from the postmortem doc and one onboarding slide. Support folks who were on the phones with angry customers for nine hours straight. And, bonus round, our SOC 2 auditor is sitting in the back taking notes because the incident is in scope.

I was incident commander, which is the part that makes this wierd: the person who made the calls is the same person presenting the account of the calls.

Here's why my first draft got thrown out. My partner is a history PhD student and she read it and demolished it with arguments I only half followed. She said my draft was doing the thing that 19th-century German historian everybody treats as the founder of modern source-based history wanted — tell it *wie es eigentlich gewesen*, "how it actually was," just the documents, no interpretation — and that ideal has been picked apart for a hundred years. She brought up Carr, and his idea that history is an unending dialogue between the present and the past, and that facts don't come pre-labeled as relevant: the historian decides which of the endless facts in the past get promoted into the account. Then she brought up Hayden White and emplotment — that any telling of a sequence of events gets slotted into a stock plot shape, tragedy, comedy, romance, satire, and the shape you pick is doing real work while pretending to just be reporting. Her closing line was "your timeline is a story you chose, and you wrote it like the logs chose it for you," which stung, because the logs did not choose it for me.

The concrete mess she's pointing at:

- The log shipper died about twelve minutes in. There's a 41-minute hole in exactly the window where the escalation decisions happened. We reconstructed part of it from Slack, one photo someone took of a Grafana panel on their phone, and two people's memories. The memories don't match.
- Raw debug-tier logs are past our 30-day retention. So the "archive" of this event is now a PDF of a dashboard, a Slack export, and four humans.
- The incident tool has a mandatory "root cause" field, one text box, and we filled it with one sentence. Everyone who was there knows it was at least four things lining up.
- Two of the war-room engineers disagree about ordering: cache flush before or after the first rollback attempt. Nobody thinks anyone's lying. The timestamps can't settle it because one of the relevant Slack messages was edited later and we don't have edit history.
- The dashboard we all stared at for nine hours was built two years ago by someone who's since left, and it graphs the six metrics that person cared about. Three of the things that actually mattered that night weren't on it, and we only realized after.
- The customer-facing timeline we published differs from the internal one in three places. Not lies — just fewer events, because a 40-event customer timeline is unreadable. But someone in that room has read both documents.

What I need from you:

1. Around 1100–1400 words, spoken, in my voice: plain, slightly dry, engineer, zero TED-talk energy. It has to leave people able to follow the three technical sessions that come after me, and it has to give the VP something honest to carry to the board — not a fiction and not a shrug.

2. It must not claim to transparently report the past. Say in plain English that the 41-minute hole means a chunk of this is reconstruction, and that the timeline is fourteen events picked out of about four million log lines, and that I picked them. No hiding behind "the data shows."

3. But — and this is the hard part — it must not collapse into "there's no fact of the matter, it's all narrative." People are going to change their freeze windows and retry backoffs because of this meeting, and two of the guys with the ordering disagreement still have to share an on-call rotation. I need the skepticism to *increase* accountability, not dissolve it. If the speech lets anyone conclude that nothing can be known so nothing needs fixing, it's a failure.

4. One moment where the speech names the plot shapes it's refusing — no tragedy with a villain, no redemption comedy where we suffered and got wiser and it's fine now, no jokey satire about how bad our tooling is — and says what shape it's offering instead, and admits out loud that this is still a choice I made.

5. Carr's dialogue idea should be *felt*, not lectured: what we emphasize today is shaped by what scares us today (we're mid-migration and we're scared of it happening again), and in six months someone will retell this incident differently without being wrong. Make an engineer in row three hear that as useful rather than as nihilism.

6. At most one name-drop. If you mention Ranke or Carr or White, do it in a clause the way a non-specialist would, not a paragraph, and paraphrase their claims rather than putting words in their mouths — I am not reading a made-up quotation in front of an auditor. No bibliography, no suggested reading, no "for more on this."

7. End by handing off to Q&A in a way that makes it possible for the two engineers who disagree about the cache flush to say so out loud without either of them looking bad, and without me, their IC, turning it into a trial. That's the bit I'm most nervous about and I want it written, not described.
