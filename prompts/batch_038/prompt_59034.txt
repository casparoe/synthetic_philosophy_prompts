Backend engineer here, no philosophy training beyond a few podcasts, so bear with me. I got dragged into a long-running group chat argument last week about whether the federal government should pass one national AI law or just leave it to the states, and one of the guys (call him Dave — he's a lawyer, which is why everyone defers to him) ended the thread by posting a screenshot of a famous line and saying something like "this is literally the founding argument for letting states do their own thing, the debate is over." The line is from a Supreme Court dissent, Brandeis I think, in a 1932 case about an Oklahoma law requiring a license to sell ice: "To stay experimentation in things social and economic is a grave responsibility. Denial of the right to experiment may be fraught with serious consequences to the nation. It is one of the happy incidents of the federal system that a single courageous State may, if its citizens choose, serve as a laboratory; and try novel social and economic experiments without risk to the rest of the country." Dave's use of it was: a California-style bill regulating frontier AI failed, so don't nationalize anything, let 50 states each try their own thing and we'll find out which works. But I can't articulate the objection.

So: can you break that one sentence down into the distinct precise claims it could be making? I'm picturing a menu, not an essay — like, maybe one claim is just about legal permission (states are allowed to legislate here), another is an epistemic claim about whether running a policy in one state actually produces knowledge anyone can use, another is a claim about transferability, another about the "without risk to the rest of the country" part, which reads to me like an assumption about spillovers rather than a fact. Sort out for each reading whether it's basically trivially true, whether it's substantive but defensible, whether it needs empirical assumptions that are probably false in a nationally integrated economy, and whether it's just false.
