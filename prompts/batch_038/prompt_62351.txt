I'm a fourth-year PhD student in formal epistemology, and I've spent two years in a reading group that has become, for me, the clearest example I know of a debate that produces no motion. The dispute is over whether a rational doxastic state must be a single probability function or may be a set of them — a representor or credal set. Both camps have their standard moves, and every one of those moves is by now completely familiar to the other side. The imprecisists press cases where any sharp number looks arbitrary: a coin drawn from a barrel whose bias-composition nobody has recorded, Ellsberg-style choices where people systematically refuse to bet on the ambiguous urn while happily betting on the 50/50 one, Keynes's worry that evidence can differ in weight and not only in direction, and the plain observation that if 0.75 is required, so is 0.75001, and nothing in the evidence settles which. The precise Bayesians reply with dilation — conditioning on a partition can make your interval wider no matter which cell you learn, so that gathering evidence can leave you less informative than you were — and with the diachronic betting cases, in which an agent with a spread of credences declines a bet she would have accepted, or misses an arbitrage that a sharp agent takes, and is thus either money-pumped or, on the friendlier reading, simply incoherent over time. The imprecisists then say that dilation is a feature, not a pathology, because it is exactly what it looks like for a model to be sensitive to which evidence arrived, whereas a sharp prior buries an arbitrary choice inside a single number and calls it precision; and they say the sequential-choice principles are derived from sharp coherence in the first place, so deploying them against a set-valued agent begs the question. Nobody in my group has budged, and the argument has begun to feel like it is being conducted in two vocabularies that only superficially overlap.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than accepted, is that this is not a disagreement about the formal machinery at all — both sides agree the mathematics is coherent, both agree dilation occurs in the models where it occurs, both agree the betting cases behave as described — but a disagreement about what the norms governing credence are answerable to. One side treats rational credence as a representation of what the evidence licenses, so that misbehaving under sequential choice is beside the point, or at worst a defect in the decision rule rather than in the belief state. The other treats credence as essentially a state you act from, so that a state incapable of supporting coherent diachronic choice does not deserve the name. If that is right, then the dilation exchange is really an exchange about whether evidential fit outranks actionability, the betting exchange is really an exchange about whether dynamic consistency is a norm of belief or a norm of a belief-plus-decision-rule package, and the two sides are answering an epistemological question and a decision-theoretic question under one heading. I want you to tell me whether that diagnosis is correct, or whether something else is doing the work: a merely verbal dispute about "rational," a hidden empirical question about how real agents represent uncertainty under ambiguity that could in principle be settled by psychology or by better elicitation methods, a hidden formal question about whether any decision rule for sets of probabilities can be both non-collapsing and dynamically well-behaved, or a sign that the very idea of a single thing called the belief state is trying to carry two independent parameters — an expectation and a weight or confidence — and should be split accordingly. In particular, I want your view on whether the question-begging charge against the sequential-choice principles is sound, since that charge is doing an enormous amount of work in the imprecisist reply and, if it fails, the debate may not be stalled at all but merely unwon.

Please answer as a formal epistemologist who has published sympathetically on both sides of this dispute and has no institutional stake in either. Work through the structure of the arguments and the points at which they actually make contact before you write anything, and be prepared to conclude that one side is simply right, or that the impasse is not a symptom of an ill-posed question but of a hard one — I want the diagnosis defended, not a menu of live options. What goes on the page should begin with your bottom line: your first sentence should state, in one sentence, what kind of disagreement this is. Then give the reasoning, drawing on what each side actually says rather than on a survey of the positions I have already sketched for you, with no preamble, no restatement of my question, and no reading list. Close by saying what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a representation theorem, an impossibility result, an experimental finding, or a change of question — or why no progress should be expected and what would have to be true for that pessimism to be correct.
