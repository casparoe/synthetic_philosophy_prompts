ok so i run the philosophy zine at my school — it's six of us, we print like 80 copies and staple them in the library — and every single issue we get letters about the same fight: whether a person can literally live on inside other people's heads. Everybody's read the part in Hofstadter where he talks about his wife dying and how much of her was still in him and in their kids and their friends, plus the bit about souls coming in different sizes depending on how much of the world's structure they carry around. Half our readers think that's the deepest thing they've ever read and half think it's just a fancy way of saying people remember you. Arguing isn't working. I want to sort them instead.

For the next issue, write me a decision tree: which version of the distributed self do you actually hold? It has to fit on one folded sheet, so 8 to 10 questions max, each node one question with two or three answer options, each leaf a position. Readers are 15-18 and most have never read any philosophy, so no node can assume they know a single technical term.

The rules for the nodes are the part that actually matters:

- No jargon and nothing that asks about labels. Every node has to be a concrete case someone can answer without knowing where it leads. Nothing like do you think you are your brain.
- Work these cases in somwhere: the conjoined twins who share a bridge of brain tissue so that what one sees the other can sometimes describe; a couple married fifty years who both have memories of events only one of them was present for; someone who dies leaving a husband, two kids, and a box of letters; a chatbot trained on everything a dead person wrote that answers brand new questions the way they would; identical twins; and a person whose parts get swapped out one at a time until nothing original is left, with everyone agreeing it's still them.
- One of the early nodes has to be the type-versus-token hinge, i.e. is a person more like a symphony that can be performed many times or more like one particular performance of it. Phrase it as a case, not as a mini lecture about types and tokens.
- Build it out of what the questions and answers allow first, and only then drop the positions into the space they land in. Don't start from a list of names and work backwards.

Leaves I need: the Hofstadter-ish one where you're a pattern smeared across a bunch of brains in degrees and souls have sizes; the psychological-continuity view in both flavors, the one where branching is fine and the one that needs a no-branching rule; the body/organism view where all the copying talk is beside the point; the view where you're the story you tell about yourself; and the deflationary one where other brains only ever hold a model of you and never any piece of you. If some combination of answers lands somewhere nobody defends, don't quietly delete that branch — mark it unoccupied and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, because it's incoherent-looking but merely unattractive, or because nobody's gotten there yet. And flag any two leaves your tree can't really seperate, rather than pretending it can.

No bibliography or reading list at the end, we just want the sheet.

One more thing: at the end tell me how confident you are that each leaf is a view real people actually hold versus one you invented to fill a box in the grid, and single out the one node you're least sure about — the one that might not be splitting anybody at all.
