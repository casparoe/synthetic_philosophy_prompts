Right, I've been putting this off for three weeks and the do is on Saturday, so please be good.

My mum retired in June. She's 66 and she was a historian for nearly forty years — not a famous one, the other kind. Her whole subject was fen drainage in Lincolnshire: the minute books of one of the old drainage boards, the commissioners-of-sewer bodies, plus some enclosure stuff in the villages round about. Most of her working life was spent in a county record office transcribing handwriting that is genuinely almost impossible, and she eventually published a big scholarly edition of the minute books that maybe twelve people have read, but she was extremely proud of it.

Saturday there's a retirement do in the function room of a pub near Lincoln, about seventy people, and I'm giving one of the toasts. Mine is the family one, second on the running order, five minutes, six at a push.

The room is split and that's the problem. Roughly twenty-five of them are her old colleagues and ex-PhD students, including the two people she spent her career arguing with — one is a very hard-nosed economic historian who thinks that if you can't count it it's gossip, the other came round to the theory stuff in the nineties and thinks an archive is a text with delusions of grandeur. The other forty-odd are family and my mates, including three software engineers, and their idea of a historian is someone who does the voiceover on a documentary about the Blitz.

Here's my hook, and I want to use it because its the only reason I have any idea what to say at all. I'm a software engineer and a big chunk of my job is writing incident reports — postmortems — after things break. Last year we were down for eleven hours. The report I wrote had a timeline section that I had to reconstruct from a logging system that had itself been down for nine minutes of the incident, so part of the record of the failure was destroyed by the failure. The "root cause" line moved three times between the first draft and the one we published. And the phrase everyone in my industry uses, "blameless postmortem", is sneakily making a promise about objectivity — that the account won't be bent by who we'd like to blame — and I don't think we can keep that promise. I read Carr's *What Is History?* and then Hayden White's *Metahistory* on trains, badly, like an amateur, and realised my job is the same argument in a hoodie.

What I want:

- The White machinery has to be structural, not named. Don't say his name anywhere — the two historians will work out what I've done and the family will just think it's a funny bit. Specifically: pick three facts from this list — the Fiesta, the edition taking eleven years, turning down a job at a bigger university to stay near the archive — and tell them twice, back to back, once in a register that makes them sound like a quest and once in a register that makes them sound like a slow farce. No new information in the second pass. Same facts, different shape, and let people feel it rather than announcing it.

- Carr's point has to be in there but in ordinary words — the historian and the facts being in some kind of endless back-and-forth, the idea that the records she fished out weren't just lying there waiting for her, that the questions she could ask depended on being alive in the 1980s and not in 1780. If a term wouldn't survive a twelve-year-old, cash it out in the same sentence.

- She also has to be *right*, and this is the bit I care about most. Don't give me everyone-was-a-bit-right mush. There's a version of the empiricist win I want you to build the speech around: her line at dinner was always that the reason the water doesn't come in is that a load of farmers once sat in a room in Boston and wrote down what they'd decided. A minute book with a damp-rotted page in it is a hole you cannot talk your way across — no reading of it, clever or otherwise, produces a maintenance record that isn't there. Her Rankean stubbornness, the whole show-it-as-it-actually-was thing she got accused of being naive about, was closer to engineering than to philosophy. That should be the emotional centre, not a concession paragraph.

- Don't put words in her mouth or anyone else's, and no verbatim quotations from any book. She'd spot a mangled quote instantly and I'm not having the head of department correct me in the car park. Paraphrase everything.

- It has to be about her, not about the debate. Usable material, all true, don't invent big new facts: a rusty Ford Fiesta she kept for nineteen years until it failed its MOT twice in one month; a boot permanently full of wellingtons and one box of photocopies; her catchphrase in family arguments, "what does the document actually say?", deployed once when my sister claimed she'd been the favourite; she taught herself a seventeenth-century hand and now says modern handwriting is illegible.

- Sayable, not written. I'm a bad public speaker. Short sentences, no subordinate clauses three deep, no "in conclusion". Mark two or three spots where I should shut up and let it land.

- End on a glass raise. The last line needs to work twice: for the family it's a nice thing about my mum, for the historians it's a real point about the record. Not a pun, and nothing about "the future".

- 700 to 850 words, then four or five lines after it telling me which bits are doing which philosophical work, so if someone corners me by the crisp bowl I can answer. Don't give me a reading list, I've got enough shame already.

Success condition: neither of her two old sparring partners thinks I've taken the other's side, and both of them think I finally understood her.
