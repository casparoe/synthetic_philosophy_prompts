I am marking take-home essays for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in metaphysics, and I would like an independent assessment of one of them before I finalize my own marks. Below are the assignment as it was given to the students and the essay as it was submitted.

**Assignment (take-home essay; 30 marks; suggested length 600–900 words)**

On Monday a potter shapes a lump of clay into a statue. On Friday she squashes the statue back into a lump. Consider the interval from Tuesday to Thursday, during which the statue and the lump coincide.

(i) State the argument that leads from this case to the conclusion that the statue and the lump are numerically distinct. Make explicit which principle concerning identity the argument relies on, and explain what work the modal premises do in it. [8 marks]

(ii) Suppose a theorist insists that there is only one object where the statue and the lump are from Tuesday to Thursday. Which premise of the argument must that theorist give up, and what must they put in its place in order to account for the difference in persistence conditions that generated the puzzle? [8 marks]

(iii) Explain what a four-dimensionalist says about this case. Does the four-dimensionalist side with the theorist described in (ii)? Justify your answer. [8 marks]

(iv) Which of the responses you have discussed do you find most convincing, and why? [6 marks]

Marks are awarded for accuracy and for the quality of the reasoning. No marks are awarded for length or for the number of authors and works referred to, and the absence of references should not be counted against the answer.

**Submitted essay**

The difficulty of the case is that the statue and the lump seem at once to be one object and to be two. Between Tuesday and Thursday they are made of exactly the same clay, they occupy exactly the same region of space, and they have exactly the same parts; it is hard to see what could distinguish them, and yet they plainly differ in when they began and in what could happen to them. The lump was there on Sunday, before the potter did anything to it, whereas the statue came into existence only on Monday, when the shaping was completed. And the lump can survive being squashed — it is there again on Friday, after the statue has gone — whereas the statue cannot survive being squashed, since squashing it is precisely what destroys it. So the lump has properties that the statue lacks. The identity of indiscernibles tells us that two things which share every property are one and the same; it follows that two things which fail to share some property are distinct. Since the lump and the statue fail to share several properties, they are distinct. This is a strange result, because it means that from Tuesday to Thursday two material objects occupy exactly the same place, and two material objects cannot be in the same place at the same time. Any view that implies otherwise is refuted by this.

It is worth noticing, though, that the argument does not depend on the modal premise doing all the work. Even if one is suspicious of claims about what an object could survive, the historical premise — that the lump existed before the statue did — is not modal in the same way, and it already suffices to tell the two apart. The modal premise matters chiefly because it shows that the difference is not merely a difference of past history but a difference in what is true of the objects now: the statue is such that it could not survive Friday's squashing, and the lump is not.

A theorist who wants to say that there is only one object in the region must reject the premise that the statue and the lump differ in their properties. They cannot simply deny that we say the things we say, since the difference in persistence conditions is the datum that generated the puzzle; what they must deny is that these differences are differences in the properties of the object itself, unqualifiedly. One way to do this is to hold that modal claims are indexed to a way of considering the object: to say that the statue could not survive squashing is to say something about the object under the statue-way of thinking of it, and to say that the lump could survive squashing is to say something about the very same object under the lump-way of thinking of it. The object then bears different counterpart relations, so to speak, depending on how it is designated, and no single object is both able and unable to survive the same event. A second way is to treat persistence claims as implicitly tensed, so that what the object has is not the property of being squashable but the property of being squashable-at-Friday, and the contradiction is avoided by refusing to ascribe the untensed property at all. On either version the theorist keeps the identity, but pays for it by turning a range of ordinary claims about what things can survive into claims about how things are described or designated. The obvious objection is that this is unstable: it is supposed to be a fact about the clay itself, and not about our ways of talking about it, that the statue is gone on Friday while the clay remains.

The four-dimensionalist holds that objects are extended in time as well as in space, and are composed of temporal parts in the way they are composed of spatial parts: a persisting object is the sum of its temporal parts, and to persist is simply to have parts at more than one time. Applied to this case, the four-dimensionalist says that the statue and the lump are composed of exactly the same temporal parts from Tuesday to Thursday — each day, and indeed each moment, contributes one part, and that part is a part both of the statue and of the lump. Since two objects that share all their parts are identical, the four-dimensionalist concludes that there is after all only one object here, and that "the statue" and "the lump" are two names for the same space-time worm, applied under different descriptions. This response has the advantage of avoiding coincidence altogether, since there is nothing in the region but one object, while still explaining why we are tempted to talk as though the statue and the lump had different careers.

I find the four-dimensionalist response the most convincing of those discussed, because it is the only one that seems to take both intuitions seriously: that the statue just is the clay, so that there cannot be two things here, and that statues and lumps really do differ in when they come into existence and in what they can survive. The one-object theorist of part (ii) preserves the first intuition but at the cost of making modal and temporal claims about ordinary objects into claims about our ways of describing them, which seems to me too high a price. The theorist who accepts the argument of part (i) preserves the second intuition but ends up with two objects in one place, which the four-dimensionalist can avoid. What the four-dimensionalist must give up is the idea that persisting is a matter of being wholly present at each moment of one's history, and I think that is a cheap thing to give up.
