Two months ago somebody handed me the solar-system wall at the science center where I do education programming. Big painted thing from the late nineties, nine planets, Pluto at the end looking a little apologetic. We're redoing it and I got volunteered for the copy. I assumed the hard part would be fitting it in sixty words. The hard part turned out to be that our board is now split down the middle and both halves have asked me something I can't answer.

Our director says: put the official definition on the wall, that's what standards bodies are for, and a regional science center has no business running its own solar system. A board member, retired engineer, extremely confident, says the opposite — that the definition was decided by a show of hands and that presenting a vote to kids as though it were a discovery is precisely what teaches people to distrust science. I've come around to thinking neither of them is asking the right question, and that the question underneath is about whether sciences carve nature at its joints.

Facts, so you don't have to trust my recall of the details:

- In 2006 the International Astronomical Union's general assembly in Prague adopted a definition requiring that a planet orbit the Sun, be massive enough for self-gravity to pull it round, and have gravitationally cleared its orbital neighbourhood. Pluto fails the third.
- The assembly also established a dwarf planet class and treated it as distinct from planets rather than a subset of them; a proposal to make dwarf planets a subcategory of planet was put to a vote and defeated.
- The committee's draft, circulated days before the vote, had been roundness-only — any non-star, non-moon body orbiting a star — which would have made twelve planets including Ceres and Charon. One of the drafters said they'd picked gravity as the deciding criterion because nature decides. Then the floor rewrote it.
- Only a few hundred members were in the room and the main definition reportedly passed without the tally even being recorded.
- The New Horizons lead has argued ever since that roundness is the scientifically meaningful criterion and the clearing requirement is politics dressed as physics, and he got loud about it after the 2015 flyby found nitrogen ice plains and possible signs of a subsurface ocean.
- An earlier committee had floated three flavors of definition: cultural (a planet if enough people say so), structural (round enough), dynamical (dominates its orbit).
- Two things that broke my brain: the clearing criterion is location-dependent, so the identical rock is a planet here and not a planet if you park it out in the Oort cloud; and Mercury doesn't strictly satisfy the roundness criterion either, yet nobody proposes demoting it. Also the definition only covers our Sun, so exoplanets run on a separate mass-based working definition, meaning there are officially two kinds of planet.

A friend of mine who did a philosophy of science degree says this is the cleanest real-world natural kinds case there is, and she gave me four accounts to think with, roughly as I understood them: (1) essences — a kind is natural when its members share an underlying microstructure that explains their surface properties, gold being atomic number 79 and all that; (2) clusters — a kind is natural when a bundle of properties reliably co-occurs because some underlying mechanism keeps holding them together, no single essence required, her stock example being species; (3) promiscuity — there are many equally real cross-cutting ways to divide the same domain and which one counts as natural depends on your interests, so there's no one privileged carving; (4) a fourth she described too fast for me, something about a kind being natural when membership is unified by an explanation you could in principle dig down to and the cluster is causally deep enough to be worth explaining — call it the explanatory-payoff account. Her own coinage, which I like: separate the question of whether there's a joint out there to carve from the question of who has jurisdiction to pick which carving we adopt, and her claim is that most of the Pluto fight was jurisdiction wearing a joints costume.

So. Run the 2006 episode through those accounts one at a time and tell me what each one makes planet-hood out to be. Which account makes the IAU look like it was doing legitimate joint-carving, which makes it look like a committee installing a category, and which makes the whole argument confused because the two sides weren't even disagreeing about the same thing? Does the roundness-versus-orbital-dominance fight actually track a disagreement between accounts 1 and 2, or am I overreading? I'd also want you to test my own hunch, which is that planet might be a historical kind — a category held together by a shared formation history in the same disk rather than by a mechanism or an essence — and say whether that hunch is a fifth account or just account 2 with different wallpaper.

Then turn it around, this is the part I actually care about: what does the episode do to the accounts? Does it confirm one, is it an anomaly for one, is it a case one of them was never designed to handle? Specifically — if promiscuity is right, the fight was idle, and yet not one person in that hall thought it was idle, including the ones arguing it was just a naming decision. Is that a problem for promiscuity, or a problem for my read on the room? And use the location-dependence point and the Mercury point, they feel like real leverage somewhere. Please be explicit where the accounts go silent — I would much rather you say an account has nothing to say about the vote count than watch you make it say something.

No references or bibliography needed, I'm not writing a paper. But if you can, end with two sentences a wall label could honestly say that don't pick a side by pretending there isn't one.
