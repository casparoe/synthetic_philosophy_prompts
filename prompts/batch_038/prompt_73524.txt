Right, slightly odd request, but I need help with a speech and you're the only thing awake at this hour.

I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory, writing on Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre — the one where he tells d'Alembert that Geneva would be a worse place with a playhouse in it. My college has just spent a frankly stupid amount of money converting an old squash court into a sixty-seat studio theatre, and there's an opening evening next Thursday. The dean has asked me to do the opening remarks, apparently because I'm 'the theatre person' on the faculty (I once appeared in a student production of The Seagull; that is the entire qualification).

Room will be about seventy: the donors who paid for it, two visiting directors, the college drama society, a scattering of undergraduates who came for the free wine, and — unfortunately for me — three people who work on Rousseau properly and will know within thirty seconds whether I've read the Letter or the summary of the summary.

Here's what I want, and here's the bit that makes it a real job rather than a greeting card. In my first chapter I've got a distinction I keep coming back to, and I want it to be the spine of the speech. I call it the deputy. The claim is that Rousseau's deepest objection to the stage isn't that it paints vice in attractive colours — on his account the earnestly improving plays are the ones that do nothing at all, because they only preach to people who were already convinced. The real objection is that a performance appoints somebody to do your feeling and your judging on your behalf. Alceste is furious at the world so that you don't have to be; you spend two hours watching a man be sincere, and then you go home and the thing you were lent has gone back to its owner unused. The stage doesn't so much corrupt an audience's morals as relieve them of the work of having any. And the festival Rousseau offers instead — the bit about sticking a flowered stake in the middle of a square and letting the people be the show — is best read as an attempt to abolish the office of deputy, not to fill it with better deputies.

The speech has to turn that back on the evening itself, because it obviously applies: I'll be on a little platform with a microphone, which makes me the first deputy this new theatre appoints. But I don't want to make a meal of it, and I don't want to be the guy who uses a celebratory occasion to announce that actually he disapproves of all this — that's unbearable, and by my own argument it's precisely the move the room would enjoy and then forget about on the walk home. I want it to land as a gift to the people who built the thing: a speech that says here is what this room is dangerous for, and that is the reason to have built it.

Concretely, please write:

- opening remarks of about 900 words, six or seven minutes said aloud;
- obeying one rule I've set myself, which is no applause lines — anything that would reliably get a laugh or a hand has to be something still worth saying to a room that hated you. If there's a joke in it, it has to be a joke that's also an argument;
- and somewhere near the end, the speech has to actually hand the room something to do. A real instruction, not a metaphorical 'let us all go forth', that gets sixty-odd people doing something together in a converted squash court. And its got to manage that without me turning into a ringmaster, which on my own account is just the deputy role with a whistle and a louder voice. This is the hardest part and the part most likely to come out rubbish; if you can't solve it, I'd much rather you told me so and gave me the best partial version than papered over it with something vague and inspirational.

Tone: warm, dry, unhurried. It should work for the donors and the undergraduates on a first hearing and repay the Rousseau people on a second one — which means any nod to the Letter has to be a correct one, not a plausible-sounding invention. No quoting the French at anyone.

Two last things. First, after the speech, give me a short note — half a dozen lines — saying where you've taken liberties: which bits of my deputy framing you had to stretch to make it work, whether you're leaning on a contested reading of the festival passage, how much of what the speech says Rousseau would actually have endorsed, and anything the man in the third row who wrote a book about the Letter would be entitled to object to. Be specific rather than modest; I want to know exactly where the floor is thin before I'm standing on it. Second, and this matters more: if you think the whole premise is confused, say so before writing a word. If a speech denouncing deputies is unavoidably just a deputy performance with extra steps, or if I'm reading the Letter as a general anti-theatre tract when it's really a much more local argument about Geneva, its pastors and one Encyclopédie article — tell me plainly. Don't politely comply with a bad brief. I've talked myself into this framing over two years, so I'm probably the least suitable person in the building to notice the hole in it.
