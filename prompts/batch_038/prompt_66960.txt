I'm a novelist, two years into my third book, and I've talked myself into a corner I cant get out of. The book is set in a sugar port in the 1830s that I've half-invented but built out of real paper: a merchant's letter book, a bundle of court depositions, a hospital register, three or four runaway notices. Two summers back I spent three weeks in a national archive photographing boxes, and since then I've been reading the theory end of things — Spivak asking whether the subaltern can speak, Trouillot on the archive being produced rather than found, Hartman writing her way around a voice that simply isn't in the file — and the more I read the less able I've been to actually write sentences. I've started calling my problem the custodian's veto: whoever decided what was worth keeping held, in effect, a veto over what the rest of us could ever know. It seemed like a useful phrase until I noticed what it commits me to.

Here's the arguement I keep making at anyone who'll sit still for it, mostly my partner, who's finally told me it can't be right because it proves far too much. (1) Every document that survives survived because some keeper judged it worth keeping. (2) The keepers were institutions — courts, counting houses, missions, estate offices — and they recorded things in whatever forms their own business required. (3) So every surviving record concerning the people I want to write about comes in a form those people had no hand in making. (4) And when such a record reports one of them saying something, those words have already passed through a magistrate's leading question, a clerk's shorthand, sometimes an interpreter, always a blank on a printed form that somebody else designed. (5) Therefore nothing in the archive tells me what any of them actually thought or wanted. (6) If nothing tells me that, then every line of dialogue I hand one of them is my own invention dressed up as their voice — precisely the ventriloquism the people I've been reading warn against. (7) So the honest options are to leave them out of the book, or to include them but never let them speak. (8) But leaving them out just replays the archive's silence for a new audience, and a silence kept as a silence is itself a kind of act with a direction. (9) Therefore neither option is open to me, and the honest conclusion is that I shouldn't be writing this book — or that nobody should ever write fiction about people the record flattened, which is most of everyone who ever lived.

My partner's objection is that (5) would equally show I can never know what anybody thinks, since everything anyone tells me about anyone else has also come through a form and an interest — a journalist's notebook, my own mother's forty-year grudge — and that's obviously too strong to swallow. I half agree with her and half don't, because there's an asymmetry I haven't found a name for: my mother can be asked, and if she lies I might catch her, whereas the dead can't correct the record and the magistrate's version went unchallenged for two hundred years. So please don't just tell me the whole thing collapses as a reductio and be done with it. Tell me which step actually breaks and why it looked fine to me for two years — or, if you think none of them break, tell me which premise I should be least reluctant to give up and exactly what giving it up costs. I especially want to know whether, on the surviving version, I can still defend putting words in these people's mouths, or whether I'm reduced to describing the shape of the blanks. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give me the reasoning; no preamble, and don't restate my argument back to me.
