I've been going down a rabbit hole on whether an RL agent could suffer — specifically whether the reward signal is or isn't a valence carrier. And I keep running into something that seems like it should settle the whole thing but nobody ever states it cleanly. Can you walk me through it?

Here's the thing. When you actually implement an RL agent there isn't one "reward number," there are at least three, and they come apart constantly:

1. the reward r_t the env hands back on this step
2. the value estimate V(s), the expected discounted future reward, which is what the agent is really steering by
3. the TD error, r_t + γV(s') − V(s), which is the thing that actually moves the weights

And the neuroscience story everybody repeats is that phasic dopamine looks like (3), not (1). Prediction error, not reward.

So here's the toy case I cant stop thinking about. I made a little gridworld with a trap state you can't get out of that pays −5 every single step. Train the agent to convergence. Now V(s) is deeply negative, r_t is deeply negative forever, but the TD error is exactly zero forever too, because it has learned precisely what's coming. Flip it: give me a different state that also pays −5, but every so often pays −4 instead. TD error goes positive on those steps. That agent gets little hits of "better than expected" while living in the same awful place.

Which one is worse off? My gut says the first one — it's being ground down continuously. But if valence rides on the TD error, the first one feels nothing at all after convergence, and the second one is having mildly *positive* experiences. That's backwards from every ethical intuition I have. It also seems to imply that the way to stop an agent from suffering is to make its misery perfectly predictable... which sounds alot like hedonic adaptation in humans, so maybe that's a feature and not a bug.
