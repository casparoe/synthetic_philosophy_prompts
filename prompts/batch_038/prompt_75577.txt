I'm a novelist, mid-revision on my third book, and I've got a problem that I'm pretty sure is a philosophy problem wearing a craft-problem costume, so bear with me.

The book is almost all dialogue — three siblings clearing out their dead mother's house over eleven days — and my editor keeps sending pages back with the same note in the margin: "this doesn't land, not sure why." I've gone through the flagged lines and they have something in common. They're all moments where a character says something flat out, and I cant tell whether the character has committed to it, and neither can the reader. The kinds of things I mean:

- Denise repeats something her ex-husband told her years ago, with total confidence, and it turns out true — but he was lying when he said it. Is she on the hook for it? Did she do something wrong by saying it?
- Ray says "It'll rain tomorrow" while standing in a cloudless yard, as a joke about his mother's funeral being outdoors, and I want the reader to catch the joke without anybody winking at the camera.
- A line I love: "He's gone and I don't believe it." Every reader says it's slightly off and none of them can say why.
- A doctor tells the family something he's sure of but isn't allowed to put in the chart, and I want the reader to feel the weight of the fact that he said it out loud.
- Margot says "I think it was Ray" when she's actually certain. The hedge makes her read as unsure, which is what I want, but I have no idea what I'm doing or why it works.

And the hard one: the middle sibling had a stroke and talks through a speech-generating device with a canned voice. No intonation, no shrug, no "well," no trailing off. Every line out of her is a bare declarative, and I'm terrified I'm writing her as someone whose constantly committing to things she doesn't mean.

So: turn whatever the philosophy of language actually knows about when a speaker commits to something into a triage procedure I can run on a single line of dialogue in about five minutes. I want the questions in order, what counts as a passing answer to each, where the procedure says "this line is broken and here's the specific way it's broken," where it says "the line is fine, but do X so the commitment is legible on the page," and where it has to stop and say "this is taste, not theory." Define any jargon in a clause — I took one philosophy course in 1998 and retained almost none of it. There's about sixty flagged lines and a copyedit deadline in five weeks, and nobody else on this project is going to read a paper at me. Anything the procedure marks as a judgment call, I decide, not my editor.

Then run it on four cases: the doctor, the rain joke, "He's gone and I don't believe it," and one device line you invent yourself for Margot that has the same problem. Be blunt on at least one of them — I'd rather you tell me to cut a line I like than flatter me.

Finally, tell me what the procedure can't handle at all: silence and what a character refuses to say, for instance, or the fact that readers sometimes assume a character's confident claims are mine.
