I co-teach an upper-level seminar on moral responsibility and punishment — fourteen students, about half of them pre-law, and the registrar just cut our final from a three-hour sit-down to a 75-minute slot. That wrecks the old question, which was basically "compare the compatibilist and the free-will-skeptic accounts of just deserts," because at 75 minutes it becomes a labeling exercise and it stopped discriminating years ago. Last year's scripts were almost interchangeable: everyone wrote the same paragraph saying Dennett is a compatibilist so he lets punishment back in while Caruso is a skeptic so he doesn't, then everyone noted that Caruso seems more humane, then everyone went home. My co-teacher, who's a criminologist and not a philosopher, called the whole thing a pageant, and honestly that's fair.

So: write me ONE essay question on the Dennett–Caruso dispute about just deserts and punishment, worth 25 marks, sized for 75 minutes, that a student can't survive by naming the two positions. The thing I need them forced to see is that both of them give up basic desert — Caruso openly, Dennett too in anything like the cosmic, balance-the-scales sense — which means the actual disagreement is about what's left over: what constrains hard treatment once desert isn't doing the work, and whether a social practice of holding people responsible can play the role Dennett wants it to play without quietly re-importing desert through the back door. I want the question to hang on two cases I've been kicking around with friends. Call them Case One, the harmless wrongdoer: someone did something seriously bad, coldly and with full competence at the time, and twenty years later is a demonstrable zero risk to anyone, with no victim wanting anything further from them and nothing at all turning on their being detained or sanctioned. And Case Two, the dangerous innocent: someone who has never done anything wrong, but whose condition makes future serious violence highly likely and is not treatable, so that on Caruso's quarantine-style reasoning grounded in a right to self-protection, with proportionality set by risk and a least-restrictive-alternative constraint, detention is on the table — while on a desert view nothing is. The question should ask students to say what each theory licenses in each case, and then, and this is the crux, to name which of the two philosophers has the resources to treat Case Two as a cost rather than a bullet to bite, without giving up the principle that produced the verdict in the first place. I'd also like the question to make them confront the worry that Caruso's proportionality-by-risk and Dennett's competence-based practice end up licensing the same institutions, so that the debate looks merely verbal — and to say what, if anything, is actually substantive about it.

Deliverables: the question itself with the case material embedded and any instructions to the student spelled out; a model answer of about 700–800 words; a marking scheme built on six must-hit points whose marks sum to 25, with one line each on the most common way a student flubs that point; and then a short list of five or so B-minus tells — sentences that read as sophisticated but show the student missed the crux, e.g. treating Dennett as a retributivist, treating Caruso as opposed to all detention, or announcing that compatibilism and skepticism "don't really disagree" without saying what would count as a disagreement. No bibliography, no page cites, no named works: the students get nothing to bring in, and I don't want the model answer leaning on citations I can't check against. It should stand on argument alone. And if you think the question as I've described it has an easy out I haven't spotted, say so and tighten it rather than writing it as specified.
