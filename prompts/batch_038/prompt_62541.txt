so last night I got into a two hour argument with a friend of mine (econ PhD, extremely Nash-brained) about the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, and it turns out I'm hosting next week's episode with a game theorist who's spent a lot of their career arguing KS is the normatively right answer and Nash's is the wrong one. Our show is a small social choice / game theory podcast, maybe 300 listeners, mostly grad students and people who read this stuff for fun, so we can get technical but nobody can do math on a mic.

Here's what my friend threw at me that I couldn't answer:

- KS gives up independence of irrelevant alternatives to buy monotonicity, which means the answer depends on each player's best possible payoff, a point that usually isn't attainable by either of them. His line was that KS "gives bargaining power to a fantasy": an outcome nobody will ever actually consume still moves the split.
- Worse, since the whole feasible set matters, you can apparently improve your own share by tossing in an option that will never get chosen but that raises your maximum. He said that means the theory rewards people for inventing junk alternatives.
- He claimed every dynamic foundation he knows, alternating offers with some small chance the talks collapse, comes out Nash in the limit, and the only way to get KS out of a game like that is to assume that on breakdown a coin flip makes one of them a dictator who takes their ideal payoff. Which he says is smuggling the utopia point in through the back door.
- He also said KS and Nash always agree on symmetric problems, so any story about which one is really right only bites in asymmetric cases, and there you can't tell whether the asymmetry in outside options is doing the normative work or the axiom is.

Draft me seven to nine interview questions, each with a follow-up underneath keyed to the answer I think they're most likely to give (usually some version of "the feasible set is a real object, not a description you get to pick" or "monotonicity is obviously right and IIA is obviously wrong"). I want at least one question on each of: whether an unattainable ideal point can carry normative weight at all; the adding-junk-options manipulation worry; the foundations problem above; why equalize the fraction of your maximum gain rather than equal absolute gains, or gains measured against some entitlement, i.e. what is actually principled about normalizing by the utopia point; and whether the solution survives going past two players. Plus one question about what it would look like to actualy run this as a real arbitration procedure where you have to get the parties to reveal their ideal payoffs honestly.
