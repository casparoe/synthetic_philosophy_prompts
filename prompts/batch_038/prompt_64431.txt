I serve on the workload committee of our faculty senate. At last week's meeting the dean rejected our proposal to cap uncompensated summer supervision of undergraduate research with the remark, "Do what you love and you'll never work a day in your life," adding that people who find the work absorbing have no standing to complain about the hours. Several of us objected but could not articulate why, and I would rather return with an argument than with irritation.

Please set out, as a numbered menu I could hand to the committee, the distinct precise claims that sentence can carry: the descriptive-psychological claim that absorbed activity does not register as effort; the one that silently redefines work as whatever is experienced as toil and so becomes true by stipulation; the career-advice reading addressed to someone choosing an occupation; the normative reading on which subjective reward settles questions of distribution; and any others genuinely in play, including the reading on which the line functions to convert enthusiasm into unpaid labor. For each, say whether it is a platitude, an empirical hypothesis about motivation, a stipulation, or false — and whether it survives contact with the fact that those who work themselves into the ground are often precisely the people who love their work. Close by identifying which readings would actually license the dean's move from "the supervision is intrinsically rewarding" to "hours are the wrong currency for a workload policy," and which leave the work fully loved and the policy still unjust.
