Our school's Learning Support Panel meets next Tuesday and I hold the pupil seat on it; I am in Year 13 at a comprehensive in the North of England. The panel administers a small bursary fund and advises the head on what pupils may use during supervised study and internal examinations. Two applications are before it, both for the same item: a battery-powered headband, bought online for about a hundred and fifty pounds, that passes a weak current across the front of the skull and is advertised as improving concentration. Both applicants are in Year 12.

Application A is from a pupil diagnosed with ADHD who cannot tolerate her prescribed medication because of its side effects and is on a long waiting list to have her treatment reviewed. She says the headband is the only thing that has allowed her to complete homework this term, and she has a letter from her GP to that effect. She also states, candidly, that she intends to wear it in the national mathematics olympiad selection rounds, in which she is already the strongest candidate in the year.

Application B is from a pupil with no diagnosis of any kind, who wants it for revision. His written case is that his family cannot afford a private tutor, that the families of several of his rivals do pay for tutors, and that tutoring is plainly an improvement on whatever level our policy has in mind — so if the panel funds tutoring-adjacent things and refuses his, the line it is using cannot be doing the work it claims to do.

For context, the panel will have in mind that the school already funds spectacles, pays for extra time in public examinations where pupils are entitled to it, permits private tutoring at parents' expense, and takes no view on energy drinks consumed in the library.

The policy we work from provides funding for support that 'enables a pupil to function at the level reasonably expected of a pupil of their age and stage', and distinguishes treating a difficulty from improving a pupil beyond that level. It does not say who fixes that level, when it is measured, or what follows if it moves.

Please write the memo I will table at the meeting, in British English. It should: give a clear recommendation on each application — grant, refuse, or grant with conditions, and if conditions, ones a sixth-former could actually meet and a panel could actually monitor; set out the reasoning in terms the other members, who are four teachers and two parents, can follow; state what criterion the panel should apply to future applications, and whether the treating-versus-improving line can bear the weight our policy places on it or should be replaced by something else; and address two awkward features head-on, namely that one pupil seeks the same device for a therapeutic purpose and for a competitive one, and that the 'expected level' in our policy is a moving target, since if a third of the year starts wearing headbands the average shifts and last year's improvement becomes this year's restoration to normal.

I do not want the memo to rest on the thinness of the evidence for these devices or on our ignorance of their effects on adolescent brains; you may note both in passing, but the panel will reach for that argument because it decides nothing, and I want to know whether the line itself survives. Nor do I want philosophers' names, a reading list, or references — the argument has to stand on its own. Please treat Application B's complaint seriously: if his reasoning is sound, say so, and say what then follows for Application A, rather than finding a way to dismiss him. End with a short section stating the strongest consideration against whatever you recommend, put in the voice of the panel's most sceptical member, and do not knock it down afterwards. Around 900 words, with headings.
