I sit in a weekly reading group on free will and moral responsibility with a colleague — call her Dana — who is remarkably consistent and remarkably unforthcoming. She will not state a principle; she only delivers verdicts, each with a one-line remark attached. Below is her full record for the term. Every case is a variant on the same template: Jones is going to kill or steal, a counterfactual intervener may or may not be present, and the question is always whether Jones is morally responsible for what he does.

1. Black, a neurosurgeon, will secretly stimulate Jones's brain if a certain involuntary neural event occurs — an event that reliably precedes Jones's forming an intention to refrain. The event does not occur. Jones deliberates for a day and shoots his business partner for the insurance money. **Verdict: fully responsible.** Remark: "The device never fired. As far as the story goes, it is a piece of furniture."

2. The same case, except that the monitored event is not involuntary: Black's device is triggered by Jones's raising his left hand, and Jones, had he begun to reconsider, would have raised it as a signal to a confederate. Jones never raises it. **Verdict: fully responsible.** Remark: "That is a different case from the first one, though I couldn't tell you how much the difference is worth."

3. Before Jones ever deliberates, Black installs a psychology in him on which the suffering of other people registers as no consideration at all; the device is then switched off and does nothing. Jones, on that psychology, shoots his partner, and would have shot him whatever the reasons. **Verdict: not responsible.** Remark: "That psychology was written by somebody else."

4. No device. A team of biologists designs the zygote and arranges the environment so that, thirty years later, Jones deliberates in a perfectly ordinary and unimpaired way and embezzles from his employer. **Verdict: not responsible.** Remark: "He runs the script well. He didn't write it."

5. No device, no designer. Jones grows up normally, deliberates for a week, sees clearly that killing his partner would be wrong, would have refrained had the victim been his own child, and shoots anyway for the money. **Verdict: fully responsible.** Remark: "He saw exactly what he was doing, and did it."

6. A credible threat against Jones's daughter; Jones embezzles in order to satisfy it. He could have refused, at terrible cost, and he endorses his choice — he says he would make it again. **Verdict: responsible, but excused.** Remark: "It does seem to matter here that he could have refused. I'm not sure what work that fact is doing."

7. No device. Jones has a pathological startle response: when the victim reaches into his coat, Jones cannot but shoot. He is appalled by what he is doing as he does it, and he recognizes fully that it is wrong. **Verdict: not responsible.** Remark: "Nothing in there is his."

Pressed on whether the world of any of these cases is deterministic, she refuses to answer, saying that it isn't what she is thinking about.

Next week we take up this case. Black's device does not wait for a sign of any kind. It reads Jones's neural activity continuously and would take over the instant that activity departs in any respect from the single trajectory that ends in Jones's shooting — including departures that are not actions of Jones's at all: a stray association, an unsought image of the victim's face. The device never activates, and Jones does not know it exists. Nothing has ever been installed in him; his history is Case 5's history, and nobody designed him. But Jones has, from birth and by no one's doing, been unable to be moved by any moral consideration whatever. He knows the propositions of morality the way one knows the rules of a game he does not play: he agrees that shooting his partner would be wrong, and nothing follows from that agreement. He is perfectly responsive to other reasons — he would have refrained for less money or for greater risk. He deliberates for a week and shoots for the money. Note what I think this case is: Case 3's psychology with the designer subtracted, inside Case 1's machinery with the trigger sign subtracted.

I would like the following.

(a) State every rule you can find that is consistent with all seven verdicts — not as labels for named positions but as explicit conditions that a case description could satisfy or fail. I expect at least three distinct families: rules about what the actual sequence must contain, rules about whether some alternative remains available to the agent at some grain or other, and rules about the agent's own stance toward the springs of his action. Add others if the record supports them. For each rule, go through the seven verdicts one by one and say which feature of each case the rule is using; flag any verdict the rule accommodates only by reading Dana charitably, and say exactly what the charity consists in.

(b) Give each surviving rule's verdict on the new case. If two rules land on the same verdict, say whether they get there by the same route, and describe what Dana would have to *say* — not what she would have to decide — for you to know which route she took.

(c) Give me your prediction and a probability, and then say which of Dana's seven remarks carries the most weight for you and which carries the least. In particular: her remark in case 2 marks a difference between two cases that, on some of your candidate rules, are identical in every respect that matters. Either explain that remark away or say what it costs you.

(d) Propose two further cases. The first must separate your leading rule from its closest rival; the second must settle whether her remark in case 6 — that it matters that Jones could have refused — is doing any work in her thinking at all. For each of these two cases, state what every surviving rule predicts.

(e) Say whether the record is stable. If some pair of verdicts cannot be absorbed by any single rule, identify the tension and the minimal revision that would resolve it, and say which verdict you would expect Dana to give up.

Work only from what is above; I want no citations, no reading lists, and no bibliography, and if a rule has a standard name, one clause will do for the name. The analysis is what I am after.
