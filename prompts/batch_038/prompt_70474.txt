I am in my final year of secondary school and am writing a paper for my philosophy seminar on the Warring States dispute about human nature. Nearly everything I have read presents the two positions as temperaments: Mencius the optimist, who thinks we are born with the sprouts of virtue, and Xunzi the pessimist, who thinks our nature is raw material and that goodness is imposed from outside by teachers, models, ritual, and accumulated practice. My teacher says that framing is lazy, that the real disagreement is structural — a disagreement about where reliable moral responsiveness comes from — and that if I want to demonstrate the structural difference rather than assert it, I need a case that cuts against the temperaments instead of confirming them.

So please construct a thought experiment in which the two accounts generate predictions that run in the opposite direction from the stereotype: a case in which the Mencian account of the sprouts predicts that an ordinary, decent person will fail to do what morality requires, while the Xunzian account of cultivated disposition predicts that a person like them will succeed.

Constraints:

- One paragraph for the scenario itself. Ordinary contemporary life — a workplace, a school, a clinic, a household. No wars, no burning buildings, no trolley-style abstractions, and no hypnosis, pharmacology, or anything supernatural.
- Everyone in it is ordinary and sympathetic: no saints, no sociopaths, no one with an exotic upbringing.
- Choose either one person in two closely matched situations or two people matched in every relevant respect but one, and state explicitly which variable you are varying and which you are holding fixed.
- The right action must be one that spontaneous fellow-feeling has difficulty being triggered by — the harm should be invisible, delayed, diffuse, or borne by people who are not in the room — while still being a genuine moral requirement rather than a mere procedural rule.
- The success may not be explained by fear of punishment, ambition, or peer pressure. The person who acts well must be acting well, not merely prudently.
- Neither verdict may be stipulated. The scenario must not smuggle in character descriptions or training histories that simply assign the outcome; both predictions have to be derivable from the theories.
- Do not retell either thinker's own stock illustrations — the child at the well, the king who spared the ox, the crooked wood steamed straight against a frame, the hillside whose trees have been cut and grazed away. Do not quote the texts or attribute specific claims to specific passages; work from the general architecture of each view and say plainly when an idea is your reconstruction.
- No bibliography or reading list.

After the scenario, please do the following. (1) State each view's prediction and give the reasoning that produces it, so I can see the verdicts being generated rather than assumed. (2) Name the single feature of the case that is doing the work. (3) Say what happens to both predictions if that feature is removed or weakened. (4) Give the strongest Mencian objection — the one that denies this is a genuine counterexample, for instance by arguing that a capacity to be shaped by training and to take satisfaction in the result is itself sprout-like — and say whether pressing that objection costs the Mencian anything, such as making the position unfalsifiable. (5) Tell me honestly whether the case exhibits a structural difference or only a difference of emphasis, and whether a Xunzian could simply accept the whole thing without conceding anything.
