I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group that's spent the last month on pain, and specifically on those cases where people report that they still feel their pain, can say where it is and how intense it is, but insist it doesn't bother them — the asymbolia stuff from insular damage and from old cingulotomies, and the argument that this is supposed to show the badness of pain lives in the affect rather than in the sensation itself. Half the group found that argument way too quick, half found it devastating, and we agreed to settle it by each bringing something funny to the last meeting that's also philosophically load-bearing, i.e. you couldnt tell the joke if you didn't know the material. I'm doing a product launch.

So: write me a parody direct-to-consumer launch page for a biotech startup selling elective pain asymbolia — a pill or a wearable, your call, and please invent the company and product names rather than using a real drug or a real researcher. Structure it as a hero blurb with a handful of bullet-pointed selling claims, a quote from the founder, a short FAQ, and then a customer reviews section with star ratings, roughly 700 words total. Voice should be that deadpan-but-breathless wellness-tech register, the kind of copy that keeps saying it isn't taking anything away from you, and it should stay in character start to finish — no winking, no authorial nudge, no footnotes or references or suggested reading, because I'll be reading it out loud.

The humor has to come from the startup being completely honest about the distinction it's selling — you keep the location, you keep the intensity rating, you lose the minding — and from what turns out to follow. Concretely: at least one review should cheerfully reveal that avoidance learning went out with the unpleasantness (someone's serious burn, described in a good mood); the FAQ should get dragged into the question of whether a customer who no longer minds their pain has actually been relieved of a harm or only of the part of pain that made it count, without ever naming that question; one hostile review should press the thing the company least wants discussed, which is whether the badness of pain just was the unpleasantness or whether the unpleasantness was tracking something bad that is still sitting there unbothered; and there should be a bit about what happens to your concern for other people's pain once your own stops bothering you. In the FAQ, work in the two rival pictures — pain as a command to protect a body part versus pain as an experience that represents itself as bad — as competing marketing claims our company is snotty about, not as a mini-lecture. The target is optimization culture treating suffering like a UX bug, plus the philosophical eagerness to conclude pain isn't really bad for you once the suffering is switched off; neither target should ever be stated outright.
