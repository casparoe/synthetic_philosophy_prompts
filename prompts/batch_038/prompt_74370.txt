so I'm TA-ing an undergrad seminar on collective decision-making and the week on judgment aggregation is where the room dies every single year — they get the Condorcet paradox fine, but the second you say "now imagine we're aggregating beliefs instead of preferences" they check out. Instead of another worksheet I want to hand them something funny. Could you write me a parody of an appellate court opinion — full apparatus please: a headnote/syllabus up top, the procedural history, an "Opinion of the Court," a concurrence that claims to speak for the court and does not, and a dissent — for an invented three-judge panel in an invented circuit hearing an invented appeal.

The case should be a bog-standard breach-of-contract or negligence matter where liability turns on two or three separate premises (duty, act, causation — your pick), each of which the panel votes on individually, and the structure should be the classic one: a 2–1 majority FOR each premise, but a 2–1 majority AGAINST liability on the bottom line, because the judge who dissents on premise one isn't the judge who dissents on premise two. The joke is that the court then has to publish something and discovers it can't — what it writes down depends on whether it aggregated issue-by-issue or on the outcome, and those two procedures point at opposite winners. I want the court to take this utterly seriously and bureaucratically: it commisions an internal report on which procedure it has been using, discovers it has been two different courts at two different times of day, starts to worry that the majority-on-the-premises is a group that exists only for the length of the vote and believes something none of its members believe, and maybe gets an amicus brief from a startup selling a proprietary aggregation protcol with a monthly fee. Footnotes should include the clerk's voting matrix with something annotated on it in pencil, and at least one judge quietly realizing that whoever controls the order of the votes controls the judgment.

Around 900 words maybe? The target is the practice itself — the fiction that a collegial court has one mind and one holding — not any real court or real judge, so invent all the names, the case, and the citation format, and please don't append a reading list or cite actual papers; the whole point is that the ideas show up in the prose rather than in a bibliography. It should be readable to a smart second-year who's never heard the phrase discursive dilemma, but someone who knows the List–Pettit impossibility material should be able to tell you got the logic exactly right, including that there's no clean fix and that the dissent is not obviously wrong. Deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader — and if you can land one genuinely sad beat near the end, the losing party never learning that the court that beat them was a majority that never existed, that'd be perfect.
