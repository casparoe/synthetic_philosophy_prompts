I'm one of seven members of a commission reporting on how executions should be carried out in a jurisdiction that still carries them out. Whether they should be is off our terms of reference — parliament has asked us only about the how. Two proposals go to a vote on Friday and we're split roughly down the middle.

Proposal A is an immediacy protocol: execution within 72 hours of the last appeal being dismissed, with the date and hour not disclosed in advance to the prisoner or their family. The sponsors say almost all of the suffering in the current system sits in the interval — the years, the reprieves, the rescheduled mornings, the hope — so collapsing it is the largest harm reduction a body like ours can actually deliver.

Proposal B is a transparency protocol: every execution filmed, two press witnesses in the room, the recording released publicly after ten years. Their case is that a penalty nobody can picture is a fiction, and that secrecy is what allows the practice to drift.

I've been back through Camus's essay on the guilottine twice this week and I think it does real damage to A, but I can't get the argument tight. Draft the memo I'll circulate: a definite call on each proposal — endorse, reject, or endorse with amendments actually written out so they could drop straight into the report — the reasoning behind it, and a serious account of the best thing the losing side can say and what conceding it would cost us. Camus has to be load-bearing rather than decoration, but two members have never read him, so the reasoning needs to stand on its own feet too. Worth bearing in mind that he wasn't all-or-nothing about this: he floated interim measures of his own, which is precisely why A looks to some of us like it could be squared with him. Say whether it can.

Please don't hand me a balanced paper. One bottom line I'd be prepared to sign, and then a short note on which step in your reasoning the sharpest defender of A would go for first, and whether you think they'd win.
