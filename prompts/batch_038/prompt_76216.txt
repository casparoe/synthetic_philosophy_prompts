**The question as set (700 words; 25 percent of the course grade)**

> Aristotle distinguishes household management from money-making in Book I of the *Politics*, and offers an account of the origin and function of money both there and in *Nicomachean Ethics* V.5. (a) Set out Aristotle's account of why money came into existence and what work it does once it exists. (b) Explain whether his condemnation of money-lending rests on the same ground as his condemnation of unlimited acquisition more generally. (c) Does Aristotle's account of money commit him to the view that all exchangeable goods are strictly commensurable with one another? Give reasons.

**The student's submission**

> Aristotle's account begins in the first book of the *Politics* with a division of the art of acquisition. Household management is the art of providing and storing those things that are necessary and useful for life; it is natural, because it serves the ends of the household and of the polis, and it has a limit, since the goods required for a good life are finite. Money-making, by contrast, aims at the accumulation of wealth without limit. Its practitioners take wealth to consist in a quantity of money, and because money can always be increased they suppose that the end of their activity admits of no boundary. It is this unlimitedness that makes the art unnatural.
>
> Money itself grows out of the natural development of exchange rather than out of any formal decision. As the story is told in the *Politics* and again in the *Ethics*, the earliest communities were self-sufficient and had little occasion to exchange; as the community grew, some members had a surplus of what others lacked, so barter arose, wine for grain and the like. When exchange reached beyond the neighbourhood, and goods had to be imported and exported, the difficulty of transporting bulky commodities led people to agree on giving and receiving some material that was itself useful in daily life and easy to carry, such as iron or silver. At first its quantity was settled by size and weight; later a stamp was added so that the measuring need not be repeated each time. Money then does three things: it measures goods against one another, it mediates exchange, and it stores value across time.
>
> In the *Ethics* Aristotle adds that money is not finally the measure of exchange but a stand-in for the thing that really measures it, namely need. If people did not need one another's products there would be no exchange at all, and it is need that holds the city together. Money is therefore conventional: its very name points to law or custom, and Aristotle says that it exists by agreement and not by nature, and that it is in our power to change it and to make it useless. Since its value is wholly a matter of agreement and it has no worth of its own, Aristotle's theory anticipates the modern account of fiat money — it is the stamp, not the metal, that makes the coin. The metal underneath is thus philosophically irrelevant, and Aristotle would regard paper notes and credit balances as an improvement on coinage, because they drop the pretence that money is itself a real good.
>
> The same conventionalism explains his condemnation of the money-making arts. Retail trade and money-lending are objectionable for one and the same reason: in each the trader takes more than he gives from someone whose need has made him vulnerable, so that the gain of one party is the loss of the other. Usury is the worst instance, and Aristotle calls it the most unnatural mode of acquisition, because the lender charges for the use of a thing that is not consumed but merely handed over, so the borrower pays for a benefit he never really receives. Both condemnations therefore rest on a single ground, which is profit taken from another's need. This is what makes Aristotle an early theorist of exploitation, and it is why his argument reappears in the medieval doctrine of the just price and afterwards in the labour theory of value: exchange must be governed by the equality of what the parties give and receive, measured by what each has contributed.
>
> Finally, Aristotle's claim that money makes goods commensurable does commit him to a single measure of value underlying all exchange. He concedes that in strictness things so different as a house and a pair of shoes cannot be commensurate, but he treats that as a practical difficulty rather than a conceptual one: if money can measure them at all, there must be some one quantity present in both. He never identifies it explicitly, but his account requires that it exists.
