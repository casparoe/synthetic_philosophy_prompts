I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have been invited to deliver the opening talk at a two-day interdisciplinary conference on religion and everyday morality. The room will be about forty people: mostly quantitative sociologists of religion, three or four anthropologists who work on ritual and collective practice, two moral psychologists whose careers were made and then remade during the replication troubles around prosociality priming, one economist who studies social capital and institutional trust, a handful of graduate students, and several theologians and clergy who read my column and have so far been gracious about it. Essentially no philosophers.

The talk defends a position I have been building in print for about a year. Let me sketch it, so the objections have a target.

First, I argue that what the survey and field literature actually licenses is not that religious belief makes people more moral, but that congregational embeddedness produces measurable cooperation among co-members — and that the effect sits overwhelmingly in attendance, small-group participation, and having one's conduct observed by people one will see again, rather than in orthodoxy, self-reported importance of religion, or any particular doctrine about a watching deity. I call this the scaffolding claim: the moral output comes from the schedule, the density, the mutual visibility, and the enforceability of local norms, not from the metaphysics that happens to be attached to them.

Second, I argue the residual is small and lopsided. Once selection is handled — the people who show up weekly were never a random draw — and once social-desirability inflation in self-reported giving and volunteering is discounted, what remains is a modest premium on helping, giving, and norm compliance directed at people already inside the congregation's orbit, with a near-zero or negative sign toward those outside it.

Third, I draw the consequence I most want the room to argue with: the live question is not whether secular societies can be moral, but whether they can construct scaffolding without metaphysics. My claim is that the highly secular societies that nonetheless post very high generalized trust did not escape the need for scaffolding; they retained dense intermediate institutions — mass-membership unions, disciplined parties, adult-education associations, conscription, state churches doing civic work at scale — that performed the scheduling and mutual-visibility function, and that where those have thinned, trust erodes among the religious and the secular together, which suggests belief was never the operative variable.

I should add one deliberate weakness of my own: I bracket the question of what "more moral" means and argue almost entirely about behavior, partly because that is where the data live and partly because I think the conceptual question gets used to stall the empirical one. I expect that move to draw fire and I would rather have the fire specified in advance than improvised at the podium.

Please prepare a Q&A sheet for me, not a defense of the thesis. Specifically:

- The eight to ten objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if it were left standing at the end of the session — not ordered by topic or by ease of reply.
- Each objection phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it, in that person's own register: the sociologist's vocabulary of measurement error, attenuation, and endogenous selection; the anthropologist's insistence that a schedule without the sacred is not a ritual and that scaffolding detached from belief may simply not be scaffolding; the moral psychologists' suspicion of any argument that quietly leans on effects that failed to replicate; the economist's demand for identification and for a reason the Scandinavian case is not confounded by homogeneity and by the welfare state itself; the theologian's objection that reducing a faith to a coordination technology changes the subject rather than explaining it.
- For each, one sentence on what the objection is really getting at, then a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds without notes, then an honest flag on where that reply is soft — what I would have to concede, or where my evidence is thinner than my prose.
- Mark which of these are objections any thoughtful lay audience would raise anyway, and which are artifacts of this particular room, because I want to know which ones I can safely under-prepare.
- Tell me separately what I should concede in the first five minutes to buy credibility, and identify the single reply that has to land for the whole argument to survive the weekend.
- And name the question I am hoping nobody asks: the one where I do not currently have an answer, with a sentence on why it is genuinely hard rather than merely uncomfortable.

Do not give me a reading list, citations, or author-and-year attributions; I have my own notes and I want the reasoning, the vocabulary, and the tactical judgment. Where an objection depends on an empirical matter that is genuinely contested, say so plainly and give me the two rival readings of the evidence rather than picking one for me.
