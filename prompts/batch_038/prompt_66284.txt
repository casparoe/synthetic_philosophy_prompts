Our population ethics group has been chewing on the repugnant conclusion for a month and I built an argument that I'm now pretty sure proves far too much. I can't locate the flaw.

Setup. Welfare is a number, 0 is a life exactly not worth living, anything above it is worth living. "Better than" means better all things considered, not better for somebody.

(1) Add: if B is just like A except that B also contains some extra people with positive welfare, and nothing about anyone already in A changes at all, then B is better than A.
(2) Trade: if B is just like A except that it contains vastly more people, everyone in B sits a little below the best-off level in A and above the worst-off level in A, and the total welfare is much larger, then B is better than A.
(3) Extend: if B is just like A except that one person's life has been lengthened by a stretch of experience that is positive, and nothing about the earlier stretch of that life changes, then B is better than A.
(4) Each of these can be applied again and again, and better-than is transitive.

Run it. Start with A: ten billion people each living eighty years at a level of welfare I'd trade my entire life for. Alternate (1) and (2) a few hundred times and the top end keeps coming down while the headcount keeps climbing, until you land on Z — a googol people, each with a life only barely worth living, the felt quality of a contented half-asleep afternoon, forever. Z is better than A. That's the repugnant conclusion, and honestly I'm close to just biting that bullet.

The trouble is (3), run on its own with no extra people and no trades. Take Mia, who gets her eighty wonderful years, and append 10^30 more years in which every moment is barely worth continuing. Each appending is an improvement by (3), so by (4) Mia's drab eternity beats her eighty years. And the one-year version of the same step says that as long as experience stays marginally positive we should keep people on ventilators indefinitely, which nobody in my group will accept, me included.

I tried to block the Mia version by saying a life isn't just the sum of it's moments, that what a life is worth depends on its shape, how it goes as a whole. My friend said: fine, but that blocks (1) too, because the only difference between the two cases is where the parts sit, inside one subject or spread across many, and (1) already grants that where they sit doesn't matter, since adding a person leaves everyone else's life exactly as it was. His view is that I get both or neither.

So — which step do I give up, and what do I pay for giving it up? I'd much rather keep Z than accept the Mia result, but I want to know whether that option is actually on the table, or whether my friend is right that they stand and fall together.
