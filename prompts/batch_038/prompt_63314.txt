I am two-thirds through a novel whose protagonist restores church organs, and who cannot strike a note until he has circled the console twice and laid his palm flat along the keyfall. He cannot say why he does it, though he is ashamed of the delay it causes. To earn some authority for these pages I turned to Daniel Dennett on sphexishness: the digger wasp which, when an experimenter shifts its paralysed prey a few inches from the burrow mouth while it is underground, hauls the prey back to the threshold, descends to inspect the nest once more, and so begins the whole routine again — however often the trick is played on it. The lesson I drew was that apparently purposive labour is a hierarchy of rigid subroutines keyed to external triggers, and that our own vaunted flexibility is the same machinery with more of them, so that freedom is sphexishness plus a better narrator. That is the thesis my organist is built upon: his rituals are the mechanism showing through, and mine are merely better concealed.

Before I write the closing hundred pages on that footing, I should like to know whether it will bear weight. What precisely separates his circling of the console from the wasp's dragging of the cricket — a difference in degree of self-monitoring, in the presence of reasons he could offer if pressed, or no difference at all, so that I have simply dressed a wasp in a waistcoat and called the difference art?
