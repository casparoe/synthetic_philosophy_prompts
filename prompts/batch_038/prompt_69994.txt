I am a junior at a public high school in the American Midwest, and for two years I have organized a student-run seminar in comparative government that meets weekly during our activity period. This spring our unit is on the European Union, and the session I need help building is the one on sovereignty, the democratic deficit, and ever-closer union. I would like a complete, runnable simulation plus debrief.

Our constraints: 18 students aged 16 to 18, one 55-minute period, a standard classroom with movable desks, no budget, and no technology beyond a whiteboard. All student-facing material must fit on two printable pages. Roughly a third of the group are debate students who will monopolize any open discussion, and another third rarely speak unless a role gives them a defined thing to say, so participation has to be distributed by structure rather than by volunteering. Nothing may be assigned as homework or require prior reading. The phrase "democratic deficit" must not appear anywhere in student-facing material until the debrief, and I want the group to reach the diagnosis on their own before anyone names it.

I would like the simulation to use a fictional union rather than the EU itself, so that the comparison does the analytical work in the debrief instead of turning the session into a trivia contest. Please build from the following setup; you may adjust figures for balance, but keep the structural features intact.

The Compact of Vess has five member states: Bremme, 22 million people and the largest economy; Ostvale, 14 million, which hosts the Compact's capital; Kelvar, 9 million, heavily dependent on Compact farm subsidies; Tirn, 5 million, upstream on the shared river; and Sabley, 2.4 million, downstream and the poorest. Total population 52.4 million.

Institutions: (1) A Secretariat, whose members are appointed by the Council and confirmed by the Assembly for a single nine-year term, holds the exclusive right to propose measures, controls the drafting, and may withdraw any proposal at any moment. (2) An Assembly of 60 members elected every five years from one Compact-wide closed list, so no member has a district; it may amend or reject by an absolute majority of all 60 seats, but it may never introduce a measure of its own. (3) A Council in which each state sends one delegate bound by a written instruction from their national parliament; passage requires three states representing at least 65 percent of Compact population. (4) Taxation, borders, and defense remain reserved and require unanimity. (5) The Ratchet: any policy area in which the Compact passes a measure moves from shared to exclusive competence permanently and can never be handed back to the states. (6) A Compact budget of 100 units, contributed pro rata by population, with an 8-unit rebate each for Bremme and Ostvale.

The dilemma: a five-year drought. Before the Secretariat sit two measures. Measure A establishes a shared hydrological registry giving the Secretariat real-time metering data from every river, aquifer, and reservoir in the Compact — cheap, technically uncontroversial, broadly wanted, and it triggers the Ratchet on water policy. Measure B funds a 60-unit pipeline and reservoir from Tirn's mountains through Sabley, flooding two Sabley villages and cutting Kelvar's irrigation allocation by a fifth. Please verify that both a passage coalition and a blocking coalition exist for each measure under the 65-percent rule, and tell me if either measure has a degenerate outcome.

Role distribution should be: five Council delegations of two (a delegate and an adviser who physically holds the instruction card), three Secretariat officials, and five Assembly caucus chairs, each holding a paper bloc whose seat totals and preferences are printed on the sheet. The Assembly caucus chairs must be able to claim they speak for the people of the Compact while being unable to point to a single voter they represent, and their seat shares should come from a Compact-wide election with 38 percent turnout. At around minute 30, hand two advisers a one-paragraph domestic news bulletin that undercuts their instruction — at minimum, make Kelvar's instruction stale, written before the drought worsened.

What I need from you:

1. A minute-by-minute timeline for the 55 minutes, including what the facilitator says and does at each transition, plus a contingency for the round ending early or dissolving into a shouting match.
2. The two printable pages: a one-page rule sheet and a one-page set of role briefs, each brief around 120 words, each with a private interest that is not shared with the room and a non-monetary success condition.
3. A debrief built as an ordered sequence of questions that leads students from what just happened in the room to the underlying problem, with the label "democratic deficit" introduced only at the end.
4. A mapping from each invented rule to the real feature of the European Union it stands in for, the standard criticism that feature attracts, and the strongest serious defense of it — including the argument that the deficit is overstated because the Union mostly regulates and legitimacy reaches it through national parliaments and governments. If you are unsure of a specific institutional detail in the real Union, describe it functionally rather than naming a treaty article, a case, or a year.
5. A frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with teenagers, and how to handle each failure.
6. An honest statement of what the simulation cannot reproduce — for instance, decades of accumulated integration, the absence of a Europe-wide media sphere and public opinion, the fact that real Members of the European Parliament do have constituencies and transnational party groups, and the fact that citizens of member states largely accept outcomes they did not vote for — and what I, as facilitator, must supply by hand to cover those gaps.

Please make everything self-contained: no bibliography, no reading list, no references I would have to look up, and no citations.
