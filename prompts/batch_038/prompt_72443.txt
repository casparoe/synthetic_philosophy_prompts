Third-year philosophy PhD here, supposed to be turning my dissertation proposal in next month on decision theory under imperfect information, and instead I've spent the last week unable to think about anything else. My advisor is on sabbatical in Lisbon, my reading group has split into two camps over an arguement I sketched on the board, and I can't tell which camp is right. Half of them think it's a clean refutation of updateless decision theory and I should write it up; the other half think it proves too much and therefore proves nothing. I'd rather hear from someone with no stake in either camp.

Some setup so the argument is assessable on its own terms. Updateless decision theory, as I understand it, says that when you're standing at a choice node you shouldn't re-decide on the basis of what you've since observed. You should instead have picked, before you had any of that evidence, the policy — the whole function from observations to actions — that maximizes expected utility as assessed from your prior, and now you just execute it, even if your current credences point the other way. The motivation is Newcomb-style cases where your observation is tangled up with your own act, plus cases like the absent-minded driver and Sleeping Beauty where what you learn is only which copy or which day you are. I find all that appealing. Here's what I built against it anyway:

(1) UDT: what it's rational to do at a choice node is to carry out the policy that, evaluated from your prior before you had any of the evidence you now have, maximizes expected utility.
(2) What a policy prescribes at a node is settled once, at selection; later evidence can't rewrite it.
(3) So at the node, UDT tells you to do what your prior would have had you do, no matter what you've since learned.
(4) Sometimes what you've since learned changes the expected utilities outright — say a reliable technician walks in and tells you the coin you're betting on already came up heads.
(5) In those cases UDT tells you to bet as though you'd never heard, i.e. to act against your own current assessment of the odds.
(6) Any theory that ever tells an agent to act against her own current assessment of the odds isn't a theory of rational choice.
(7) So UDT isn't a theory of rational choice.
(8) The standard repair — of course the policy is a function of observations, so it can say "if you hear heads, bet heads" — doesn't save it. Either the prior gave that news positive probability, in which case UDT's verdict at the node is just what an ordinary conditionalizing agent reaches there, so UDT stops disagreeing with the standard view in any case where it was supposed to, contradicting what its fans say about Newcomb; or the prior gave it probability zero, in which case the policy is silent about exactly the surprising evidence that made you reach for a decision theory in the first place.
(9) So UDT is either irrational or empty.

My friend Mira says the whole thing is universal acid and that I should be embarassed. Her objection is to (6), not from UDT's side: if a theory is disqualified for ever telling you to act against your current assessment of the odds, then every form of self-binding is disqualified too. I precommit in September not to check my phone during writing hours; every October morning my current assessment is that five minutes costs nothing and the paper would benefit from the citation. Odysseus at the mast is the textbook version. And a causal decision theorist who pays to precommit before Newcomb — which everyone, including me, thinks is the rational move if you can buy it — then acts against her own assessment at the node. So (6) refutes precommitment as such, precommitment is sometimes plainly rational, therefore (6) is false and my argument is worthless. She won't say which of (2) through (5) actually breaks, just that something is being slid around and she isn't doing my work for me.
