We're doing 12 Angry Men at my local rationalist meetup's movie night in a couple weeks. About fifteen people, mostly the usual crowd who have all read the Brennan excerpts and think they're done with this topic. I want to hand out something short beforehand that reads the film against the epistocracy-versus-democracy debate properly, and I'd rather you write it than me, because every time I try I end up making the same three moves everyone else makes.

So: read the film as a test case for epistocracy, not as an illustration of it. Assume we already know the setup — the competence principle and the hobbits/hooligans/Vulcans thing, the surgery-license analogy; Estlund's pushback that knowing better doesn't by itself get you the right to boss anyone around, and that whatever test you use to pick out the knowers will itself be reasonably disputed by the people it screens out; the Condorcet jury theorem and its two famous assumptions; and that Hong-Page diversity result people cite constantly, along with the standard complaint that it only does the work people want if all the agents share an objective function and are each individually good enough to be searching rather than flailing. You don't need to explain any of that to us, you need to use it.

What I actually want:

One, go through the twelve and say who an epistocrat would disenfranchise, then ask separately which of them does the epistemic work in the room. Those lists don't match, and I want to know how much weight that mismatch can bear. Juror 4 is the film's Vulcan — cool, evidence-focused, doesn't care who wins — and he's the one who ends up wrong, and he isn't beaten by a better argument so much as by an old man noticing the impressions a pair of glasses leave on a nose. Juror 5's contribution is having grown up around switchblades. Juror 11, the immigrant, is the one who makes the solemn speech about why what they're doing is worth doing, and he has no slot at all in the taxonomy. Is the film doing that deliberately or am I just wanting it to?

Two, independence. The theorem needs the jurors to not influence each other and the entire movie is influence — one man converting eleven in a locked room over an afternoon, including a secret ballot he has no particular reason to expect will go his way. Pick a horn: either deliberation repairs the competence problem and trashes the independence assumption, or it's a cascade wearing a suit and the group would have done better voting silently and going home. Tell me which one the film actually shows, scene by scene, and don't let it have both.

Three, what the votes are made of. Juror 7 switches sides because he wants to get to the ballgame, then gets called on it and switches back for a reason he can't articulate. Juror 3 holds out to the end and then loses it over a photograph of his son and votes not guilty, having been given no new evidence at all. The tally doesn't care why the votes came in — a converted mind, a bored one, and a grief-stricken one all count identically. Is that a fatal flaw from the epistocrat's side, or is it the actual point, and does the film know which?

Four, my own take, which I want you to attack rather than flatter. First: the film is only an argument for the system if you keep the camera inside one room. Zoom out and what you've got is a procedure with enormous variance across jury rooms and no error correction at the level of the population of them, which seems to me like Brennan's argument, not democracy's. Second: we never learn whether the kid did it. The instruction they're given isn't an accuracy rule, it's an asymmetric error-cost rule, and if the institution isn't aiming at true verdicts in the first place then both the epistemic defense and the epistemic critique are aimed at something the film isn't about. Tell me if either of those is overreading, and I'd genuinely rather you say so.

Five, where the reading breaks. Be honest about what in the film resists the whole epistocracy frame — moments that are about cruelty, or class, or a hot room, and are not doing political epistemology at all. And then close on what the movie gives you that the papers don't. My guess is that it's the felt cost of being the one dissenter: Juror 8's first argument, the duplicate knife, moves nobody, and he keeps going anyway, and he buys time with a ballot instead of winning with an argument. Nobody writes about that part.

No bibliography, no reading list, no quoted lines from the script — I don't have it and neither do you, so paraphrase. Recap plot only where a specific moment is doing argumentative work. Twelve to fifteen hundred words is fine, longer if it needs it. And it's okay to conclude the film is on the epistocrat's side; I just don't want it to be a foregone thing.
