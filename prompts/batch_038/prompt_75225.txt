I cover physics and the philosophy of science for a general-interest magazine, and I'm nearly reported-out on a feature about what the "now" is supposed to be. The occasion was a public symposium last month on time and consciousness. During the Q&A, a graduate student in cognitive science asked whether the phenomenology of duration — the specious present, the way experienced intervals stretch and compress under attention, anaesthesia, and fear — might count as evidence bearing on whether the present has any objective thickness at all, or whether it is merely a way that brains parcel up a world already fully laid out. The panelist answering, a well-regarded cosmologist, cut him off with: "The flow of time is an illusion — physics settled that a century ago, and nothing about how your brain samples the world is going to reopen it." The room laughed, the moderator moved on, and my editor now wants the exchange in the piece. Before I write it, I need to know what sentence he actually said.

Could you take that one line apart for me? I want the distinct precise claims it could be making, laid out as a numbered menu rather than as a single preferred interpretation: readings on which it is a claim about the metaphysics of time (that there are no monadic past, present, and future properties, say, or that all moments are equally real); readings on which it is only a claim about physics (that no experiment could detect a moving present, or that special relativity admits no frame-independent relation of simultaneity); readings on which it is a claim about the semantics of tensed language; and readings on which it is an empirical claim about perception — about what temporal experience represents and whether it represents it correctly. For each, state the claim in a form a philosopher would recognise, then give a verdict: platitude, substantive but defensible, contentious, false, or incoherent. Where a reading depends on a further assumption — about what "illusion" could mean when the thing allegedly illusory is the seeming itself, or about whether a feature of reality that no experiment could detect is thereby unreal — say what that assumption is and whether it, rather than the slogan, is doing the work.

Then bring it back to the exchange. Which of these readings, if any, would actually license dismissing the graduate student's question rather than merely restating a metaphysical preference? Is there a reading on which the slogan is perfectly consistent with presentism, or with a growing-block view, such that the cosmologist's confidence outruns his premises? Which reading do people who repeat the line in popular settings most often have in mind, whether or not it is the one that would serve them? And since I am unlikely to get him on the record again: if you had to attribute one reading to him in print, which would you choose, and how would you phrase the attribution so that it is fair to him while still letting the reader see what the exchange did and did not settle?
