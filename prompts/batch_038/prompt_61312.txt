I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I'd ask you to state the philosophical positions in plain terms rather than assuming I have the vocabulary — but I would like a careful audit of an analogy that keeps getting deployed in my team's arguments, and I'd like to know how much weight it can actually bear.

Here is the background. We were arguing about whether a service that has been refactored module by module over three years, with no single release that changed its stated purpose, is still the same system. Someone observed that this is just the ship of Theseus, and the conversation drifted into the general question of how objects persist through time. Two colleagues then took opposite sides on that general question and each defended their side using the same source domain: version control.

The first holds roughly the view that objects endure — that an object is fully present at each moment of its existence, rather than being spread out across time. His argument: a program is a running process, and at any instant the process is completely there, holding its entire current state. Identity is the continuity of that state, not the record of how it was reached. On his telling, the working tree at a given checkout is the object, and the commit history is bookkeeping we maintain for our own convenience — it is not part of what the object is.

The second holds roughly the view that objects perdure — that they are four-dimensional wholes composed of temporal parts, none of which is the whole thing. Her argument: the repository is the object. The commits are its temporal parts, the commit graph is its career through time, and HEAD is a convention for indicating which slice we happen to be pointing at, playing exactly the role that the word now plays in ordinary talk. Nothing is wholly present; what the first colleague calls the process is one slice among many, and the repository, not any working tree, is the system.

The conclusion she draws, and the one I most want tested, is this: the dispute between the two positions is not a substantive disagreement about the world at all, but a choice of representation — analogous to choosing between snapshot storage and an append-only event log — and once you see that, questions like whether the system survived the refactor have no answer beyond the representation you picked.

What I would like from you:

1. An explicit correspondence table for each version of the analogy. For the process/snapshot mapping and separately for the repository mapping, say what plays the role of the object, its temporal parts, the times at which it exists, its whole career, the present moment, and the identity relation over time. Where a mapping has no entry for one of these roles, say so rather than forcing one.

2. The disanalogies, sorted into those that genuinely threaten the intended conclusion and those that are merely incidental. I suspect the difference between a branching, rewritable history and a single linear life is one of the important ones, but I can't tell whether it matters for her inference or only for the surface plausibility of the picture.

3. What the analogy smuggles in. Git was deliberately designed so that identity is explicit, revisable, and negotiable: branch names move, tags can be reassigned, a rebase rewrites what the past commits were, and the underlying objects are immutable and content-addressed in a way no physical object is. Does that engineered negotiability do covert work in her move from we are free to choose a representation to there is no fact of the matter?

4. Whether her deflationary conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. In particular, is there a residual question the analogy cannot absorb — for instance, whether a choice of representation can be mistaken about the world in a way that choosing a different branch name cannot?

5. If neither mapping is adequate, a better one drawn from software practice, and an honest statement of what the replacement can and cannot support. Candidates I can think of include copy-on-write structural sharing, layered container images, and multi-version concurrency control in a database, but I don't know whether any of them actually improve on the original or just relocate the problem.

I am not looking for references or a reading list; I want the analysis itself, with the weak points of the analogy marked rather than smoothed over.
