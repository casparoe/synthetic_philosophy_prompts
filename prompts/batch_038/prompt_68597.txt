For six years I have kept ant colonies in a shed behind my house, and I have come to believe something I cannot quite defend: that a colony is one animal that thinks, rather than a crowd of very small ones that each think a little. My reasons are plain ones — no single ant knows where the food is, yet the nest finds it, remembers it, and gives it up when a better patch turns up; and when I split a nest in two last spring, both halves simply carried on as if nothing had happened, which is the part of my view I cannot explain. I have fifteen minutes at my county naturalists' club next month. The room is mostly people who have watched ants in the field for decades: they know the recruitment trails, the way a worker's tasks shift as she ages, and how a nest picks a new home site by something like a headcount, and they are allergic to anything that smells of mysticism. Two members build swarms of small robots that coordinate the way my ants do; they will be friendly to my talk, but for engineering reasons rather than mine.

Please give me a prep sheet rather than a defense: exactly five numbered objections this room will actually raise, ordered from most damaging to most survivable, each phrased the way the objector would phrase it rather than the way a philosopher would, followed by two sentences I can deliver from the podium in reply, followed by one sentence naming where that reply is thinnest. Mark which of the five is the one I am secretly hoping nobody asks. No introduction and no conclusion.
