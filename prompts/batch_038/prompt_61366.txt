I am chairing the opening session of an interdisciplinary workshop I have organised on the apparent malleability of the instant: the phenomenon in which observers, shown a stationary disc of one colour that is suddenly replaced by a surround of another, report having seen the disc move and change colour before the change ever occurred. The room will hold vision scientists and computational neuroscientists who have spent their careers on this literature, several philosophers who work on the metaphysics of temporal experience, and roughly a dozen graduate students from both sides who have never met the problem before. The three days are likely to be fractious, because the central disagreement is not about the data but about the shape of the theory: whether the brain genuinely edits an experience that has already been lived through and briefly reported in one form, or whether what later surfaces as experience is composed to its finished order before it is ever conscious, so that no editing occurs at any point.

Please write my opening remarks, about four minutes when read aloud. They must make that disagreement fully intelligible to the students without boring the specialists, and they must do so without using either of the two political labels the debate has acquired, and without naming the colour experiment at all. I would rather the students meet the distinction naked than recognise it as something already in print.

I also want the remarks built around a metaphor I keep reaching for in my own thinking. One kind of mind is like a censor who alters the back issues in the archive; the other is like a copy desk, where the paper is only ever written once, in final form, and no earlier version exists. I want to press the second half of that image: a mind of the copy-desk kind has no morgue, in the newsroom sense of a clipping library, because it keeps no file of what experience was before it was published. The remarks should take that absence seriously as an epistemological point rather than as ornament. If introspection can only ever consult the published record, what could count as evidence for or against there having been a revision? I want the metaphor to make each of the two positions vivid, and then to make that question unavoidable, so that the room has something to argue about for three days. Please close by stating what I think the workshop ought to treat as settled, and what as genuinely open.
