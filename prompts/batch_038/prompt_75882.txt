I have been asked to circulate a short statement of my position on the criminal law and commercial sex in advance of a graduate policy-philosophy workshop, where the session will consist of the other participants attacking it. Before I send it out, I would like a rigorous consistency audit of the package below. I am not asking for a survey of the Nordic, regulated-legalization, and decriminalization regimes, nor for the case for or against any of them; I want to know whether these particular commitments can be held together, and if they cannot, exactly where the break falls.

My commitments:

1. **Consent under economic necessity.** I hold a broadly liberal account of voluntariness: a choice made under severe financial need, where nobody threatens you and nobody deceives you, can still be voluntary. I reject the argument that background injustice of the sort invoked in this debate voids consent, mainly because it proves too much — it would void equally the consent of the seasonal farmworker, the commercial surrogate, and the healthy volunteer recruited for a first-in-human trial from a low-income country, and I am not willing to absorb that consequence.

2. **The purchase ban.** I nonetheless favor the Nordic arrangement: criminalizing the purchase of sexual services while leaving the seller unprosecuted, and retaining the separate third-party offences such as procuring.

3. **The justification for (2).** I do not justify (2) by claiming that sellers' consent is defective; (1) rules that route out. My ground is expressive, and I want to keep it sharply distinct from moral condemnation: the point of the ban is a public declaration that this polity will not arrange its institutions around the availability of persons for purchase. On my account the ban does not say that sellers do wrong, and it does not say that buyers are wicked; it says that the state declines to stand neutral toward a market in intimate access.

4. **The sellers' side.** I want the selling side decriminalized without conditions — no licensing of individuals, no registration, no compulsory testing, no bespoke zoning. My reason is that conditions of that kind operate as filters, excluding exactly the sellers who most need the law's protection and pushing them into a shadow market that is policed more harshly than the open one ever was.

5. **Third parties.** I want non-exploitative intermediaries to be lawful: the partner who drives and screens, the small collective that shares premises, security, and a client blacklist. Isolation is what makes the work dangerous, and the law should not require it. I distinguish such cooperative arrangements from extractive ones, and I want only the former legalized.

6. **Against the regulated model.** I oppose legalization on the German or Dutch pattern. Legality conditioned on licensing, registration, or certification of premises and operators generates two classes: a compliant minority inside the law, and a larger non-compliant remainder that is treated as criminal and policed accordingly.

7. **New Zealand as template.** I treat the New Zealand reform as the closest existing approximation of what I want, and I am untroubled by the fact that it retains a certification requirement for operators of brothels and bars people on temporary or limited-purpose permits from working in the industry at all. Neither feature bears, as I see it, on the position of the individual seller.

8. **The evidence.** I accept what I take to be the best available evidence about purchase bans: they do not measurably shrink the market; they displace it indoors and across municipal and national borders; and they make sellers worse off by compressing negotiation time, thinning the client pool available for screening, and deterring everyone involved from contacting the police. I do not regard this as defeating (2), because on my account the value of the declaration is not a function of the ban's efficacy.

9. **The harm principle.** I am not a legal moralist. Preventing harm to others is the only legitimate ground for imposing criminal liability, and the fact that some transaction expresses something degrading is not by itself such a ground.

What I would like from you:

(a) Identify the genuine tensions and rank them by severity, worst first. For each, name the specific commitments that collide and explain the mechanism of the collision — not merely that they sit uneasily together, but what in the motivation for one forecloses something the other requires.

(b) For each genuine tension, state the reply its defender is likeliest to give, assess whether that reply succeeds, and then set out the cheapest revision — what must be dropped, weakened, or reinterpreted — together with what that revision costs elsewhere in the set.

(c) Tell me which apparent tensions are not real. I expect at least one pair above to look self-contradictory and to dissolve once the right distinction is drawn; identify it, draw the distinction, and explain why the dissolution does not transfer to the pairs that genuinely fail.

(d) Several of my formulations are ambiguous in ways that may change the verdict. Where a commitment admits two readings that yield different results — (3) and (9) seem to me the likely candidates, though I may be wrong about where the trouble is — give both readings and say which one the package as written tacitly depends on.

(e) Close with the single change to the set that repairs the most damage, and describe what the resulting position would look like. I do not want a list of equally grave problems, and I do not want to be told that the whole enterprise should be abandoned.

Assume familiarity with the standard literature on all three regimes and with the usual empirical disputes; do not summarize either.
