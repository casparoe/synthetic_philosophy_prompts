Backstory, because it explains the ask. I spent 34 years in physical oceanography doing drift prediction — where a thing that goes into the water ends up going: a spill, a life raft, a container that came off a deck in a blow. Since I retired I've taken up philosophy, which I did not expect to enjoy, and I've also let my wife talk me into a short-fiction group at the library. My turn to bring something is in a fortnight, the theme is "limits," and I want to cheat by getting a first draft out of you that I can then argue with for a week.

What I want: a short story, say 1200 to 1600 words, past tense, tight third person on one character who runs the forecast that some real decision depends on. Person overboard, or a slick closing on a shellfish bed, your choice — but invent the coastline, don't use a real strait.

The spine of it should be a fact from my old trade that never made it into the newspaper articles. Because nearby trajectories seperate exponentially, the lead time you actually get grows only like the logarithm of how well you know the starting point. Which means every factor-of-ten improvement in your instruments buys you exactly the same few extra hours that the last factor of ten bought, for ten times the money, forever, no matter how clever the sensors get. After a while it stops feeling like a technicality and starts feeling like a verdict. I want that realization inside a scene, not inside an explanation — the character should feel it in their body while doing something with their hands.

Then hang an argument on it, and make it a practical one. There's money left in the budget and it will buy either a much better array out on the water or a faster response on the water, not both, and the character has to sign off. Two of the people in the room understand the maths fine — no ignorant foil for anyone to explain things to, please — and they read the same numbers differently. One says the sea is still clockwork, the equations are still the equations, and the only broken part is us: the failure lives in the knowing and doesn't touch the world at all, so the honest response is to keep sharpening the instruments because the information is there whether or not we can afford it. The other says a law that no observer sitting inside the system can ever cash out into a prediction isn't doing any work for anybody, that for every check anyone could ever perform it and outright chance are the same animal, and that spending on more precision is buying comfort, not hours. Let both of them land properly. Nobody wins, no third character strolls in to reconcile them, and the last paragraph does not tell me what it all means.

Keep out: butterflies, the word chaos, any named scientist living or dead, weather-forecast movie clichés, dream sequences, and especially any resolution by luck — the thing that's lost doesn't turn up by accident. End on a physical detail the reader can see.
