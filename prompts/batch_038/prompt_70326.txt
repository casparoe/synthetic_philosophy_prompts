I sit on the advisory panel for a mid-sized city that's debating whether to issue its own scrip — a "civic dollar," spendable at local businesses and good for settling city fees and parking tickets. The motion came from a colleague who'd been reading Berkeley's Querist, and her pitch to us was basically his: money isn't wealth at all, its true idea is "altogether that of a ticket or counter," and it's useful only so far "as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour." Which means, she says, a city with empty factories, idle workers and obvious unmet local needs is nonsense to call poor — Berkeley's opening query was whether there was ever an industrious nation poor or an idle rich. The treasurer calls it fantasy: the scrip will leak out of town, get hoarded or trade at a discount, and with nothing behind it theres no reason anyone would take it in the first place.

Write the opinion I'll circulate before we vote. Take a side — issue it, dont, or issue it under conditions you spell out — and give reasons the treasurer's office would actually have to answer, including the strongest thing the losing side has going for it. I want the Berkeleyan position engaged on its own terms, not as a museum piece: where does treating money as a mere counter genuinely buy you, and where does it quietly assume something the treasurer is right to deny? American English please.
