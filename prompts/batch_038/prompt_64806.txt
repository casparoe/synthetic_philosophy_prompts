I am in the second term of a course on medieval Jewish philosophy, and this week our seminar turned to Maimonides on the sacrificial cult. Two members of my reading group ended up in a sustained argument which I have been asked to referee in a short paper, and I could not settle it myself. I should be grateful if you would adjudicate it move by move. Please write in British English throughout.

So that you are not dependent on recalling the texts, here are the facts we were working from:

(a) In the Guide of the Perplexed, third part, chapter 32, Maimonides argues that it is impossible to pass suddenly from one extreme to the other, and hence impossible for human beings to give up at once everything to which they are accustomed; he illustrates the point with the development of a newborn mammal, which cannot move straight from milk to dry food and must be weaned gradually. Applying this to the Law: the mode of worship current in Moses's day consisted in sacrificing animals in temples containing images, bowing down before them and burning incense before them. Divine wisdom therefore did not command Israel to discontinue all such service, since obedience to such a command would have been contrary to human nature — it would have struck people as a prophet would strike us today who told us in God's name not to pray, not to fast, not to seek help in time of trouble, and to serve God in thought alone with no action at all. Instead God transferred that worship to Himself: a temple, an altar raised to His name, sacrifices, bowing, incense, and a priesthood maintained by designated gifts, so that the traces of idolatry were blotted out and the existence and unity of God firmly established without unsettling the people.

(b) In the third part, chapter 26, he explains the details of the sacrificial species historically: Egypt venerated the ram; certain Sabian sects believed jinn took goat form; the slaughter of cattle was abhorrent to pagans who venerated them. Hence the Law commanded the offering of precisely those kinds — of the herd and the flock — in order to erase such beliefs.

(c) In the Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings and Wars 11:1, he writes that the messianic king will arise, restore the Davidic kingdom to its former sovereignty, build the Temple and gather the dispersed of Israel, and that in his days all the laws will return to their previous state: sacrifices will be offered, and the sabbatical and jubilee years observed in the manner the Torah describes. He adds that one who does not believe in him or does not await his coming denies the words of the other prophets and of the Torah itself. Two of the code's fourteen books are devoted to the Temple service and to the sacrificial laws in full detail.

The argument ran as follows.

1. Yael: chapter 32 is an origin story with an axiological punch. The cult is retained because it could not be abolished, not because it is good; its value is instrumental and tied to a historical condition — the universality of image-worship — which has since lapsed. So the Guide's Maimonides must regard the restoration of sacrifice as a regression, and the code's statement is either a concession to his audience and to the charge of heresy he himself lays down, or the exoteric half of a two-register project. The Guide states his real position.

2. Tomás: that is the noble-lie reading, and it founders on Maimonides' own principle that every commandment proceeds from divine wisdom and has a rationale, whether or not we perceive it; the failure to perceive is our ignorance, not a defect in the Law. If sacrifice is nothing but an anti-idolatry device, the restoration passage has no rationale at all, and the author of the code is committed to a commandment he cannot account for — precisely what his own method forbids. Moreover chapter 32 nowhere says the cult is to be discontinued: the weaning parable concerns how the Law was given, not how long it stands.

3. Yael: the parable is not neutral on duration. Milk is not the food of the adult, and the point of weaning is that the earlier diet is superseded. And the counterfactual is about the present, not the past: a prophet who commanded purely intellectual service today would be disbelieved — his point being that we are still not ready, not that intellectual service is anything less than the ideal. So sacrifices belong to the immature stage, and the code's restoration passage must be read as a statement of what the Law decrees rather than of what is best.

4. Tomás: then you have conceded that the two works are not contradictory. One states the ideal, the other the decree, and Maimonides holds both that decrees bind and that their rationales may outrun our grasp. On the reconciliation our tutor mentioned — the one due to Henshke — both works agree that sacrifice has no theurgic effect and that its sanctity is a matter of the Law's designation, so its messianic restoration is obedience to a standing command whose point is conformity to the eternal divine will.

5. Yael: that reconciliation empties the Guide's argument. If the cult is retained for reasons unconnected with idolatry, the historical explanation in chapters 26 and 32 does no work: the choice of species, the priestly gifts, the incense, the siting of the Temple all become incidental. You cannot hold both that the details are anti-idolatrous devices and that the whole institution is a permanent symbol of obedience. And it leaves untouched Nahmanides' objection in his commentary on Leviticus — that if the offerings were intended only to disabuse the wicked and the fools, the Torah's own presentation of sacrifice as pleasing to God is falsified and the Lord's table is treated lightly — along with the further objection often pressed beside it, that a God opposed to idolatry might simply have forbidden sacrifice rather than commandeering it, since the pagans sacrificed to their own gods and not to His.

6. Tomás: Nahmanides offers a rival theology, not a demonstration of inconsistency, and Maimonides would reject its premise, since he does not take the pleasing-aroma language to describe a divine need. The stronger point on your side is the modern one — Soloveitchik's judgement that Nahmanides penetrates the complex concept of sacrifice whereas Maimonides' account in the Guide is pure instrumentalism. But an account can be philosophically thinner and still be Maimonides'. Note also Emden, who found the tension so unbearable that he concluded the Guide could not be Maimonides' work at all, which suggests the tension is real; whereas Hendel's noble-lie reading and Henshke's reconciliation both presuppose that it is not.

Please do the following. For each of the six moves, give a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with two or three sentences justifying it. Then say who is right about which specific questions: is the Guide's account a claim about origins, about value, or about duration, and does it matter that these come apart; can the weaning parable bear the weight Yael puts on it; does the code's statement about disbelief constrain how the restoration passage may be read; is chapter 26's argument about the species independent evidence for either side. Then say what both of them are missing. Finally, give a verdict on who is more right overall, together with a short account of the general methodological lesson — what happens when one insists on reading a legal code and a philosophical work as parts of a single consistent system, and whether the availability of esoteric and exoteric readings is an explanatory resource here or a way of immunising Maimonides against refutation.

Around 1,200 words, please, and no bibliography, notes or citations: I have supplied the passages above, and my seminar leader has said explicitly that we are marked on the argument rather than on our referencing. Where you think a move turns on a detail of the texts that I may have reported loosely, flag it as such rather than building your rating on it.
