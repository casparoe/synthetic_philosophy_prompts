I retired four years ago from a career in experimental plant physiology — thirty-five years of dose-response curves, knockout lines, and factorial designs — and have since been auditing courses in history and philosophy and, occasionally, leading sessions in our institute's continuing-education programme. Next month I have been asked to give a seventy-five-minute session to a reading group of about fifteen retired professionals, most of them former laboratory scientists and engineers, on overdetermination and multi-causal explanation in history. They will have read a couple of general chapters assigned by the group's convenor; they will not have read anything technical, and only two of them have any philosophy at all.

I do not want the session written out for me. I want the plan for it: the order in which the components must be introduced and why, the single case that should carry the explanation, the misconceptions this particular audience will bring or form and the exact point in the sequence at which each is cheapest to head off, and a check for whether the idea has landed. Specifically:

**The dependency structure.** Which ideas must be in place before the word "overdetermined" can even be stated without misleading them — do they need counterfactual dependence first, or the notion of an intervention, or the distinction between a cause and a mere background condition, or the difference between several necessary conditions and several sufficient ones? Give me the order, and for each step state what in the later steps presupposes it. I want to see the load-bearing relations, not a list of topics in a plausible sequence.

**The reversal I expect to be the hinge of the session.** These people are trained on intervention: a variable is a cause if manipulating it changes the outcome, and their instinct is that anything you cannot isolate you have not explained. But in the historical cases that historians most want to call overdetermined, typically no single candidate cause is such that removing it would have prevented the outcome — so the one test they trust most appears to disqualify precisely the explanations historians rate most highly. The plan must tell me where that apparent reversal belongs: before the case, inside it, or after it, and what must already have been said for it to come out resolved rather than merely paradoxical.

**The carrying case.** Name one and commit to it. Not the July Crisis of 1914 — everyone reaches for the assassination, and the multiplicity collapses within ten minutes into a single dramatic event. Not the Challenger O-rings; half this room has already sat through that talk. The case must be one where the multiplicity of causes is genuinely at issue in the historical literature rather than decorative, where a non-specialist can see what sort of evidence bears on which candidate cause, and where there is at least one candidate that is causally real and yet makes no difference to the outcome, so that contributing and being necessary have something to bite against. Tell me what the case must have and what it must lack, and then say why yours satisfies those conditions.

**A taxonomy of mine, to be used, repaired, or discarded.** I have been tentatively sorting multi-causal explanations into three shapes: pile-up, where several independently sufficient causes are present at once; chain, where each link is individually necessary but no single link is the cause; and threshold, where many contributions accumulate toward a limit and no individual one is separately identifiable. I do not know whether this is a real distinction, a relabelling of something standard, or three names for one thing. Tell me which. If you keep it, say at what point in the session it is introduced and which of the three shapes does the most work on the case you have chosen. Related question, and please decide it rather than describe it: a philosopher I correspond with insists the vocabulary I should be using is Mackie's — a cause as an insufficient but non-redundant part of a condition that is itself unnecessary but sufficient — and I cannot tell whether that machinery earns its keep in a room with no prerequisites, or whether it buys precision nobody there will use.

**The misconception map.** Expect at least these: that "overdetermined" means "we do not know which cause it was"; that a properly explained event should have its causes ranked by percentage contribution, the way one apportions variance; and that if an event was overdetermined, historical counterfactuals are frivolous. Someone will also observe that the word comes out of psychoanalysis, and someone else will say it entered historical writing by way of Althusser, and both will be partly right — I would like to know in advance what I am conceding if I let that exchange run, and whether to cut it short. Give me the misconceptions I have not thought of, which are specific to people with experimental training rather than general, and for each one the place in the sequence where preventing it is cheapest together with the single sentence that does the preventing.

**Declared omissions.** Seventy-five minutes, no prerequisites. Tell me what I should announce at the outset that I am not covering, and what I should decline to answer if asked. I expect questions about whether history is a science at all and about determinism, and I would rather have the refusal planned than improvised.

**The check.** One question I can put to the room at the end whose answers will separate those who have grasped the idea from those who have merely been agreeably talked at. Say what a good answer looks like and identify the bad answer that would nonetheless sound satisfactory to me.

Please organise the reply under exactly these headings, in this order, and keep to roughly the lengths indicated: **The Audience's Starting Point** (about 150 words); **The Order of Ideas** (about 600 words, each step annotated with the presupposition it discharges); **The Carrying Case** (about 300 words); **The Taxonomy and the Machinery** (about 300 words); **The Misconception Map** (about 450 words); **Declared Omissions** (about 150 words); **The Final Check** (about 120 words).

One further requirement of manner. Make decisions; do not hand me menus. Wherever the plan rests on a substantive philosophical choice — whether to teach overdetermination as a feature of the world or as a symptom of our explanatory interests, whether to run the session on counterfactuals, on interventions, or on something else entirely, whether the historical cases are genuinely overdetermined or merely complicated — take a position and give me your reason for it in a sentence or two. If you think my three-shape taxonomy is worthless, say so plainly and build the session without it. I have spent a career reading methods sections that enumerate every available option and recommend none, and I have no appetite for another.
