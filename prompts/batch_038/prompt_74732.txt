I've been in a reading group for about a year now that has split into two camps on risk-weighted expected utility — the view where you run probabilities through a risk function before taking the expectation, so that an agent's attitude to risk is a separate parameter from the shape of their utility over outcomes. One camp says this whole apparatus is either redundant or already refuted: everything the risk-weighted crowd wants to explain is, on their view, available from a suitably concave utility function plus the discipline of evaluating bundles of decisions instead of isolated one-offs, and the risk function itself is just a probability-weighting device that's been around for decades and that buys its flexibility by giving up the independence-style axiom that all the money-pump and dynamic-consistency arguments depend on. The other camp insists there's a genuine residue — that common-consequence preference patterns of the Allais sort cannot be reproduced by any expected-utility maximizer no matter how you bend the utility function, that a risk attitude is a real property of the agent and not a disguised belief or a disguised desire, and that applying the risk function to your total situation rather than decision-by-decision defuses the pump arguments. Neither side thinks the other is stupid, they just never finish a sentence about the same thing.

What made me care is a case of my own. A friend offered me a one-shot bet: 55% chance of winning $2,000, 45% chance of losing $1,500 — positive expected value by about $425, and I turned it down. He then offered me twenty independent repetitions of the identical bet as a single package, and I'd have taken that without much hesitation, even though the package is just twenty of the things I'd refused. His verdict is that my pair of preferences is flatly incoherent for anyone with a concave utility over wealth — turn down one and you're committed to turning down the bundle — so I must be misreporting what I actually prefer. The risk-weighted half of my group says the opposite, that a convex risk function applied to the aggregated gamble predicts exactly my pattern and that this is the clean case where their framework does something the other one can't. I also don't buy the easy fallback of just making utility more concave, because of the calibration-style worry that an expected-utility maximizer who refuses small favorable gambles at any level of wealth ends up committed to refusing absurdly large ones, and I'm pretty sure I'd take a favorable bet on my life savings.

So: write the document the collaboration should have produced. Exactly five numbered entries, and nothing else — no introduction, no summary paragraph at the end, no table of contents. Each entry should have (a) a claim about risk attitudes, aggregation, or the formal apparatus that both camps could genuinely sign, in a sentence or two; (b) the residual disagreement in that same area, stated precisely and neutrally, with each camp's own position described in wording that camp would accept as fair; (c) the concrete test, argument, or worked case that both would agree actually bears on the disagreement; and (d) each camp's prediction about that test, written down in advance and tagged so we can check later who was right. At least one entry has to be about my bundled-bet case specifically, and it should say whether the expected-utility camp really is committed to calling my preferences incoherent or whether there's a respectable EU-friendly story — broad bracketing, utility defined over something other than wealth, reference points, whatever — that absorbs it without needing a risk function, and if so, what the risk-weighted camp says is still left over after that story is granted. If you conclude that some part of the whole dispute is verbal — for instance that the two camps aren't using "risk aversion" to mean the same thing — say which part and why inside the entry where it belongs rather than as a separate caveat. Don't strawman either side, and don't hedge into a both-have-a-point shrug: the point is to find the exact place where they actually diverge.
