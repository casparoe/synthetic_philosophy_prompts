Some background about me. I am a retired professor of physical chemistry; I left the laboratory six years ago and have since taken up philosophy with the amateur's enthusiasm and, I hope, the scientist's suspicion of unearned conclusions. Whitehead came to philosophy from mathematics and physics, which is the same road I have travelled in miniature, and his essays on education — collected in the volume *The Aims of Education and Other Essays* — arrested me because they diagnose a failure I watched for forty years in my own students.

In six weeks I give a forty-minute invited lecture, followed by half an hour of discussion, at my former university's annual symposium on undergraduate science teaching. My thesis, which I want you to attack rather than admire, is as follows.

1. What is usually called inert knowledge — material a student can reproduce on an examination and cannot deploy in an unfamiliar problem — is not a general failure of transfer. It is a sequencing failure. Our science curricula deliver what Whitehead calls precision (formalism, nomenclature, technique, drill) before the student has had the prior stage of romance: the unstructured, first-person encounter with the phenomena that makes the formalism worth acquiring at all. Worse, they stop at precision, thereby suppressing the third stage, generalisation, in which the learner takes up the disciplined material again and uses it freely.
2. Assessment-driven reform aggravates the problem rather than relieving it. Because generalisation is the stage that resists measurement, and every incentive in the institution rewards only what can be measured, we are systematically pruning the very stage that produces the competence we claim to want.
3. My constructive proposal is a "rhythm audit." For each unit of a course, one records where in time the romance, precision, and generalisation phases fall, and what fraction of contact hours each receives; one then re-sequences and re-budgets. In the lecture I will present an audit of an undergraduate thermodynamics sequence that I reconstructed from publicly posted syllabi, showing precision occupying roughly the first two-thirds of the term with generalisation crowded into the final fortnight.
4. I intend to claim that Whitehead's threefold rhythm is not merely an edifying metaphor for good teachers' intuitions but a hypothesis about scheduling that could in principle be tested — and that it stands in genuine tension with two current orthodoxies: the prerequisite-gated foundational sequence, and the view that spaced retrieval practice should begin as early as possible in any unit.

The audience will number about one hundred and twenty, and it is not a philosophy audience.
