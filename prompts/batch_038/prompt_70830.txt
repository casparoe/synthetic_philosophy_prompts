I cover science, philanthropy, and public ethics for a magazine, and for the better part of a year I have been reporting on a proposal that keeps resurfacing in grantmaking and policy circles under the label moral trade: the suggestion that a large class of entrenched moral disagreements should be handled by exchange rather than by argument, on the grounds that the parties usually disagree about the weights attached to considerations rather than about what counts as a consideration at all. For a long feature I arranged three working sessions between a proponent and a skeptic. The sessions produced roughly ninety minutes of recorded dispute, a good deal of mutual irritation, and no convergence, and my editor has now asked me to run alongside the piece a twelve-hundred-word sidebar that is not my own summary of the fight but rather a document both participants could put their names to: the joint statement their collaboration should have produced. I would like you to draft that statement.

So that you are not guessing at the parties, here is how each of them actually put the case. The proponent holds that most real moral conflict is about relative weight and that, once that is conceded, mutually beneficial exchange becomes available in situations we currently treat as zero-sum; that what a moral trade moves is never a permission but a resource, so a deontologist who trades need not have acted impermissibly herself; that the objection to trading one's convictions tacitly assumes morality is a kind of property an agent owns and may or may not alienate, which is precisely what is in question; and that the alternative to trade is not principled unanimity but political combat, in which the same concessions are extracted anyway, less efficiently and less transparently. The skeptic holds that certain commitments are non-alienable in the same way that self-sale is not a contract anyone can validly make, so that some things cannot be on the market at all without ceasing to be what they are; that an institutionalized market for moral concessions will pay its highest premium to parties whose convictions are most shocking, and therefore creates a standing incentive to acquire and advertise outrageous convictions rather than to moderate them; that trades bind third parties who were never at the table and who cannot bargain; that there is no common metric, so in practice every trade is settled by whoever has more money, more votes, or more willingness to walk away; and that what the proponent calls moral trade is ordinary political compromise relabeled, with the relabeling doing covert work by making concessions look like gains.

Please run the statement through four cases both of them argued about, and do not let the parties retreat to their slogans in any of them. First, two foundations that weight global health and animal welfare very differently each escrow six million dollars for the other's cause, conditional on reciprocity and on a joint disclosure of how each arrived at its weights. Second, a legislative vote trade in which one bill is treated by one coalition not as a policy difference but as a violation of rights, so that trading it away is, on that coalition's own description, selling the thing that is not for sale. Third, a university that keeps the contested name on a building in exchange for naming rights to a new research centre and an endowed chair in the disputed field, after alumni threaten to withhold a gift. Fourth, a conservation group that withdraws its opposition to a mining concession in exchange for the operator funding a larger reserve elsewhere, where the people living inside the concession were not represented and nobody sought their consent.

The deliverable should be a constructive text, not a verdict and not a diagnosis of why the two sides talk past each other: no editorial asides, no winner, no both-sides hedging, and nothing either party would have to strike out before signing. I want a numbered section of claims both can genuinely endorse about the cases above and about moral disagreement generally, each claim phrased so that it is neither vacuous nor slanted, and I want you to say explicitly where the agreed section stops carrying weight. Then a disagreements table in which each surviving dispute is stated twice, once in wording the proponent would accept as her own and once in wording the skeptic would accept as his. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of the two, with, for each crux, the case, argument, or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it and each side's prediction recorded in advance rather than after the fact. I expect at least one crux to concern non-alienability and at least one to concern whether trade manufactures the extreme convictions it then rewards, but you should decide the rest. Finally, a short closing section that identifies which parts of the dispute are verbal rather than substantive, naming the terms doing the double duty, and states, in one sentence each, what either party would have to be shown in order to concede the argument outright.
