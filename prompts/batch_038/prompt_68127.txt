For a little over two years I have kept a daily mood log in one of the mainstream mood-tracking apps, and I have become interested in what the design of such an app commits its makers, and by extension its users, to. The mechanics are the same across the whole family. At the top of the screen sits a row of five or seven cartoon faces running from miserable to delighted; I tap the one that best matches how the last few hours felt. Optionally I tap activity tags from a preset list — work, exercise, sleep, friends, coffee, family — and sometimes I type a note. The app then does a great deal with that single tap. It plots a continuous line chart of my mood across weeks and months; it computes an average, overall and by weekday, and informs me that Tuesdays are my low point; it ranks my activity tags by the mean score attached to each and reports that days tagged exercise run about six-tenths of a point above my personal baseline; it sends a reminder when I have not logged. Some of these apps add a second question, how meaningful or how stressful the day was, and a related family asks me instead to place a dot on a grid with pleasantness on one axis and energy or agitation on the other. But the headline output, the thing that gets the chart and the average and the notification, is always the one face I tapped, treated as a number.

I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of pleasure that this practice presupposes. What has pleasure to be like for a tap among five faces to yield a quantity at all? I want the presuppositions teased apart rather than merely listed: that every pleasurable episode shares some one measurable property, so that the pleasure of a cold drink after a run, of a good joke, of relief when a worry turns out to have been unfounded, and of being absorbed in a piece of music are all readings of the same instrument; that the instrument reads the same way in March as in November, and in my hand as in anyone else's; that readings of this kind can be averaged and subtracted, which is exactly what the weekday means and the exercise bonus require; and that I can consult the magnitude on demand, at the moment, without the asking itself altering what gets reported.

Then tell me which rival views the design rules out, and whether it rules them out or merely has no room for them. I have in mind at least three: the thought that pleasures have nothing in common beyond a family resemblance, or differ in kind and not only in degree, so that no single scale spans them; the thought that there is no introspectible quantity there to find, and that answering the question is a construction, an interpretation of the day rather than a measurement of it; and the thought that pleasure is not a feeling at all but a way of standing toward a state of affairs — a being-pleased-that something is so — on which two episodes could feel indistinguishable and yet not be equally pleasant.

I also want to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, because I suspect a good deal of it would survive the collapse of the single-dimension picture. Rankings of activities may need only order rather than a common unit, and an app could in principle let me sort days into kinds instead of scoring them. Say what the design strictly needs — cardinality, commensurability across kinds, a metric stable over time and across persons — as against what merely fits it comfortably. And say whether it is at odds with itself: the second question about meaning and the separate tagging of activities look like quiet concessions that one valence axis is not the whole story, yet the app still reports a single number as the summary. Does that combination commit the design to hedonism, or to a pluralism about wellbeing that it then declines to display?

Two further questions. How does all this stand to the older project of a hedonic calculus? Bentham distinguished seven circumstances — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent. Say which of these the app preserves, which it collapses into the tap, and which it simply drops; the activity correlations look like a fumbling at fecundity and purity, while extent, which concerns everybody other than me, appears to have no home in a log of my own mood. Is the app an heir of that project or a different thing altogether? And finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposition were replaced. If the designers were persuaded that pleasures are heterogeneous and admit no common measure, what could such an app record, what could it not, could it still draw a chart, still warn me about Tuesdays, still deliver what its marketing promises? If it could not, is that a defect in the alternative view or a discovery about what the original app was really selling?

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among the philosophers and psychologists who work on this. Some of the measurement-theoretic points about what an ordinal rating can and cannot bear are, I take it, close to settled; whether there is a single hedonic dimension underlying affect ratings is not, and there are serious people on both sides of it. Do not present a contested position as the consensus, and where you are inclined to take a side, say plainly that you are taking one. And please answer in continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — the way one would write a letter.
