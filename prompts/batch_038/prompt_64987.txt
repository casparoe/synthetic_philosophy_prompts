I am a second-year student at a public high school, and for my independent study in philosophy I have been working on the puzzle of the statue and the lump of clay. My seminar teacher left in November, so I am now working from my own notes and a handful of printouts, and I cannot get into the university databases she used, which is why I would rather not be sent to sources at all; please keep everything self-contained, and if you name a position, name it by what the view says rather than by author, book, or date, since I have no way of checking any of that. What I want first is the specification, not the theories: before I read one more account of material constitution, I want to know what I should be demanding of an account. So please write the list of desiderata — what any adequate theory of what it is for one thing to be made of another has to deliver. I want the cases it must handle correctly, the distinctions it is not permitted to collapse (for instance, between constitution and identity, between numerical and qualitative sameness, and between the persisting object and the matter that composes it at a time), the logical and semantic constraints it must respect, including whatever it has to say about how many objects are in a given region when we count, and any results from logic, language, or ordinary practice that it may not contradict. For each entry, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference belonging to a particular camp. Then tell me which subset of the requirements cannot all be satisfied at once, because if there is such a subset, the list is itself the interesting result; and tell me which single requirement does the most work in eliminating otherwise attractive views.

Part of the reason I am asking is that I have come up with a case of my own that none of my printouts seem to cover, and I want the desiderata to be able to judge it. My school's maker space has a 3D printer, and last year our history club printed a bust of an alumna for the display case. About halfway through, the print failed; we ground the ruined piece back into pellets, mixed those pellets roughly half and half with virgin filament from a fresh spool, and printed the bust again, successfully, from the same file. That file still sits on the club's shared drive, so nothing about the shape was ever destroyed — it survived, in a medium other than the matter, through the interval when no bust existed. Six months later the display case was knocked over and the second bust shattered, and the club simply printed a third one from the same file, in the same week that another school, a hundred miles away, printed an identical bust from a copy of the same file. I would like the requirements list to be explicit about what a theory owes us here: whether a form that is stored digitally rather than materially can make a later object the same object as an earlier one, what becomes of the counting constraint when one file yields two busts at once, whether the half-recycled filament forces the theory to admit vague or graded constitution, and whether anything about this case is genuinely new or whether it is just the classic reassembly puzzle with extra machinery, in which case please say so plainly.

Finally, once the criteria are laid out, give me a short assessment of the standard competing positions against them — keep this brief and keep the weight on the requirements themselves, since the requirements are where the thinking is, and a table is fine. Alongside that, please state how confident you are in what you claim: for each item you mark non-negotiable, say whether that is a settled consensus in the literature or your own judgment about what the debate ought to look like, and single out the one point in your whole answer that you are least sure of, with a sentence about what would settle it. If you suspect that one of my supposedly neutral requirements already smuggles in a partisan assumption, flag it rather than leaving it buried in the list.
