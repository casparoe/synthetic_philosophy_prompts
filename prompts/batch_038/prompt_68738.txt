I was hired seven weeks ago out of a philosophy department to co-lead a small group at a rehabilitation-engineering lab. We build assistive systems that infer what a user wants from what the user does: a robotic arm that learns which objects to offer, a daily scheduler that learns which activities to protect when the user is fatigued. The two senior researchers I now sit between have been in open disagreement for the better part of a year, and our director has asked them to bring a joint statement to next month's internal review — something both of them could sign, setting out what they agree on and exactly where they still differ. They have both, generously or otherwise, delegated the first draft to me. I would like you to write that draft.

The two positions, in the vocabulary each side prefers for itself:

The **measurement faction** holds that an inferred reward is an estimate of a real psychological quantity. Behavior is generated by preferences operating under beliefs, costs, and a bounded planning procedure; if we model the planning procedure honestly and collect enough data, including data from varied contexts, then the fitted reward is a measurement of the user's preferences in the ordinary sense — noisy, corrigible, but about something. On this view, the assistant's default posture is deference: when the model is confident, its output is a legitimate stand-in for the user's own judgment about what to reach for, and the user's moment-to-moment choices deserve the same authority we would grant anyone's choices.

The **design faction** holds that what our fitters return is a proposal, not a discovery. A reward is never recovered on its own but only relative to a hypothesis class we chose, a planner model we chose (discount, noise, myopia, cost of asking), and a dataset we chose by deciding which episodes count. The same behavior trace can be rationalized by many reward–planner pairs, and our selection among them is driven by tractability and fit statistics rather than by evidence about the person. On this view, the label "preference" carries a normative commitment we have not earned, especially since much of the behavior we fit is habitual, coerced by circumstance, shaped by embarrassment, or the residue of adaptation to what has been available; the assistant's default posture is therefore to treat the inferred reward as something the user must be able to inspect and ratify.

Four technical matters must be dealt with explicitly, because both sides keep invoking them at cross purposes:

1. The invariance results — reward is identifiable only up to positive affine transformation and up to transformations that leave the optimal policy set unchanged. Both sides need to say what survives these invariances and whether what survives is enough to be called a preference.
2. What I will call the reward–planner entanglement: in the offline setting, ambiguity between a person's reward and our model of their planning is not merely a small-sample problem. The statement must record whether each side takes this to be underdetermination in principle or in practice.
3. The validity criterion: held-out predictive accuracy on future choices, versus the user's informed retrospective endorsement of what the system did on their behalf.
4. The cooperative formulation in which the robot is explicitly uncertain about the human's reward and queries it — whether this dissolves the dispute or simply relocates it into the design of the query.

The document should be built around a case both principals already accept as legitimate. We call it the Thermos case. A user with a spinal cord injury operates an arm that fetches items. Across forty days we log reaches, acceptances, and refusals. The user often refuses an item they rank highly in interview when retrieving it would require asking the on-duty caregiver to reposition it, and often accepts a mildly aversive item when retrieval is unaided. Two models fit the held-out choices about equally well. M1 posits the reward the user states in interview, plus a large negative weight on requiring assistance — a stable preference against being a burden. M2 posits a reward with no assistance term at all, plus a planner that is myopic about the social friction of the moment. The deployments diverge: under M1 the arm should keep declining assistance-requiring options as the user's settled wish; under M2 the arm should sometimes decline to honor the observed reluctance, because the reluctance is a planning artifact the user disavows when asked calmly afterward.

Please produce:

- A numbered section of agreed claims — the formal, empirical, and normative propositions both factions can sign as written. Where a claim is signed for different reasons by the two sides, add a one-line parenthetical saying so. Where a claim can only be signed with a qualification, put the qualification in the claim rather than in a footnote.
- A disagreements table with columns for the issue, the formulation the measurement faction would accept as a statement of its own view, the formulation the design faction would accept as a statement of its own view, and a judgment of whether the residual difference is substantive or verbal. Nothing in a cell may be slanted; each side should be able to read its own row without wincing.
- The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. For each, name the case, dataset, experiment, or argument that both factions would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, stated precisely enough that abandoning it later would cost something. For the Thermos case specifically, I want a prediction from each side about whether any further observational or interventional data — varying the cost of asking, offering a route that removes the social friction, comparing blocks in which the caregiver is and is not present — separates M1 from M2, and about whether the fitted assistance weight tracks the user's post hoc endorsement.
- A closing section in which each side states what would count as evidence against its own position, not against the other's.
- One paragraph on whether the cruxes change when the user's capacity to endorse is itself fluctuating, since roughly half the people we serve are in that category.

Two constraints on the writing. Do not give me a survey of the literature and do not include references or a reading list; the positions are fully described above and what I need is the drafting. And be willing to conclude that part of this dispute is about words — if you think the argument over whether the fitted quantity deserves the name preference is verbal while the argument over deference versus override is not, say so plainly and specify what vocabulary both sides would have to adopt for the substantive residue to become visible.
