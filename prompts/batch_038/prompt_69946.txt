I am preparing an internal standard for what counts as a safety case at the lab where I work, and the argument I built to discipline my own thinking turns out to prove considerably more than I want it to. It runs as follows.

1. A safety guarantee for a system S is an argument establishing that S will not produce a catastrophic outcome in deployment.
2. An argument whose conclusion is itself probabilistic does not establish that S will not produce a catastrophic outcome; it establishes only that S probably will not.
3. Hence a genuine safety guarantee must be a worst-case guarantee: one that holds for every input S may receive, every environment S may be placed in, and every adversary S may face.
4. For any system whose behavior is produced by learning from data, or whose objective was inferred from human feedback, there is some input on which it behaves catastrophically: the training distribution has finite support, every learned decision function has error, and adversarial instances can be manufactured on demand for anything differentiable.
5. Hence no learned system admits of a safety guarantee in the sense of (3).
6. Whether an agent acts recklessly depends on the warrant for the agent's belief, not on how the belief turns out; so deploying a system for which no safety guarantee is available is reckless however well that system performs.
7. Hence no learned system ought to be deployed.
8. But (7) is intolerable. It condemns statistical process control, infusion pumps with learned components, spam filtering, terrain-aware flight control, and ordinary structural load assumptions; and because human competence is likewise acquired from a finite sample of experience, it condemns delegating anything to anyone.

I accept (8), so the argument is unsound, and my difficulty is saying precisely where. I would be grateful for the following.

- Name the step or steps at which it fails. If you think it is valid, say instead which premise it is least costly to abandon.
- For each step you flag, explain what made it plausible in isolation. I need the diagnosis to account for why three colleagues and I all initially found the argument compelling, not merely to record that it is not.
- Price the alternatives. For each premise I might give up instead, state what I lose, with particular attention to the two concerns that pushed me toward worst-case reasoning in the first place: distribution shift, and the fact that an aggregate bound certified on one population is silent about the population one actually deploys into.
- Say whether the argument equivocates on what "worst case" ranges over — instances, distributions, adversaries, or the training sample itself — and identify which reading makes (3) true but empty and which makes it substantive but false.
- Say whether (2) conflates the certainty of a bound with the content of the bound, and whether anything of (1) through (3) survives once the two are separated.
- Close with the strongest argument in this neighborhood that you regard as sound, and state what it implies for a lab whose current case for deployment consists of aggregate benchmark performance, a red-team pass rate, and a runtime monitor that can veto an output only after it has been generated.

Two notes on my own position, offered as data rather than as conclusions. I would rather keep (6) than (4), since (6) is the premise that ties epistemic warrant to permissibility and I am unwilling to lose that tie. And I half-suspect that (8) is the weak link rather than any of the earlier steps — that the human-delegation analogy simply does not carry the weight I put on it, and that the real conclusion is that we should deploy nothing until the analogy is repaired. I recognize that this suspicion may itself be the error.

Please give me a diagnosis of this argument rather than a survey of positions on it.
