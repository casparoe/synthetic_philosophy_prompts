My mother died seven weeks ago. She was a physician who spent thirty-four years running a clinic for uninsured patients in a mid-sized city, and by every public measure she was admirable: she drew no salary after the first decade, she spent a night in a jail cell for blocking the entrance to a hospital slated for closure, and two generations of medical students sought her out because she was the only attending who would teach them how to sit with a dying patient. The clinic's board has asked me to write the opening essay of a memorial volume that will be handed to every trainee who rotates through. They asked me because I teach philosophy, and they assumed that made me good at this. It has not.

There is a complication, and I want to think it through before I write a sentence, because I suspect my instinct is the wrong one. In her last two years, a colleague she had recruited and defended for twenty years was credibly accused of abusing patients. She ran her own inquiry, judged the evidence thin, and persuaded the board to settle quietly and let him retire rather than refer him to the licensing authority. I now believe there were at least two further victims after the settlement. She told me once that a referral would have cost the clinic its federal funding and closed it, and that forty thousand patients against two was an arithmetic she was not prepared to leave unexamined. She may have been right about the arithmetic. That is part of what makes the case hard rather than merely sordid.

So I want an essay on the general question my situation has made unavoidable, and I want you to take a position on it rather than survey it. The question is whether holding up an individual life as an object of admiration and imitation — which is what the memorial volume is for, what the saint's life was for, and what Plutarch's Lives are for — necessarily involves falsifying that life. I do not want a history of biography. I want an argument with a verdict.

Please build it out of specific material rather than abstraction, and argue with Plutarch rather than merely describing him. Take seriously the methodological claim he makes at the opening of his Alexander: that what he is writing are lives and not histories, and that a small thing — a remark, a jest, a gesture — often discloses character more reliably than a battle with thousands slain. Take seriously the paired architecture of the Lives, a Greek set beside a Roman with a formal comparison at the end, and tell me whether that structure solves the hagiography problem, merely evades it, or makes it worse. Take seriously that Plutarch reproaches the elder Cato for treating his slaves as instruments and selling them off once they were worn out, and uses the occasion to observe that kindness reaches further than justice, since we owe goodwill even to those who can never repay it. Take seriously that he reports the younger Cato's divorce of Marcia and her transfer to the childless Hortensius, and her recovery after Hortensius died and left her a fortune; that he reports the enemies who read this as a profitable loan of a wife; and that he declines to credit that reading. Ask what a biographer is doing when he records a damaging story and then disbelieves it. And ask what the undignified physical detail of Cato's death at Utica — the failed stroke, the sutured wound, the torn reopening — contributes to a life meant to be imitated.

Set that against the other pole: the conventions of the saint's life, where precocious signs, the transfiguration of faults, and a death scene that recapitulates the meaning of the whole are not accidents but the point, and where a reader is being given something to imitate rather than something to judge. Then bring in whatever philosophy earns its place — exemplarist accounts of virtue that make admiration the detector of the exemplary, and the obvious objection that admiration is miscalibrated and must be educated; the situationist attack on the very idea of the stable character traits a life is supposed to exhibit; Aristotle's insistence that a life cannot be assessed until it is complete; the problem of dirty hands for someone whose office requires choosing between atrocity and ruin; Boswell's protest that he is writing a life and not a panegyric, and that even a great and good man must not be assumed to have been entirely perfect. Explain each position briefly as you use it; assume I know the Lives decently and have read none of the moderns.

Then I need four things from you.

First, your verdict on the general claim. Is exemplarity structurally hagiographic — does the demand that a life serve as a model entail that the life be falsified — or can an honest exemplar exist? Defend whichever you choose; do not split the difference.

Second, a discrimination among the kinds of falsification at issue: omission, transfiguration, selective emphasis, generic stylization, and the difference between lying and merely working within a genre's conventions. Say which of these actually destroy the moral use of a life and which are compatible with it, and why.

Third, a decision about my essay. Should the settlement and its aftermath appear in the volume, yes or no — conditionally if you must, but decided. And if yes, say plainly what including it does to the book's function: can the trainees still be given my mother as a model, and if so, a model of what exactly, and of what is she no longer a model?

Fourth, tell me which premise of mine you think most likely to be false, and where your own argument is weakest. I am not looking for reassurance, and I would rather not be told that this is a matter for my conscience or that I should consider talking to the family. Give me the reasoning and let it end somewhere.
