I am helping compile an annotated casebook for a graduate seminar on the foundations of statistical physics, and the entry that has stalled us is the one on determinism. Our convener — call her Professor R. — drafted the first ten entries herself before leaving for a semester at an observatory with unreliable email, and she has told us flatly that she will not sign off on any further entry whose classification does not follow the same rule as hers. Her ten rulings, all in answer to the same question, "Is this system deterministic?": (1) a frictionless pendulum swinging in a vacuum from a specified release angle — yes; (2) Lorenz's three-variable convection model, with the initial data given exactly as rational numbers — yes, with the remark that chaos constrains forecasters rather than the world; (3) a one-dimensional cellular automaton of the rule-30 kind, started from a single active cell — yes, and although no shortcut is known for reading off its state ten thousand steps ahead, that, she said, is a fact about us; (4) a coin or a die modelled as a rigid Newtonian body — yes; (5) a pseudorandom number generator whose algorithm and seed are known — yes; (6) a uranium-238 nucleus on the textbook reading of measurement, where the decay time is irreducibly stochastic — no; (7) the same nucleus treated in a pilot-wave theory, in which the particle configuration is definite at every instant — yes, the unpredictability of the decay time being a consequence of the equilibrium distribution rather than of any indeterminism; (8) the dome case familiar from the philosophical literature, in which a frictionless point particle rests exactly at the apex of a dome whose profile makes the equation of motion fail to be Lipschitz, so that both the eternal-rest solution and solutions on which the particle slides off at an arbitrarily chosen later time satisfy the laws — no, on the ground that there is nothing there for the state to determine; (9) five point masses attracting according to Newtonian gravity in a configuration on which one of them is flung outward with ever-increasing speed and reaches infinite distance in a finite time — no, because, as she put it, the laws run out; (10) a block of metal whose temperature profile evolves by the heat equation from a smooth initial distribution — yes, and she added that the fact that the same profile is compatible with a great many earlier histories does not trouble her in the least.

My co-author wants to dispose of the remaining entries by the test he has read into her: a system is deterministic exactly when its state obeys ordinary differential equations with a Lipschitz right-hand side. I have pointed out that this cannot be her rule, since it misclassifies at least two of her ten outright and does not even apply to several of the others, but I cannot say what her rule is, and I suspect more than one candidate fits everything she has said. Three entries remain for us to draft, and they were chosen precisely because nobody in the group is sure about them. The first is a classical massless field in three space dimensions obeying the wave equation, with initial data — field profile and initial time-derivative — that is computable, in the sense that an algorithm can produce it to any desired precision, but whose value at some later time, at some point of space, is not a computable real number; take the underlying result in computable analysis as given for the purposes of the entry, and note the contrast with ordinary differential equations having computable right-hand sides, where computable data yield computable solutions on whatever interval a solution exists. The second is an ideal billiard, a point particle moving frictionlessly among fixed convex disks, launched from a point whose coordinates are not computable — say, their binary expansions encode the answers to the halting problem; nothing about its dynamics is exotic, its trajectory is unique, and the only oddity lies in how its initial point is specified. The third is a viscous incompressible fluid in a periodic box governed by the three-dimensional Navier–Stokes equations from smooth initial data, where it is not known whether smooth solutions persist for all time.

So: what rule is Professor R. actually running? State each candidate as a criterion precise enough to be applied, say which of her ten verdicts rules out which tempting criteria — I have in mind criteria built on continuous dependence on initial data, on the recoverability of the past, on the existence of exceptionless statistical regularities, and on the difference between a system's being law-governed and its states' being fit to figure in laws — and confirm whether the ten really are jointly consistent with every candidate you keep; if some pair of verdicts is in tension under all of them, say so rather than smoothing it over. Then give me the verdict each surviving criterion delivers on each of the three remaining entries, and commit to a single best guess for each entry, with your reasons, including whatever pressure her rulings on the dome, on the five masses, and on the pilot-wave nucleus exert on that guess. Tell me which of the three would most sharply separate the candidates, or whether it takes a pair of them to do the work, and if some further system you can construct would separate them better, describe it. Finally, for each of the three, say what substantive claim about determinism our casebook would be endorsing by classifying it one way rather than the other — whether determinism is being treated as a feature of laws alone or of laws together with states, whether it is a metaphysical or an epistemic notion, and whether it demands histories that extend indefinitely in time. I do not need references; I need the reasoning, and I would rather be told honestly that her verdicts underdetermine a classification than be handed a confident one that papers over the gap.
