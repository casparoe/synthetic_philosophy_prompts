Been chewing on this for a while, want your take.

Take the 1976 US swine flu vaccination program and run it through the cluelessness objection to consequentialism — the one that says our acts' consequences ramify through history indefinitely, we can't trace most of them, so we're in no position to rank options by expected total value, and therefore consequentialism can't actually guide action in any serious real-world case.

Quick version of the facts as I remember them. Correct me if I've mangled something, but dont let the analysis turn on exact figures. Early 1976: a flu illness kills one recruit at Fort Dix and sickens a couple hundred more; CDC types the strain as a swine flu related to the 1918 pandemic virus. The CDC director writes a memo calling for mass immunization, Ford is briefed in mid-March, convenes a panel including the famous vaccine developers, goes on TV and announces a plan to vaccinate every American. Congress appropriates about $135 million. Manufacturers refuse to produce without government indemnity against liability, wich drags on for months. An unrelated pneumonia outbreak in Philadelphia (Legionnaires') briefly looks like the pandemic arriving, then isn't. The Fort Dix strain never spreads beyond the base. Shots start going into arms in October; reports of Guillain-Barré syndrome among vaccinees pile up — on the order of one extra case per 100,000 shots, so low hundreds of excess cases and maybe two dozen deaths, though people still argue about the numbers — and the program is shut down in mid-December after something like 40-odd million people have been vaccinated. No pandemic ever comes. The standard post-mortem is that it wrecked public trust in vaccines and in the agencies, a hangover that got cited very explicitly during the 2009 H1N1 episode.

Here's what I actually want. The usual examples for the cluelessness objection are enormous and remote — colonial extraction, whether to have a child, anything whose payoff lands centuries out. 1976 interests me because it's the opposite: the catastrophe landed inside ten months, and most of the fatal unknowns were near-term. So work through:

- Who is the consequentialist agent, and at which moment? Ford in March, the CDC director in February, Congress in April, whoever made the December call — the option sets genuinely differ at each, and I want the mapping done properly instead of "the government decided to vaccinate everyone."
- At each decision point, what kind of ignorance was in play? Use a distinction I made up myself, it's not standard terminology so don't go hunting for it: call it *enumerable* uncertainty, where you can write down the candidate outcomes but can't get good probabilities on them (pandemic vs. no pandemic; how big the GBS risk is), versus *unenumerable* uncertainty, where the outcome space isn't even listable because the relevant possibility never occurred to anyone (the trust damage, the indemnity precedent, the way 1976 would be recycled as a cautionary story thirty years on). Say which kind bites where.
- Then the honest question: does cluelessness explain why this went wrong? My suspicion is no, or only partly — the post-mortems blame speed, an election year, groupthink, and a failure to do ordinary bounded cost-benefit reasoning about tractable near-term stuff. If the real failure was bad reasoning about the tractable rather than cosmic ignorance, does that weaken the objection in general, since its stock cases seem to need the far-future ramification to do the work? Or does it just mean this isn't the right lens for this episode, no more?
- And run it the other direction. The trust/hesitancy consequence is exactly the sort of long-tail ramifying effect the objection points at, yet it proved traceable enough that people reasoned from it explicitly in 2009 — real, large, and still usable decades later. Does that push you toward cluelessness coming in degrees, toward "knowable in kind but not quantifiable," or toward thinking the objection was never really about knowledge and should be recast as something about objective versus subjective rightness, or about decision quality? Pick one and defend it rather than listing all three.

Give me your bottom line in two sentences at the top — confirms the objection, strains it, or simply isn't a case for it — and then the analysis. No bibliography or reading list, I'm not looking anything up. Hard cap of 900 words, and please actually respect it.
