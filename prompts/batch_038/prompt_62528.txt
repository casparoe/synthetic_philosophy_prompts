I'm 71, retired after a career in condensed matter physics (low-temperature transport, mostly), and for the last three years I've been reading philosophy in the evenings, which my wife says is only slightly better than the model railway. Right now I'm stuck on the argument about moral testimony — whether its OK, or whether there's something wrong with forming your moral beliefs on somebody else's say-so. As best I can tell it's been going twenty years or more and it hasn't moved an inch. One camp says of course testimony can give you moral knowledge, there's no special epistemic problem here, look at all the other domains where it works fine. The other camp says sure, granted, but that's not the point — something is off about the person who does it, they're missing understanding, or they act from the wrong kind of motive, or they're handing over a judgement that was theirs to make. Then you press them and they trade places and the whole thing starts again one level up.

My hunch, which you should feel free to demolish: there isn't one debate here, there's three wearing one name. Can testimony transmit moral knowledge? Can you act well on a moral belief you got that way? Would a decent person want to have got it that way in the first place? And nobody moves because a win on one of those gets reported by everyone as a win on all three.

Two things from my old life I want you to weigh up. First, in science testimony is the default and there is no puzzle whatsoever: I believed in the existence of things I never measured myself, on the say-so of people I never met, and I'd have been an idiot not to. If the moral case really is different, the difference has to be pinned down somewhere specific, and I'm not convinced the argument has pinned it down rather than asserted it. Second — and this is the one I care about — the intuitions being used as data here look to me like single measurements with no error bars. Someone invents a case, a woman asks her friend whether she should stay with a husband who beats the dog, the friend says no, and we're told what we'd think of the woman afterwards; but nobody seems to check whether the verdict survives small perturbations, like whether the friend is a priest, a lawyer, your sister, or someone you have independent reason to distrust. Maybe part of the stall is that the two sides don't even agree about which cases count as data.

So diagnose it for me. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one — are they really fighting about the word 'understanding', or about what 'moral knowledge' is supposed to mean — three different questions under one heading, or a hidden empirical question about how moral belief actually forms that nobody is measuring? Tell me which of my two complaints holds up and which one is a physicist overreaching. And then say what would count as progress: a distinction, a better case base, a real empirical result, a change of question — or argue that we shouldn't expect progress and why not. I don't want a survey of who says what. I want the structure of the impasse.
