I'm the philosopher on a three-person panel that has spent the last four months writing the assumptions annex for a municipal appraisal of a coastal flood-defence scheme — a project whose costs fall almost entirely in the next decade and whose benefits are spread over a century or more. The dispute that has now spilled over into email is about the discount rate, and it has been conducted almost entirely by analogy. Our consulting economist's version is this: a society deciding how much to forgo today for the sake of later is exactly like a household deciding how much of this year's income to save; the trade-off has the same structure in both cases, so the right social rate is the one households actually reveal when they lend and borrow in capital markets, and pure time preference is in any event a taste rather than a belief — you don't argue about tastes, you read them off behaviour. On this view the long-running quarrel between those who set pure time preference at a tenth of a percent a year and those who insist it be calibrated to observed returns and saving behaviour is simply a quarrel about which reading of the same behaviour is better, and the policy conclusion follows: use a market-consistent rate, and the scheme's distant benefits largely evaporate.

The ethicist on the panel pushed an analogy in the opposite direction. Giving a person's welfare less weight merely because that person happens to live further in the future, she said, is like giving it less weight merely because the person lives further away, or because of their sex or their race: temporal location is no better a ground for down-weighting than distance is, so a positive pure rate of time preference is not a parameter to be estimated at all but a form of discrimination against people who cannot complain about it. On her account the Ramsey-style decomposition is beside the point, since what needs justifying is the impatience term itself, and nothing about growth or the elasticity of marginal utility can do that work.

I suspect each analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear, and that they are carrying it in opposite directions. Could you audit both? For each, give me the correspondence explicitly — a table of what plays the role of what (in the first: the household, the saver, the bank rate, the household's finite life, consumption smoothing; in the second: the spatial distance, the stranger, the arbitrary property, the weight withheld) — and then separate the disanalogies that actually threaten the intended use from the ones that are merely decorative. In particular I'd like to know whether the household analogy can get from a market interest rate to a *pure* rate of time preference at all, or whether it silently identifies the impatience term with the whole decomposition into impatence, elasticity, and growth, and with the opportunity cost of capital; whether it licenses importing the component of private impatience that reflects the household's own chance of dying before collecting, when the society in question does not die; what it presupposes about who bears the cost and who receives the benefit when the saver and the beneficiary are not one continuing subject, and whether the capital that is supposed to carry the transfer really does; and whether "it's a taste, not a belief" survives the fact that revealed private impatience is time-inconsistent in ways economists themselves treat as a bias rather than a preference. On the other side: whether the discrimination analogy still holds when the people further in time are, unlike distant strangers, predictably richer, causally dependent on choices we make now, and not yet determinate as to who they will be; whether it targets pure impatience only or is quietly deployed against discounting in general; and whether the inability of the far future to reciprocate, complain, or trade with us is a feature the analogy is entitled to ignore. Finally, tell me which conclusion, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table — a market-calibrated rate, a zero pure rate, or a division of labour between them — and, if there is one, what better analogy would serve each piece of reasoning the two of them were pressed into doing.
