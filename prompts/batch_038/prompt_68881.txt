I am contributing an essay to a volume on the philosophy of work and leisure, and my piece sets Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day (1989) against Keynes's Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren (1930). I want a reading of the novel through the essay that stays disciplined about what Keynes actually argues, and I would rather be told where the pairing breaks down than have it forced.

Begin by separating the claims Keynes runs together in a single essay. There is the prediction: that capital accumulation and technical progress will solve the economic problem within roughly a century, that the standard of life will rise several-fold, and that working hours will fall toward something like a fifteen-hour week. Then there is the normative-psychological claim about what people will do with the time thus released — that the love of money as a possession, as distinct from the enjoyment of what money buys, will come to be seen as the semi-pathological propensity it is; that we shall value ends above means again; that leisure will be occupied with art, affection, beauty and the enjoyment of the present. And there is the uncomfortable transitional counsel: for another hundred years we should go on pretending that the meanest motives are virtues, because the accumulation they generate is what pays for the escape. Tell me which of these three components the novel is genuinely in dialogue with, and which it never touches.

Then work from the novel's own terms rather than from the essay's. Stevens defines himself by dignity, understood as the professional's refusal to be dislodged from his role by anything, and the book opens and closes with his anxiety about the bantering that his new American employer, Mr. Farraday, expects of him — a form of sociability that consists precisely in enjoying the moment for its own sake, which Stevens rehearses as though it were a duty and cannot perform. In between: the 1956 motoring trip undertaken to persuade Miss Kenton to return to service; the recollection of Lord Darlington's slide from post-war magnanimity toward appeasement; the dismissal of two Jewish maids that Stevens carries out without protest and spends the rest of his life not quite acknowledging; his father dying upstairs while the conference continues below. The evening on the pier at Weymouth, where a stranger tells him that the evening of the day is its best part, is where any Keynesian reading must finally cash out.

I would like the essay to do five things. First, identify the scene or settled habit that best exhibits the pathology Keynes describes: a man whose entire sense of worth consists in means-valuation, and who therefore has nothing left over when the means are withdrawn. Second, mark where the novel resists — Stevens's ruin follows from the moral and political content of what he served, not from the length of his working week, and Keynes's essay has no vocabulary for complicity; say plainly which scenes the lens illuminates and which it flattens. Third, take up the timing problem: Keynes imagines a century-long re-education of a whole society, while Ishiguro gives us one man on whom that re-education arrives after the character has set. Does the novel thereby assert something Keynes's argument cannot supply — that the capacity for ends must be cultivated before the margin arrives rather than after — or does Stevens's late, partial acquisition of bantering qualify as a vindication of the claim that the habits of purposiveness can be laid down once abundance makes them unnecessary? Fourth, address class: Keynes wrote for readers who already had an economic margin, whereas Stevens's dignity is the achievement of someone for whom the economic problem was never solved and whose purposive labour helped finance other people's leisure. Decide whether the novel exposes a blind spot in the essay or supplies the missing case that completes it, and defend the choice. Fifth, say whether the closing movement is tragic or reparative, and how much the verdict on Keynes depends on that judgment.

Close by stating what the novel contributes to the debate about the good life that argument alone cannot: something about the phenomenology of arriving at leisure with no ends worth having.

On method: this is criticism, not plot summary, and roughly 1,500 words. No bibliography, no citation apparatus, no reference lists. Do not quote either text at length — paraphrase scenes and locate them by description. Where you are unsure of a detail of the novel, flag the uncertainty instead of filling it in; a misremembered scene will do my argument more harm than an acknowledged gap. And do not let the novel dissolve into an illustration: if at some point the better account of Stevens requires abandoning Keynes, I want that said explicitly rather than papered over.
