I'm in an upper-level philosophy of religion seminar and we've spent the last two weeks on Clifford vs. James, and my paper is due Friday so I'm trying to figure out whether an analogy our prof keeps reaching for can actually bear the weight he puts on it. He uses it to argue that James wins outright, and I want to stress-test it before I build a section around it.

The analogy is that religious faith is like falling in love, or more precisely like deciding to trust one specific person — a partner, a close friend, someone you're going into business with. His argument goes: you can't finish collecting the evidence about whether that person is trustworthy before you commit, because the trustworthiness is partly a function of whether you trust them. Hold back, hedge, run little tests and you produce exactly the guarded, evasive behavior that then gets logged as confirmation of your suspicion. So the evidence is partly manufactured by your stance toward the question, and insisting on evidence-first is a way of guaranteeing you never get any. He says that's structurally the same thing James is claiming about religious belief: an option that's living, forced and momentous, where suspending judgment isn't a neutral safe harbor but a decision with its own costs, and where believing can help bring about the state of affairs that would verify it. Which is why Clifford's shipowner is just the wrong model — the shipowner's evidence about the hull was sitting there fixed and available, he simply declined to look at it, whereas the lover's evidence about the beloved isn't fixed in advance at all. So the duty to not believe beyond the evidence never gets a grip on the religious case.

Then my friend in section threw what I think is a genuinly nasty counter-case at it: the jealous partner. That person also has a belief that manufactures its own evidence — checking the phone, interrogating friends, staging tests — and the resulting weirdness gets filed away as confirmation, in a self-sealing loop. Structurally that's the same shape as the trusting lover's loop, but everybody agrees the epistemics there are awful, not admirable. If the love analogy is what licenses the move from "your stance helps create the evidence" to "so Clifford can't complain," the jealous partner seems to show that move is neutral between good and bad faith, and the analogy can't be carrying the normative weight the prof wants it to.

Can you audit this for me? I want (1) an explicit mapping — what in the love case plays the role of what in the religious case: the beloved, the commitment, the evidence, the stakes, the living/forced/momentous structure, and especially the harm-to-third-parties that Clifford is so worked up about; (2) the disanalogies sorted into the ones that actually threaten the prof's use of it and the ones that are decorative, since I suspect the load-bearing ones are something about the object of belief (a particular person who can respond to you and give you feedback vs. a claim about the whole order of the universe) and something about publicity (love is private and its costs land on you, whereas Clifford's worry is that credulity is contagious and lands on strangers — the emigrants on the ship are kind of the entire point of his example); (3) whether the jealous-partner case really does neutralize the analogy or whether there's a principled difference between the two loops a defender could hold onto — I keep feeling there has to be one, maybe something about whether the belief stays responsive to what the world does back, but I can't state it cleanly; and (4) if the love analogy fails, whether some better analogy does the job James needs, one that keeps the stance-dependence of the evidence without collapsing into self-sealing. End with a last line starting "Verdict:" saying whether the analogy can carry the prof's conclusion as stated, can only carry a restricted version of it (say which restriction), or collapses.
