I am the secretary to a statutory body — call it the Recognition Commission — in a European country that has just abolished direct parliamentary grants to its two historic churches and replaced them with a designation scheme of a kind that already exists elsewhere on the continent. Under our statute, each taxpayer may direct eight per mille of their assessed income tax either to one recognised belief organisation or to a state social fund; sums left undesignated are then distributed among the recognised bodies in proportion to the designations each received. Recognition is conferred on application, reviewed every seven years, and revocable for cause. The Commission has just begun work and has no procedure at all.

The criteria, as enacted, are these. The applicant must be (a) "a community of belief, religious or comparable in character"; (b) continuously active in the country for at least ten years, with at least one thousand resident members; (c) possessed of purposes including worship, pastoral care, or moral formation offered to its members; (d) governed accountably to its members, publishing accounts to a standard we set; (e) respectful of "the constitutional order and the rights of others"; and (f) non-discriminatory, in the conduct of any activity funded through the scheme, on any ground prohibited by our general equality legislation. The same statute forbids us to assess the truth, the internal coherence, or the acceptability of any body of belief, and to rank one against another; our legal adviser reminds us constantly that this prohibition is not merely local but follows from our obligations under the European human rights system, and that a decision which looks like an evaluation of doctrine will not survive appeal.

What I want from you is a decision procedure seven part-time members can actually run on an application file. Concretely:

1. The questions to be asked, in order, with the sufficiency conditions for each: what discharges the question, what fails it, where the file terminates in refusal, where it terminates in recognition, where it must be put to a vote because it turns on a value the Commission cannot resolve by argument, and where the correct output is a recorded dissent rather than a decision. Assume two hours of committee time per new application, one staff officer's preparation, one legal adviser, roughly forty-five applications and twelve renewals a year, and written reasons that must withstand appellate review. Include how we are to verify the membership threshold under (b) without constructing a state register of adherents, and what we do when an organisation's own count includes the long-dead, the unchurched but loyal, and people who ticked a box once.

2. For each question in the procedure, an explicit statement of which theoretical commitment is doing the work: whether neutrality means treating all belief organisations alike or supporting belief-formation as such with evenhandedness; whether religion is a distinctive good with distinctive institutional needs or one comprehensive doctrine among many; whether recognition is fiscal plumbing or a public status whose conferral can itself demean those left outside it; and whether the state may condition public money on how a group organises itself internally. Where two commitments are both defensible and yield different answers, the procedure must choose one, justify the choice in terms a lay member can follow, and mark it as a choice rather than a discovery. Please do not send citations, references, or a reading list — we have no research budget — but do identify an argument by name where a name is the shortest way to it.

3. An honest account of the failure modes: thresholds that exclude the new and the small by construction; the way any test of "religious or comparable" invites applicants to present a doctrinally respectable version of themselves; the risk that criterion (f) turns us into arbiters of orthodoxy by the back door; and the pro rata distribution of undesignated funds, which pays recognised bodies for the apathy of the majority and therefore rewards being recognised over being supported.

Then run the procedure on three applications now before us.

First, a humanist association, thirty-eight years old, thirty-one thousand members, which conducts non-religious funerals, weddings and naming ceremonies and provides ethics teaching in schools on request; it files ordinary charity accounts, and a paid celebrant service now generates a third of its income. It denies in terms that it is a religion, and objects to criterion (c), saying it will not describe its work as worship or pastoral care merely to fit the statute.

Second, a religious order, twelve years old, nine hundred members, which ordains men only, admits adherents alone to its rites and to its school, and requires members to break off relations with those who leave. Its accounts are exemplary; it seeks recognition for clergy stipends and a pastoral visiting programme; and its counsel maintains that criteria (e) and (f) govern only activities offered to the public, so that its internal discipline is none of our business.

Third, a community of two hundred and fifty, six years old, whose central rite involves ingesting a substance scheduled under our criminal law, for which there is no exemption; it has no buildings, meets in homes, and remits part of its income to a parent body abroad. The substance is not otherwise associated with the harms that led to its scheduling.

For what it is worth, my own instincts are that the first should be recognised and the second refused. If your procedure produces the opposite results, say so plainly and explain what that reveals about the statute rather than about the groups. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do — which applications will always be decided by whoever drafts the minutes, which questions are not ours to answer, and what should be sent back to the legislature with a request for amendment.
