I am a novelist, and I have run into a difficulty in my manuscript that craft alone will not resolve, so I should like to set it out as plainly as I can and ask you to help me think through it.

For three years I have been writing a novel about the 1923 lockout at a company-owned herring-curing station on the northern coast — I call the place Invermara, though the company papers call it something else — and the book is built almost entirely out of the station's records, which now sit in a county archive in thirty-one boxes.

Here is the trouble. The men's side of the dispute survives in abundance: a strike bulletin printed locally, letters home, minutes of the committee, a petition bearing some two hundred signatures, and recordings made in the 1970s with two surviving strikers. The women, who were perhaps two-thirds of the workforce and did the gutting and the packing, survive almost wholly as the objects of other people's sentences. There is the company wage ledger: names, days worked, and fines entered against individual packers for spoiled or underfilled barrels. There are the superintendent's monthly reports, in which the women are restless, prone to combining, and a difficulty. There is a visiting philanthropist's pamphlet on the state of their morals. That is very nearly all.

One exception, and it is a strange one: a single deposition taken by a magistrate from a packer in the week before the lockout. But the magistrate wrote it down, in his idiom, in answer to his questions, and she may well have been performing for a man holding a pen.

There is also a pattern in the ledger that took me months to see. The fines cluster tightly in the eleven weeks before the lockout; they are always entered on a Thursday; and they move among a shifting but bounded group of names. It reads like the trace of something organized. Nothing in any document ever says what it was.

I have three chapters to write, and three ways to write them.

The first is to render those chapters entirely from inside the superintendent's papers, so that the reader meets the women only as the company met them, and feels the flatness of it.

The second is to write from inside a packer's consciousness in free indirect style, supplying the interior the record withholds, including a Thursday.

The third is a hybrid: give a packer the Thursday fines and let her think about them, but keep the narrator visibly reconstructing, so that the prose itself signals inference rather than testimony.

Two readers have advised me in opposite directions. A historian says the second is a species of forgery: that the absence of these women's voices is the finding, and that filling it with plausible feeling only replays the arrangement that produced the silence — a person with leisure and literacy furnishing words for people who were refused both. A novelist says the first is worse: that if I decline to imagine them, the superintendent has the last word, and my scrupulousness confers on the archive precisely the authority it acquired by exclusion.

So: is one of them right, and is there a principle by which to settle it, or is this finally taste dressed as ethics? In particular —

Does the mediated deposition outrank anything I might invent honestly, or is the gap between the two smaller than I would like? Is there a real difference between imagining what such a woman might have thought and asserting that she did think it — and if there is, can prose make that difference visible to a reader rather than merely footnoting it? Does the Thursday pattern change the answer, given that it is evidence of agency the documents refuse to name?

Tell me which of the three chapters you would write, and what would make that choice something other than a preference. If you think there is a fourth option I have not seen, I would rather hear that than be reassured.
