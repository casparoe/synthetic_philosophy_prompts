So my partner Dana did two years of a philosophy degree before she switched to nursing, and the only leftover symptom is that she has extremely fast, extremely confident verdicts about whether a given person is fooling themselves. She's basically never wrong. I've been privately keeping a list of her calls for years because it's a fun party trick, and the list has recently stopped being fun because the next entry is me.

Her calls so far:

- My brother-in-law Marcus. Hates his job, has said for six years that he's staying "for the kids' stability." Turned out he'd quietly redone his CV and interviewed twice. If anyone brings it up he snaps or immediately pivots to the mortgage. Dana: self-deception, no hesitation.
- My friend Priya. Her coauthor got the two-book deal Priya was supposed to get. Priya is genuinely, verifiably thrilled for her — hosts her, pitches the book to people, zero scorekeeping — and her own thing got picked up separately a few months later. Dana: not self-deception. She said there was nothing false in there to be deceived about.
- My dad. "My blood pressure is fine." Hadn't let anyone measure it in six years, binned the pharmacy reminders, got very jolly and steered everyone toward the barbecue when my mom pushed. It was not fine. Dana: obvious self-deception.
- Terry and his food truck. Cheerfully certain he'd be profitable by year two. His numbers were garbage, but he'd show them to anyone, invite criticism, spend an hour going through the spreadsheet with you. No ego in it, he'd have closed up and been relieved. Dana: that's not fooling yourself, that's just being bad at math.
- My sister Jo, when her husband was in treatment. "He's going to make it." Calm, didn't avoid anything, read every pamphlet, could recite the statistics better than the doctors. He didn't make it. Dana, both at the time and after: not self-deception, that was hope, and hope isn't the same thing.
- Me, four years ago. I was insisting my grad program was "paused, not dead" and I got genuinely angry when she said otherwise. She told me the anger was the tell. She was right — it was dead, I'd stopped going months before that conversation.

Now the new one. I've been with someone five years who lives in another country. Three springs running she's said "this is the year, once the lease is up." This month she signed another twelve-month lease and took a promotion there. I believe she's moving in the spring. I know how that sounds, but here's what makes it weird: I'm not angry and I'm not avoiding anything. I re-read the messages, I built a timeline, I asked the friend who can't stand her to go through it with me, I bring it up at dinner constantly and happily, and I have gone looking for reasons to think otherwise and found them unpersuasive on the merits. The cracks, such as they are: I started typing the other reading into a note on my phone once and deleted it before I finished, and about twice a month I wake up around 3am with a horrible sinking feeling that is completely sorted and reasonable by the time the coffee's on.

She's visiting Sunday and she'll have a verdict inside four minutes, she always does.

So — what does she say? I want the candidate rules she could be running that all stay consistent with those six calls, because there has to be more than one, and then what each of them predicts about me, because I'm fairly sure they don't agree. Give me your best guess at her actual verdict and why you'd back that rule over the others. Then describe the case that would settle it: one new person, plus what each candidate rule would say about them, such that whichever way Dana ruled you'd know which rule she's actually on.

One more data point I don't know what to do with. She once said something to the effect that you can't deliberately fool yourself, because then who's doing the fooling. I can't tell if that constrains any of this or if she was just showing off.
