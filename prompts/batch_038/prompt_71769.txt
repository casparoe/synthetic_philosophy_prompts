I spent thirty-eight years in a physiology laboratory, the last twenty of them working on interfaces between living neural tissue and synthetic devices. Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of personal identity with a layman's enthusiasm and, I expect, a layman's blind spots. My complaint against the literature is that the thought experiments are under-specified in ways that matter, so I have built one of my own and I should like to see two capable people argue over it rather than over the familiar teleporter.

The case. A patient, whom I shall call Nadia, undergoes replacement of her cortical neurons, one at a time, by synthetic units designed to be exact functional equivalents: same input–output mapping, same sensitivity to the same modulatory signals, same activity-dependent plasticity rules, same spontaneous firing statistics. Three engineered features distinguish this protocol from the usual gradual-replacement story.

1. Shadow window. Each synthetic unit is implanted and, for roughly forty hours, runs in parallel with the biological neuron it is to replace. It receives the same afferent signals and generates its own output train, which is recorded but not connected downstream; it is calibrated against the living neuron's actual behaviour during that window rather than manufactured to a design specification. At handover the biological neuron is silenced and the unit's output is switched in.
2. Rollback. Throughout the window and for a further six hours after handover, the biological neuron can be reinstated and the unit disconnected, with no loss of function or of memory.
3. Order. Units are taken in a pseudorandom sequence chosen so that no anatomically or functionally defined region passes a few percent replacement until the cortex as a whole is past the halfway mark. Total duration is about eleven months. Nadia is awake, conversant, and continuously self-reporting throughout.

My former institute wants to know how the consent form should describe the endpoint: whether it should speak of the patient, or of a person who will remember being the patient. That is the practical question the dialogue should be about.

Please write a dialogue between two philosophers who have been asked to advise on that wording and who disagree about it. One maintains that the person who wakes on the last day is Nadia, strictly and literally, and that this is a fact about the world's structure rather than about our conventions or our concerns: what makes for a single persisting person here is the non-branching continuity of the process that carries information forward, and she is willing to accept indeterminacy in nearby cases rather than surrender the claim in this one. The other maintains that what survives is an excellent imitation and that the smoothness is an artifact of my pacing: at each handover a realizer is extinguished and a different one takes up its causal role, and doing that several billion times in succession is no more survivable than doing it at once. This second philosopher must offer a positive account of what "extinguished" means in this setting that does not lean on intuition about teleporters, and must answer the obvious objection that the same reasoning makes ordinary molecular turnover fatal.

Requirements on the exchange:

- Neither side is a strawman, and neither is permitted a point the other simply fails to answer. Each must respond to what the other actually said.
- The characters must engage the specific features of my protocol — the shadow window, the rollback provision, the pseudorandom order — and not drift back into the generic uploading debate. If one of those features turns out to do no work, one of them should say so.
- At least once, one of the two must make a genuine concession or narrow their claim, and the dialogue should end with the disagreement relocated to a sharper place rather than resolved.
- The dialogue should not exceed 800 words. After it, add a note of no more than 120 words and nothing further.

In that note I want an epistemic accounting rather than a summary. Tell me which claims made in the dialogue reflect broad agreement among people who work on this subject, which are genuinely contested, and which are idiosyncratic to the positions I assigned — I have no way of checking, and I would much rather be told that one of my two protagonists holds a view nobody competent holds than read a polished exchange built on it. Say plainly if either character is being allowed to speak as though a disputed position were the default. And if some feature of my protocol has a standard diagnosis that I have described wrongly, correct me there.

Please give no references, no bibliography, and no titles of books or articles; I cannot look them up, and I would rather you spent the words on the argument. Names for the two characters are welcome but any will do.
