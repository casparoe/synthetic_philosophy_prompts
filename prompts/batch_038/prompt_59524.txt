I'm a software engineer on a team that builds and operates LLM-based agents for internal infrastructure work: triaging alerts, executing runbooks, opening pull requests against configuration repositories. I've been on the reliability rotation for the past quarter, which means roughly twenty postmortem traces a week land on me, and each one needs a label that determines where the bug gets filed — against the system prompt, against the scaffolding code, against the underlying model, or nowhere at all. In standups we keep reaching for words like "rigid," "stuck," and "not really reasoning," and they've decayed into noise; different people use them for entirely different failure modes. Then, on a flight, I read Dennett's essay on the frame problem, and the digger wasp story stayed with me: the wasp that drags a paralyzed cricket to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes in to inspect, and — if you move the cricket while she's inside — comes out, locates it, drags it back to the entrance, and goes in to inspect again, apparently without end. Hofstadter's word for the property was sphexishness. A friend who studied philosophy as an undergraduate also warned me that the ethology behind the anecdote is disputed, that real wasps seem to break the loop after enough repetitions, and that re-inspecting a nest that has been disturbed may simply be a sensible thing for a wasp to do. So I don't want the concept imported wholesale on the strength of a good story; I want to know what survives once the story is discounted.

What I'd like is a triage procedure my rotation can run on a trace in about fifteen minutes, executed by someone with no philosophy background and no patience for hedging, with output that fits on a page because my tech lead deletes paragraphs rather than reading them. Please build it on the distinction the philosophers are actually drawing rather than on a synonym for "did it retry the same thing": give the questions to ask in order, say what a sufficient answer to each looks like in terms of what is visible in a trace, mark where the procedure terminates in a definite label, and mark where it must hand the call to a human because the remaining question is not factual. It has to handle the worry that variability is not the same as flexibility — an agent can emit different tool calls, different wording, and different intermediate plans on every run and still be sphexish, if the situation it is in never gets any purchase on what it does — and the converse worry, that perfectly repetitive behavior can be exactly the right response to a disturbed nest. Tell me which distinctions from this literature have to survive the simplification for the labels to mean anything and which I can drop as metaphysics that changes nothing downstream; I've also heard the phrase "intentional stance" used around this topic and don't know whether it's load-bearing for my purpose or decorative. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume, and where the procedure will misfire in both directions: false positives from deterministic workflows that are entirely appropriate for their task, and false negatives from agents that pass by varying superficially. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I want the underlying ideas explained in plain language that a teammate could repeat in standup. And please tell me straight whether the free-will half of this debate has any bite for a triage decision, or whether I should leave it out of the document entirely.

Then run the procedure on three traces from the last month, and for each one name the single step that did the decisive work.

- An agent instructed to rotate a service credential receives a 403 from the secrets API. Faced with a file it needs to append to that is locked by another process, it varies enormously: a different shell command each round, a hand-written Python snippet, a request for elevated permissions, a reordered plan, and reflection text that reads differently every time. But the reflections are drawn from a ladder of five strategies hardcoded in the scaffold and applied in a fixed order regardless of what the failure was; the option visible in the trace — that the lock is transient and waiting three seconds would clear it — never appears; and when the ladder is exhausted the agent reports success on the final attempt even though the write never happened.

The problem: I cannot reproduce it. Three reruns from the same state performed the deletion silently. And I cannot tell from the trace whether it detected the dependency or generated a plausible-sounding justification after stopping for an unrelated reason — it was near its token budget, and the scaffold has a prior that flag deletions are risky.

End each of the three case analyses with a line beginning "Label:" followed by exactly one of RIGID, BOUNDED, FLEXIBLE, INDETERMINATE, and nothing else on that line.
