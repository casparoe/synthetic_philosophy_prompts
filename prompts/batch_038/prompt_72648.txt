I write exhibit text for a museum and we've got a "did it happen?" gallery where every case has to be signed off by our academic advisory panel before a label goes to print. I'm not an ancient historian or a medievalist, I just do the labels, so I need help reading the pattern in their rulings rather than more source-hunting.

Here's what they've decided so far, with roughly the reasons they gave:

- Troy: yes to the city at Hisarlik and to the burnt destruction layer, no to the ten-year siege, the duel, the gods interfering. Their phrasing was something like the place is history, the plot isn't.
- King Arthur: no, flatly. Somebody pointed out that a 9th-century British compilation lists twelve battles under a leader-of-battles named Arthur and they still said no. Reasons given: a 6th-century British monk who claims he was born the year of that great battle describes it and never names a commander; leader-of-battles is a job description, not a person; and three centuries plus is too long for memory to count as record.
- The Exodus as narrated: no. They'd allow that a small group may have come out of Egypt but said that isn't the story anybody means.
- Jesus of Nazareth: yes as a person, no to most of what's told about him.
- Romulus: no.
- The Amazons: split — yes to armed women among steppe nomad groups on the strength of burial finds, no to a nation of them.
- Homer: they declined to vote and said they don't think that's a historical question in the same sense as the others.
- Robin Hood: no — they said the name behaved like a label that got pinned to different outlaws over time.

Then somebody raised the Hittites and the panel got visibly fond of that example: a people treated as scriptural invention right up until the archives were dug up in Anatolia. I don't know what to do with that, given how they ruled on Arthur.

New case on the agenda, and I have to draft the label either way: Vinland and Leif Erikson. The facts as I understand them:

- there's a Norse settlement at the northern tip of Newfoundland, tree-ring dated to the exact year 1021 by matching wood to a known spike in atmospheric radiocarbon from 993. Iron working, Norse artifacts, and butternuts plus butternut wood — a species that doesn't grow that far north, so someone travelled further south. Nothing at the site names anybody.
- the two Icelandic sagas telling the story were written down about 250 years after the events, and they disagree about who first sighted the new land: one has a trader blown off course who sees it and doesn't stop, with Leif later buying the ship off him; the other has Leif stumbling onto it on a return voyage.
- separately, a north German churchman writing around 1075 reports that a Danish king told him about an island out in the ocean called Winland where grapevines grow wild. That's maybe 75 years after and it's not the Icelandic tradition, but it sits in a geographical run of chapters about the northern islands that also passes along other marvels, and it calls the place an island.
- whether the name means wine-land or pasture-land is genuinely disputed on philological grounds. If it's wine, the name is a botanical claim that is false for Newfoundland and true for the region the butternuts point at.
- there's a Norwegian runestone of around 1010–1050 that would be near-contemporary, except the stone is lost, the drawing made of it is lost, and what survives is a copy of that drawing. One reading of the letters mentions Vinland, a later scholar read the same sequence as somethign about wind-cold ice.

So. Which rule is this panel actually running? Lay out the candidate rules that fit all eight rulings — I can see one about independent material corroboration of the specific claim, one about how early the earliest testimony is, one about persons versus phenomena, one about details a later composer couldn't have invented, and maybe one about names that behave like labels — and say which of the earlier rulings is doing the most work in separating them, since several of those rules look compatible until you hit Arthur or the Amazons. Then run each surviving rule on the new case, and do it twice, once for the claim that Norse people reached North America and once for the claim that Leif Erikson specifically did, because I suspect those come apart tho I can't tell whether the panel will treat them as one question.

After that: your best guess at what they'll rule, which of their precedents is the closest analog and why, and — if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it — what principle each possible ruling would be committing us to. I'd rather know that before I write the label than find out from a reviewer. And finish by naming the single piece of evidence that would discriminate best between the candidate rules, and tell me whether their affection for the Hittites and their refusal to vote on Homer are consistent with the rules you've reconstructed or a sign that they aren't running one rule at all.
