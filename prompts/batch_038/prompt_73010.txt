**Draft: Leased Politics, or the Perishability of an Artifact's Political Properties**

*1. The question as the seminar frames it.* Winner's 1980 article asks whether artifacts have politics and answers that they do in two distinct ways. First, some technical arrangements become mechanisms for settling the affairs of a community, either because they were built for that purpose or because the process of building them was so biased in one direction that it reliably produces winners and losers; the illustration my class uses is the set of low-clearance overpasses on the Long Island parkways, which on the standard telling kept buses — and therefore a great many poor and Black beachgoers — away from Jones Beach. I am aware that the historical accuracy of that telling is disputed, and I use the case only because it is the case in front of me. Second, some technologies are inherently political: they require, or are strongly compatible with, a particular form of political association, as a large-scale nuclear weapons system arguably requires centralized and hierarchical authority where small-scale solar generation permits dispersed arrangements.

What interests me is that both categories locate an artifact's politics at the moment of its making. Whether the property is inscribed by design or emerges from the technical form, it is treated as something the artifact *has* — a standing disposition, on a par with its weight or its clearance height, retained for as long as the object endures. My claim is that this is a category mistake for a large class of artifacts, and that correcting it relocates the dispute between determinists and their constructivist critics.

*2. The observation.* Some artifacts have political properties that decay on the timescale of weeks unless labor is continuously supplied. Others have political properties that are, for practical purposes, permanent. And a third group has political properties that were made permanent deliberately, by removing the human labor that used to supply them. Call these frozen, leased, and hardened politics.

Leased politics: the painted bus-only lane, whose priority for transit riders exists only for as long as enforcement is performed; the public-housing elevator, which functions as an accessibility arrangement — and hence as a determinant of who can live on the twelfth floor — only while it is serviced; the wheelchair lift on a city bus, which is legally installed and politically inert on every day it is out of order; the staffed gate, the checkpoint, the signal box whose timing an operator sets by hand.

Frozen politics: the clearance height of a bridge; the concrete curb line of a separated bus lane; the gradient of a ramp; the footprint of a highway that divided a neighborhood. These produce their political effect without anyone renewing a decision.

Hardened politics: arrangements built so that nobody has to decide again. The automated gate that opens only to a credential. The prepaid utility meter that cuts supply when credit is exhausted. The bench with an armrest at its center. In each case a task formerly performed by a person with discretion — whether to admit someone, whether to disconnect a household, whether to ask a person to move along — has been delegated to a device whose defining feature is that it cannot be persuaded, bribed, threatened, pitied, or shamed. Here I am borrowing freely from two ideas in the seminar reader: Akrich's notion of a script that a designer writes into an object, which users may follow or deviate from, and Latour's example of delegating the work of holding a door shut to a spring, which purchases reliability at the cost of giving up control over when the door is shut.

*3. Two tests.* (i) The zero-maintenance counterfactual: if the maintenance budget for this artifact went to zero for a year, would it still perform its political function? If yes, the politics is frozen or hardened; if no, it is leased. (ii) The discretion audit: is there a human being whose job it is to apply this artifact case by case, and can that person decline? If yes, part of the artifact's politics is a politics of that person's labor, and it is contestable at the level of the labor, since the person can be organized, persuaded, bribed, or frightened. If no, contest must move either to the moment of installation or to the moment of removal, and there is nothing in between.

*4. What this does to the determinism debate.* The determinist says artifacts constrain; the constructivist replies that constraints are contingent social achievements that could have been otherwise. The maintenance view says each is right about a different class of case. Frozen politics is where the determinist is right: once capital is committed, the constraint ceases to be up for grabs, and changing it requires a new capital project or demolition — which is why opposition to a protected lane is fiercest in the week the curb is poured, and why removal campaigns afterward are so conspicuous. Leased politics is where the constructivist is right: the constraint is remade daily by people who could do otherwise, and so it remains permanently and cheaply contestable.

The prediction I take to be the interesting consequence: conflict over infrastructure should be episodic where politics is frozen and continuous where it is leased; and there should be a historical drift from leased toward hardened arrangements, because hardening is a way of purchasing durability. The elimination of discretion is a purchase of political stability, paid for in advance, in capital.

*5. Where I think the draft is weak.* (a) Almost everything requires some maintenance — a bridge needs inspection and eventually replacement — so the frozen/leased line may be a difference of degree rather than kind, and a distinction that everything satisfies to some extent does no argumentative work. (b) Hardened politics may simply be Winner's first category relabeled: designing an artifact to settle a question once and for all is precisely what he describes. (c) The zero-maintenance counterfactual may be counterpossible, since no jurisdiction actually lets a bridge go unmaintained for a year, and reasoning from an impossible antecedent licenses anything. (d) The drift claim in section 4 is asserted rather than argued.
