I'm a retired analytical chemist — thirty-five years in process labs, mostly resins and additives — and since I stopped going in I've fallen into philosophy head first, to my wife's considerable amusement. At the moment I'm doing a weekly Zoom tutorial with a philosopher who works on classical Indian epistemology, the Nyaya school and its four sources of knowledge: perception, inference, comparison, testimony. She's in Pune, I'm in Fife, so we meet at half four on a Tuesday with the dog snoring under my desk. The way it works is that I bring her cases out of my own working life — real ones, from the notebooks I kept — and she tells me whether the person in the case knew anything, and if so which pramana did the work. I find the whole inference/postulation boundary deeply confusing, which is why I'm writing to you rather than to her.

So far she's given me the following. A postdoc who'd never seen a bittern was told by a warden that it looks like a heron with a thicker neck and stands stock-still in the reeds; three weeks later she saw one in the field and knew what it was — my tutor said that was comparison, and was firm that it wasn't inference and wasn't the warden's word doing the work, "the knowledge lands when she sees it, not when she's told". A blank where a peak should have been in a chromatogram, from which a colleague concluded there was no contaminant: knowledge, not inference, "the absence is right there in the trace" — but when I pushed her on whether that was perception or a seperate source altogether she shrugged and said it wouldn't change anything we'd want to say about the case. A colleague who could reliably pick the right solvent for a stubborn coupling without being able to say why, and was right nine times in ten: not knowledge, "he can't tell you how he knows, so he can't tell you that he knows". Someone with a perfectly sound rule about smoke and fire who is actually looking at steam off a cooling tower: no knowledge, the mark isn't there so the rule has nothing to grip. I asked what happens if a trustworthy person flatly contradicts your eyes — the eyes win, every time, testimony is a real source but a dependent one. I asked about the mass spectrometer, surely it tells you things — "the machine isn't a speaker, it can't be trustworthy or untrustworthy; what you have is perception with a lot of apparatus in between, and it's only as good as your calibration". And once, when I asked how you get hold of a general rule without checking every instance, she said you don't get it by counting, and left it there, and I didn't have the nerve to ask again.

The case I'm taking her next Tuesday is a real one from 1994. We had a coating resin whose cure stalled at about sixty per cent conversion, reproducibly, run after run, same batch of everything — and both our purity checks said the resin was clean. Those two things can't both be true: clean starting material doesn't stop at a fixed number unless something in the pot is eating a fixed amount of the curing agent. So I said there was a scavenger in there, below the detection limit of both methods, and I dosed in extra curing agent in exactly the quantity the arithmetic implied, and the cure went to completion. I never detected the thing. Nobody ever did — the supplier quietly changed their process a year later and the problem went away. Nobody told me it was there, I never saw it, I had no list of similar cases to generalise from (its a new class of resin, we were among the first making it), and I couldn't have written down a rule linking that stalling pattern to that kind of impurity, because I'd never seen either end of it before. My old works manager, who I ran this past, just said "of course you knew, that's why we paid you".

Predict what she'll say. Which source will she name, or will she say I didn't know and only guessed well? I can see two readings of her track record and can't choose between them. On one she's a strict four-source person and she'll say I was inferring all along, that the rule was there and I simply hadn't spelled it out, and she'll point at the dosing experiment as the confirmation that settles whether I knew. On the other she'll say the two facts couldn't be held together otherwise, that this is a different sort of warrant from any rule-following, and that she'll call it postulation and be done with it. So: lay out the candidate rules she could be running; say which of my precedents actually pull weight and which are dead neutral; name the closest analogue to the 1994 case; give me your best guess at her verdict and why you prefer that reading; and then give me one further case — from a lab, a field site, anywhere, it doesn't matter — that I could put to her the week after and that would make the two readings come apart, saying what each predicts about it. If you think I've rigged the case as described, say so rather than playing along: if the dosing experiment already settles it, or if there's a general rule smuggled into my telling, I'd sooner hear that now than have her find it on Tuesday.

Two housekeeping things. Please write in British English. And be straight with me about how sure you are — put a rough confidence on the verdict you're predicting, and single out the one point in your answer you're least certain of; I've spent enough years looking at error bars to prefer knowing where someone is guessing to having it all sound equally firm.
