I'm one of seven trustees on a small land trust in a river valley — about 900 acres across four preserves, office rented above a hardware store, and everybody's a volunteer except our part-time stewardship person Dee, whos been here eleven years. I don't have any training in any of this, I run a print shop, and I ended up on the board mostly because I'm the one who fixes the trails. Here's the fight. Beavers have moved into the next watershed on their own in the last couple years, and Dee has spent a year putting together a plan to get them established on our Mill Brook preserve — a few flow devices so they don't wash out the township road, and then just letting them dam up about twelve acres of hardwood bottoms into wetland. Ecologically it's the best thing on our list: more birds, more amphibians, the brook stops going dry in August, and a donor left money specifically for "water in the valley." But Dee put numbers in her writeup and that's what blew up the last meeting. Her estimate is that the flooded soil and the new standing water support something like tens of millions of individual insects, worms, and midge larvae over fifty years, plus the beavers will girdle and kill a few hundred mature trees that then rot slowly, plus the mosquito hatch goes way up. One trustee, Ray, says that makes this wetland the single largest act of animal suffering our organization will ever commit, and that we're morally required to weigh it. Another, Marguerite, says that's insane, we can't count mayflies, and if we start doing arithmetic on invisible bugs we'll never do anything again — and she's not wrong that Ray's numbers swamp every other project we've ever funded, including the ones she's proudest of. So Ray has moved a standing rule: every project that comes to a board vote has to arrive with a written estimate of the suffering it creates and the suffering it prevents, and if the estimate is "unknown," that counts against the project. That rule and the Mill Brook vote are both on the agenda for the 14th, and I've been asked to circulate something beforehand because I'm supposedly the neutral one.

What I keep going around on, and what I actually want this memo to deal with, is that Ray and Marguerite both seem to assume that bringing an animal into a hard life is the same kind of thing as hurting an animal that's already here. I don't think it is but I can't say why in a way that'd survive Ray pushing on it. My own version — which I made up and which is probably wrong, or probably already somebody's idea — is that there are three kinds of bad in play, not two: suffering we cause in an animal that exists, suffering we could have prevented in an animal that exists and didn't, and suffering that only exists because we brought the sufferer into being. That third kind seems like it doesn't happen *to* anybody, so it isn't a harm to anyone, so maybe it doesn't belong on a ledger at all. But I notice that if you let the third kind off the hook entirely, you can excuse any amount of miserable life just by pointing out none of those animals would otherwise have existed, and that feels like a cheat. And I genuinely don't know what to do about Ray's scale problem: even if you say a beetle counts a hundredth of what a beaver counts, there are enough beetles that nothing else our trust does registers, and a rule that makes every other decision look pointless isn't a rule a seven-person volunteer board can actually run itself by.

So please write the memo I'll circulate. Two pages at most, plain words, no jargon — Marguerite has threatened to walk out if anyone says "sentience-weighted." It needs to give the board a clear yes / no / yes-with-conditions on both the Mill Brook project and Ray's standing rule, with reasons a trustee could actually argue back against, and one honest paragraph making the strongest case for whichever position you end up recommending against. Take my three-kinds idea head on and tell me whether it holds or where exactly it breaks. And if you think Ray's rule is a bad idea as written but his underlying worry is real, describe what a version of it we could live with would look like — who does the estimating, what happens when nobody knows, what a tie-breaker is. Start straight in on the memo itself: no preface, no summary of what I just told you, and put your recommendation in the very first line so that anyone who only reads one line knows where it landed.
