ok, slightly weird request, bear with me. my brother does agronomy consulting and he left a pesticide product label on my kitchen table last weekend — the full regulatory thing: signal word in caps up top, active and inert ingredients, precautionary statements, environmental hazards, directions for use with a reentry interval, storage and disposal, and that boilerplate about it being a violation of federal law to use the product in any manner inconsistent with its labeling. i read the whole thing over coffee like a weirdo and realized the genre is already a piece of applied moral arithmetic: it's a document that specifies exactly how many of which creatures may be killed, at what dose, at what time of day, and which bystander species have to be protected. so i want you to write me a parody of one that takes that arithmetic seriously rather than just poking fun at chemical companies.

the setup i want: a made-up firm — call it Verdanis Crop Solutions or something you like better — has just registered a new broad-spectrum systemic insecticide (invent the active ingredient, don't use a real trade name), and after some campaign pressure it has bolted an entirely new mandatory section onto the label called something like "Insect Welfare Considerations," drafted in-house with help from its Insect Welfare Advisory Panel. Write it as the label itself, ~650 words, in the visual register of a real label: headers, ALL-CAPS fragments, numbered statements, a small table, footnotes in tiny-print style. No narrator, no framing story, no joke about how funny this is — deadpan all the way through, and don't wink at the reader anywhere.

the jokes need to come out of the actual arguments people make about insecticides and insect suffering, so please build in at least these beats, each disguised as ordinary compliance language: (1) a moral-weighting table by taxon — aphids at 1.0, caterpillars higher, honey bees much higher — with a footnote that the factors were arrived at by consensus and are "subject to periodic review," i.e. calibrated to nothing; (2) sentience discounting, where harms are multiplied by a probability-of-sentience coefficient that the label conspicuously declines to state numerically; (3) a net-harm subsection reasoning that treated larvae would otherwise have died of starvation, fungal infection, or being eaten alive from the inside by parasitoid wasps, so the knockdown is partly a welfare benefit — immediately followed by the awkward admission that clearing this season's population leaves the field's carrying capacity fully available for next season's hatch; (4) a directions-for-use note that under-dosing is bad both for resistance management and on welfare grounds because sublethal exposure means slower deaths, so applying generously is framed as the kind thing to do; (5) the standard pollinator carve-out — no application during bloom, treat at dusk when bees aren't foraging — presented as though the reason were the bees' moral status rather than their usefulness; (6) an offsets scheme where the company purchases predator-habitat credits per hectare treated, and the label's own wording lets a careful reader notice that this relocates suffering rather than reducing it; and (7) a storage-and-disposal clause about the empty container and rinse water that raises a problem the drafters clearly didn't intend to raise.

a few constraints: nothing cited, no references, no real company or product names, and no footnotes explaining the punchlines. the target is the practice and the style of discourse — the way a genuinely hard question gets flattened into compliance prose — not the people who work on insect welfare; if someone who has actually read the arguments about wild insect suffering and insecticide use read this, i want them to laugh and think it's fair, not think you strawmanned the position or that you've never heard of it. so the reasoning inside each section should be the reasoning that's actually on offer in that debate, including the parts that are genuinely hard to resolve, just rendered in the flat voice of a document whose only job is to keep the applicator out of trouble.
