I am in my last year of high school and I am writing an independent research essay on whether the predictive success of a scientific theory is good evidence that the theory is true. I have assembled a list of historical cases and my own verdicts on them, and the verdicts will not settle into anything coherent. I would like to know what principle they add up to, if there is one, and which of them I have to abandon.

My cases and verdicts:

1. Fresnel's wave theory of light. It implied a bright spot at the center of the shadow cast by a circular disc, which nobody had expected and which was then observed. Its mechanism was an elastic solid ether filling all of space. Verdict: the theory was substantially right about light, the ether is nothing whatever, and the equations that survived count as a real preservation rather than a coincidence.

2. The caloric theory of heat. Carnot derived the upper limit on the efficiency of a heat engine by treating heat as a fluid falling from a high temperature to a low one, as water falls through a wheel. That limit is still taught and still exactly right. Verdict: caloric was simply false, nothing about it was preserved, and the theory's success is closer to a miracle than the ether case is. I realize these two verdicts cannot both be right on any obvious criterion. That is one of the places I want pressure applied.

3. Ptolemy's astronomy. Verdict: not even partly true. Yet the epicycle machinery is arguably still with us, since any periodic motion can be built out of circles on circles, and Ptolemy's tables predicted positions as well as anyone needed. I judge this case far more harshly than Fresnel's, and I cannot say what entitles me to.

4. Newtonian mechanics. Verdict: genuinely true inside its domain, and it is reasonable to go on believing it there. But an astronomer in 1600 could have said exactly the same thing about epicycles, with the same evidence, and been wrong, and I do not see what entitles me to think my position is different in kind rather than merely later.

5. Phlogiston. Verdict: wholly false, nothing preserved, and the term failed to refer, so statements made with it were neither true nor false rather than false. However, I also think that "water" as used in 1750 and "water" as used today pick out the same thing, even though everything believed about water changed. I am applying to phlogiston a treatment I refuse to apply to water, and I cannot articulate the difference.

6. The Bohr atom. It recovered the Balmer wavelengths, and wave mechanics still yields them. But the successor theory does not merely omit definite electron orbits; it rules them out. Verdict: the predictions were preserved, and still this theory seems to me worse off than Fresnel's, not equally well off. I cannot say why.

7. Electrons and quarks today. Verdict: these refer, and they will survive. But my reason seems to be that our best instruments and our best equations are saturated with them, and in 1887 the best interferometers in the world were saturated with the ether.

My tentative principle, which does not work: a discarded theory T is partly true just in case, for each of T's successful predictions, there exists a derivation of that prediction within the successor theory S that mentions none of T's abandoned vocabulary. I keep wanting to add a second clause — that the abandoned ontology must not have been load-bearing in T's own derivation, so that if it was mere decoration its loss costs nothing, while if it did the work the preservation is illusory. I suspect this clause is only a way of marking the exam after seeing the answers, and I want to be told so plainly if it is.

So: give me the principle, or a small number of candidates, stated precisely, and say which of my cases motivates each clause of it. Then tell me what it delivers for three cases I have not listed — Mendel's factors before anyone knew what a gene was made of; the vital force of early nineteenth-century chemistry; and dark matter today. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts must go, and in particular whether I have to surrender the caloric verdict or the ether verdict. I would rather have a simple principle that overturns one of my verdicts than an elaborate one that saves them all, but I want to be shown the trade rather than handed a system that quietly absorbs every case. I am not looking for sources or a reading list, only the principle and its consequences.
