I was at the potluck after our department's seminar last night and two people got into an argument that ran for like ninety minutes, and I never once managed to get a word in. Neither of them is a Buddhist studies person — R is in political philosophy, D is doing a masters in religious studies and reads Pali in a study group — so a lot of it was freelancing, but neither one was strawmanning and I honestly still can't tell who won. It started because D said Buddhist ethics is just consequentialism with different vocabulary: karma is a causal ledger, what determines the moral weight of an act is the intention behind it, the target is the reduction of dukkha, and the tradition itself contains override cases — the skillful-means story about the ship captain who kills a bandit to save the five hundred merchants and spare the bandit the karmic fallout of mass murder, and gets praised for it rather than blamed. That, D said, is not what a deontology looks like.

Here's roughly how it went after that. (1) R said D had misread the story: the captain is commended for the state of mind he acts from, and he takes the karmic hit on himself, which is an ethics of the agent's motivation, not of outcomes — and on the ordinary level the five precepts are framed as training rules someone undertakes, with the rule against false speech for monastics close to exceptionless even where the truth would be catastrophic, and if the theory were consequentialist you'd expect a whole literature of override cases and there basically isn't one. (2) D said that's a taxonomy trick: consequentialism doesn't have to be agent-neutral or textbook-maximizing, it's just the claim that acts are right in virtue of promoting some good; and if the good is the lessening of dukkha, made agent-neutral by the absence of any substantial self — Parfit makes basically this move about the separateness of persons and even notices his reductionism looks like anattā — then Buddhism is committed to impartial aggregation harder than Bentham was, and intention-based karma is just consequentialism with a longer causal model and a different currency, namely your own future experience. (3) R said two things: first, if non-self dissolves the agent it dissolves the patient too, so there's no one whose suffering is being minimized, and the thing promoted is the stopping of the whole process rather than any outcome inside the world — optimizing welfare within samsara is like rearranging the deck chairs on a burning ship, the target is getting off; second, merit transfer treats moral credit as a transferable currency rather than something located in consequences, and the bodhisattva vow is about as agent-relative as a requirement gets, since it's a promise I make about me, not something that falls out of an impartial calculus. (4) D said the vow is just the maximizing constraint with a personal pronoun attached — "I won't enter final nirvana until all beings are liberated" is literally a global optimization target — and merit transfer is a mechanism for spreading the good, which is what a total-welfare consequentialist would want. (5) R said D had by now defined the good to include compassion and wisdom as constitutive rather than merely instrumental, and a consequentialism whose good already contains the virtues predicts nothing, so the label stops doing work. (6) D said elasticity cuts both ways, that "not consequentialist" is just as cheap, and challenged R to name one case where the tradition forbids the act that plainly minimizes dukkha for reasons that aren't about the agent's own purity. (7) R offered the monastic rules — no handling money, no storing food, no false speech even to save a life — and D said those are internal rules of a voluntary order, like the regulations of a guild, not moral requirements at all, and that conflating the two was R's mistake from the start. They both went home annoyed.
