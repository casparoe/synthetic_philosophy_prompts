I'm in a translation theory seminar this semester, and we've just worked through Hofstadter's Le Ton beau de Marot — the book that collects dozens of English versions of one short poem by Clément Marot, each attempting to preserve the original's tight formal constraints, and then argues at length about what the successes and failures reveal. I'd like to turn that question back onto ordinary publishing practice instead.

When a university press issues a new English version of a poem written in strict rhyme and meter — a sonnet sequence, a rhymed narrative — the translator nearly always delivers unrhymed free verse, and the preface explains that rhyme and meter are simply casualties, while sense, imagery, and voice are what the translator is answerable for carrying across. Yet the same industry handles adjacent tasks quite differently: adapters are paid to fit translated dialogue to actors' lip movements and shot lengths, and hymn and opera translators write entirely new words into a fixed melody and syllable count, where the constraint is arguably more severe than a rhyme scheme.

What conception of what a poem is, or of what meaning consists in, does the free-verse default presuppose? Is it committed to the claim that a poem's content is separable from its form and survives intact once the form is dropped, or could the practice be justified without that claim — on grounds of cost, readability, or the risk of distorting the sense in order to land a rhyme? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely happens to fit it; say whether the dubbing and hymn-tune cases are genuinely comparable or differ in some relevant respect; and then describe what publishing practice would have to become under the rival view, the one on which a version that abandons the pattern is not a faithful but damaged copy of the poem, but a different work altogether.
