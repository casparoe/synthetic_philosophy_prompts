A journal is brokering an adversarial collaboration between two parties who have argued for a decade over when an inherited practice may be abolished and have never managed to produce a single document both could sign. Draft that document.

The parties are to be characterized by their commitments, not by any school or lineage:

**Party A (the Fence Evidentialist).** When a practice has persisted a long time and its practitioners cannot say why they keep it, that conjunction is itself evidence that the practice serves a function no one has articulated. The reformer therefore bears a burden of proof that rises with the practice's age and with the degree of its opacity.

**Party B (the Selection Debunker).** Persistence is produced by many processes indifferent to function: concentrated benefit paired with dispersed cost, coordination lock-in, adaptation of preference to whatever option is available, and the recruitment of articulate defenders from among those the practice advantages. Longevity and opacity are therefore weak evidence on their own, and the conservative owes a positive account of what the practice is doing.

Both parties accept the following stipulated cases as their shared test set. Treat the stated facts as exhaustive and do not supplement them.

*Case 1 — the legacy requirement.* A jurisdiction requires candidates for a building-inspector license to complete 400 hours of instruction in a drafting method no practicing office has used in fifty years. The requirement was introduced roughly ninety-five years ago, on the proposal of a members' association whose records survive only in part. Current inspectors cannot explain its purpose and, when pressed, offer mutually inconsistent guesses. Removal is projected to cut training cost by a fifth, and a five-year observation window shows no change in inspection pass rates; no data exist on failure modes with latencies longer than five years.

*Case 2 — the second signature.* A hospital requires an independent second sign-off before discharging patients from one ward, adding a mean delay of eleven hours. No current staff member knows what it prevents. The policy predates the electronic record. It has been proposed for removal three times; each proposal was withdrawn within a month of an unrelated adverse event elsewhere in the hospital. Readmission data for the ward before a certain date do not exist.

*Case 3 — the setback.* A municipal code requires every new dwelling to stand nine metres back from the street line with nothing built or parked in the band. The rule is about 120 years old. Surveyed residents overwhelmingly dislike it and cite land cost. It survives because the code can be amended only as a whole and no coalition has formed. There is a plausible story that the band was once needed for turning vehicles and fire apparatus; the fire authority has neither endorsed nor denied it, and no record of the original specification's rationale survives.

*Case 4 — the transparent permit.* A licensing regime fixes the number of permits for a service; the permits are transferable and have appreciated substantially. Every party, including permit holders, can state exactly what the regime does: restrict supply and raise incumbent returns. No one claims an unarticulated function. Holders advance quality and safety arguments; those arguments have been tested and the evidence is mixed.

The document must contain:

1. **Agreed claims**, numbered. Include at minimum: what opacity does and does not license an observer to infer; the distinction between a function of a practice and a mere effect of it; whether opacity comes in degrees or is a categorical condition, and whether each party's position survives the other answer; and the error asymmetry each party accepts, expressed as a trade ratio — how many practices abolished that later prove functional each side will tolerate per dysfunctional practice successfully removed. Where the same claim is signed for different reasons, record both reasons.

2. **A table of residual disagreements**: Party A's position and Party B's position, each worded so that its own party would accept it as its view; a column marking the disagreement substantive or verbal; and, where verbal, the terminological fix that dissolves it.

3. **The cruxes** — those sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one party. For each, state what kind of evidence or argument both parties accept as bearing on it, then record each party's prediction for all four cases, written down in advance and specific enough to be wrong.

4. **Concessions.** For each case, name the party the facts favor and state whether the other party concedes it. I expect at least one concession on each side; if you cannot produce one for a party, explain what that inability shows about the character of the disagreement.

5. **A closing determination**: whether the dispute about the evidential weight of persistence is resolvable on the merits or bottoms out in differing priors about how often non-functional selection processes operate — and, if the latter, whether the parties nonetheless converge on the same verdict for most actual practices, and what such convergence would imply about the stakes of the dispute.

Do not quote, cite, or supply a bibliography or reading list for any classic statement of either position; the value of the document lies entirely in its formulations. Where you judge that one party has committed a category error, include the charge in the document, phrased so that the accused party would accept it as a fair statement of the objection even while rejecting it.
