Our decision theory reading group is doing a session on the Pasadena paradox and I'm on the hook for bringing a case that isn't just the original gamble read back at everyone. You know the one: toss a coin until heads, payoffs grow without bound and alternate in sign, so the expectation is a conditionally convergent series that can be rearranged to anything. Half my group thinks the whole thing is a mathematical parlor trick, and their line is basically: the game itself fixes an ordering of the outcomes — heads on toss 1, heads on toss 2, and so on — so write the series in that order, get ln 2, done, there's a perfectly good expectation and the "undefined" talk is just people ignoring that Riemann rearrangement needs you to *choose* a weird order. I want to hand them a case where that reply has nothing to grab onto, and I think the way to do it is to make the outcome space two-dimensional so the description builds in two equally legitimate orderings. No references or bibliography please, I know the literature and I don't want you guessing at page numbers.

Here's the spec. Two independent, physically ordinary random processes, each of which stops after some finite random number of steps — two coins each tossed until heads, two printers that each jam after a random number of pages, whatever you like, but it has to be something you could set up on a table. Let n and m be the two stopping times. One payoff, given as a rule statable in a couple of lines (it may key off simple relations between n and m, like "if the two processes stop together" or "if m is exactly twice n", but not a big lookup table), unbounded in magnitude and alternating in sign, such that: (a) the resulting expectation series is condtionally but not absolutely convergent — terms go to zero, the sum of absolute values blows up — so there's no strong expectation in Nover and Hájek's sense; and (b) the two natural iterated sums, i.e. summing over m within each fixed n versus summing over n within each fixed m, both converge and converge to *different* finite numbers. Condition (b) is the whole point: neither grouping is more faithful to how the game is described than the other, so "just use the order the game gives you" doesn't tell the agent which number is the expectation. Two hints since this is fiddly: you'll probably find it easier if one of the two iterated sums comes out exactly zero, and you'll want the probabilities of the joint outcomes to decay fast enough that the huge payoffs still leave terms tending to zero. Dress the thing up as something other than a casino making you an offer — a grant, a bet between two labs, a maintenance contract — but keep the mechanics mundane, no predictors, no exotic physics. Scenario in about two paragraphs of prose a non-mathematician can follow, with the formulas saved for the demonstration.

Then show me it actually works. State the payoff rule, compute both iterated sums explicitly with the steps visible, and say in plain terms why the double series fails to converge absolutely. After that, do the symmetry check honestly: argue that neither conditioning order is privileged by the description, and if you end up thinking one of them *is* privileged, say so and tell me how to fix the case rather than papering over it. Then tell me which single feature is doing the work — my hunch is the independence of the two processes, but I could be wrong — and run the removal test: what happens if you collapse to one process, if you cap one process at a finite maximum, or if you bound the payoffs. Finally, give me the best attacks available to someone who wants to dissolve the case instead of accepting it: the bounded-utility response, the "weak expectations are all decision theory ever needed" response, and the "no finite bank can back this game" response — and for each, say whether it gets them out of *this* case or only out of the one-coin original. Last line should start with "Verdict:" and give one short phrase saying whether the natural-ordering reply survives the case.
