I'm drafting a chapter for an edited collection on cross-border reproductive markets, and I've run into a piece of rhetoric that my co-author and I keep tripping over. She defends the American model — compensated gestational arrangements brokered by agencies in the states that will issue a pre-birth parentage order — against the continental European position, where such agreements are treated as void because the human body is held not to be at a person's disposal to alienate, and where at least one country has recently made it an offence for its own citizens to commission a surrogacy in a jurisdiction where the practice is entirely legal. Her argument runs through a picture both sides use: the carrier rents out her womb. Pressed, she cashes it out as a lease. The carrier is lessor; the intended parents are lessees; the demised premises are the uterus for a fixed term of roughly nine months; the compensation is rent; the behavioural covenants in a standard agency contract — no alcohol, travel restrictions, agreement to terminate on medical advice — are covenants as to use; the handover of the child is surrender of possession in the agreed condition; and independent legal advice, disclosure of medical history, and psychological screening are the ordinary due diligence one expects before granting a term. Her conclusion is that nothing in this structure is distinctive, since we permit leases of bodily labour and of body-adjacent capacities all the time, from employment contracts to compensated plasma donation to the wet-nursing that European families bought openly for two centuries; so if the arrangement is a lease, the European objection is squeamishness about the price rather than about the structure, and the right response is lease-style regulation — standard-form terms, cooling-off periods, caps on liquidated damages, mandatory separate counsel — rather than prohibition. I find this persuasive and unpersuasive at the same time and cannot yet say which.

Could you audit the analogy for me? First, set out the correspondence table properly: what in a compensated gestational arrangement actually occupies each of those roles, and which roles have no occupant at all. I especially want to know where the child sits in the mapping, since a lease appears to have no slot for a third person produced during the term — and if the child is the product of the lease, then the picture has quietly turned from a conveyance of use into a contract for the manufacture and delivery of goods, which is precisely the charge the analogy was meant to rebut. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for her intended use from those that are merely picturesque. Three candidates I'd like pressure-tested: that a lease transfers possession and control of the premises to the lessee, whereas the carrier never loses possession or control of her own body, so the so-called covenants as to use are not restrictions on the use of a thing but directives about another person's conduct; that, as far as I understand the American cases, no court has actually ordered a carrier to comply with those covenants, the remedy being damages at most, which makes it unclear what legal work the lease vocabulary is doing; and that a lease presupposes an inert, fungible subject matter whose condition can be assessed at surrender, whereas the relevant subject matter here is a person whose interests do not end with the term. I'd also like you to say what the framing imports that nobody has argued for — that the lessor's stake is purely economic, that the term ends cleanly with no residual obligations, that third parties are strangers to the instrument rather than beneficiaries of it.

Finally, does her conclusion survive the disanalogies, or was it carried by the analogy all along? Please take seriously the opposing reading that a colleague of mine pressed, namely that the analogy does not answer the commodification objection but simply states it: describing a pregnancy as the grant of a term of years already concedes that the body is alienable property, which is exactly what the European position denies, so the two sides are not disagreeing about the facts but about whether that concession has been made. Is that reading right, or is it a rhetorical slide of its own? And if the lease is the wrong source domain, which alternative would do better and what would each license — a personal services contract with the familiar limits on enforcing one, a bailment, an agency relationship in which the carrier acts for the intended parents, a joint venture, or a contract for work and materials? Be concrete about what each choice would imply for the live regulatory questions: enforceability of the handover, the status of the behavioural terms, and who may withdraw and when. One constraint: no bibliography and no survey of who has said what, since I'll add my own references, and I'm not interested in the empirical facts of the two markets either. What I need is the structure of the comparison.
