I spent forty years in a sensory physiology lab, retired two years back, and have fallen into philosophy of mind — specifically the higher-order stuff, the idea that a mental state is conscious only if some further state takes notice of it. Please play the part of a philosopher of mind who actually reads the vision literature and has thought hard about these theories. Think carefully, and before you commit to anything, brainstorm: list three or four candidate principles for what makes a state conscious, run each one against my cases below, and show me the scores in a little table. Then pick one and defend it.

Here are my verdicts, mostly from things I saw at the bench, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them all.

1. When I was really absorbed setting up a rig, a colleague could say something to me and I wouldnt hear it — but two minutes later I could repeat it word for word. At the moment he spoke, I want to say there was nothing it was like to hear him.
2. A stimulus flashed and masked so the subject sincerely denies seeing anything, yet it shifts their reaction times on the next trial. No experience, though the information was plainly there.
3. Same verdict for the blindsight patient who localises well and insists he saw nothing.
4. My old dog chasing a ball. Absolutely something it was like for him. And I doubt very much he had any thoughts about his seeing.
5. Twelve letters flashed for about a twentieth of a second. I could report only four, but I have a strong feeling I saw all twelve — the whole grid was there and then faded. I want to say I did see them all.
6. The two-dots illusion where the colour seems to change halfway across, before the second dot has even appeared (colour phi, I think its called). The subject reports it that way and I want to take the report at face value: the experience got written up afterwards, and that doesn't bother me. Except it does bother me a bit, because it seems to make case 1 shaky — maybe I did hear my colleague and my experience was simply edited.
7. My own case, not from the literature. Suppose you could arrange, with a headset and enough suggestion, a subject who is utterly confident he saw a red square on a blank screen — the "noticing" fires with nothing underneath it. I want to say he has no red experience, he's just mistaken. But if he is introspectively and behaviourally indistinguishable from someone hallucinating a red square, I can't say what's missing. I keep flip-flopping on this one.

5 against 1 and 2 is the pair I can't reconcile, and 7 makes it worse.

Now, my own tentative principle, made up out of my own habits of thinking and possibly nonsense: a state is conscious iff some monitoring process reads it out *and* that read-out can adjust the gain on the state itself — a closed loop, running at the time, not a one-way report filed somewhere else. I like it because the dog only needs a crude loop, no concepts, and because case 1 is just a loop that wasn't closed on the auditory channel. Does it survive contact with 5, 6 and 7? Does it collapse back into the higher-order view, or into something more like a global broadcasting story, and is that a problem or a feature?

What I want out of you: state the principle you end up with precisely, clause by clause, saying which of my cases each clause is there to handle. Then tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and what it says about two cases I havent listed — driving home and realising you don't remember the last five miles (did you experience them?), and one further case of your own choosing that you think most embarrasses the principle you've settled on.

No bibliography, no rundown of who said what — I know the main names roughly and I'm not going to look anything up. And tell me how confident you are: which of my verdicts you'd bet against overturning, and the single step in your reasoning you're least sure of. I'd much rather you commit and mark your weak point than hedge everything into mush.
