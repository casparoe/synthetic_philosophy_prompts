I'm in a philosophy of mathematics seminar on informal evidence and I need an essay rather than a survey. The case I want worked: Cramér's 1936 random model treats each integer n as prime independently with probability 1/log n, and the same presumption of independence underwrites both the Hardy–Littlewood counts of prime constellations and the model's prediction that the largest gap below x grows like (log x)². Maier's theorem then shows the presumption is literally false at short scales — the number of primes in intervals of length (log x)^A fluctuates instead of settling — and the response was not to abandon the family of conjectures but to keep them and replace the constant 1 with 2e^(-γ).

Write an essay arguing whether a heuristic whose stated independence assumption has been refuted can still confer rational support on its conclusions. Take both poles seriously: the view that refutation simply voids the inference, and the view that Maier discredited a crude model rather than the reasoning that generated the conjectures. Then assess a distinction I keep reaching for — that statistics averaged over many disjoint blocks survive weak dependence, while extremal statistics, set by the single worst block, do not — and say whether it can be made principled or is only a post hoc rescue of the heuristics we already like. Commit to a verdict you can defend; don't end by balancing considerations.
