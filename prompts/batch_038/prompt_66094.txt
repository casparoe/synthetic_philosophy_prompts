I am co-organizing a two-day workshop that mixes developmental biologists, cognitive scientists, and philosophers of science, and the recurring fight has nothing to do with anyone's data. One faction says that any time you cite a higher-level process — a developmental bias, a cultural transmission channel, a selection regime operating over niche-modified environments — you are either covertly re-describing the lower level or smuggling in a mind-first lifter that presupposes the very competence it was offered to explain. The other faction says the first faction keeps skipping levels in its eagerness to fasten everything to the foundation, and explains away what it has not explained. Both sides invoke Dennett's crane-and-skyhook contrast and the greedy-versus-good reductionism distinction, and both use them as insults rather than as instruments. I want to replace that with an actual sorting device: a decision tree whose input is one researcher's answers about one proposed explanation, and whose output is the name of the stance that researcher has adopted. Seven to nine nodes, each phrased as a concrete question about the explanation under discussion — not about the researcher's allegiances — and answerable by someone who has never encountered the vocabulary. No node may contain the words crane, skyhook, reductionism, or emergence; if the labels appear in the questions, the instrument sorts people by what they call themselves, which is exactly what I do not want. Order the nodes by diagnostic power, and at each one say briefly what a yes and what a no each commit you to.

The leaves need to include at least: reduction that keeps the intermediate accelerators in view; level-skipping reduction of the nothing-but variety; the view that the higher level is real but causally redundant because the lower level already fills the causal role; the view that the higher level is real, novel, and in principle underivable from the base while still governing it; the weaker view on which the higher level is in principle derivable but only by running the whole process forward, so the derivation exists yet tells you nothing usable; the view that the phenomenon is real and irreducible but that we cannot and will not explain how it arises from the base; the view that the account does not discharge the burden so much as relocate it to some other domain; and a genuine top-down lifter, plus whatever else you think belongs in the space. Cap it at about twelve leaves. Any cell nobody actually defends should be labeled unnamed rather than filled with a name you have to stretch for, and every leaf should carry one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders least like being asked about.

Then run six proposals through the tree, tracing the path in each case and, for each, naming the single node whose answer, if flipped, would move the proposal to a different leaf: (a) an account of cross-linguistic regularities in basic color naming that derives them from cone-opponent physiology plus the statistics of natural illumination, treats linguistic categories as shifting boundaries only, and claims no appeal to those categories is needed to predict the naming data; (b) a colleague's proposal that coded translation could not have arisen from a substrate as simple as the one available, so the machinery arrived from elsewhere, with the further condition that what happens at that elsewhere is explicitly out of scope; (c) a single-mutation account of syntactic embedding on which a small genomic change with a large effect produced the capacity directly, gradual selection on communication being ruled out — but the same theorist holds that once the capacity existed, cultural transmission amplified it enormously; (d) an account of extinction-size distributions in terms of interacting ecosystems poised near a critical state, requiring no external catastrophe; (e) our visiting speaker's proposal that a certain measure of integrated internal differentiation is a fundamental physical property, and that systems above a threshold thereby hold causal powers over their own parts that the parts alone do not possess; and (f) an eliminativist about machine understanding who says the folk term picks out nothing at all, yet insists the replacement description must be pitched at the level of the training dynamics rather than the token statistics. Note which of the six change leaf depending on auxiliary commitments the theorist has not stated. Finally, lay out the logical space the tree generates: list the combinations of answers it permits that no leaf occupies, and for each say in one line whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply tried it; and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate, saying what additional question would separate them. Keep it to something I can print on two pages for a mixed room of working scientists and graduate students, and please do not append a bibliography, a reading list, or suggestions about whom we should assign — I only want the instrument and the diagnosis.
