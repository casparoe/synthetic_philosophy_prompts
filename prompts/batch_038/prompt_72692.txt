Panel on housing and labor policy in three weeks and I'm supposed to be the "consistent" one on it, so: audit me. Here's what I actually hold.

1. Price controls get judged on outcomes only — what they do to the level and distribution of the price, to quantity, to quality. I don't think there's anything wrong as such with overriding a voluntary deal both sides agreed to.
2. The minimum wage should go up a lot. Low-wage labor markets are monopsonistic — employers are wage-setters — so a well-placed floor raises pay without costing jobs, and maybe even adds them. No real efficiency tradeoff to argue about.
3. Rent control is bad policy. It lets maintenance slide, mismatches people to units that no longer fit them, and over the long run chokes off new supply.
4. But housing supply in expensive cities genuinely is inelastic, because of zoning and permitting — incumbent landlords have real market power.
5. If you want to help poor tenants, the right instrument is a subsidy aimed at them, not a cap on the rent. More broadly I think targeted cash is first-best for almost any distributional problem, though feasibility counts: a policy no legislature will enact helps nobody.
6. The near-unanimous economist hostility to price floors and ceilings is a good deal of status-quo bias. And I do think there's something degrading about someone working full time who can't cover rent.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, ranked by how bad the damage is, and which only look like they conflict? For each real one, say what would have to give, and then tell me the cheapest single revision that makes the whole set cohere and what it costs me. Please don't just re-run the standard case for or against either policy — I want to know if I'm applying my own logic evenly.
