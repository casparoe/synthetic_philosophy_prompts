I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have just published a long feature arguing that the ongoing crisis over how we measure the capabilities of machine-learning systems is, at bottom, a revival of the problem of induction. My editors were skeptical; the piece ran anyway. A workshop on evaluation methodology, co-located with one of the large machine-learning conferences, has now invited me to give a thirty-minute talk drawn from the article, followed by twenty-five minutes of questions from the floor. I would like a preparation sheet for that question period.

The thesis I intend to defend, in the order I will defend it:

1. A benchmark is not a measuring instrument but an inductive one. A score is a projection from a finite, examined sample to a claim about an unexamined competence, and that projection is licensed only if the predicate it uses is projectible in Goodman's sense.
2. Goodman's predicate *grue* — examined before some fixed date *t* and green, or not so examined and blue — shows that "every emerald examined so far is green" and "every emerald examined so far is grue" are equally supported by the same evidence while projecting incompatibly. The field's standard predicates (accuracy, held-out loss, contamination-free score, parameters, tokens) are projectible because they are entrenched through long use, not because they carve nature at a joint.
3. My own coinage, which carries most of the weight: the exam-time clause. Every evaluation carries an implicit date index — the training-corpus cutoff, the test-set embargo, the vintage of the task family. Historically that clause was a formality. It has stopped being one, because the systems under evaluation now generate a substantial share of the text that later systems are trained on, and because benchmark authors increasingly draft tasks with model assistance. Predicates whose extensions were once fixed by examination are becoming projectible through sheer accumulation of examination. I call this retro-entrenchment, and I argue it explains why a number that used to function as a capability measure now often functions as a measure of overlap.
4. A second coinage: predicate debt. A laboratory that runs the same task family for years accumulates confidence that is not evidence, because entrenchment of the predicate is silently doing the work that sample size appears to be doing. The debt is repaid discontinuously — as a sudden collapse of trust in a whole family of results, rather than as a gradual decay.
5. My prescription: evaluations should be engineered to be grue-resistant. Rotate task families faster than models can be trained against them; report capability claims only relative to a declared predicate and an explicit date index; and treat any benchmark that has survived more than roughly two model generations as presumptively contaminated.

Objections I expect in broad outline, though I cannot predict their form: that *grue* is a linguistic curiosity already defused by Putnam's observation that time-indexedness depends on the language one starts from; that an appeal to entrenched predicates is conservatism dressed as a criterion; that everything I describe is ordinary overfitting, leakage, and distribution shift, all of which have standard remedies and none of which require Goodman; that I am conflating the new riddle with the underdetermination of theory by data; that I am not a practitioner and my account of how benchmarks get built is assembled from abstracts; and that my prescription is unimplementable, since rotating task families destroys the longitudinal comparability that is the main reason anyone builds a benchmark in the first place.

The room will hold about sixty people: benchmark builders and applied researchers whose professional standing depends on leaderboard credibility; several members of industrial evaluation teams who are contractually obliged to be diplomatic; two or three statisticians who think in exchangeability, priors, and error bars and who regard philosophical framing as upholstery; one philosopher of science who knows this literature properly, has attended because it is her area, and is not friendly; and a scattering of policy people who will hear all of it as an argument for regulation and will ask whether I am making one.

Please prepare me as follows. Give me eight to ten objections, each marked for likelihood and for damage, and ordered by expected damage rather than by the sequence in which they will arrive. Phrase each one the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it, in that person's own vocabulary — the statistician should not sound like the philosopher, and neither should sound like the policy person. For each, supply a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes of speech, and a note on where the reply is soft: the step an objector can press once I have stopped talking. Then tell me which two or three points I should concede in my opening five minutes to disarm the room, and in what exact form the concession should be made; identify the single reply that has to land for the talk to be judged substantive rather than a well-written outsider's essay; and give me the question I am hoping nobody asks, with a two-sentence holding answer in case it comes anyway.

Two constraints. I will not be able to look anything up at the lectern, and I do not want to spend the week before the talk reading philosophy, so give me no bibliography, no citations, no suggested further reading, and no attributions you are not confident of; if a name or a date is load-bearing, include it only if you are sure of it. Names of positions and standard technical terms are welcome. Finally, do not write the talk for me — write the preparation, and close with one blunt paragraph naming the weakest of my five moves above and saying what I should stop claiming because of it.
