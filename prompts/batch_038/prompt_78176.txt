I am a third-year graduate student in political philosophy, and for the past year I have been volunteering two evenings a week at a legal-aid clinic that does intake for asylum claims. The clinic's director — a former immigration judge, not a philosopher, and one of the sharpest people I know — defends our triage policy with an argument she reconstructed for me after I pressed her on why we turn away some applicants we could plainly help. I find every premise attractive and the conclusion intolerable, and I have spent three weeks failing to locate the break. Her chain, cleaned up and numbered by me:

(1) What makes someone a refugee in the morally relevant sense is not why they fled but that they are unprotected: they face serious harm, and no state that currently has them is willing or able to shield them from it. (2) The persecution test in the 1951 Convention, as generalized by the 1967 Protocol — a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, coupled with being unable or unwilling to avail oneself of the protection of one's country of nationality — is best read as a crude administrative proxy for (1), since persecution is simply the most common mechanism by which a state renders its own citizens unprotected. (3) So the criterion for who counts is the protection gap, and the criterion for who must take them in is a separate matter of allocation: a duty to fill a gap falls on whoever can fill it at the lowest cost, because a duty that no allocation principle sorts out is a duty nobody discharges. (4) Relative to the states adjacent to most crises — typically poorer, and typically already hosting far more displaced people per capita than we are — wealthy states with functioning asylum systems can fill protection gaps at lower cost. (5) Therefore wealthy states, ours included, are duty-bound not merely to fund and not merely to accept a token quota, but to admit those in protection gaps whom they can reach. (6) Yet nothing in (1)–(4) bounds the set of people a wealthy state can reach: it takes in people sitting in camps on other continents, people exposed to drought or to the collapse of a health system, and people whose own governments are willing but too poor to protect them; and because (3) allocates by capacity, that set expands as our capacity expands and contracts only when other states grow richer. (7) A criterion of who counts and who must take them in that assigns our state an unbounded and permanently growing admissions duty — one that gets larger the more competent and generous we prove ourselves to be — cannot be the right criterion; worse, it makes our duty heaviest precisely where our causal contribution to the gap is weakest. (8) So (1) is false after all: why someone fled does matter, and the persecution requirement is doing independent normative work rather than serving as a proxy.

I would like a diagnosis, and I would like it to be specific. Which step do you reject, and why did that step look unobjectionable when I first read it? For what it is worth, I would keep (1) and (4) at almost any price, and I am most suspicious of (3), but I cannot articulate the suspicion: every attempt I make to reject (3) ends with me saying something like capacity matters, only not in the way (3) has it using capacity, which sounds like a redescription rather than a denial. So tell me whether some step is actually invalid — and if so, under what reading of which phrase the premises stop supporting the conclusion — or whether the chain holds and I am simply unwilling to pay what (8) costs. Then price the alternatives: what does the clinic owe if it gives up (3), what does it owe if it gives up (5) instead, and does the collapse of anything before (8) actually entitle (8), or is (8) riding along on the argument's failure rather than following from it? Finally, apply the diagnosis to a case. Two applicants came to intake in the same fortnight. A is a journalist against whose government's fabricated charges she is being prosecuted, and whose lawyers expect a warrant within months. B fled a district where the water table has collapsed; her national government is willing to help but cannot feed the district, and our country's aid programs have been operating there for six years. We had resources to process exactly one file that quarter, and (5) seems to route both of them to us while offering no way to rank them. Does a correct diagnosis of the argument supply the ranking, or does the ranking depend on something the argument never mentions?
