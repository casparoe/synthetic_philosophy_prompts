I am a junior and the editor of my school's literary magazine. In the spring our submissions policy was changed so that only work addressing an identifiable social or political question is eligible for publication, and the faculty adviser who proposed the change defended it by appeal to Du Bois — specifically to the essay we read in class on the criteria of Negro art, where he holds that all art is propaganda and must ever be. I am paraphrasing rather than quoting him, since I am working from the class reader and do not want to put words in his mouth.

I have spent the last few weeks trying to reconstruct that defense as an argument, partly because I think it deserves a better version than the one I heard in the meeting, and partly because I now suspect the version I built proves considerably more than anyone in that room was prepared to accept. It runs as follows.

(1) A work is propaganda if and only if it is a communication designed to move its audience's attitudes rather than to state facts about the world.
(2) Every work of art expresses its maker's attitude toward its subject and is offered to an audience in order to move that audience's attitude; a piece of writing that did neither would be a specimen, not a poem.
(3) Therefore every work of art is propaganda. This is not a claim about some art or about art in some circumstances; it follows from what art is.
(4) If every work of art is propaganda, then there is no property a work has in virtue of being art that it does not also have in virtue of being propaganda.
(5) Aesthetic value is a property a work has in virtue of being art.
(6) Therefore aesthetic value is a property a work has in virtue of being propaganda, which is to say that judging a work aesthetically is judging how effectively it performs the function that (1) assigns to propaganda.
(7) Therefore aesthetic criticism is a department of political criticism, and an appeal to beauty that declines to say what effect the work is aiming at is either confused or evasive.
(8) Therefore a policy admitting only work with a discernible political aim is the correct general policy for a literary magazine.

I cannot accept (7) or (8), and I would like to know why they fail rather than simply asserting that they do. I want to flag one thing about my own sympathies so that you can calibrate the answer: I am not trying to rescue art for art's sake at Du Bois's expense. I find (3) genuinely persuasive and would much rather keep it than give it up, and I suspect a defender of our policy could retreat to a weaker claim that survives intact. What I cannot do is locate the failure. My unease is concentrated in (4) through (6), because those steps are supposed to carry the whole weight of turning a claim about what art does into a claim about what art is worth, and I cannot tell whether they are invalid or whether they are valid and resting on something false that merely looks true.

Please give me a diagnosis. Name the step or steps you reject; for each, explain why it looked unobjectionable to me — which reading of a term, or which familiar pattern of inference, makes it feel as though it follows — and if any term is doing double duty somewhere between (1) and (8), say where the switch happens and what the argument loses at that point. Then price the alternatives for me: what Du Bois's position costs if (3) survives but (4) does not; what an aestheticist position costs if (4) survives but (3) does not; and what our faculty adviser is actually entitled to keep once the argument is repaired. Finally, tell me whether there is a version of this chain you regard as sound, and if so, how much weaker its conclusion has to be than (8).
