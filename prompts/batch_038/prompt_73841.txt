Background first, so this doesn't come out of nowhere. I spent thirty-eight years doing trace-element work in a geochemistry lab, mostly ICP-MS, which is to say a career spent arguing with people about what counts as a real signal when the thing you're after is sitting down in the noise. Retired four years ago, and I've fallen into philosophy the way other men fall into gardening. Lately I've been chewing on the spectrum arguments — the family of cases where you grant that an hour of agony is worse than a day of mild irritation, then you build a chain of outcomes each adjacent pair of which differs only trivially, and you're supposed to conclude that "worse than" can't be transitive, since otherwise every link in the chain has to be a real step down and yet the ends don't line up. I've seen versions of this attached to Parfit and to Temkin and to Rachels among others; please don't fuss over the attributions and don't send me a reading list or citations. I can't check any of it from where I sit, and I'd rather have your reasoning than your references.

Here's my difficulty, and it's a lab-shaped one. In my old life, "resolvable from" was non-transitive as a matter of routine and nobody lost any sleep over it: sample A distinguishes from standard B, B distinguishes from C, and A does not distinguish from C. That's just what a threshold does. What I never once saw in a lab was anyone conclude from that non-transitivity that the underlying quantity wasn't there, or wasn't ordered. If you spike a solution in increments each one far below the detection limit, no single increment is ever detectable, and the accumulation ends up unmistakable. A chemist who said "no increment made a detectable difference, therefore the total can't have made a difference" would be laughed out of the building. The instrument's coarseness was never treated as evidence about the sample.

So the question I want the essay to attack is whether that parallel carries over, and where exactly it breaks. The spectrum arguments seem to move from facts about our inability to tell adjacent outcomes apart to a claim about the ordering of those outcomes themselves. Is that a legitimate move, or is it a hidden-variable situation — I mean, is the natural reading that there's a real, fully ordered scale of betterness underneath and the discrimination failures are just poor resolution on our part? If it's a hidden-variable reading, what would settle it, given that we can't inspect the scale directly any more than I could inspect a concentration directly?

Write it as an essay on that controversy and come down somewhere. The options as I see them are roughly: betterness really is intransitive and we should stop expecting otherwise; or the chain can't actually be built because there are genuine lexical jumps between kinds of outcome, so some link is a big step after all; or transitivity survives and we bite the bullet that no number of the lesser bads ever outweighs the greater one; or — and I suspect this is the interesting one — the whole argument rests on a bad inference from indiscernibility to equality of value, and once you name that inference the problem dissolves. You can take one of those, or combine them, or argue for something I haven't thought of, but I don't want a survey that ends shrugging. Defend a verdict, and be explicit about what you'd have to give up to hold it, since in my experience every position in this area costs you something and the interesting question is always which bill you're willing to pay.

Also address one concrete wrinkle, because I think it's where the chemistry analogy starts to strain: in the lab the increments are quantitative and I can say how much I added even when I can't detect it. In the moral cases, when I move from one outcome to the next along the spectrum, it isn't obvious there's any amount that's being added. Is that a difference that does real work, or is it just the difference between a case where we happen to have units and a case where we don't?

A couple of practical things. I'd like a proper argument rather than a summary of positions, and it can run as long as it needs to — two or three thousand words wouldn't bother me. And please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter or an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered sub-parts. I'm reading this on a screen that's already too small and the visual furniture just gets in the way.
