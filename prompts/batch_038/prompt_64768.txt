I am four years into a novel whose subject is an atrocity committed against a people I invented, and I have been keeping a single page on which I have written down, as carefully as I can, what the book is committed to about the word "genocide." A lawyer friend and a historian friend have both agreed to read the page and attack it, and I would rather be attacked by you first. Below is the page. Please treat it as a set of claims whose joint tenability is the question, not as a manifesto to be summarized.

First, what the book contains, because the commitments are only assessable against it. The state in my novel does not massacre anyone. It closes the schools in which the minority language is taught and criminalizes its use in public. It requisitions and burns the printing houses and the ecclesiastical archives, and renames every village. It outlaws the liturgy. It takes the children to residential schools in the dominant language, where they are registered under new names and forbidden contact with their families. It resettles the adults into scattered labor districts so that households cannot re-form, and it separates spouses by assignment. The death toll is modest; the people survive as individuals and are, by the end, numerous. Some of my early readers tell me that this defeats the novel's central claim, since no one is killed in numbers and the destruction is cultural rather than physical or biological, and the law is supposed to require the latter.

Second, the commitments themselves.

(a) In the world of the book, and in my own thinking about it, the operative meaning of genocide is the one fixed by the 1948 Convention: a closed list of acts, done with the intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such, in whole or in part. I want the novel answerable to that standard rather than to a looser colloquial one, because the standard's severity is part of what makes the book's claim mean anything.

(b) I believe that standard is a mutilation of what Lemkin had in mind. His conception reached the destruction of a group's institutions, culture, language and religion, and it was not confined to the four categories the Convention protects; those features were cut out during the drafting, and cut out for reasons that were political rather than analytical. I want the novel to register that loss rather than to launder it.

(c) The omniscient narrator says, flatly and without hedging, that what is being done to my invented people is genocide. The sentence is not placed in a character's mouth, not attributed to a partisan, and not framed as one account among others. The book asserts it.

(d) I hold that the word must be reserved for the gravest things. A term stretched over every large wrong loses the capacity to stop a room, and writers who spend it freely are doing harm to the people who will need it later.

(e) I hold that intent is the hinge. A policy that foreseeably kills hundreds of thousands, without anyone aiming at the destruction of a group as such, is not genocide, and I do not want the book to imply otherwise, however much I may deplore such a policy.

(f) I hold that the group's own account of what is being done to it carries authority. If a people describe their situation as the destruction of their people, that description is not merely a perspective to be weighed against the perpetrator's and the bureaucrat's; the novel sides with it, and I think that siding is correct rather than merely sympathetic.

(g) I chose invented names, an invented geography and an invented century precisely so that the book cannot be read as an allegation against any actual state. I believe that invention is what entitles me to make the flat claim in (c) without conscripting the novel into anyone's campaign.

(h) I believe the novel's proper business is experience rather than classification: that the categories belong to courts and commissions, and that a work of fiction that argues with them has overstepped.

An audit, please, not a lecture, and no directions to further reading — I want your judgment of these eight items and of nothing else. Rank the tensions you find by severity, and for each one say what would have to be given up and what the giving up would cost the book concretely: which sentence, which voice, which motive. Tell me separately which pairs only look contradictory and survive inspection, and say why; I suspect I have at least one such pair and I would like to know whether I am flattering myself. Address in particular the objection from my early readers, quoted above in substance: is it right that (c) fails (a) because the destruction my novel describes is cultural, or is that objection resting on a confusion about what the enumerated acts include? And note that I have deliberately not told you which of (a) through (h) I would most regret losing, because I do not know, and I do not want your ranking to be an echo of my own preference. Finish with the single revision that would buy the most coherence across the whole page, and be honest about what it would make the novel worse at.
