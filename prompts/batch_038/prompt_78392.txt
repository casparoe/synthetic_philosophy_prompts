I spent thirty-six years in an industrial analytical chemistry laboratory, most of them worrying about what a given instrument reading does and does not license — calibration drift, baseline subtraction, the difference between a signal and an interpretation of a signal. Since retiring I have been auditing philosophy courses at our state university and sitting in on a reading group that meets on Thursday evenings; my fellow members are a retired judge, two graduate students, and a physician who keeps insisting that neuroscience has already settled the free-will question. This term we are working through a popular anthology on free will, and one of the assigned chapters, written by a professional philosopher who writes for a general audience, contains a compressed account of Libet's experiments that our group found very persuasive. Because the passage makes several moves in quick succession, and because I cannot tell from the inside which of them are load-bearing and which are rhetorical, I should like an expert audit of it. Here is the relevant stretch, which I have transcribed from the chapter:

> In the early 1980s Benjamin Libet asked volunteers to flex a wrist whenever they felt like it while watching a dot sweep around a clock face, and to note the position of the dot at the moment they first became aware of the urge to move. Scalp electrodes meanwhile recorded the readiness potential, the slow negative wave over motor areas that Kornhuber and Deecke had shown in the mid-1960s to build up before self-initiated movement. The result was arresting. The readiness potential began, on average, about 550 milliseconds before the muscle fired, whereas subjects placed their awareness of the urge only about 200 milliseconds before it. The brain had therefore already decided some 350 milliseconds before the person experienced deciding; consciousness arrived at the scene after the fact and took credit for a choice it had not made. Libet's own escape route — the suggestion that we retain a veto that can abort an action already under way — does not survive scrutiny. The veto window is only the hundred or two milliseconds between the reported urge and the movement, which is far too brief to accommodate anything one would call a considered choice, and in any case neuroimaging has since shown that the intention to veto is itself prepared by unconscious activity well before the person is aware of it, exactly as the intention to move is. Nor has the interval shrunk as methods have improved: brain-imaging studies can now tell, up to several seconds before a subject presses, which of two buttons he will press, so the neural process runs further ahead of the reported decision than Libet ever claimed. What these experiments do not show is that we have no say in our lives generally; they concern arbitrary, spontaneous acts such as flexing a wrist, not deliberation about a career or a house, where reasoning may run for minutes. But within their proper domain the conclusion is hard to avoid: the feeling of deciding is a report the brain files after the event.

Could you go through this claim by claim and tell me how much of it a neuroscientist who works on voluntary action, and a philosopher who works on free will, would each accept? I want the assessment sorted into three piles — what is straightforwardly accurate, what is a defensible popular simplification, and what is actually wrong or a conflation of two claims that the literature keeps apart. In particular, I am unsure about four things and would like them addressed explicitly: whether the onset of the readiness potential is properly described as the moment at which the brain "decided," or whether that treats a gradual buildup as though it were an event; whether "consciousness took credit for a choice it had not made" is what Libet's data showed, or a different and stronger thesis imported from elsewhere; whether the chapter's dismissal of the veto is sound, given that it seems to assume a veto must be preceded by conscious deliberation in order to count as free, and given that the imaging claim about vetoing is asserted rather than distinguished from the far weaker claim that forming an intention to withhold has identifiable neural correlates; and whether the move from decoding a button choice seconds in advance at better-than-chance accuracy to "the decision was already made then" is legitimate. I would also like to know what the passage omits that a specialist would consider indispensable — anything about how the clock-report method itself may distort the timings, or about whether the readiness potential has been reinterpreted since — and, finally, whether the chapter's own closing concession, that all this concerns only arbitrary wrist flexions, undercuts the general conclusion it draws, since that tension is what our group argued about for an hour. For what it is worth, I come to this inclined toward compatibilism and suspicious that timing data cannot bear the metaphysical weight put on it, but I do not trust that instinct, so please tell me where I am wrong as readily as where I am right. Please write the response in American English, in prose rather than bullet fragments, and without any bibliography or list of further reading — I want the reasoning, not the citations.
