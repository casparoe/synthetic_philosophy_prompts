I've been reading the takeoff-speed arguements since the Overcoming Bias days and honestly most of them feel like they'd survive any possible evidence, which bugs me. So before I read one more timelines post I want the spec, not another prediction.

Write the desiderata for a theory of AI takeoff dynamics — an account of how fast capability climbs once the system is meaningfully contributing to its own improvement. What does it have to deliver to count as a real theory instead of a mood? For each: the requirement, the case or consideration behind it, and a tag saying whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp (the smooth-continuous-growth people, the discontinuity people, the bottleneck people, the outside-view people).

Stuff I'd want in there, add to it or throw parts out:

- it has to say what its capability metric actually is, and what happens to its claims when that metric saturates or gets re-benchmarked — otherwise "doubling time" doesn't mean the same thing across decades
- it has to name the constraint that binds and say what changes when it's relaxed; an account that can't say which bottleneck is first in line isn't a theory of speed at all
- endogeneity: the inputs (compute, data, evals, the researchers, the tools the researchers use) are partly produced by the thing being modeled
- backtestability: could a version written ten years ago have been scored against what's happened since, and would anyone have bothered
- no free knobs: it can't be able to accommodate both a slow and a fast outcome by turning a dial
- consistency with growth rates we've actually observed in other transformative technologies — pick your own comparisons, I'm more interested in what you'd demand of the comparison than which ones you pick
- some way to tell a genuine jump from a measurement artifact or a scope change

Then the part I most want: which pair of these can't be jointly satisfied? My suspicion is backtestability and no-free-knobs are definately in tension, since the regime the theory is about hasn't happened yet — but argue it rather than just agreeing with me. If the list itself turns out to be a result, say so plainly. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular accounts currently in circulation. Skip the scorecard of actual forecasts and skip the reading list, I've read all of it, that's the problem.
