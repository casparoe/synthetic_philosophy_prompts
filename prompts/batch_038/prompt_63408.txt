I sit on the curatorial advisory panel of a mid-sized museum of archaeology and world cultures, and next month we have to settle a decision that has already split us three ways. Our new permanent gallery — budgeted, scheduled for fabrication, working title "Collapse: How Civilizations End" — moves through four rooms: the eastern Mediterranean palatial systems around 1200 BCE, the southern Maya lowlands in the ninth century, Rapa Nui, and a closing room on the present that was pitched to our board, and funded, as an Anthropocene warning. Two panel members now want the word struck from the title and from the label copy altogether, arguing that it is a nineteenth-century inheritance that licenses a story of collective suicide-by-mismanagement and flattens what are mostly stories of reorganization. A third, who secured the money, says that scrubbing the word is exactly the kind of hedging that lets an institution avoid saying anything with consequences, that visitors are entitled to be told plainly that complex societies can and do end badly, and that the chair's enthusiasm for the gallery traces to a bestselling book about societies choosing to fail or succeed — a framing that brought in the gift and cannot be quietly walked back. My own view is that we are fighting at the wrong altitude: the draft script uses one word for at least three separable things — the loss of a population, the loss of a political order able to mobilize surplus and keep records, and the loss of a transmitted repertoire of practices, techniques and symbolic forms. On that decomposition the four rooms come out differently. In the Aegean the palace system and its script genuinely go, and writing does not return for centuries, while the villages, the farms and the gods persist. Among the Maya the great southern centres empty as the north flourishes, the writing system falls out of use although its languages do not, and millions of people speak Maya languages today. On Rapa Nui the palms go, but the population appears to have tracked what the island could support rather than overshooting and crashing, and the demographic catastrophe belongs to the post-contact record: slave raiding in the 1860s and the epidemics that follow reduce the island to a little over a hundred inhabitants by the late 1870s.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the panel a week before we meet. It needs an actionable bottom line — keep the title, keep the word in the body copy but not on the wall, rename, or restructure the gallery into two differently framed halves — and it needs to say explicitly what should happen to the closing Anthropocene room, since that is the room the board understands as the point of the whole gallery and the room where our vocabulary choice has the largest present-day stakes. It also needs a test the curators can actually apply room by room and label by label: a procedure, not an essay on the meaning of collapse, that tells a writer when the word is defensible, when some intermediate term (rupture, reorganization, regime shift — pick and justify one) is defensible, and when neither is, and that a non-specialist label writer could follow without a background in the scholarly debate.

Two things must be handled head-on rather than footnoted. First, the funder's case is the strongest thing on the losing side: that "transformation" language can sanitize real suffering and real failure, that the people who died in these episodes did not experience a reorganization, and that a museum which refuses the plain word forfeits the right to say anything urgent about the present. Give that its full weight and then say why your recommendation survives it or, if it does not, change your recommendation. Second, a colleague has raised an objection to my three-part decomposition that I cannot answer: the repertoire axis can only be measured from what survives, and the archaeological record is heavily biased toward durable, monumental and administrative things — stone, script, sealings, store-rooms — so we may be systematically mistaking the disappearance of what preserves well for the disappearance of what was actually practised, and calling that a collapse. If that objection lands, does your test still work, and what would a curator have to do differently to respect it? Note also that our Rapa Nui room requires sign-off from a Pacific advisory council under our consultation protocol, so the memo should say what, if anything, in the recommendation changes depending on their response. Please reason from the four cases as I have described them; do not give me a bibliography or a list of further reading, and do not pad it — two to three pages is plenty.
