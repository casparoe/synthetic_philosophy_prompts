I'm a junior concentrating in political philosophy, and I'm in a seminar on libertarianism that has spent most of the term working through Anarchy, State, and Utopia. We reached the animals section this week, and I want to test whether I actually understand the proposal there rather than merely being able to name it: as I read him, Nozick suggests that we may treat animals on a utilitarian basis, weighing their interests against one another and aggregating them, while persons sit behind side constraints that forbid exactly that kind of pooling. For the optional creative component of the seminar I've decided to write a mock op-ed, and I'd like your help drafting it. The setup is this: a large poultry integrator, which I'm calling Cumberland Valley Protein, has been hit simultaneously by a county ballot measure that would ban new concentrated feeding operations and by an open letter from a university philosophy department. Their reply runs in a trade magazine under the byline of their director of animal wellbeing, a sincere and reasonably well-read manager who has genuinely absorbed that two-tier scheme and is delighted by it, because he believes it vindicates his operation instead of condemning it.

The piece should run about 650 words and stay deadpan from start to finish, with a headline on the first line and then nothing but continuous prose. I'd like the humor to come out of the argumentative structure rather than out of cruelty jokes: he takes animal welfare completely seriously as a quantity to be maximized and nonetheless concludes that his barns are a moral improvement, for instance by proposing to retire a fixed number of short-lived, comfortable broilers and replace them with a larger number of marginally less comfortable ones, and by reporting the arithmetic with real pride. He should also find the side constraints administratively exasperating, since harms to his human night-shift crew cannot go into the same spreadsheet as harms to the birds, and he should have to explain how his compliance department keeps two ledgers and reconciles them at quarter's end. Please give him three further difficulties that arise for him specifically because the scheme has two tiers: what to do about the barn cats, who hunt mice and are therefore conducting a small utilitarian atrocity inside his own facility; a written demand from his liability insurer that he state exactly which property places a being on the protected side of the line, and the way every candidate he offers seems to sort somebody into the wrong column; and the accusation that his business is a case of one species dominating another, which he should rebut in a manner that makes clear he has not grasped the accusation. He should also dispose of the objection that taste is too trivial a ground for killing, by explaining that his marketing department has stopped describing flavor as an appetite and started describing it as a heritage.

Two constraints on the execution. First, he has to remain internally consistent: he never denies that the birds' suffering counts, and he never reaches for the language of animal rights, because his entire position is that aggregation, taken seriously and applied generously, is enough to justify him. The reader should feel the familiar objections — where the line is drawn and whether species membership is doing work that a moral criterion ought to do, whether replaceability follows from a utilitarian rule for animals, what we owe to predators and to prey, whether a two-tier system can be administered at all — without the author ever announcing any of them as an objection or winking at us. Second, write it so that a reader who has never encountered the argument can follow along, with the scheme explained in the character's own managerial vocabulary as he goes. And for my own filing, please return the whole thing as continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables, and no closing note to me about your choices, just the headline and the op-ed exactly as it would appear in the magazine.
