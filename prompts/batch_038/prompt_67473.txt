So I run a small philosophy-of-physics reading group and I want to open next week's session with a piece of flash fiction instead of an argument, and then argue afterwards. Can you write me a short story set in a rotating universe?

The setup to assume, and to inhabit rather than explain: Gödel's 1949 exact solution — a cosmos of pressureless dust all rigidly rotating together, held up by a negative cosmological constant tuned against the densitiy. It's homogeneous, so the rotation has no axis anyone could point at; every point is like every other. Far enough out from any event the light cones tip over until closed timelike curves appear, and they pass arbitrarily close to every point, which means there's no global time function at all — not a choice between slicings, no slicing. The logician who found it took that to show the passage of time isn't objective; his more famous walking companion accepted the mathematics and doubted the universe was anything like that.

What I want the story to turn on is the distinction most time-travel fiction flattens. This isn't "go back and change things." It's that "now" has no global reach. So have at least one character start out sure it's just the ordinary relativity of simultaneity everyone's heard of, and get corrected. And please put in one scene where somebody is laying down a network of clocks and synchronizing them pairwise by light signals, and the procedure doesn't close — you work your way around and the last clock disagrees with the first by a nonzero amount, no matter how carefully you do it.

Format: exactly five numbered scenes, no title, no introduction, no closing note. Keep each scene to a sentence or two, spare, like beats on index cards, so the whole thing comes in around 150 to 200 words. Land the fifth scene on what it feels like rather than on the physics.
