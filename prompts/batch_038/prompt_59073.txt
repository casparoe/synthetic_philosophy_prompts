I write a science-and-ideas column and I've been chewing on a piece about how people are supposed to update on evidence that never quite lands — inconclusive AI evals, a radiologist squinting at a scan, a poll that might have been badly worded. Everyone I talk to eventually gestures at Jeffrey conditionalization: instead of conditioning on some event becoming certain, you redistribute probability across the cells of a partition and hold the conditional probabilities rigid, so P_new(H) = Σ P_new(E_i) × P_old(H | E_i). I interviewed two formal epistemologists last week and they mostly talked past each other, which is honestly why I want a third party to commit to something. My editor wants an actual number in the piece, so here we are.

The proposition: for an ordinary agent in an ordinary ambiguous-evidence situation — say you glimpse a warbler in failing light and your experience shifts your credences for buff-breasted vs. yellow-breasted from 0.5/0.5 to 0.75/0.25 without settling either one — the rigid rule above isn't just a handy approximation but is rationally required. Meaning: there's no case of that general shape where a rational agent should also be revising the conditional probabilities P(H | E_i) at the same moment as the E_i probabilities. My own number is about 0.55, which I recognize is a coward's number.

So: give me yours, then break it into the parts it's actually made of, with a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification for each. The parts I'd expect, though feel free to add or toss any of these: (a) whether an agent's prior conditional probabilities on their own possible experiences are even well-defined before they've had them — I keep hearing this is the soft spot; (b) whether any ambiguous experience can be redescribed as a certain experience about having a certain experience, which would turn the whole thing into ordinary conditionalization in disguise and arguably dissolve the question instead of answering it; (c) whether expertise cases break rigidity — think a perfumer or a birder with twenty years of practice whose one glance both shifts the partition probabilities and teaches them something new about what the cue would have meant; (d) whether order-dependence is a bug — running two ambiguous updates in different orders and landing somewhere different — or an acceptable feature of a rule about non-certain evidence; and (e) whether rationally required even has a determinate answer here, or whether we're just choosing between explications and calling it a discovery. Then tell me which of those your total is most sensitive to: which term, dragged from your number to 0.9 or to 0.1, moves the bottom line furthest, and what kind of argument or empirical finding would most change your mind about that one. Definately no sources or name-dropping needed, I'm not footnoting any of this — I just want the reasoning out in the open where I can see where it creaks.
