American and British anti-bribery law diverge over a single, very small payment. Under the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, a company commits a crime when it pays a foreign official to influence a decision the official has discretion over — awarding a contract, granting a license he might withhold, conferring an improper advantage — but the statute expressly withdraws from its reach a payment whose purpose is only to expedite or secure a routine governmental action, something the official is already legally obliged to do: releasing cargo, processing paperwork, connecting power and water. Britain's bribery statute criminalizes that identical payment, leaving only whether to charge to prosecutorial discretion. Yet the American code separately makes an illegal gratuity to a domestic official a federal crime — a payment made for or because of an official act, whether or not the act was one he could have withheld.

What conception of corruption renders that pattern coherent? Reconstruct it as precisely as you can: does the carve-out suppose that corruption lies in the purchase of an official's judgment rather than of his action, that what is being bought is a place in a queue rather than a decision, that an official already under a duty cannot be corrupted by payment to discharge it, or that the wrong is the distortion of competition for business rather than the degradation of an office? Say which rival conceptions the exception forecloses — in particular a role-integrity view on which an office is corrupted whenever it is put up for sale, whatever is being sold. Then separate what the statute strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably, and mark where the practice is underdetermined, such that two incompatible theories would license the same line. Finally: is the transatlantic disagreement about what corruption is, or only about what is worth prosecuting — and if the role-integrity view were adopted, what would have to change in the text and in the compliance routines built around it?
