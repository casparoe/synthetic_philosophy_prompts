Forty years of neutron scattering, and only since retiring have I had time for philosophy, so bear with a beginner. At my library reading group last week two members got stuck on Bertrand's chord problem and I couldnt referee it. (1) Dov said the three stock answers -- 1/3, 1/2, 1/4 -- show there is no such thing as the probability of a random chord, that the principle of indifference is broken beyond repair, and that a probability is only ever a report of somebody's state of information. (2) Hana said that's overreading it: the question is merely ill-posed, and the moment you name the physical procedure that makes the chords -- tossing long straws onto a chalk circle on the floor, say -- the answer is fixed and the paradox disappears. (3) Dov said she has only pushed the arbitrariness one step back, since "tossed at random" is itself a phrase that admits several measures, and the invariance trick people use to single one out can be made to deliver a different answer if you apply it to a slightly different feature of the setup. (4) Hana said the difference is that her version can be settled by doing it: toss a few thousand straws and the frequency comes out, whereas nothing could ever settle Dov's version. (5) Dov said the frequency comes out only because the tossing rig already contains one of the measures, so the experiment is an expensive way of agreeing with yourself. Give me exactly five numbered items, one per move, each rating it from flat wrong to flat right with a sentence or two of justification, no introduction and no summary at the end; and if the two of them are jointly missing something a working statistician would have said, put it in the item where it belongs.
