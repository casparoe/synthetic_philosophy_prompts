I co-convene a small working group that pairs our philosophy department with a hospital pain service, and this term we are trying to settle, before anyone argues for a particular view, what we would even be entitled to demand of a theory of pain's badness. The proximate trigger was a case one of the anaesthetists brought us: a patient with a left-hemisphere lesion involving the insula who, during a procedure under regional block, described the traction on his abdomen accurately, localised it, distinguished sharp from dull, and predicted what the surgeon was about to do next — while reporting, unprompted, that none of it bothered him. Afterwards he did not seek help when comparable sensations arose at home, and had to be taught by explicit verbal instruction to withdraw his hand from a hot surface. The group is split about whether that patient was in pain, whether his pain was bad, and whether we were obliged to treat it, and I have come to suspect that all three disputes are downstream of people deploying different, unstated adequacy criteria. So I want the criteria written out first.

Give me the desiderata: a numbered list of what any adequate account of why pain is bad — and of what it is for an episode of pain to hurt — has to deliver. For each item, state the phenomenon or case that motivates it and what goes wrong for a theory that misses it, then mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference belonging to one camp that could be surrendered without incoherence. The list should cover at least the following, and I would like you to tell me where I am wrong to include something: the dissociation cases in which a subject reports a bodily sensation with normal localisation and intensity discrimination but no aversiveness (asymbolia, and the similar reports from cingulotomy patients and from some opioid analgesia, where people say they still feel the pain but that it no longer matters); the mirror-image cases, familiar from certain forms of masochism and from psychogenic pain presentations, in which intense aversiveness arrives with little or no identifiable noxious input; congenital insensitivity, where the sensory channel is absent and a child must be protected from her own body; chronic pain that persists long after the tissue has healed; the modulation of aversiveness by attention, expectation, and meaning, which a purely peripheral account cannot capture; and the extension of the theory to subjects who cannot report at all — neonates, the severely demented, non-human animals — without that extension being purchased by quietly assuming the answer. I have been working with a rough three-component scheme: a sensory-discriminative component, an affective-aversive component, and what I have been calling aversive endorsement, the subject's own taking of the state to be a reason to stop. I want to know whether your desiderata force that scheme on us, or whether it is optional and should be struck from the specification as one camp's ideology.

Two further requests. First, the impossibility results. If some subset of these requirements cannot be satisfied jointly — for instance, if no theory can simultaneously hold that unpleasantness is an introspectible phenomenal property of the experience, that it is the property in virtue of which the subject is motivated to avoid the stimulus, and that it can come apart from the sensory character of the very same experience in the way the dissociation cases seem to show — say so explicitly and give the shape of the argument, since that is what the group most needs. And tell me which single desideratum does the most exclusionary work: the one that, if genuine, the largest number of otherwise plausible theories fail. Second, keep the specification neutral as to vocabulary. An account that never mentions phenomenal qualities and works entirely in terms of representation, function, or imperative content must remain a live candidate rather than being ruled out by the wording of the list itself. Skip the full scorecard against the existing views — hedonism about pain, evaluativism, the imperative and representational accounts, and the pluralist or disjunctive proposals — except for a closing paragraph of a few sentences saying which you expect to survive the list and which is most likely to be knocked out by the requirement you identified as most exclusionary. A table is acceptable for the main list, provided each row still carries its motivating case. No bibliography, please: I want the reasoning, not the references.
