Three months ago I got put on the benefits committee at work — the little group that argues with our health insurer about what the plan covers — and in those same three months my sister finally got an endometriosis diagnosis after roughly nine years of being told it was stress and that she should lose weight. Between the two, I've read our plan document about forty times and it's started looking like a philosophy text. I have questions I can't ask my coworkers without sounding deranged, so I'm asking you.

Here's what the rulebook actually says, boiled down (I'm not making any of this up, it's a big standard employer plan):

1. Nothing gets paid without a diagnosis code from the standard list attached. No code, no claim. You cannot file "I have been in agony for two years."
2. The code has to be tied to a covered condition, and "covered condition" means a disease, an injury, or a congenital defect. Not a circumstance, not a preference, not a phase of life.
3. Once you have a condition, what's paid is only what's "medically necessary," which they define as care that's appropriate to the diagnosis, not primarily for the convenience of the patient or family, and delivered in the least costly setting that can achive the same therapeutic result.
4. Cosmetic procedures are excluded, but the same surgery on the same body part is covered if it's reconstructive after injury, after disease, or to correct a congenital deformity.
5. Anything "experimental or investigational" is excluded, and they decide that by whether there's adequate published evidence, not by whether the thing works on you.
6. Statins for someone with elevated cholesterol and no illness whatsoever: covered, and there's a code for the number. Bariatric surgery: covered above a BMI cutoff (40, or 35 with a second condition), not below it.
7. Vaccines and screenings for perfectly healthy people: covered at 100 percent. Contraception: covered. Infertility treatment: covered, where infertility is defined as not conceiving after a year of trying.
8. Meanwhile our employer's own sick-leave policy, which sits in the next binder over, doesn't want a diagnosis at all. It wants one sentence from a clinician saying this person can't do their job right now. Nobody asks what's wrong. Nobody codes it. A person can be legitimately off work for six weeks with no disease entity ever named, and then file a claim the same afternoon that requires a code.

The stuff that's eating me: rule 3 puts the price of the care inside the definition of "medically necessary," which seems to make it not about medicine at all. Rule 6 covers people who are not sick in any ordinary sense — the code names a measurement, not a malfunction. Rule 5 lets the state of the research literature decide whether your treatment is real. And rule 8 uses a completely different criterion from rules 1 and 2, in the same building, on the same bodies. Our wellness program's front page, incidentally, quotes the WHO's line about health being complete physical, mental and social wellbeing rather than merely the absence of disease, which does not sound anything like what the claims department does all day.

I don't know the technical labels for the competing positions here, so if you need names, name them and define each in a clause.
