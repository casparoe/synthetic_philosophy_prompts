I'm TA-ing a constitutional theory seminar this term and one of my students has a paper draft whose whole frame is that entrenchment is just Odysseus at the mast: the founders tie the polity down so future majorities can't steer into the sirens. It's a clean analogy and I want to hand back a thesis that's harder to swallow than the obvious objection.

The obvious objection everyone reaches for is the divided-self one — that precommitment is really just an earlier majority bullying a later one, so a constitution has no authority over people who never consented to it. Fine, but that's Elster territory and it's been done to death. Here's the disanalogy I want pressed instead. Odysseus's device has two components and people only ever talk about the first. The ropes bind him; the beeswax deafens the crew. The binding is paired with an information filter — the men can't hear what he hears, so their obedience never has to be argued for, it's simply the default state of affairs. A constitution is ropes with no wax. Every generation hears the sirens at full volume and in high fidelity, and is actively educated about what the mast is for and why the ancestors lashed themselves to it.

So the tempting conclusion is that constitutional precommitment is a weaker, leakier version of the Homeric thing. I want the reverse argued, and argued seriously: that the missing wax is exactly what lets entrenchment hold up over centuries, and that a precommitment device which which also cuts off information is the fragile kind — it survives one voyage and then collapses the first time somebody notices the ropes.

Give me exactly five numbered points in support of that thesis, each one a sentence or two, no introduction, no conclusion, and don't restate the thesis up front. They need to be five genuinely distinct lines of argument rather than five paraphrases of 'reasons do the work the wax would otherwise have to do,' and at least one of them should say something concrete about what happens when a later generation does decide the ropes are worth cutting.
