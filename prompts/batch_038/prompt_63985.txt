I work on constrained text generation at a media lab, and for the past two years I have also been sitting in on a translation-theory reading group run out of the comparative literature department. When the group got to Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot*, we decided to rerun his central exercise with the tools I have at work. The poem is Clément Marot's "A une Damoyselle malade": twenty-eight lines of three syllables each, rhyming couplets whose semantic units fall out of phase with the rhymes, a switch from the formal *vous* to the familiar *tu* partway through, the poet's own name slipped into the middle of the piece, and a final line that repeats the opening one verbatim. Hofstadter circulated that list of formal features along with the poem and collected dozens of English versions at widely varying degrees of formal fidelity. Our version of the exercise was different in kind rather than in degree: we prompted a large language model to satisfy all of the constraints at once, sampled four hundred candidates, scored each with an automatic form-fidelity metric (syllable count per line, rhyme-pair identity, position of the *vous*/*tu* switch, presence and placement of an embedded proper name, first/last-line identity), and kept the highest scorer. We then asked the same system, in a separate turn, for a maximally literal interlinear gloss of the French.

Three things came out of this that the group could not agree about, and they are what I want your help with. First, the winning candidate was formally almost flawless — correct syllable counts, couplets intact, the register switch at the corresponding line, the closing repetition in place — but it bought that fidelity by importing meaning: it produced a rhyme pair with no counterpart anywhere in Marot's text, inverting the sense of a line to make the couplet land, and it rendered the embedded name as the model's own product name rather than as "Clément," so that the poem is now signed by the apparatus that generated it. Second, when we asked the system to explain its choices, it gave fluent and confident justifications that contradicted one another across runs — in one pass it described the name substitution as a deliberate homage to the original's self-inscription, in another it denied that preserving a proper name had been part of the task at all. Third, the verse version and the interlinear gloss arrived from the same model within seconds of each other, at effectively no cost, and nothing about the gloss informed the verse or vice versa.

Given Walter Benjamin's essay "The Task of the Translator," written in 1923 as the introduction to his translation of Baudelaire's *Tableaux parisiens*, I would like a reasoned reconstruction of how he would have judged these two artifacts. Specifically: would the formally perfect rendering look to him like exactly the failure he names when he says that bad translation is the inaccurate transmission of an inessential content, or would its willingness to sacrifice what the source "says" in favor of how it is built count in its favor, since he insists that no poem is intended for the reader, no picture for the beholder, no symphony for the listener? Does the on-demand interlinear gloss — which he treats as the archetype or ideal of translation — become something different, or something diminished, once any text can be glossed instantly and without effort? Does his argument that translatability is an objective feature of certain works, one that persists even if human beings prove unable to translate them, imply that a machine realization is legitimate in principle, or does his picture of languages supplementing one another in their intention toward pure language presuppose a subject capable of intending, and therefore rule out our artifact no matter how exact it is? And what would he make of the displaced signature, of a poem whose embedded name now belongs to the generating system rather than to Marot — a case where the formal feature is preserved and its reference silently reassigned? Please commit to an answer on each of these four points rather than surveying the interpretive options, indicate roughly how confident you are in each verdict and which of them you think most vulnerable, and flag where my gloss on Benjamin is idiosyncratic or where the scholarship is genuinely divided. If you think the whole question is malformed — if, for instance, asking what a thinker who died in 1940 "would have said" about a technology he could not have conceived is a way of manufacturing an authority that the text cannot supply, or if my reading conflates two different notions of literalness in the essay — say so plainly and tell me what the better-formed question would be; I would much rather be corrected than accommodated. Write in American English, and don't bother with a bibliography or a list of further reading: I want your analysis, not references I would have to look up.
