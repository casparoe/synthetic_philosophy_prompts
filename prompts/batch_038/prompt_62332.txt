I am drafting the framing argument for a research proposal on nationalism and distributive justice, and I want one number pinned down before I commit to it in writing. What follows is a request for your credence and for the derivation of it — not for a map of the field, and not for a list of who holds what.

The proposition, stated as tightly as I can manage:

(N) The historical recentness of a nation's traditions is a genuine defeater for national partiality. That is: if a political community's origin narratives, rituals, symbols and standard language turn out to have been largely assembled, standardized and backdated over the last two centuries, then — on the best justified account of why co-nationals might owe one another more than they owe outsiders — that discovery removes a substantial part of whatever justificatory weight the shared past was supplying.

This is meant to be the strong constructivist thesis in its normative rather than its historical form. I take the historical half roughly for granted: the invention-of-tradition and imagined-communities arguments (Hobsbawm, Anderson, and the debates after them) have persuaded me that the documentary trail behind most European national traditions is short, contrived and political. My question is whether that trail has any normative purchase at all.

Three stipulations before you answer, because I suspect the number turns on them.

First, separate two versions of constructivism that the literature tends to run together. Call them fabrication and bricolage. Under fabrication, a tradition was invented more or less wholesale — a costume, a ceremonial, an epic — and then presented as ancient. Under bricolage, genuinely old and regionally various materials were recombined, pruned, standardized and re-narrated to serve a new political frame, so that almost nothing was created from nothing and almost nothing survived unchanged either. If (N) holds for one and fails for the other, say so, and give me both figures; if they come to the same thing, tell me why the distinction is idle.

Second, work from a fixed case rather than from real countries, so that we are not smuggling in intuitions about particular histories. Take a state consolidated in the 1830s that commissioned a national epic from a court poet; selected one of three mutually intelligible regional dialects as the language of administration and schooling; adopted as national dress a tartan-like costume that had been court wear in a neighbouring region; and dated its founding to a ninth-century battle that its own historians now believe was fought largely by mercenaries from elsewhere and first recorded in a sixteenth-century chronicle. Its citizens today report strong felt obligations to one another, and its government redistributes heavily. Nothing depends on this being any actual polity; treat it as a thought-experiment with a paper trail.

Third, be careful about the direction of the genealogy. There is a familiar structure in which showing that a belief arose from processes insensitive to its truth undermines it, and a familiar objection that the causal story is perfectly compatible with the belief's being true anyway. Whether that objection has bite here may depend on whether what is being explained is a belief about obligations or the obligations themselves, and I do not think those can be handled in the same move.

Now the deliverable. Break (N) into the sub-questions it actually depends on and assign each a rough probability with a line or two of justification. I expect at least the following, but I would rather have your list than mine: whether any genealogical undermining works in the normative domain at all; whether the depth or authenticity of a shared past is the operative ground of special obligations, as against ongoing cooperation, shared institutions or collective self-determination, any of which might carry the weight without it; whether a nation is the sort of entity whose obligations could be weakened by an account of its own origins; and whether solidarity that was successfully manufactured thereby loses normative force or merely acquires a disreputable pedigree. Say how you combine the terms — multiplication, weighting, something else — and why that rule rather than another. Then give the resulting figure for (N).

Finally, three things about the number's structure. Which single term does it hang on most, in the sense that shifting that term by twenty percentage points moves (N) furthest? Which term are you least confident in, and what argument or evidence would move it? And my own credence in (N) sits near 0.25, which I suspect I have reached for one of two reasons: either I discount genealogical arguments in ethics across the board, or I have already conceded that the shared past was never doing the justificatory work and (N) simply looks false to me by default. Use your decomposition to say which of those is responsible for my figure, and say whether the two would ever come apart — that is, whether your terms admit any case in which the past demonstrably was carrying the normative weight, or whether on your numbers the two explanations are indistinguishable in practice.
