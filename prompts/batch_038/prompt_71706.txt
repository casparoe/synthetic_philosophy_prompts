I've come round to the strong reading of the side-effect effect, and I want it pushed on rather than summarized for me. The claim I actually hold: the harm/help asymmetry in intentionality attributions reflects the concept of intentional action itself being morally loaded — not conversational implicature, not people secretly reading "I don't care about helping the environment" as expressing a desire, not a demand artifact of the yes/no probe. I think the deflationary accounts are epicycles. What would count against me?

Sort the defeaters by how much they'd actually hurt. Which results, if they turned up, would be fatal as opposed to merely annoying — a population where the asymmetry reverses rather than just shrinks, a phrasing that makes it vanish, bad side effects that people call intentional while blaming nobody? Then tell me which of those a committed Knobe-style defender would concede as damaging and which they'd explain away, and where the two accounts make genuinely different predictions (implicit measures, forced choice between competing descriptions, that sort of thing). I'd also like an honest read on whether the drift from "blame drives the judgment" to "one core competence sensitive to both praise and blame" has left the view with nothing left at risk — and if it has, whether that's a special problem for a position that was sold as doing empirical work.

One more thing. I'm running two variants myself: one where the harm lands on the CEO and he's distraught about it, and one where the norm violated is aesthetic rather than moral (he knowingly makes the new building uglier). If the asymetry shows up in those too, what does that do to the moral-loading story — and what would I need to rule out before I could trust it as a test of anything?
