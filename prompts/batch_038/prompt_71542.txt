For something like fifteen years I have followed the game-theory threads on the rationalist blogs and forums, and whenever dominance is at issue the example that gets reached for is the Prisoner's Dilemma, with Battle of the Sexes and matching pennies as the alternates. I am drafting a post on a narrower point and I need a worked example that does not already exist in a hundred textbook editions.

The point is this: the step in iterated deletion of dominated strategies that everyone waves through as a technicality — permitting a *mixed* strategy to serve as the dominating witness — is where essentially all the substantive bite lives. A theorist who allows only pure-strategy dominators and a theorist who allows mixtures can look at the same tiny game and reach radically different conclusions about what common knowledge of rationality rules out. I want a game in which that gap is maximal, and in which what the mixed procedure rules out is precisely the outcome both players most want.

Construct me a two-player normal-form game meeting every specification below.

1. Three strategies per player, all payoffs integers, presented as a single bimatrix table.
2. An interpretable, realistic setting — two firms, two labs, two platform teams, a pair of collaborators — with meaningful names for the strategies rather than letters, plus one short paragraph on why the payoff pattern is plausible. Ordinary stakes; no exotic payoffs, no science fiction.
3. There is a cell that is Pareto-optimal within the game and that both players strictly prefer to the game's unique Nash equilibrium. Call it the utopia cell.
4. At the outset, no pure strategy of either player is strictly dominated by any other pure strategy, and none is even weakly dominated by a pure strategy. A pure-dominance-only theorist therefore deletes nothing at all and rules out none of the nine cells.
5. The utopian strategy of at least one player — preferably of both — is strictly dominated by an explicit mixture of that player's other two strategies, with exact rational weights, and is dominated by neither of them taken alone.
6. Iterated deletion with mixed dominators, carried to completion, leaves exactly one cell. Show every round and confirm that the surviving set does not depend on deletion order.
7. No relabelings or affine rescalings of a standard example; a reader should not recognize your game.

Then the demonstration, with arithmetic rather than assurances: the dominance certificate for each deletion (the mixture, and the strict inequality checked against every strategy the opponent still holds); each player's best-response list in the original game, which should make visible why the utopian strategy is never a best response to any belief; and every Nash equilibrium of the game, with an argument that your enumeration is complete.

Then the analysis, which is the part I actually want for the post.

- Prove the general claim that in an arbitrary finite game a pure strategy strictly dominated by a mixed strategy is never a best response to any belief over the opponent's pure strategies, and draw the corollary for my utopia cell: at that cell the utopian player strictly prefers some *pure* alternative even holding the partner's utopian choice fixed. Say why this follows from linearity of expected utility in the probabilities rather than from your particular numbers.
- A reader will object that the whole argument is an artifact of expected-utility orthodoxy: an ambiguity-averse player using a maxmin criterion does not care whether her strategy is a best response to some belief. Test that objection against your game by computing the relevant value for each of the three strategies, and report honestly whether it rescues the utopian one.
- If your game comes out symmetric, a superrationalist of Hofstadter's kind will object that each player should expect the other to do as she does, and that the utopia sits on the best of the diagonal cells. State what that objection assumes and whether your game answers it. If your game is asymmetric, give the nearest analogue of the objection and answer that instead.
- Compute each player's minmax value, or bound it tightly enough to settle the question, and say whether the utopia payoff is sustainable in the infinitely repeated version of your game. If it is, say what becomes of my thesis: does the post survive, and with what qualification?
- Finally, identify the single feature of the construction doing the work, describe what the game looks like with that feature removed, and flag any place where you made an arbitrary choice a critic could contest.

Two closing constraints. The verdicts must come out of the payoff structure and the dominance argument, not out of the labels attached to the strategies; if the setting is doing work the numbers are not, say so. And the specifications are jointly satisfiable — if you conclude that some subset of them is not, prove the obstruction rather than quietly weakening a spec and presenting the result as meeting it.
