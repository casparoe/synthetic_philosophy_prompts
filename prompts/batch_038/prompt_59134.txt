I spent 38 years in an analytical chemistry lab and retired three years ago, and for the last fifteen of those years I was the guy who wrote and maintained the standard operating procedures — instrument calibration, sample chain of custody, the whole genre, right down to the little line at the bottom of every page saying the printed copy is uncontrolled. Since retiring I've gotten unreasonably deep into philosophy: our community reading group did personal identity last spring and this term we've been slogging through the extended mind material, the 1998 paper and the endless replies to it. Half the group thinks it's obviously right, half think it's a word trick, and last Tuesday it struck me that nobody in that literature has ever done the one thing that would show how much of it they actually believe — write it up as an SOP. If the notebook really is part of your cognitive system, then a lab's quality system has to treat it the way it treats a pipette or a reference standard, and that has consequences people never spell out. So I want a handout for our end-of-term get-together: a parody Standard Operating Procedure, roughly 800-900 words, numbered clauses with sub-clauses, issued by some invented research facility that has adopted the extended mind thesis as official policy and therefore now regulates external cognitive artifacts. Full institutional QA voice — Purpose, Scope, Definitions, Responsibilities, Procedure, Records, Revision history — plus a made-up document control number, an owner, an approver, an effective date. The cover should carry the thesis's own battle cry as a motto, "Cognitive processes ain't (all) in the head!", with an attribution line that's bureaucratic rather than scholarly.

The humor has to come from getting the philosophy right, not from sneering at it, so here's what I'd like actually encoded in the clauses: the parity idea written as a scope rule, i.e. any operation that would be unhesitatingly counted as cognitive if it happened in the head is counted as cognitive when it happens in a registered artifact; the acceptance criteria for registering something as cognitive apparatus — constant availability, retrieval without excessive difficulty, contents automatically endorsed rather than re-interrogated, and endorsed by the user at the moment of entry — plus the awkward question of whether a phone app still meets the automatic-endorsement criterion once an algorithm is choosing what you see; a severity classification for loss or destruction of registered apparatus and what the incident form makes you declare; a flat prohibition on lending (temporary cognitive merger, unauthorized annotation of someone else's beliefs); a seperate clause for a shared notebook, which would need dual custody and some kind of review; and the boundary problem handled exactly the way a QA office would handle it, as an unresolved grey area with an instruction to contact the document owner — because someone has read the same reference manual four hundred times and nobody can say at which reading it became part of them. Let the scope creep visibly: the fire alarm, the building, the coffee machine.

One structural request: the internalist objection should never be stated as an objection. It should arrive procedurally — as a filed nonconformance report against the scope clause, from an auditor arguing that being causally coupled to a process is not the same as constituting it, backed by the sort of analogy internalists reach for (fire hydrant and fire engine, coffee machine and the lab's digestive system) — plus a change request to narrow the scope and a note on what happens to the lab's accreditation if the creep isn't stopped. Same deadpan register throughout, no winking anywhere, no line admitting it's a joke. And please don't name any real philosophers, journals, or universities, and don't give me a references list or bibliography at all; the document-control block and the revision history should do that job instead, with the revision entries quietly telling a story (initial issue, scope narrowed after an audit finding, one clause reinstated after an incident). Case examples can use subject IDs — Subject O., who can't form new unaided memories and carries a notebook everywhere, and Subject I., the internal-recall control — and the difference between them should be treated as a records-management problem, not a philosophical one. Someone who's never read the literature should still find it funny as a piece of bureaucracy, but its the specialists in my group I want to annoy into arguing afterwards.
