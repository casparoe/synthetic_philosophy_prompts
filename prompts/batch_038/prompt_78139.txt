I'm TAing the big intro epistemology course for the first time in the spring and I've been handed the discussion sections for the skepticism weeks, which is exactly the part I remember hating as an undergrad: twenty-five people agreeing that obviously they have hands and then forty minutes of nothing. My advisor says participation is part of how sections get evaluated and last year's TA basically had a sit-down strike in week six, so I want to head that off by running an anonymous paper diagnostic in the very first section, before anyone's read anything, and then in week six putting students into groups by what they said and making them argue with the people who disagreed. So — write me a sorting quiz, nine or ten questions, that sorts a total beginner by which response they'd give to Moore's hand-proof. For anyone reading this who needs it: the proof is just the bit where he holds up his hands in front of the audience and says in effect that here's one hand and here's another, hands are external objects, so there are at least two external objects, so an external world exists.

The constraints are the whole game for me. Every question has to be a concrete little scenario with two or three options phrased as things a person would actually say out loud, and not one question may contain the words knowledge, justification, skeptical, evidence, reliable, or any philosopher's name or any -ism whatsoever. I want people sorted by their reactions, not by their self-image — I've got two students who've read pop philosophy over the break and they will absolutely pick whichever option sounds most like the thing they read. Also, please don't collapse this into a single "do you accept Moore?" axis. I think there are at least three seperate stances hiding in there and I want the quiz to pull them apart: that the argument is a genuine proof, that its conclusion is something you could know, and that you're entitled to be sure of the conclusion even if you couldn't prove it. My guess is that most of my students are in the third camp without knowing it, and it would be great if the key could show them that.

Some seeds so you get the flavor, use them or don't: (1) a punch at a party was spiked with something that, in roughly one person in fifty, produces a full day of vivid and internally consistent hallucination of their own body and surroundings, and you have no idea whether you're one of the fifty — you look down, your hands are there, pressing them together feels right. (2) Someone you trust completely, holding what look like real medical records, tells you that you've been a brain in a tank since birth and everything you're seeing is being fed in; eleven minutes later they say it was a hoax and there never were any records. What, if anything, changed between minute one and minute twelve, given that your hands looked identical the whole time? (3) Your left arm is under a blanket and a doctor told you this morning there's some chance it was amputated while you were unconscious — does the proof still go through off the right hand alone, and does the conclusion come out weaker, or just shorter? (4) Your hands are prosthetics so good that they sweat, hurt, and have fingerprints, and someone says so you don't really have hands: is that person wrong about you, or using "hand" differently than you do, and does the same question come up if someone says so there's no external world? Two of your questions should come as matched pairs — identical scenario except that in one version a character voices the worrying possibility out loud and in the other the narrator just mentions it in passing — and the key should tell me which answers are supposed to shift between the two versions and which aren't, because that's the pair I most want to argue about in section.

Then the scoring key. Not point totals: for each leaf I want the answer pattern that puts someone there, stated as what's necessary rather than what's merely typical. The leaves have to include the ones my students will be embarrassed to land on — full-blooded skepticism, the view that the question is somehow misworded and there's nothing there to answer, and the idealist who thinks the conclusion is just false but not alarming. Name the positions properly once you've got there, but build the thing out of the answer space first: I'd much rather see which combinations of answers are logically available and simply unoccupied, each with a line on why (incoherent, unattractive, or nobody's bothered). And flag any two leaves your quiz genuinely can't tell apart, and either give me the extra question that would seperate them or say flatly that there isn't one. Fifteen minutes to fill out, on paper, by first-years with no background.
