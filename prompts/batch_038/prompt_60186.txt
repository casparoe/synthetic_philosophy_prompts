Write a sestina — six six-line stanzas with the standard rotation of end-words, followed by a three-line envoi — in the voice of a deontological theorist confronting a case built to break her view: a threatener will kill five people unless she herself kills one innocent person. Four of the six end-words are fixed for you in advance: a word for the single victim, a word for the five, a term of impersonal comparison (better or worse), and a term of permission (may). Choose the other two yourself.

The rotation must carry the argument rather than decorate it: the same small vocabulary returning in new orders, with no new term ever admitted that could join the evaluative words to the deontic ones, is the shape of the difficulty. If the constraint against killing is justified by how bad killing is, then in this case it should require the killing; her refusal to say so concedes that badness was never what the constraint tracked — and yet she still holds that five deaths are worse than one. Keep the comparative end-word and the permissive end-word out of any shared line through all six stanzas, so that the envoi is the only place the two vocabularies meet. The envoi should not reconcile them; it should name what has to be given up — agent-relative value, the constraint, or the assumption that permissibility can be read off a ranking of outcomes.

She does not act. The poem should end with five dead and her hands clean, which is precisely what makes the ranking she still accepts unbearable. Accuracy matters more than drama: she should finish in the position the view actually occupies, not in a caricature of it.
