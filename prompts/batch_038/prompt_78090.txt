I lead the knowledge-representation effort for a hospital network's clinical ontology, and I am fluent — professionally so — in that working vocabulary: description logics, TBox and ABox, the open-world and closed-world assumptions, class and property axioms, non-monotonic consequence, three-valued logics, and the distinction between unknown and not-applicable in a NULL semantics. Separately, I read Buddhist philosophy on my own time. I have gone through an English translation of Nāgārjuna's root verses with some commentary, and I know roughly what the four-cornered scheme (catuṣkoṭi) is supposed to be, but I have no Sanskrit and no training in the commentarial tradition. What I want is therefore a translation into the framework I actually think in, followed by an honest account of what the translation fails to carry.

My current reading, which you should treat as a hypothesis to be tested rather than a premise to be respected:

1. The four corners — that P holds, that not-P holds, that both hold, that neither holds — are a scheme of semantic values, and the fourth corner is a fourth value of the same general kind as a not-applicable marker: a case in which the question is malformed rather than merely unsettled.
2. Emptiness is a claim about the world: things lack intrinsic nature, in roughly the way a node in my graph has no properties in itself and is individuated entirely by its edges.
3. Nāgārjuna's refusal to endorse any corner, and his insistence in the companion work where he answers the self-refutation charge that he advances no thesis of his own, is a consequence-relation move: a logic in which deriving absurdity from every available option does not license a choice among them.

Restate the Madhyamaka use of the tetralemma, and the doctrine of emptiness that motivates it, in the vocabulary above — corner by corner, claim by claim — as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report:

(a) For each of the four corners, does it map onto something in my vocabulary, does it map only with distortion, and if so what precisely is distorted?
(b) Does the node-and-edges picture of emptiness hold up, or does it smuggle back in a substrate that emptiness is meant to deny — something that *has* the relations rather than being nothing over and above them?
(c) A structural worry I cannot shake: my formalism requires that, relative to an axiomatization, every individual be determinately classifiable, and it treats failure of determinacy as an artifact of incomplete information. Is that requirement itself a doctrine of intrinsic nature in disguise? Is the open-world assumption compatible with emptiness, or does it presuppose what emptiness denies?
(d) Is the use of the tetralemma material — steering us toward the fourth corner — or dialectical — exhausting the corners so as to refuse all of them? And does a semantics that must assign every formula a value have any resource for expressing refusal, as distinct from assigning a special value? If refusal belongs to pragmatics rather than semantics, say so plainly, and say what is lost when I insist on keeping the discussion inside my logic.
(e) Does the claim that emptiness is itself empty have a counterpart — something like an axiom that cannot be internalized in the language it governs — or is that a false friend?
(f) For every item that does not survive, tell me which diagnosis applies: my vocabulary is missing something and could in principle be extended; the Madhyamaka utterance is not a claim and is inexpressible by design; or, once translated, it is trivially true and not worth the argument. Then say where you think the interesting content actually lives.

Two test cases, to keep you honest. First, a live dispute in my own ontology: whether heart failure is a disease, is not a disease, is both a disease and a syndrome, or is neither, on the grounds that disease is not a natural-kind term but a role conditions play relative to purposes. Run the tetralemma on that and tell me whether Nāgārjuna would treat the fourth corner as an answer or as a withdrawal of the question. Second, the self-refutation charge: if his own statements are empty, do they fail to state anything, and does my framework have any way to represent an assertion that dissolves on inspection?

No bibliography and no literature survey; I will supply references myself. Do not lecture me on OWL or description logic. Where your reading of Nāgārjuna turns on a contested interpretation — for instance, whether he is best read as endorsing contradictions or as asserting nothing at all — flag the dispute in a clause rather than resolving it silently. Where you are extrapolating beyond what the tradition says, mark it as extrapolation. Aim for roughly two thousand words.
