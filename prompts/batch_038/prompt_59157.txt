I am a fourth-year PhD student in political theory, and this term I am running the weekly discussion section for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of social explanation. My section has sixteen students and meets once in a double period of 110 minutes; this is week six, and they have just come off Marx's 1859 Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* together with Cohen's defence of the base–superstructure picture in *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defence*. Next week they read Elster's *Making Sense of Marx* and the methodological-individualist objection, so the discussion I want to set up now is not "is Marx right," but something narrower and, I think, harder to argue with in the abstract: what kind of claim the primacy thesis actually is, and what would count as its failing. The section has an unfortunate composition for this. Two economics concentrators have decided in advance that the whole framework is unfalsifiable and are eager to announce it in the first ten minutes; the remaining fourteen are broadly sympathetic to Marx and have started to treat any methodological question as a political attack. Between the two camps, discussion usually degenerates into the sympathetic students offering ever more ingenious readings of a text and the skeptics scoring the ingenuity as evidence of the problem. I would like an activity in which the difficulty arrives as an experience that both camps have in common before either of them gets to name it, and in which the word functional explanation does not appear until the debrief.

Here is what I want them to come away seeing, roughly in this order. First, that the base feels extremely constraining when you are handed a superstructural fact and asked to explain it, and remarkably unconstraining when you are handed a base and asked to predict from it — and that they should notice this asymmetry in their own performance rather than be told about it. Second, that a social explanation can be simultaneously compelling, well-motivated, and non-discriminating, in the specific sense that it would have been offered with equal confidence for a society with a quite different base; I have in mind some kind of matching or identification mechanic where the students' own explanations are shuffled and their peers have to work out which society each was written about, so that the match rate is the datum. Third, and most delicately, something about the selection-shaped form of the thesis: that a disposition is said to explain a structure because structures of that type suit the level of the forces, and that this is close to saying that whatever prevailed was suited, unless the suitability criterion can be pinned down independently of which outcome occurred. I would like the third point to emerge from a round in which they must fix their criterion of suitability before being shown an outcome, and then get the chance to revise it, with the revision rate recorded openly on the board.

Please write this up as something I can actually run. I need: the number and composition of teams; a full set of invented dossiers for four or five fictional societies, each specifying the level and character of the productive forces, the property relations, the class structure, and a couple of ecological or geographic constraints, all in the same format so that the differences are the only differences; the exact instructions and the fixed menu of superstructural items the teams predict or explain (household form, dominant legal doctrine on contract and inheritance, the prevailing religious or cosmological commitment, the form of political authority, the content of the admired virtue, and so on); the sequencing with minute-by-minute timing across the 110 minutes; and a scoring scheme that creates real competitive pressure without using grades or money, since my department prohibits both as in-class currency. Two practical constraints: the room has a whiteboard, a paper cutter, and no working projector, so everything must run on handouts and index cards; and I have a TA who can circulate and keep score. Finally, please tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I expect at least one team to game the matching round by writing deliberately vague explanations, and I would like to know whether to build a scoring rule that punishes vagueness or to let that happen and use it in the debrief — and be frank about which feature of the base–superstructure problem the activity cannot reproduce at all and I will simply have to supply myself. My own suspicion is that it cannot reproduce diachronic depth, since the thesis is about sequences of modes of production over centuries and my game is unavoidably about synchronic fit across fictional societies, but I would like you to confirm or correct that, and to say what else falls through.
