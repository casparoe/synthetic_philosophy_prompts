I am coordinating a two-day workshop session on the ethics of war, and the two participants I have paired — an absolute pacifist and a contingent pacifist — have spent their preparatory correspondence talking past each other so thoroughly that I have given up on the panel format and want to hand them the document they should have produced jointly. Please draft that document for them.

Their positions, as accurately as I can render them:

Participant A, the absolute pacifist, holds that killing noncombatants and taking part in institutions whose ordinary function includes killing noncombatants are wrong irrespective of the aggregate consequences, and that war's moral character is fixed by what it does to those who wage it, not only by what it achieves. A refuses any supreme-emergency exemption. A's distinctive move is this: a constraint that yields whenever the stakes get high enough is not a constraint but a cost-benefit calculation wearing a deontological costume, so the exemption is not a detail of the view but its undoing.

Participant B, the contingent pacifist, accepts the full apparatus of just war reasoning — just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, proportionality, discrimination — and holds that in practice almost no modern war satisfies it, so B's verdicts coincide with A's nearly everywhere. B insists the coincidence is contingent on facts about how wars are actually fought and what they actually produce, and that a war which did satisfy the criteria would be permissible. B's distinctive move: refusing that concession turns pacifism into an identity position immune to evidence rather than a moral theory that could be shown wrong by the world.

Three things in their exchange look to me like the real fault lines, though I offer these as hypotheses for you to test, revise, or reject rather than as a brief:

1. The elasticity clause. B's view has a built-in concession — any sufficient improvement in discrimination, targeting, or intelligence appears to reopen the permissibility of some war. A reads this as proof that B is not a pacifist at all but a sceptic about current technology; B reads it as the only thing that keeps the view honest. Is the clause a feature or a bug, and can both sides say so in wording they would each sign?

2. The assurance asymmetry. A argues that in extremis B's constraints always give way, because noncombatant immunity cannot be conditioned on an adversary's compliance in the way that proportionality calculations can be renegotiated under pressure. B replies that this is a claim about political psychology, not about the principle, and that A is smuggling a sociological prediction in as a normative objection. Determine whether this is a substantive normative disagreement, an empirical one about institutions under stress, or a case of the two using 'constraint' differently.

3. The no-credit counterfactual. B's central empirical claim — that wars almost never satisfy the criteria — depends on comparing outcomes to what would have happened absent the war, and that baseline is never observed. So A and B may not disagree about principles at all, but about how much evidential weight an unobservable counterfactual can bear when the decision is irreversible.

My further suspicion, which you should treat as a suspicion and may conclude is wrong, is that these are not three disagreements but one disagreement appearing in three places.

Produce the joint statement in four parts.

Part one, Agreed claims: a numbered list of propositions both can sign, each one or two sentences, each tagged with a category drawn from exactly this vocabulary — normative, empirical, conceptual, strategic, or evidential-standard. Do not pad the list with platitudes both would sign because nothing turns on them; every entry should be a claim that does work later in the document, either by narrowing the dispute or by being the premise one side builds on and the other accepts.

Part two, Disagreements table, with these columns exactly:

- id: D1, D2, and so on.
- contested proposition: the disagreement stated neutrally, in wording neither side would call a strawman.
- B's own formulation: how the contingent pacifist would state their side of it, in the first person, phrased so B would sign it.
- A's own formulation: the same for the absolute pacifist.
- type: one of factual, normative, methodological-evidential, strategic-prudential, or verbal.
- crux: the single sub-question whose settlement would actually move at least one side, not merely restate the dispute.
- admissible evidence: what both sides would agree counts as bearing on that crux, or an explicit statement that they do not agree on the admissibility conditions and why not.

Part three, Pre-registered predictions on a stipulated case, as a JSON array. Use this hypothetical, which is stipulated and needs no real-world data: an ongoing genocide in a state with no external allies; a coalition holds near-total air and space superiority and intends no ground forces; munitions and targeting are as precise as any currently fieldable capability allows; intelligence is independently corroborated by two states that are otherwise adversarial; the perpetrating regime has already demonstrated both the intent and the means to kill the population itself if pressed; and no negotiated exit is available on any timeline shorter than the killing. Each side must record, in advance, whether their own view permits a limited air campaign to halt it, and what the campaign's actual outcome would do to their general priors about war. Each array object should have these fields: crux_id (referencing the id from part two), question, absolute_pacifist_prediction, contingent_pacifist_prediction, absolute_pacifist_movement_condition (what observation would make A revise, or the honest statement that none would and why that is not stubbornness), contingent_pacifist_movement_condition, and disagreement_about_admissibility (true or false, plus one clause of explanation).

Part four, A short prose section, roughly two hundred words, identifying which portions of the dispute turn out to be verbal or definitional — I expect at least one of 'last resort', 'intention', 'noncombatant', and 'proportionality' to be doing double duty — and saying why the verbal portion matters rather than being swept away as a terminological quibble.

Constraints on the whole document. Aim for 1,200 to 1,600 words. Do not resolve the debate or tell me who is right. Do not collapse it into a discovery that A and B secretly agree; if a large residue of genuine disagreement remains after the drafting, say how large and of what kind. Do not invent statistics, historical examples with specific casualty figures, or empirical claims about particular wars; where the parties' disagreement depends on facts, describe the shape of the facts in dispute rather than asserting them. Mention thinkers by name only where a name is doing real argumentative work, and produce no bibliography, reading list, or citations. Write in a formal register suitable for circulation to both participants as something they could put their names to.
