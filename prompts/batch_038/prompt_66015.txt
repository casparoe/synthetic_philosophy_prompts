ok, context first: I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy background, but about a year ago I started writing my dreams down every morning because I wanted to build a little tool that would find patterns in them, and that rabbit hole ended with me reading Norman Malcolm's book Dreaming and then Dennett's paper that asks the question right in the title. I've written down where I've actually landed and I want a consistency check, because two friends who read this kind of thing keep insisting the list can't all be true and I can't tell if they found something real or they're just enjoying themselves.

Here's the set:

1. Dreams are experiences. While I'm asleep and dreaming, something is happening that I live through at the time. I don't buy that the whole thing is a file getting written at 6:47 in the morning.

2. But I don't trust my dream reports. Huge chunks of what I tell people I dreamed — the specific faces, the layout, the fact that it was my old apartment instead of the one I have now — I think get filled in after my eyes open, and I'm totally fine saying those parts never happened.

3. Going further: if some detail wasn't settled while I was asleep, there's no fact about it. I don't mean a fact that's hidden from me. I mean there isn't one.

4. Lucid dreaming is evidence for 1, and I mean the boring lab version, not the internet version — people agreeing beforehand to move their eyes in a set pattern when they notice they're dreaming, and that pattern showing up in the REM recording. That reads to me like a dream running in real time with somebody making decisions inside it.

5. Dreamless sleep is really dreamless. And if I wake up with no impression of having dreamed, there's nothing left over to explain. I don't want a theory that says I was having rich experiences I simply lost.

6. The rule behind 3 and 5, which I picked up from Malcolm: a supposed mental fact that could never show up anywhere — not in a report, not in behavior, not on a recording — isn't a fact. I know Malcolm uses that to get to the dreams-aren't-experiences conclusion and I think I'm using it more narrowly, but I couldnt tell you exactly how much more narrowly.

7. I'm a plain physicalist about minds. No zombies, no dualism, and I think experience is a real thing that happens rather than a manner of speaking.

8. The case I actually care about: a couple weeks ago I woke up sure I'd had one short dream about a flooded basement and nothing else. My girlfriend says I was talking about work for like twenty minutes, and the tracker shows a long REM block. I told her ther'd been another dream and I'd lost it. I'd still say that today.

I'm deliberately not saying which one I'd drop, partly because I don't know and partly because I don't want to steer you.

So: audit it. Tell me which of these actually pull against each other and which ones only look like they do — I'm pretty confident at least one of the pairs my friends keep poking at is fine once you say it out loud, but I can't tell you which. Rank the real problems worst to least bad instead of treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say specifically what has to go or get reworded to fix it and what that fix costs me somewhere else on the list. And if the whole thing is salvageable with a single change, just say so.
