I chair the five-member Confidentiality Adjudication Panel of a regional consortium of twelve nonprofit addiction-treatment and community mental-health clinics. Our governing charter contains a clause, drafted by an administrative committee rather than by anyone trained in ethics, that had never been tested until this month: the panel exists "to keep to a minimum the number of client confidences breached within the consortium."

Two of my colleagues read that clause as a target. On their view, what matters is the count, and the panel should act so as to make the count as low as it can be made, whatever the source of the breaches in question. A third insists the clause is a constraint wearing odd clothes: it authorizes us to refuse third-party requests for disclosure, but never to breach a confidence ourselves, since a breach by the panel is precisely what the charter exists to prevent. A fourth thinks the clause is simply silent on cases where breaches originate outside the consortium entirely. The vote is likely to split along these lines, and I want the interpretive question argued on its merits before Friday rather than decided by tally.

The case. Three weeks ago a client of one of our residential programs — call her M — told her outreach worker, in a session covered by the charter's guarantee, that the care home where she works nights has been sedating elderly residents with unprescribed antipsychotics to keep them manageable; that she has been instructed to administer the drugs; and that identification would cost her the job and, because her immigration status is irregular, would very likely cost her her presence in the country. She refuses to authorize any disclosure, including an anonymized one, which she considers re-identifiable because only three staff work those shifts. Our outreach worker has since independently corroborated medication-log discrepancies affecting roughly forty residents. The state inspector general has told us informally that a named, corroborated source would open an immediate investigation, while an anonymous tip almost certainly would not.

Two further facts bear on the forward-looking part of the decision. Our clinics' intake forms promise confidentiality in unqualified terms, and the staff union has warned that any known exception will suppress disclosures of exactly this kind for years. Conversely, one panel member argues that we should not publish our reasoning at all, since publicity about exceptions is what erodes trust; another replies that a confidential exception policy cannot be applied consistently by a rotating panel, and that in any case the charter clause itself is a public document.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate ahead of Friday's meeting. It should:

(a) recommend what the panel does with M's information, as a concrete sequence of steps with a time frame, including what we tell M and when;

(b) settle the interpretive question — whether the minimizing clause counts only breaches the consortium itself commits or all breaches however caused, and whether it states a goal to be promoted or a constraint to be observed — and justify that reading by reference to what a charter clause of this sort can plausibly be for;

(c) state the rule the panel will apply to future cases of this shape, and whether that rule will be public, with reasons for the publicity choice rather than an assumption that publicity is costless;

(d) give the strongest case for the recommendation you reject, in terms its proponent would recognize as accurate, and explain why it nonetheless loses.

Take a position and defend it; a memo that surveys the options and abstains is worse than useless to us. Argue from the case and from general moral reasoning — I want a document the panel can act on and the dissenting members can argue with, not a literature review, and no citations or references are wanted. Roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words.
