You are a philosopher of science who also has real experience evaluating economic forecasts — someone equally at home with the Popper–Lakatos debate over falsification, auxiliary hypotheses, and degenerating research programmes and with the practice of out-of-sample model evaluation. Spend a few lines planning your mappings before you write anything substantive.

I am a quantitative macro forecaster. My working vocabulary is specification, calibration, in-sample fit versus out-of-sample performance, loss functions, structural breaks and regime shifts, omitted variables, overfitting, identification, and forecast evaluation over a finite horizon. I am fluent in it, so do not define terms for me.

Your task is to restate the Malthus debate in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. Cover at least three pieces: (1) the core claim of the 1798 Essay — that population, when unchecked, grows faster than the means of subsistence, so that real income is pinned near subsistence by famine, disease, and war; (2) the right-but-early defense — that the mechanism genuinely bound for essentially all of pre-industrial history, that English real wages around 1800 were on some economic historians' estimates little better than medieval ones, and that the escape arrived only through industrialisation and the demographic transition, i.e. after the fact; (3) at least one rescue move, such as Malthus's later-edition addition of moral restraint as a further preventive check, or Boserup's reversal, in which population pressure itself induces agricultural intensification.

Then give me the residue report, piece by piece: which claims translate cleanly (is Boserup simply a sign flip on a feedback term? is moral restraint a free parameter added after the residuals came in?), which survive only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. Specifically, I want to know whether my framework can distinguish a theory that was false from a theory that was true of a regime which merely ended; whether it can say anything about a prediction whose evaluation window exceeds the evaluator's lifetime; and whether "right about the mechanism, wrong about the timing" reduces to a claim about parameters, or to nothing I could ever score.

One constraint: do not smuggle. If translating into forecasting language quietly presupposes that only finite-horizon testable claims are meaningful, you will have settled the question by fiat instead of translating it — flag every place you feel that pressure. Close with a verdict on whether the residue marks a genuine gap in my vocabulary, or shows that the right-but-early defense was less coherent than it sounded.
