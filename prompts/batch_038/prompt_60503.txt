Ok, so I'm four chapters into a political theory thesis (seventeenth century, French side of things) and I've hit a wall on Pascal that I'd much rather sort out now than get taken apart over at the viva.

My chapter's central arguement is that Pascal's fragments on justice and force are basically the wager transposed into politics: we can't know whether the established laws are actually just, so obedience is a bet, and we should take it because the downside of losing — civil war, the whole edifice coming down — is effectively infinite while the upside of getting the justice question right is finite. Its not a reading I've seen anyone push back on in what I've read, and it lets me line Pascal up against Hobbes very tidily.

But my supervisor read a draft and said, unhelpfully, that I'm not reading Pascal, I'm reading a rational choice model dressed in a seventeenth century coat. I want to know whether that's fair, and if it is, where exactly the slide happens. Could you talk me through these?

1. Is the decision-theoretic structure actually there? Does Pascal anywhere attach payoffs or probabilities to obedience, or is the reason to obey the established order a completely different kind of reason — not a bet at all but something about there being no better title available? I'm worried I've assumed that because one famous fragment is framed as a gamble, the whole corpus must be.

2. Relatedly: when Pascal has the line about how, not being able to make what is just strong, we made what is strong just — is he endorsing that, describing it, or lamenting it? Does he think force is what makes law just (which would make him a Hobbesian of some stripe), or does he hold on to a standard of justice that the established order fails to meet but that we also can't reach? These feel like very different Pascals and I don't know which one I've been writing about.

3. If it isn't expected value, what's doing the normative work in the recommendation to obey? Is it the Fall — a theological claim about our inability to know justice in a corrupted nature — or the Montaignean scepticism he picks up in the conversation with Sacy, or just a prudential preference for peace over the alternative? I keep flipping between these three and I suspect they don't have the same status in his thought at all, which would be a problem for the chapter.

4. Where do the three orders fit in? I've been assuming charity somehow grounds or at least limits the political fragments, but if the political material sits entirely in the orders of body and mind then I may have imported a theological foundation that isn't doing any work whatsoever.

And do feel free to bring in the Discourses on the condition of the great and the Sacy entretien even though neither is straightforwardly from Pascal's own hand — I'm treating both as evidence about his views and I know the attribution is contested.
