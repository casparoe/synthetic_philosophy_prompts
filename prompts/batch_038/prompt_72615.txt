I sit on the clinical review board of a hospital that just ran a pilot of a new post-surgical rehabilitation protocol. Assignment was random: of 1,000 hip-fracture patients, 500 received the new protocol and 500 received standard care. Over the following six months, 65 percent of the new-protocol patients were walking unaided, against 60 percent of the standard-care patients — a modest win for the new protocol.

Our staff statistician then broke the results down by whether a patient had developed a wound infection in week three. Infections were far rarer in the protocol group, 10 percent against 40 percent, which we expected, since the protocol includes daily dressing changes. But among the patients who stayed infection-free, 80 percent of the standard-care group were walking unaided versus 70 percent of the protocol group; and among those who did develop an infection, the figures were 30 percent versus 20 percent. The protocol therefore loses in both subgroups. Her conclusion is that the overall 65-versus-60 advantage is an artifact of the two arms having different infection rates, that the only honest comparison is the within-subgroup one, and that we should discontinue the protocol.

I suspect she is making a genuine error, but I lack the vocabulary to name it, and I would rather not walk into Thursday's meeting with nothing but an intuition and an accusation that she massaged the numbers. Is her reasoning sound? What exactly should I say to the board, and is there any question about this protocol for which her subgroup figures would be the right ones to look at?
