Act as a philosopher of mind who also designs active-learning sessions for advanced undergraduates. Think the design problem through carefully before answering: begin by sketching two or three candidate activities in a few sentences each, say which one you judge most likely to survive the constraints below and why, and then develop only that one in full.

Setting: an upper-level philosophy-of-mind seminar, 16 students, a single 80-minute meeting, ordinary seminar room with a whiteboard. No laptops for participants, no video or audio playback, no budget beyond a few dollars of stationery.

What I want them to leave seeing is not the familiar headline about split-brain patients — that severing the corpus callosum yields two streams of consciousness in one body, or that it does not. I want them to run into a less advertised and harder problem. First: the evidence available from the inside and the evidence available from the outside both underdetermine the question, because a single fluent, confident first-person narrative of unified agency is precisely what one should expect to hear whether or not there is one subject there, given that whatever generates the narrative need not be what generated the behaviour it narrates. Second, and easier to lose sight of in the excitement: outside the highly artificial lateralized-presentation conditions these patients lead lives that look remarkably integrated, and the fragmentation shows up only in narrow engineered circumstances, so any account that predicts wholesale disunity owes an explanation of that.

Hard design constraints. This must not be a lecture, a thought experiment read aloud, or a seminar discussion of a described case. The students must do something, and both of the points above must surface in what happens in the room rather than in anything I say beforehand. Nothing is to be named — not the patients, not the procedure, not the philosophical debate — until the debrief.

Beyond that, four constraints I particularly want honored:

1. Build the activity around an information barrier that is genuinely asymmetric and genuinely opaque to whoever has to speak about it. At least one participant must end up producing sincere, detailed, confident explanations of actions whose real source is inaccessible to them, and the observing students must be able to see from the outside that the explanation is a fabrication which nonetheless hangs together and survives casual cross-examination.

2. Give the observers an explicit judgment instrument: a short list of criteria they apply, round by round, in deciding whether the unit in front of them counts as one agent or two. The criteria should be of the sort philosophers actually appeal to in this debate — tight behavioural coordination, a single coherent narrative self-report, shared access to what has been learned, unified attention, the capacity to act on reasons the whole system can endorse, and whatever else you think belongs there. Engineer the rounds so that by the end some criteria are clearly satisfied and others are clearly not, and so that the class cannot converge on which ones are decisive. That disagreement is the objective; do not settle it inside the rules.

3. Include at least one control round in which the barrier is lifted and everything else is held fixed, so that the contrast is visible rather than merely asserted.

4. Tell me plainly which of the two points above the activity reproduces well, which it reproduces only crudely, and which it cannot reproduce at all and which I will therefore have to supply myself.

Deliverables, in this order: (a) a materials list; (b) the roles, with exact instructions written so that I can print and hand them out, including what each role is permitted to say, see, and do; (c) a minute-by-minute timeline for the 80 minutes; (d) the observers' criteria sheet as it would actually appear; (e) a debrief that maps particular moments of the session onto particular claims in the debate, at the level of "when X did Y, that corresponds to Z," not generalities; (f) a list of what will predictably go wrong the first or second time this is run, with fixes — I expect several of these to concern the participants being far too good at reading one another, since ordinary people have each other's cues and severed hemispheres do not; (g) a prepared response to the objection I fully expect from the sharpest student in the room, namely that the activity begs the question: it places two people where the live question is whether there might be two subjects of experience inside one person, so the disunity it puts on display is trivially real and tells us nothing about the actual case. Say whether that objection succeeds, how much of it the design can defuse in advance, and what I should simply concede out loud.

Epistemic register: throughout, and especially in the debrief, keep a firm line between what is empirically settled about these patients and what remains a live philosophical dispute among people who know the material well. Do not present a contested interpretation as though it were the consensus, and do not manufacture controversy where the science is not actually in doubt. Where the design of the activity itself presupposes one side of a disputed question — I expect it will, in more than one place — flag that explicitly rather than letting it pass unnoticed.

You may characterize positions, arguments, and lines of thought descriptively. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I will supply those myself.
