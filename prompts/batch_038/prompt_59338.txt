I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy and, this term, the instructor of record for an upper-level undergraduate epistemology course: twelve students, all of whom have completed the standard sequence on knowledge and justification, with the last four weeks given over to knowledge-first epistemology — the luminosity of mental states, margin-for-error principles, and the KK principle. I am now writing the three-hour final and want one question on this material, worth 25 marks, to be answered in roughly fifty minutes under closed-book conditions. Because the students cannot consult the readings in the exam room, the question must be entirely self-contained: every stipulation, every piece of notation, and both principles stated in full on the page, rather than left to memory.

Here is the shape I have in mind, and I would like you to draft it properly. Begin by setting up a gradual series of cases α₀ … αₙ for a single subject, in which the subject feels cold at α₀ and does not feel cold at αₙ, and in which, for every i, αᵢ and αᵢ₊₁ are cases the subject cannot discriminate between with respect to whether she feels cold. Then stipulate luminosity for an arbitrary condition C — whenever C obtains at a case, the subject is at that case in a position to know that C obtains — together with the margin principle: for every i < n, if at αᵢ the subject knows that C obtains at αᵢ, then C obtains at αᵢ₊₁. Part (a) should ask the students to derive the contradiction, to identify precisely which step in the derivation requires the non-discriminability clause, and to say what is lost if that clause is dropped.

Part (b) is the point of the exercise, and it originates in an objection one of my students pressed in office hours. Let the condition in question now be the subject's knowing some fixed proposition p, so that luminosity for that condition is exactly KK. The student's claim was that the margin principle cannot be deployed against this condition in the same shape, because second-order knowledge at αᵢ is knowledge about αᵢ itself: what it demands, on pain of contradicting factivity, is only that the subject know p at αᵢ, and nothing in it requires her to know p at αᵢ₊₁. Hence, the student concluded, the anti-luminosity argument leaves KK untouched. I want part (b) to ask whether that objection succeeds, and to require the students to state explicitly whether it amounts to a point about how the margin principle should be formalized when the condition is knowledge, or to a substantive claim about the basis on which second-order beliefs are formed; and further, whether an objector who accepts the margin principle for first-order conditions such as feeling cold is entitled to reject it here, and if she is not, what she must surrender instead.

Please supply, alongside the question, a model answer of about 900 words and a marking scheme totalling 25 marks. The model answer should commit to a verdict on part (b) rather than survey the options. The marking scheme should allocate marks to particular argumentative moves rather than to topics, should state what an A-range answer must contain that a competent but B-range answer may omit, and should name the specific mistakes that cost marks — at minimum, the mistake of collapsing the margin principle into factivity, and the mistake of supposing that the argument needs non-discriminability to be transitive. Since the exam is closed book and I am not asking the students for sources, neither the question nor the model answer should turn on quotations, page references, or attributions of who said what, and please do not append a bibliography or reading list. One further constraint on your reply to me: write it as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. The marking scheme should nonetheless be explicit about which move earns which marks; simply do that in full sentences.
