I am a junior, and I have one year of high-school geometry plus a little self-taught set theory, so please define any technical term the first time you use it. In our geometry unit we were given Euclid's five common notions, and the fifth, "The whole is greater than the part," has since turned up twice at our school's philosophy club in arguments that do not sound like the same argument. A senior used it against Hilbert's Hotel: since the hotel's guests end up matched one-for-one with a proper subcollection of themselves (the guests in the even-numbered rooms, say), the common notion is violated, so there can be no such thing as a completed infinite collection, only a potentially growing one. A week later another student used the identical sentence far more modestly, to insist that the notion of "how many" simply stops applying once a collection is infinite, so that it is a mistake to say the guests and the rooms are as numerous as one another at all. Both of them thought the sentence had settled the matter.

I want to be able to say exactly what that sentence could be claiming, because I suspect the two of them are not contradicting each other and that at least one use of it is much weaker than advertised. Please lay out the distinct propositions it might express as a numbered menu with a verdict attached to each, rather than an essay defending your favorite interpretation. At a minimum, I want the claim about how many members a collection has separated from the claim about the length or area of a geometric magnitude, from a claim about wholes and parts in the sense of physical objects made of physical objects rather than sets made of members, and from a claim about which methods of comparing sizes are permitted in the first place. For each reading, tell me whether it is a triviality nobody disputes, a substantive claim worth arguing about, or flatly false, and give me the shortest counterexample or argument that decides it. I would also like the following answered directly:

- Is there a reading on which the sentence comes out as a theorem about finite collections instead of an axiom about everything, so that its failure in the hotel is not a scandal but the very thing that marks the collection as infinite? What has to be granted about one-to-one matching before you can say that, and what does the matching criterion rule out?
- What does a defender lose by insisting on the sentence without restriction? Which ordinary things we say about sizes — about subsets of the natural numbers, about the square numbers and the counting numbers — would they no longer be able to say?
- Which reading does the senior need in order to reach the conclusion that no completed infinite collection exists? Does any reading that is actually true get him there, or does his argument switch readings between its premises?
- Which reading does the more modest student need, and is it consistent with him also saying that we can count the guests in the hotel?
- If both of them had stated their positions with full precision at the start, what would they have found themselves disagreeing about, if anything?

Please end with a short note on the one point where you think a high-school audience will most likely be misled by whatever answer you give, so that I can raise it at the meeting myself.
