I'm in a book club that picked a popular history about how Americans see themselves, and I'm also sitting in on my partner's intro American politics class, which has left me weirdly cross-eyed about one term. The book's chapter on American exceptionalism goes roughly like this (from memory, but close enough):

'American exceptionalism began with Tocqueville, who coined the term in the 1830s after touring the young republic and concluding that the Americans' position was quite exceptional: no feudal past, no hereditary aristocracy, seemingly endless land, and therefore no real class politics. That sociological claim later got welded onto a much older religious one, the Puritan idea from 1630 that the new colony would be a city upon a hill with the eyes of all people on it, which Americans have invoked ever since as evidence that the country has a providential mission to spread liberty. In practice the two are the same claim wearing different clothes: because America developed differently, it is different in kind, and because it is different in kind it is not bound by rules that bind other states — which you can see in how often Washington stays out of treaties and international institutions that everyone else signs. The verdict: the descriptive half doesn't survive contact with the record, since America had slavery, segregation, empire and spectacular industrial violence just like every other industrializing country; the normative half is chauvinism with better PR; and the package as a whole is best read as an ideology that gets dusted off whenever a president needs a justification. So there is no real debate about whether exceptionalism is true, only about who profits from asserting it.'

My problem is that the professor in the other building uses the exact same phrase in a completely bloodless way — she'll say the US is exceptional with respect to welfare-state development, or exceptional on incarceration, and it's obviously not a compliment, more like a variable that scored weirdly — and none of my friends will accept that as a legitimate use; they think the term is just a euphemism for "we're the good guys." I'm inclined to think the book is being unfair but I can't say where, so can you do an accuracy audit of that paragraph? Go through it claim by claim: what someone who actually works on this concept would accept, what they'd call a fair simplification, what they'd wince at, and what is simply wrong.

Two things I half-remember and want checked. First, I have a feeling the book has the Tocqueville story backwards — I think the phrase itself is much later and started life as a term of abuse in some communist faction fight in the 1920s, which would be funny given how its used now. Is that right, and if so does it break the chapter's genealogy or just complicate it? The thing I most want you to dig into is the slide in the middle, though. Are "America is an outlier" and "America doesn't have to follow the rules" one claim or two? And then the ending: if the descriptive version turns out false, does that actually do any work against the normative version or against the ideology, or can an ideology outlive the total collapse of the facts it advertises — and if it can, does the "nothing left but propaganda" conclusion even need the descriptive part to fail?
