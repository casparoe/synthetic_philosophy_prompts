Been going round in circles on this since Tuesday and my supervisor hasn't replied, so: I put together an argument against Jeffrey conditionalization which I'm fairly sure is broken, because the conclusion is insane, but I can't locate the break.

Setup, so we're working with the same object. Old credence function p, new one q. Jeffrey's rule says: when your experience shifts your probabilities over a partition {E1, ..., En} to values r1, ..., rn, rationality requires q(H) = Σᵢ p(H|Eᵢ)·rᵢ for every H. The conditionals on the partition cells are held fixed and everything else is dragged along with them.

(1) A norm of rational belief is a rule which, given your old credences and your evidence, tells you what your new credences have to be.
(2) Jeffrey conditionalization is offered as that rule for the case of uncertain evidence.
(3) By (2), all the rule actually demands is the equation above.
(4) But the equation says nothing about where the rᵢ come from. They are inputs to the rule, not outputs of it.
(5) And they don't need constraining, because for any p and any q at all there is a Jeffrey update that takes you from p to q. I did this on paper: take the partition to be the atoms A1, ..., An of the algebra, assume p gives each of them positive probability, and set rᵢ = q(Aᵢ). Then p(H|Aᵢ) is 1 when Aᵢ entails H and 0 otherwise, so Σᵢ p(H|Aᵢ)q(Aᵢ) = q(H) for every H. You land exactly on q.
(6) So relative to any old credence function, every new credence function is Jeffrey-permissible.
(7) A norm under which every change of mind is permitted is a norm that forbids nothing.
(8) A rule that forbids nothing is not a norm of rationality, it's a redescription.
(9) So Jeffrey conditionalization isn't a norm. And since plain conditionalization is just the degenerate case where one cell of the partition goes to 1, the same verdict applies to it.
(10) Therefore there is no evidential constraint on credence whatsoever: belief isn't answerable to experience, and "rational updating" is an empty phrase.

I'm attached to (1) and I definitely don't want (10). Partly because (10) makes my whole thesis topic pointless, which I'll grant is a bad reason, but also because it seems to prove that the Bayesians who've spent sixty years building this machinery were doing nothing.
