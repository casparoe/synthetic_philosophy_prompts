I am drafting a paper for a graduate seminar on vagueness. The instructor is a committed epistemicist; the teaching assistant defends a supervaluationist semantics; and I wanted an argument that would put both of them on the same side for once, because I suspect the standard objections each of them faces are really the same objection wearing different clothes. What I ended up with worries me, since it does not merely embarrass the two views: run as a dilemma, it seems to destroy both of them along with a claim about the world that I am not prepared to abandon. The seminar meets again in nine days and I would rather hand in something defensible than something clever. Here is the argument as it currently stands.

(1) Suppose 'heap' has a sharp cutoff: there is some number of grains n such that n grains constitute a heap and n−1 grains do not.
(2) If there is such an n, it is unknowable in principle, and not merely unknown to us. Knowing that n grains make a heap would require being able to know that n−1 grains do not, and knowing that would require knowing that n−2 do not, and so on: a margin-for-error constraint on knowledge propagates our ignorance all the way down to the small cases we handle effortlessly, so no one could ever be in a position to identify the cutoff.
(3) Any putative fact that could never in principle be known, and that could never make any difference to what we would say, believe, or explain were it otherwise, is not a fact but an idle posit.
(4) The cutoff of (1) is exactly such a posit: no possible evidence, no explanation, and no difference in competent use could ever distinguish the true cutoff from its neighbours.
(5) Therefore 'heap' has no sharp cutoff.
(6) If 'heap' has no sharp cutoff, then either the sorites is sound, in which case one grain is a heap, or the predicate is gappy or admits of degrees.
(7) One grain is not a heap, so the predicate is gappy or graded — the supervaluationist or degree-theoretic option.
(8) But on that picture, the boundaries of the gap are fixed by which ways of sharpening 'heap' are admissible, and each admissible sharpening has a cutoff of its own; which sharpenings are admissible is likewise something we could never come to know and could never test.
(9) So (3) applies with equal force to the admissibility facts: there are no such facts, and hence no fact about where 'heap' stops applying.
(10) If there is no fact about where a predicate applies and no fact about where it fails to apply, the predicate has no extension at all, in the way that 'witch' and 'phlogiston' do not.
(11) So nothing is a heap, and nothing is a non-heap.
(12) There is a heap of sand on the beach below my window, and I take that to be true rather than merely a convenient way of talking.
(13) Contradiction.

Note that (1) is discharged rather than endorsed: the argument is a dilemma, and both horns arrive at (11). A classmate's diagnosis was that I have simply rebuilt the sorites with additional steps, which I do not think can be right, since the sorites depends on tolerance and nothing in this argument does — but I cannot say what the argument does depend on, and I cannot locate the step at which I lose the right to keep it. So: tell me which premise or inference to reject, and why it looked acceptable in the first place. I want the reading under which it seemed true and the reading under which it is false, because I suspect I am shifting between them without noticing. Then tell me what rejecting it costs, and specifically whether the epistemicist and the supervaluationist can block the argument at the same step or whether it in fact drives them apart, which is not the result I was hoping for. Finally, suppose I weaken (3) so that it quantifies over justified belief rather than knowledge: a putative fact is idle if it could never make any difference to what we would be justified in believing. Does the argument survive that patch, or does it die earlier — and if it dies earlier, which step kills it? If I am forced to choose, I would rather keep (12) than (3), since (3) is the only thing that has stopped me from becoming an epistemicist by default; but I want to know the price before I pay it, and I want to know whether some repair would let me keep both.
