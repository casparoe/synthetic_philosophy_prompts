Our team is drafting an internal style guide for evaluation reports, and the contested item is whether a report may assert flatly that a model believes something — "the model believes the user is a minor," "the model believes its outputs are being monitored" — or whether it must always retreat to "the model behaves as if it believes." A senior colleague defends the flat version by analogy with corporate belief. His argument, compressed: we say without embarrassment that Apple believes the handset market has peaked, that a central bank believes inflation is transitory, that a newspaper believes a candidate is corrupt; nobody hedges those to "behaves as if," and nobody takes them as merely poetic; a corporation's belief is assembled out of the dispersed contributions of many parts and issued through a single designated mouthpiece, which is precisely the structure of a model assembled out of the dispersed contributions of many documents and issuing them through a single output interface; therefore the same attribution practice should be available for models, and the demand for a hedge is squeamishness without a principled basis.

I would like an audit of how much weight that analogy can carry, written for someone who has to decide what the style guide actually says.

Please do the following:

1. Lay out the correspondence explicitly; a table is fine. What in the corporate case plays the role of the many contributors, of the procedure that aggregates them, of the mouthpiece, of institutional memory, and of the corporation's stake in being right? What is the model's counterpart in each slot, and is that counterpart genuinely present or merely stipulated by the analogy?

2. Separate the disanalogies that matter for this use from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular: the corporate case appears to require a decision procedure that resolves internal disagreement and commits the organization to a single view, so that a flatly contradictory pair of corporate avowals counts as a pathology to be repaired. Does anything in a model play that role, given that the same checkpoint will assert P under one framing and not-P under another and that no mechanism exists whose function is to eliminate the pair? Does the corporate case further require that the belief persist across time and guide action in ways a per-session artifact does not? And are corporate belief attributions disciplined by consequences — litigation, fines, contradiction by one's own filings — in a way that model-belief attributions are not?

3. Say whether the analogy inherits a contested source. Corporate belief attribution is itself disputed: the group-agency literature divides between those who hold that organizations are genuine intentional agents over and above their members (List and Pettit, for instance) and those who hold that the idiom is a useful fiction cashing out into individual beliefs plus decision rules. If source and target are contested in the same way, can the analogy settle anything, or does it only relocate the dispute?

4. Say whether the colleague's argument proves too much. If what he is really invoking is Dennett's point that the intentional stance is a legitimate predictive strategy rather than a claim about inner constitution, then it licenses belief talk about thermostats, spreadsheets, and arbitrage routines as well — and it licenses exactly the hedged formulation he objects to, since the intentional stance is explicitly an "as if" stance. Does his argument, pushed to its logical form, undercut his own conclusion?

5. Identify what the analogy quietly imports that the target lacks, and what it omits that the target has. For instance: a model's avowals are produced under something like a role or a conversational demand, and I want to know whether that has any counterpart in the corporate case at all, or whether its real counterpart is marketing copy rather than corporate belief.

6. Close with the practical deliverable. What should the style guide permit: flat belief ascriptions, flat ascriptions under a stated convention, or something else? Does the answer differ between ascriptions grounded in what a model says and ascriptions grounded in what it does on tasks it cannot verbalize? And if a different source domain fits the structure better — a parliament, an improviser playing a part, a crowd, a compiled digest of many witnesses — name it and specify which attributions it licenses and which it refuses.
