After thirty-five years in a university physiology department I have retired and taken up philosophy, and I now convene a fortnightly reading group composed largely of former colleagues — experimentalists, a statistician, two retired engineers — none of whom has any formal training in the subject. In five weeks it is my turn to lead, and I have chosen Scanlon's contractualism: the criterion of wrongness in terms of principles for the general regulation of behaviour that no one could reasonably reject, together with the aggregation problem that criterion appears to generate. I have forty-five minutes and a whiteboard. What I require is not an explanation of the material but a plan for how I should explain it.

Specifically: what must already be in place before the criterion can be stated intelligibly to this audience, and what depends on what in the order of introduction; which single case should carry the whole session, and what such a case must have and must conspicuously lack. I had intended to open with a decision from my own working life — whether to accept a small cumulative risk of serious injury to one technician in exchange for forty researchers each losing a modest amount of bench time — and I suspect this is a mistake, though I cannot articulate precisely why. Tell me how the concession that complaints of comparable magnitude may sometimes be weighed together should be introduced so that the group does not conclude the theory has been patched up ad hoc. Map the misconceptions I should expect from people with this training — I anticipate that the statistician will immediately reach for a sum, and that someone will object that the theory therefore always sacrifices the many to the one — and mark the point in the sequence at which each must be headed off.

Close with one question I can put to the room that would reveal whether the idea has landed, and state plainly what I ought to leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out.
