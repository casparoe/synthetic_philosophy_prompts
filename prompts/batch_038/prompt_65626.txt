Last academic year I attended a reading group on distributive justice in my department as a non-participant. Two of the regulars — Ilse and Marcus — argued at length about whether the leveling-down objection establishes prioritarianism, and the rest of us could not agree afterward about who had actually won. Below is the exchange as I have reconstructed it, in order, with each speaker's central claims compressed into a single move.

(1) Ilse began by claiming the objection is decisive. Compare a world in which half the population is at welfare 100 and half at 50 with a world in which everyone is at 50. The second is worse for many and better for no one, yet the telic egalitarian must call it better in some respect. A good that can be realized only by making people worse off, that no one could rationally prefer and no one has reason to promote, is not a good but an artifact of bookkeeping. Equality therefore carries no intrinsic weight, and we should be prioritarians: benefits count for more the worse off the recipient is, but any change that leaves everyone worse off is worse simpliciter.

(2) Marcus answered that Ilse had shown far less than she claimed. The objection, he said, runs entirely on the premise that nothing can be better unless it is better for somebody, and that premise is precisely what the telic egalitarian rejects — so deploying it is question-begging. Worse, the premise is independently false: we take there to be genuine reasons to prefer states of affairs that make no one better off, as when we hold that the explicit wishes of an already-dead friend ought to be honored even on the view that the dead are not themselves benefited by their fulfillment.

(3) Ilse replied that the two cases are disanalogous. The reason to honor the dead friend descends from a prior relationship and a prior commitment; it is not generated by the impersonal shape of the resulting state of affairs, which is all that leveling down offers. And she pressed her real charge: ask of the leveled-down world whether anyone can point to it and say that it is better, and better for some assignable reason. Nobody can. A purported value that never recommends an act, never gives anyone cause to regret the alternative, and never alters what anyone should feel has been drained of content by the qualifier rather than rescued by it.

(4) Marcus said this is what a plurality of values looks like rather than a failure of them. The egalitarian respect is systematically outweighed wherever realizing it would require making someone worse off; a stable ordering of weights is not emptiness. He then turned the attack. Prioritarianism, he said, cannot meet Ilse's own standard, because it holds that the moral weight of a benefit depends on the recipient's absolute welfare level — including in a world containing exactly one person, where there is no distribution, no gap, and nobody to be compared with. And an absolute scale of that kind requires a meaningful zero point, which nothing appears to fix. If the weighting function was designed to capture the intuition that gaps between people are bad and is then announced to be non-comparative, it is comparative concern wearing a disguise.

(5) Ilse made two responses. First, the zero-point charge misfires: the weighting tracks how well a life is going for the person living it, not a bare numeral, and diminishing marginal moral value is no more mysterious than diminishing marginal utility of income. Second, Marcus's pluralism has a structural vice of its own. If equality has any positive intrinsic weight, then for a sufficiently large increase in inequality there must be some change that is better for one person and worse for no one which the view nevertheless ranks as worse overall; the only way to avoid that is to set the weight at zero. Prioritarianism, by contrast, never ranks a Pareto improvement below the status quo.

(6) Marcus granted the last point in form but denied its significance. He said he was willing, in sufficiently extreme cases, to accept that a change benefiting someone and harming no one could be worse all things considered, since the intuition that enormous gaps matter is not nothing. He closed by arguing that the whole dispute had been aimed at the wrong target: the leveling-down objection defeats monistic egalitarianism and nothing more, and every replacement view — prioritarianism included — still has to state how much weight distribution carries, so the objection relocates the disagreement rather than settling it.

Adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six, say whether it is right, partly right, or wrong, and give a brief justification of that rating rather than a restatement of the move. Then answer three further questions: whether any of the moves trade on a confusion between better in some respect and better all things considered, and if so which ones; which of the two is more right about the question they were actually arguing, namely whether the leveling-down objection establishes prioritarianism; and what the most important thing both of them overlooked is.
