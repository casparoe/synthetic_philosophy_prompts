John Maynard Keynes's 1930 essay on the economic possibilities for our grandchildren rests on a distinctive two-part account of what abundance would do to us. On the material side, he argued that roughly two centuries of compound growth in capital combined with technical improvement would, within a hundred years or so, solve what he called the economic problem — the struggle for subsistence that had been humanity's permanent and pressing problem throughout its history — to the point where three hours a day of paid labour might suffice. On the psychological side, he divided human wants into two classes: absolute wants, felt independently of what others have, which he thought could be satisfied at a fairly modest level; and relative wants, whose force derives from the desire to feel superior to one's fellows, which he regarded as effectively insatiable. He paired this with a warning he expressed with something like dread: that the ordinary person, trained over many generations to strive rather than to enjoy, would not know what to do with leisure at all, since such a person needs a problem to solve or a duty to discharge in order to function, and that the re-adjustment of habits and instincts might take centuries. Only those able to cultivate the art of life itself, and who refuse to sell themselves for the means of life, would be equipped to bear abundance.

I want your reconstruction of how Keynes would assess a feature of the present that he had no vocabulary for. The marginal cost of producing much of what now carries status and self-expression — synthetic images, written work, music, portfolios, public personas measured in followers and rankings — has fallen close to zero, even as market working hours in advanced economies have barely budged and as a large share of workers describe their jobs as pointless. Some commentators have begun to suggest, on this basis, that what has become cheap is not output but agency: the felt sense that one's own effort issued in a result of one's own making is now available almost free, and therefore commands no price and no social standing. Call this the cheap-purposiveness hypothesis. If it were right, Keynes's transition problem would be inverted — the danger would not be that people cannot bear leisure, but that they can no longer bear effort that produces nothing scarce.

With that in mind:

(a) Would Keynes treat the collapse in the cost of cultural production as the arrival of the abundance he predicted, or as beside the point, given his account of relative wants? Note in particular that status competition, on his analysis, does not require scarce physical goods — only that others lack what one has. Does the migration of rivalry into costless attention metrics confirm or undermine that analysis?

(b) Would he read the enthusiasm of amateurs for making things with generative tools — and the deflation many report when the tool does the work for them — as an instance of the flight from leisure he anticipated: a demand not for the product but for the experience of being purposive?

(c) Would he accept the cheap-purposiveness hypothesis, or would he regard it as mistaking a trained habit for a real want? His treatment of purposiveness as a bad inheritance rather than as a genuine need seems to cut one way; his treatment of relative wants as insatiable seems to cut the other. Explain which way the argument actually runs.

(d) What would follow for policy on his view — a shorter statutory week, an unconditional income, or neither? Would he think the current debate about machines taking jobs rests on a category mistake, and if so, whose mistake?

Throughout, please keep a clear separation between positions you can attribute to Keynes on the strength of what he wrote and extrapolations you are constructing on his behalf, and flag where his Bloomsbury formation — the good conceived as certain states of consciousness and personal relations rather than as aggregate welfare — would resist a straightforwardly utilitarian reading of the essay. I am after your reasoning rather than references, so please do not include a bibliography.
