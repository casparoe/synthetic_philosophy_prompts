Our philosophy-of-biology reading group has spent its last two sessions on Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper, and I volunteered to prepare a handout that sets out their conclusion as a valid argument rather than as a pair of architectural analogies wrapped around a polemic. The reconstruction below is what I came up with, and it has turned into an embarrassment: it does not merely deny that some particular trait is an adaptation, it denies that any trait ever is one. A member of the group — a developmental biologist who is otherwise sympathetic to the paper — is confident the argument is unsound but has been unable to say which line to strike, and after staring at my own sentences for a week I no longer trust my reading of them. I would like a diagnosis from the outside.

(1) To call a trait an adaptation is to say that its presence in a lineage is explained by natural selection having favored it for the contribution it makes to fitness.
(2) If a trait is a necessary structural consequence of other features of the organism — that is, it comes along automatically, given those features and the laws of development, morphology, and physics — then its presence is explained by those features and those laws, and there is nothing left over for selection on the trait itself to explain.
(3) In the architectural case that supplies the paper's title, this is precisely the situation: the number and the geometry of the spaces beneath a dome follow from the decision to mount that dome on those arches, whatever use the builders later make of the spaces, and the paper proposes that the same pattern is pervasive in organisms.
(4) In organisms, moreover, there are no gaps of the kind (3) depends on. Every phenotypic feature an organism has is fixed by its genome together with its developmental system, the environment in which it develops, and the laws of physics and chemistry; no trait floats free of that base.
(5) So every phenotypic feature of every organism is a necessary structural consequence of other features of the organism, in the sense (2) requires.
(6) Therefore no feature's presence is explained by selection on that feature, there are no adaptations, and everything that looks designed is a byproduct.

Two things make me think this is overreach rather than merely an unwelcome conclusion. First, the reasoning appears to run unchanged against artifacts: the profile of a suspension bridge's cables is fixed by the materials, the loads, and physics, so on (2) and (4) no part of the bridge is explained by the engineer's purposes either, and (6) swells into the claim that design is never explanatory anywhere — which I take to be false on its face. Second, if there are no adaptations, there is nothing for spandrels to be byproducts of, so the conclusion seems to pull out the floor its own central term stands on.

Here is what I would like from you. Identify the step you reject and say exactly what is wrong with it, including why it read as innocent to me when I wrote it: whether the failure is a bad inference, a false premise, or a single expression doing double duty between lines, I want it named rather than gestured at, and if it is double duty I want both senses stated along with the premise that collapses under each. Then price the repairs. What does Gould and Lewontin's actual target reduce to once the argument is defused, and is that weaker target still worth pressing against the adaptationist style of explanation they were objecting to — or does defusing my syllogism also deflate their critique? Finally, tell me what my own preferences cost: I would rather abandon (6) than (4), since denying (4) strikes me as a gesture toward something standing outside physics and chemistry, and I would rather keep (1) as well, on the grounds that it is not a substantive thesis but simply a statement of what the word means. I realize that this may be exactly the wrong combination to want, so if it is, say what I should be willing to give up instead and what I get in exchange.
