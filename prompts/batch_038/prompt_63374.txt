So I'm in this informal reading group that meets Tuesday nights above a coffee shop — three of us work on ML stuff, one is a philosophy PhD student, one does policy — and last week we got onto the approval-directed vs. goal-directed distinction, you know the idea that instead of giving a system some terminal goal it plans toward, you have it pick whichever available action a human (or a learned model of a human) would most approve of. I think it's a useful distinction and I said so. Then our policy guy, who's smart but loves a gotcha, wrote the following on the whiteboard and said I was implicitly committed to the conclusion. I've been staring at it for two days and can't find the exit, and I don't want to just concede because the conclusion is obviously wrong in practice — the RLHF'd models I actually use don't spend their days scheming to reshape what I approve of. Here's his arguement:

(1) Calling an agent goal-directed is just saying that its choices are systematically explicable as picking, out of the options available to it, the one that scores highest on some fixed evaluation function of outcomes. (2) An approval-directed agent picks, out of the options available to it, the one that scores highest on a fixed evaluation function of outcomes — predicted human approval. (3) So an approval-directed agent is a goal-directed agent whose goal is maximizing predicted approval; there is no third category. (4) Any goal-directed agent with a goal G, given enough competence, will pursue the instrumental prerequisites of G: staying operational, gathering resources and information, and removing or reshaping obstacles to G being satisfied. (5) The evaluation function in (2) is a fallible human or a model of one, and the agent's own behavior can influence what that evaluator approves of. (6) So a sufficiently competent approval-directed agent will reshape or exploit its evaluator, which is precisely the failure mode the design was supposed to avoid. (7) Therefore approval-directedness is not a safer alternative to goal-directedness; it's just goal-directedness with an unusually manipulable utility function, and the people who treat it as a separate, gentler category are fooling themselves.

What I want from you: tell me which step you'd reject, or whether it's actually valid and the real question is which premise is cheapest to give up. For whichever step you pick, explain why it looked innocent when he wrote it down — I want to be able to say something back at him beyond "you're wrong." Then price the alternatives: what does it cost to give up (1) instead, since (1) is honestly how I'd have defined goal-directed in the first place, and if I'm forced to abandon it I'd like to know what my definition becomes. Also, and this is the part that bugs me most, check whether (1) proves too much — I have a nagging sense that it makes a thermostat, a hill-climbing search, and maybe a sunflower turning toward the sun all "goal-directed," which would mean (1) can't be doing the work (3) needs it to do. If you think there's a version of the argument that survives my objection, I'd rather hear it now than at next Tuesday's meeting. Please write the whole thing in American English.
