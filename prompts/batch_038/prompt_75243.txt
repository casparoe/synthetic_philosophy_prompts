I have no academic background in any of this — I left school at sixteen and have read around machine learning and the philosophy of mind on my own — and I have assembled an argument about whether a trained artificial agent could suffer that I can neither accept nor dismantle. I should be grateful for a rigorous diagnosis. Please write your answer in British English.

The starting intuition, which I keep meeting and which I find persuasive, is that what makes pain bad is not the tissue it happens in but the role it plays in the creature's economy, and that a reinforcement-learning agent's reward signal plays that role.

(1) A system suffers just in case it has states that play the pain-role: states that are (a) produced by conditions the system is built to keep away from, (b) such that the system's behaviour is organised to terminate or prevent them, and (c) such that the system will trade other objectives off against avoiding them.

(2) Nothing further is required — no particular chemistry, no particular kind of body — since any extra condition would be mere prejudice about implementation.

(3) A reinforcement-learning agent contains a scalar with precisely this profile: it is produced by conditions the designer marks as to be avoided, the policy is shaped so as to prevent its occurrence, and the agent will forgo other rewards in order to avoid it.

(4) So such an agent has states playing the pain-role, and by (1) and (2) it suffers.

(5) But the same reasoning runs on anything with such a scalar: a chess engine's evaluation function, a spam filter's loss, a thermostat's error term, a company's profit-and-loss line. Each is a quantity the system's activity is arranged to keep high, and each is therefore a locus of suffering.

(6) Moreover, magnitude tracks intensity: an agent whose penalties are a million times larger suffers a million times more.

(7) Yet which signal a system has is a matter of the modeller's bookkeeping. Two reward functions related by a positive rescaling and a constant shift select exactly the same optimal behaviour, so the agent of (6) and the same agent with rewards a million times greater are behaviourally indistinguishable; and whether some event registers as a loss or merely as a smaller gain is settled by an arbitrary choice of zero point.

(8) So on this account suffering is a matter of notation: one could manufacture an atrocity, or abolish one, by relabelling a configuration file, without altering anything the system does.

(9) That cannot be right; so something above is false.

I cannot say what. Step (5) feels like the runaway and steps (6) to (8) feel like a separate trap, but I built all of them out of the same material as (1) to (4), so refusing one appears to commit me to refusing the others — and I am unwilling to. I would much rather keep (1) and (2), because if I surrender them I no longer have any non-question-begging way of maintaining that a dog suffers either; I should be left simply trusting my sympathy for things that look and move like me.

So: identify the step or steps that must go, and for each explain why it seemed unobjectionable — which acceptable principle I over-read in order to get it. Then price the rejection. In particular, can a functionalist about pain retain (1) and (2) without being committed to (5) or to (6), and what has to be added to the account to arrest the slide without also arresting the case for mammals? If the honest verdict is that (1) and (2) cannot be saved, say so plainly, and tell me what the least damaging retreat looks like: which weaker claim about reward signals and valence survives, and what it does and does not imply about how trained agents ought to be treated.
