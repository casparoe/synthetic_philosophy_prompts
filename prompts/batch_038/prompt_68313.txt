I am convening the closing session of a graduate seminar on causation and explanation in history. The seminar has worked through the standard metaphysical apparatus: symmetric overdetermination and the firing-squad case; preemption and trumping chains, and the consequent failure of simple counterfactual dependence to track causation; Mackie's INUS analysis; the treatment of causal selection and of the distinction between causes and mere conditions in terms of abnormality, voluntary intervention, and the inquirer's interests; and the more recent proposal that a cause ought to be commensurate or proportional with its effect, so that a coarse-grained factor may be a better cause than the enormously specific conjunction that would also have sufficed. We have also debated whether counterfactual reasoning about large historical outcomes is legitimate at all, given that such outcomes often look robust under the removal of any single factor. For the final session I want to turn this apparatus on the longest sustained literary engagement with the problem that I know: the essayistic digressions of Tolstoy's War and Peace, particularly those clustered around the 1812 campaign and the second part of the epilogue, in which Tolstoy maintains that an order issued by a sovereign is never the cause of the event it appears to bring about, that historical events issue from an incalculable sum of individual volitions, and that the great man is a label attached to an event after the fact rather than its author. I am aware that abridged editions routinely delete these chapters or relegate them to an appendix; please treat them as integral to the work and read them against the narrative rather than as a detachable doctrinal summary.

Please write the framing piece for that session. I have the novel to hand, so paraphrase rather than quote, and do not attempt to reproduce Tolstoy's wording. What I want is criticism that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the novel to an illustration of a theory of causation, and it should not bend the theory until it fits. Specifically, I would like you to address the following.

First, diagnose the causal structure Tolstoy attributes to 1812. Is it genuinely overdetermined, in the technical sense of many independently sufficient routes to the same effect, or is it conjunctive, in which nothing is individually sufficient and the real target of his polemic is not the world but the historian's practice of selecting a cause? The two readings imply different things about whom Tolstoy is attacking and how, so argue for one of them, or for a third, using the material: the crossing of the Niemen and the essay on the order that precedes it; Napoleon's cold on the morning of Borodino and Tolstoy's insistence that the battle would have unfolded identically; Kutuzov's passivity and refusal to issue dispositions; the council at Fili and the abandonment of Moscow; Tushin's battery at Schöngrabern, which fights decisively while its action goes unattributed; the partisan detachments and Petya's death during the retreat.

Second, ask whether Tolstoy slides from a claim about causal redundancy to a claim about necessity. Robustness under the subtraction of any one factor is not the same thesis as the inevitability of the outcome given the past and the laws, and the epilogue's determinism seems to want the stronger claim while his historiographical argument supplies only the weaker. Does the novel have resources to resist that slide, or is the slide part of its point?

Third, consider event individuation. Whether an outcome is overdetermined depends on how finely the effect is described: the French invasion of Russia and this invasion, beginning at this hour, along this stretch of the river, may not have the same causal structure. Does Tolstoy's theory covertly trade on a shift between such descriptions, and if so, does that undermine it or merely relocate it?

Fourth, treat Kutuzov as a candidate positive account of agency rather than as a mere rebuke to Napoleon: the commander who attunes himself to a movement already under way instead of attempting to originate one. Is this the practical counterpart of the compatibilism of the epilogue, on which freedom is the consciousness of one's own volitions and necessity is the law discerned only retrospectively? Decide whether it amounts to a stable position or to resignation dressed as wisdom.

Fifth, confront the possibility that the narrative contradicts the essays. The novel invests enormously in particular decisions, and its emotional stakes depend on their mattering. Does that constitute internal evidence against the theory, or evidence of a two-level view the essays understate? Say plainly where the reading I am proposing strains, and where the honest critic must concede the point to Tolstoy's critics.

Finally, state what the novel contributes to this debate that argument alone cannot: something like the first-person phenomenology of acting inside a process one does not cause, and the fact that participants experience themselves as authors of outcomes to which they may make no difference. Is that datum a genuine problem for difference-making and proportionality accounts, which discard it as confusion, or is the theory entitled to explain it away? Close with a considered verdict on whether Tolstoy's philosophy of history should be counted as a serious contribution to the metaphysics of historical causation, as a moral and political polemic against great-man historiography wearing causal dress, or as neither.
