For thirty-eight years I worked as an experimental physicist, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more time than method. My city recently convened a volunteer panel to advise on a scoring tool used to rank applicants for a municipal small-business loan guarantee program, and I agreed to serve, largely because I like reading the underlying papers.

At our second meeting a panelist, a well-read man with no mathematical training, resolved a difficult exchange by announcing that there is no free lunch when it comes to fairness: any model accurate enough to be worth deploying will be unfair to somebody, and this is a theorem rather than a grievance. Heads nodded. The agenda item on which fairness criterion the panel should ask the city to adopt was withdrawn as meaningless, and we spent the remaining hour on procurement.

I know the phrase from learning theory, where it names precise and, as I recall, quite modest results about worst-case distributions. I also gather there are real incompatibility theorems in this area, something to the effect that a score cannot be calibrated with respect to groups while also equalizing false positive and false negative rates across them, except in special cases. What I cannot do unaided is separate the propositions that a single sentence like his might be expressing.

So I should like a menu. Take "there is no free lunch in fairness" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could stand for, ranging from the near-vacuous to the ambitious. For each reading, give me a formulation specific enough that one could ask whether it has been proved — naming the quantities involved, the pairs of criteria, the population, and where in the pipeline from raw data to decision the claim bites — and then a verdict: trivially true, a genuine theorem, an empirical generalization, or false. Say which readings turn on group base rates differing, which are really about the choice of outcome variable rather than about the classifier, and which would be bad news for our tool without being mathematics.

Finally, tell me which of these readings would actually support withdrawing the agenda item, and which reading a thoughtful non-specialist most likely has in mind when he says the sentence — and whether that one is the one that is true.
