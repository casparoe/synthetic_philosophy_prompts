I'm the faculty rep on our university's Academic Accommodations Appeals Panel — I'm in the philosophy department but my actual area is early phenomenology, so I'm well out of my depth here and I'm pretty sure I got volunteered. We've got a case Thursday, the panel is split 3-2 and it's getting heated, and I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion beforehand that everyone reads before they walk into the room. Can you draft it for me? Please take on the role of someone who's both a specialist in the philosophy of mind work on extended cognition and has actually sat on these panels and knows what a dean will and won't sign off on. Think carefully before you write — if it helps, privately sketch the two or three candidate criteria the decision could turn on, then commit to one rather than listing all of them.

The case. We run a two-year graduate program in cardiovascular perfusion; it's licensure-track and our graduates go on to sit a national certification exam administered by an outside board. One of our second-years — call her M — had a temporal lobe resection for epilepsy about four years ago and has a documented, moderate verbal memory and retrieval impairment. Her reasoning is intact, her clinical judgment is by all accounts excellent on placement, but she can't reliably hold and pull up drug doses, protocols and contraindication lists unaided. So far she's had extra time and a reader. Ahead of her final qualifying exam — closed-book written plus an oral built on simulated cases — she's asked to be permitted to bring what her brief calls her second hippocampus: a locked tablet with no network access holding a corpus of notes she built herself over three years (her own lecture notes, flashcards, protocol summaries, annotated papers), an on-device search over that corpus, and a wrist cue that vibrates when she's been stalled on a step too long, prompting her to look something up. She's trained with the setup daily for two years. Her argument is explicitly the extended mind one: the tablet satisfies the conditions people standardly give for genuine cognitive integration — it's reliably and constantly available, she automatically trusts and endorses what it returns, she's used it long enough that it's woven into her habits, she'd be lost without it — so it's literally part of her cognitive system rather than an external aid, and the gap between her using it and a non-impaired candidate using their own recall isn't a morally or epistemically interesting one. She also leans on the parity principle: if the same process ran inside the skull we'd happily call it remembering, so we shouldn't downgrade it just because the storage is in a bag on the desk.

Here are the complications, and I want the opinion to actually work through them instead of gesturing:

1. The certifying board's handbook requires candidates to demonstrate unaided competence, never defines unaided, and our own policy just cross-refers to it. The hawks on the panel think approving this exposes the program's accrediation, since the board could later decide our graduates aren't certifiable and we'd be the ones who signed them off.
2. Our ed-tech officer inspected the tablet and pointed out the search isn't keyword lookup — it's embeddings-based similarity matching and returns synthesized paraphrases that don't appear verbatim anywhere in her notes. So is this retrieval, or is it offloaded inference? Does that distinction change the verdict, or is it a philosopher's comfort blanket?
3. Two parallel requests have landed since. One is from a student with ADHD asking for the identical setup on the identical grounds. The third has no diagnosis whatsoever and argues — not stupidly, in my view — that if the panel really accepts the extended mind thesis then the unaided-competence standard is conceptually confused for every candidate, and the honest move is to let everyone bring a scaffold and redesign the exam around it. Do we have to answer that, and if so, what do we say?
4. M also wants the scaffold in the hands-on simulation component, where part of the panel's reaction is some version of "would you want someone tapping at a tablet while they're running a heart-lung machine." I suspect that's a genuine consideration wearing a practical costume, and I'd like the memo to say what the real content of the worry is.

Deliverable: a memo of roughly 1000–1300 words that the panel could act on. It needs a definite bottom line — approve, deny, or approve under conditions you actually specify, and separately for the simulation component — plus a criterion the committee could adopt as standing policy for when an external resource counts as part of a candidate's cognitive system for assessment purposes, formulated so a future panel could apply it to a case none of us has seen yet rather than just re-describing this one. And it has to take the strongest case for denial seriously on its own terms: I think that case isn't about cheating and isn't really about fairness to the other two students, it's about what the exam is evidence of and who gets to rely on that evidence downstream, and I'd like the memo to explain why that version of the objection is harder to answer than the complaints people have actually been making in our meetings. No literature survey, no hedging into on-the-one-hand-on-the-other, and please don't end by telling us to seek legal advice. Keep the register formal enough that I could forward it to a dean unchanged — the typos in this brief are mine, not the memo's.
