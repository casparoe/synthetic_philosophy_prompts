I spent my working life in optical physics; since retiring I have taken up philosophy with some seriousness, and I write to you about a difficulty that combines both. I own a long refracting telescope of the 1780s whose objective was figured by a well-regarded London maker of that period. Last year a crack developed in one element of the objective, and rather than let the instrument stand idle I had a former doctoral student of mine reproduce that element from an interferometric map of the old surface, so that the new glass matches the cracked one point for point; by my own bench measurements it is the superior optic, with lower wavefront error than the piece it replaced. The damaged element remains in my possession, so the telescope is in every functional respect what it was, and slightly more so. A museum curator to whom I described all this told me the instrument has ceased to be of any real interest and could not honestly be offered to a collection; a colleague replied that the curator is being superstitious, that a telescope exists to be looked through, and that nothing whatever of value has been lost. I find myself nearer the curator than my colleague, but I cannot produce a reason that would survive my colleague's mockery, and I am not sure my feeling is anything better than sentiment. Which of them has it right? And what ought I to do with the instrument: leave the new element in place and go on using it, refit the cracked one and accept a poorer view of the sky, or sell it as it now stands?

Please state your conclusion in your opening sentence and then set out the reasoning that supports it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
