I am in the second year of a PhD in the history of science, and my department runs a small internal competition each spring that funds one summer of uninterrupted research. The application is a five-page prospectus, roughly two thousand words, read by a committee of five that includes two working historians who are, to put it politely, unpersuaded that philosophical reflection on historical method ever yields anything a historian could check or use. My topic concerns the perennial argument over whether large-scale historical outcomes were inevitable or contingent, but I do not want to write yet another restatement of the familiar standoff between structural explanations, which lean on demography, geography, fiscal capacity, and institutional path dependence, and narrative explanations, which lean on decisions taken by identifiable people under uncertainty. That standoff is well mapped and I would add nothing to it.

The idea I want to develop came out of a reading group on counterfactual conditionals, where I noticed that after two sessions nobody in the room could agree on what counted as a small change. When an historian asserts that a given outcome was highly likely, or that it was essentially unavoidable, that claim is implicitly a quantification over some set of nearby possible histories, and almost nobody says how the set is built. My working hypothesis is that most of the disagreement in the inevitability debate is tacit disagreement about a metric on the space of counterfactual histories: about which perturbations are admissible and small (reversing one minister's advice in one cabinet meeting? reducing a harvest by a tenth? delaying a war by five years? relocating a port?), about how far the perturbation is allowed to propagate before we judge whether the outcome survived, and about whether an outcome counts as the same outcome when it is individuated by its causes, by its effects, or by a description pitched at some particular level of grain. I want the prospectus to propose treating contingency as a parameter-dependent, potentially measurable quantity rather than a metaphysical verdict, so that the interesting historical claim becomes not that some revolution was contingent but that a specified outcome is robust under perturbation of institutional variables and fragile under perturbation of individual-decision variables, which is a claim a historian can investigate, dispute with evidence, and be wrong about.

Please write the prospectus itself, in American English, to a standard I could hand in without further drafting. It needs a precise statement of the research question, framed so that it has a determinate answer rather than dissolving into a plea for more nuance; an account of why the existing positions leave the question open, which you may do entirely by describing arguments and stances since I will supply all the citations myself and would rather you invent nothing; a concrete account of what I would actually do week by week across the twelve funded weeks, including one pilot case study that you should select and justify from among the fall of an ancien régime, the decision of a neutral power to enter a general war, or the timing of a major technological adoption, or else propose a fourth and say why it is better; an explicit defense of this approach against the obvious alternatives, in particular simply importing a possible-worlds semantics wholesale, simply commissioning more case studies and hoping a pattern emerges, and simply declaring the question ill-posed; a statement of what a successful result would look like, what an inconclusive result would look like, and what specific finding would count as the project having failed; and a candid assessment of what could make the scope unmanageable in a summer. Please also include a short passage addressed directly to the skeptical historians on the committee, explaining why the apparatus I am proposing does more than relabel judgments they already make, and what it would cost them to adopt it.
