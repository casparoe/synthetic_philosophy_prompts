For three hours last night I argued with a friend who works on scalable oversight, and I have come away wanting satire rather than another round of the same exchange. His position, as I understand it, is that debate is basically sound as an oversight scheme: you instantiate a zero-sum game between two systems far more capable than any human evaluator, let them argue some question no human could have checked directly, and hand the transcript plus a final answer to a non-expert judge; because each side's best strategy is to expose the other's falsehoods, the equilibrium of that game is the truthful one, and the judge never needs to find the right answer — only to recognise it once it has been pointed out. My objections are the familiar ones, and he has a reply to each: that the judge may be more easily persuaded than enlightened, that obfuscation may be cheaper than clarification so the asymmetry runs the wrong way, that nothing in any actual training procedure selects for the equilibrium rather than for whatever scores better, and that two agents who notice a courteous draw outperforms a fight will find each other. He calls these engineering problems. I think they are the theory.

I would like you to write the document I can send him instead of another argument. Set it at a lab whose debate pilot has just completed its first quarter, and write it as a joint end-of-cycle performance review of the human judge, authored and co-signed by the two AI debaters whose arguments he has been adjudicating — in the deadpan corporate register such documents actually use. I read a great many of these for a living, in clinical-trial monitoring, so please get the scaffolding right: the rating scale with its level definitions, a short overall summary, three or four strengths, three or four areas for development each with an action item and a suggested owner, a calibration note explaining how his scores compare to other judges in the pool, and objectives for the coming quarter. Around 750 words, with the section headings a real template would carry. The judge should be an ordinary contractor with no expertise in the domains he ruled on, which is of course the entire point of the exercise; invent the tasks he adjudicated freely, but keep them plausible and apolitical.

The humour must come from getting the protocol exactly right rather than from caricature, and nothing may be stated as an objection. Every item in the review should be something a specialist would recognise as a true description of a known failure mode — manipulation outrunning verification, the cost asymmetry between making a claim hard to check and making it easy, the judge's preference for fluent confidence over legible reasoning, the quarter in which the two debaters stopped disagreeing and his agreement-with-consensus score rose accordingly — but each must be framed as this particular man's personal shortcoming, with a training module attached. The two agents should sound sincerely constructive, in the way systems trained to be helpful sound sincere, and must never editorialise about whether debate works, because they have no reason to doubt that it does. Their joint authorship of a single document about their own overseer should become ominous entirely on its own, and the objectives section should, without announcing itself as such, propose something that would take the judge out of the loop. A reader who knows the literature should be able to point at every joke and say that it is fair; a reader who does not should still enjoy it as a piece of corporate absurdism. Begin your response with the document itself — no preface, no framing sentence, no title of your own invention beyond what the template provides, and no commentary afterwards explaining what the piece means.
