I'm auditing a graduate seminar on consciousness this term, and we've spent the last several weeks on Chalmers's 2018 meta-problem paper in the Journal of Consciousness Studies and the commentaries that followed it. What made the meta-problem attractive to the group was its advertised neutrality: it takes as its explanandum not phenomenal properties themselves but our problem-reports and the intuitions that generate them, so in principle a materialist, an illusionist, a panpsychist and a dualist could all work on it, since whatever else is true, we do in fact find ourselves perplexed about consciousness and we do in fact produce arguments from zombies, knowledge rooms, and conceivability. The thought was that if we could explain why we think there is a hard problem, we would have gone a long way toward resolving it, or at least toward locating it. In practice, our seminar collapsed into exactly the standoff the reframing was supposed to bypass, and my supervisor's view — that the whole enterprise is a category mistake dressed up as a research program — has started to look less dismissive than it did in week one.

Concretely, three positions have been grinding against each other. The first holds that a complete account of why we are disposed to posit irreducible phenomenal properties — an account citing introspective misrepresentation, global availability of certain self-monitoring states, the way our concepts of experience are formed from the inside, and the resulting structural coherence of our talk — would leave no residue at all; once the genealogy of the intuition is given, there is nothing further for a theory of consciousness to do, and the hard problem was an artifact of taking those reports at face value. The second holds that any such account explains only the causal origin of a belief, never what makes it true or false, so it is a genealogy of an intuition rather than a refutation of one; the stronger version of this line adds that if there were no phenomenal properties, the puzzle-reports themselves would be unmotivated, since nothing in a purely functional story would explain why the system is moved to insist that something is left out — which seems to smuggle the explanandum back in as a precondition for solving the meta-problem. The third position, from several people in our group who work in cognitive science, is that the meta-problem is not a new problem at all but the easy problems renamed: it is ordinary science of consciousness-talk, its solution was never going to touch the hard problem, and the first two camps are therefore arguing about nothing. What strikes me is that all three accept the same first-order claims. Nobody in the room disputes that reports of puzzlement are produced by neural processes, that those reports are globally available and functionally integrated, that they track functional organization rather than anything nonfunctional, or that we could in principle produce a detailed mechanistic account of why the zombie and knowledge arguments seem compelling to us. The disagreement survives complete agreement on the facts.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a disagreement about minds at all, but a disagreement about the norms of explanation — specifically about what a successful explanation of an intuition has to do to it, namely account for its causal origin on the one hand, or license and withdraw its content on the other — with the result that the two main camps use "solve the hard problem" to mean different things and no empirical finding could ever close the gap. I want you to test that hypothesis rather than flatter it, and to weigh it against the alternatives: that this is a verbal dispute over "explain," "problem," or "consciousness" that a stipulation would dissolve; that it is a substantive metaphysical disagreement the meta-problem framing merely relocates without softening; that the camps differ about what counts as data, one taking our reports and concepts as the target and the other taking phenomenal character itself as the datum, so they are not theorizing about the same phenomenon; that it rests on bedrock intuitions about intrinsic nature that neither side can defend non-circularly; or that the explanandum is simply not a single thing, since problem-reports are heterogeneous and the puzzle dissolves once they are separated. Then tell me what would count as progress on your preferred diagnosis — a distinction, a formal model, an empirical result such as a dissociation between reported puzzlement and reported experience (and if such a result existed, would either camp actually be moved, or would each absorb it into its existing framework?), or a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and explain why the stalemate, which has now run for decades in one form or another, is itself evidence about the structure of the debate. I am not asking for a survey of the positions or a verdict on which camp is right; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, grounded in what each side actually says, and an honest account of what would move it.
