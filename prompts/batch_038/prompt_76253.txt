Ok so this has been bugging me for years and I've never found a word for it. Is there a name for this?

I read a lot about nuclear weapons and deterrence — not academically, I just find the history gripping — and I keep running into the same shape of argument. When a crisis goes right to the edge and then backs off, that gets written up as deterrence working. When a state gets invaded anyway, that gets written up as deterrence failing. But when nothing happens at all, for decades on end, the nothing is never counted as evidence of anything: it gets explained away as the other side having no reason to attack in the first place, or as diplomacy, or as the fact that a nuclear exchange would have been suicide for both sides regardless of what anyone threatened.

The part I keep coming back to is that the asymmetry seems built into what would have to be true for a success. For the policy to have worked in any given year, several things all have to hold at once: the other side had to be capable of the thing, had to have seriously weighed doing it, had to have been moved by the threat specifically rather than by anything else, and then had to refrain. Three of those four are invisible. You can't observe an intention that got formed and quietly dropped, and the weighing part only ever reaches us through memoirs written thirty years later by people with a stake in how its remembered. The failure case, by contrast, needs exactly one observable fact. They did it.

So the theory can only ever accumulate disconfirmations, and every apparent confirmation can be re-absorbed by saying the feared thing wasn't really on the table anyway.

And there's a second-order thing that follows, which I also can't name. Because success leaves no trace, the only way anyone can demonstrate that the threat is live is to produce something that looks an awful lot like failure — crises, alerts, forces on hair trigger, shows of force, the whole brinkmanship business. A policy that can only ever be validated by near-misses seems like it would start manufacturing them. Is that part of the same concept or a seperate one?

Two things I'd want out of an answer:

1) People point me at the deterrence paradox when I describe this, and I'm fairly sure that's about something else — whether it can be rational or right to intend a retaliation that it would be irrational to actually carry out. And the Sherlock Holmes dog-that-didn't-bark line gets waved around in this connection too. Are either of those actually it, or just neighbors? What else is in the neighborhood — I can think of the way flood defenses and pandemic stockpiles get judged once the disaster doesn't come, and survivorship bias, but I don't know how close either really is.

2) If there's no established term, coin one, and define it tightly enough that I could tell it apart from (a) an ordinary unfalsifiable theory, (b) a theory whose confirming cases are rare but genuine, and (c) survivorship bias. My own guess is that the difference from survivorship bias is that here the sample isn't skewed, the success condition itself is unobservable — but I'd want that spelled out and checked, not just accepted. And where's the edge of the thing: is there any observation at all that would legitimately count as confirming that a deterrent worked, or is the whole enterprise unfalsifiable, and if so is that fatal for it or just an occupational hazard?

Don't bother with a reading list, I'm not writing anything. I just want the concepts named and carved up properly.
