The in-class question was this, 25 minutes allowed, no notes:

"Q3 (15 marks). State Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as precisely as you can, making explicit what is assumed about the individual preferences and what is assumed about the social preference. Then answer: (a) why does this result not contradict Arrow's impossibility theorem? (b) what, if anything, does the theorem establish about the interpersonal comparability of utility? (c) is the theorem by itself sufficient to establish classical utilitarianism? If not, name the gaps and say what would have to be added to close each one."

Here's the answer, transcribed as written including the shorthand:

"Setup: a set of prospects (lotteries over outcomes), individuals i = 1..n, each with an ordering over prospects satisfying the vNM axioms, so each is represented by u_i, unique up to positive affine transformation. There is also a social or ethical ordering over the same prospects which likewise satisfies vNM, represented by u_0. Assume Pareto indifference: if every individual is indifferent between two prospects then so is society.

Theorem: then u_0 = a_1u_1 + ... + a_nu_n + b, with each a_i >= 0; a_i > 0 as soon as you also require that no individual's prospects be socially irrelevant (i.e. a weak Pareto clause). Given the u_i's as fixed, the a_i's are unique, and u_0 itself is unique up to positive affine transformation.

(a) No contradiction. Arrow's domain is profiles of ordinal orderings over sure alternatives and his IIA bans exactly the cardinal information Harsanyi is using, so the two theorems are operating on different information bases. Harsanyi's is basically what you get when you drop IIA: once the social ordering is allowed to see the intensities, unanimity is enough to pin down the linear form. Also Arrow needs a social ordering for every profile, Harsanyi only characterises the form of one social ordering satisfying the Pareto condition.

(b) This is the bit people get wrong. The theorem is supposed to show you dont need interpersonal comparisons as an input. The only individual-level assumption is that each person is vNM-rational, which by itself says nothing about how much one person's utility is worth relative to another's. But the aggregation delivers a single u_0 on a single scale, and the weights a_i are precisely the exchange rates between people. So comparability comes out of the theorem rather than going into it, which is why the result is so strong: cardinal and comparable social utility from purely non-comparable ingredients.

(c) Not quite sufficient, two gaps. First, the theorem gives weights but not equal weights, so you need an anonymity/symmetry assumption to get the classical sum. Second, u_i is preference-satisfaction, so you need a premise identifying a person's utility with their wellbeing before the sum is a sum of welfare. Add those two and the social objective is the total of individual wellbeing, which is the utilitarian criterion. One more thing worth noting: the linear form rules out prioritarianism, since prioritarianism weights the worse off more and any such weighting is nonlinear in the u_i's."
