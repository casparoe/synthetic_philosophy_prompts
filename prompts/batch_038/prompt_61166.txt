I run model validation at a UK building society — six of us, and nothing goes to the board for use until weve signed it off. We get maybe twenty-five challenge memos a year from people who think a model we already approved is wrong: someone from the business side, someone from the inventory team, occasionally an external auditor. Each memo gets three working days from me or a senior validator, then goes to the Model Oversight Committee, two of whose members have never built anything and can smell jargon a mile off. Internal audit sees the entire log.

They've just given me a finding, and it's embarrassing. What they did was take our unwritten habit, write it out as an argument, and point out that it's a famous paradox. Roughly, when a validator is asked why they won't reopen a model that passed validation eighteen months ago, some version of this comes out: we know the model is sound, because we tested it and signed it; so if fresh evidence turns up saying it isn't sound, that evidence is misleading; and if we already know in advance that any such evidence would be misleading, we're entitled to dismiss it before we even look — arguably we'd be irrational to let it shift us. Audit cited the dogmatism paradox (Kripke's, in the form Harman printed) and said our practice is either indefensible or has a principled core that nobody has written down. I'd like to think it's the second one.

So please write me the procedure — not a paper about the paradox, a thing a validator can run on a challenge memo inside three days and hand up to a committee in plain English.

What I need in it:

- The questions to ask, in order, and for each one what counts as a sufficient answer rather than a shrug.
- Where the procedure stops with "no reopening" and says so, and where it hands the decision to the committee because the remaining question is risk appetite rather than fact. Those two exits have to look different on the page.
- What to do in the situation we're actually in most of the time, which is that you cannot tell whether the challenger's evidence is misleading or our original approval was wrong. A procedure that only works when you can tell is useless to me.
- Which distinctions from the epistemology of this have to survive the simplification for the procedure not to be nonsense — the gap between a model being right and being entitled to ignore evidence about it, say, or the gap between dismissing evidence you've actually looked at and refusing to look — and which distinctions can be dropped because no validator has three days for them. Be explicit about that trade-off, because that's where I expect the real work to be.
- Which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. I'd rather be told than find out from audit.

Then run it on three live ones:

**A.** A portfolio manager says our buy-to-let PD model is too pessimistic. His evidence is that arrears across his region have come in below modelled predictions for eleven consecutive quarters. He wants the model recalibrated downwards.

**B.** A graduate on the model inventory team noticed that a correlation input in the secured portfolio model is numerically identical to the one in the legacy model it replaced, with no documentation of where it originally came from. She can't say the number is wrong. She can say that nobody here can show it's right.

**C.** An external auditor re-ran our unsecured backtest over a window that includes the payment-holiday period and shows the model substantially underestimating defaults. We knew about the payment holidays; the model was never meant to cover them. But nothing in the documentation says that, and the auditor's window is a defensible window.

One of these is going to come out badly for us, and I've a suspicion which. Say it plainly anyway — the whole point of the finding is that we've been protecting ourselves.

Finish with a short section on what this procedure cannot do: the kinds of challenge it will systematically get wrong, and why.
