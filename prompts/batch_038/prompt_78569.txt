Something in my family has rearranged how I think about classification: my son was diagnosed with one developmental disorder at seven, and at eleven the diagnosis was replaced wholesale, yet nobody involved seemed to think the first one had simply gotten a fact wrong. I co-facilitate a monthly philosophy reading group at our public library — around eighteen adults, mixed backgrounds, no professional philosophers — and I want to devote our next 75-minute session to whether the categories used in the sciences, and in medicine, carve nature at its joints or are made by us.

Design a hands-on activity for that session in which participants sort a deck of invented specimen cards and are then scored on how well their sorting predicts features they have not yet been shown, so that naturalness becomes something they can feel as predictive success rather than accept as a slogan. Build in two turns of the screw: first, a change in the group's stated practical task that makes a different sorting of the very same deck equally successful; second, a newly revealed property on which two sortings that had been tied come apart. No lecture beforehand, and no technical vocabulary until the debrief.

Give me the card design and the exact property list, materials I can print cheaply, a scoring scheme that needs no money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what just happened onto the realist's and the conventionalist's positions and ends on the diagnostic-revision question I opened with. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including the risk that the tie-breaking reveal lands as a gotcha rather than a discovery — and be frank about which feature of this debate no card game can reproduce, so I know what I have to supply myself.
