I am preparing a session for a mixed seminar in political philosophy and literature, and the pairing I have chosen is Allen Ginsberg's "Howl" against the contemporary social science of coordination failure. What I have in mind is not the whole poem but its second part: the long litany in which the speaker turns to address Moloch directly, invoking the god again and again by name and locating him in the machinery, the money, the war, the factories, the government, and the skyscrapers of postwar America. My seminar is built around the vocabulary of multipolar traps, races to the bottom, and collective-action problems — a vocabulary that, as it happens, took the name Moloch from Ginsberg in the first place — and I want to use the poem to test that vocabulary rather than to decorate it.

Please write me a critical reading that does the following. I have the text in front of me, so there is no need to quote from it; describing the relevant moves and images is enough. Likewise, please do not assemble a bibliography or a reading list.

1. Sort the poem's targets against the standard taxonomy. Take the images the litany accumulates under the name of Moloch and ask, of each family of them, which recognized structure it belongs to: an arms race, a tragedy of the commons, a regulatory or competitive race to the bottom, a filtering or selection dynamic, a principal–agent problem, a public-goods failure. Which settle comfortably into a single box? Which straddle two in a way that suggests the taxonomy is cutting at the wrong joints? And which refuse to sort at all — and for those, is the refusal a limitation of the framework or a failure on the poem's part to have a determinate target?

2. Address the personification problem. A multipolar trap is supposed to be a structure without a villain: every participant does the locally sensible thing, and the equilibrium is bad for all of them, which is precisely why no one can be indicted for it. Ginsberg's rhetorical form is the opposite — apostrophe, an accusation delivered to a hungry, deliberate, devouring agent who demands sacrifice. Does that personification purchase something the agentless description cannot, by making a diffuse structure available to grief and outrage and giving a name to what no one chose? Or does it misdescribe the phenomenon, by implying that there is someone to hold responsible and therefore something that could be abolished, when the analysis says the point is exactly that abolishing the operators would change nothing? Where does the poem seem alert to that tension, and where does it simply cash in on the ambiguity?

3. Take up what I expect to be the hard objection, which concerns endogenous preference. Talk of a race to the bottom presupposes agents who share a bottom and who would all rather not be standing on it; the analysis holds their wants fixed and locates the entire problem in the coordination structure. Much of the poem's complaint looks instead like a claim about the wants themselves — that Moloch has not merely trapped people but remade them into its own shape, so that the appetite driving the machine is produced by the machine. If that is the diagnosis, can the multipolar-trap vocabulary state it at all, or does stating it require a theory of preference formation that the framework deliberately brackets in order to get its results? I would like a clear verdict on whether the residue here marks a limit of the framework or an incoherence in the poem's complaint — and please pursue the disturbing implication either way, namely that a trap whose victims have been reshaped to want what it gives them is not a trap they experience as one, which would make the poem's own addressees the least reliable witnesses to their condition.

4. Consider exit and voice. What does the poem offer, or refuse to offer, as a way out? Do any of its gestures correspond to the remedies the literature recognizes — enforceable agreements, altered payoffs, a coordinating authority, iterated interaction, exit from the game — or is the only departure available to its figures a defection that destroys the defector, undertaken at ruinous personal cost, which is not an exit in any sense a modeler would accept as a solution?

5. Close by saying what the poem contributes to the debate about coordination failure that the formal treatment cannot supply, and be equally frank about where the reading I am proposing has to be abandoned — where the poem is doing something other than diagnosing a social structure and the analyst who insists otherwise is simply projecting. I want the piece to be fair in both directions, since half my students will be defending the poem and half will be defending the models.
