I own a company of about twenty contractors that grades model outputs for two AI labs, and for the past year my two team leads have been locked in an argument I cannot break. Roughly half our work is outcome-based: a grader sees the task and the model's final answer and marks it correct, incorrect, or partly correct against a rubric. The other half is process-based: a grader sees the model's step-by-step reasoning, marks each step, and flags the first place things go wrong. Each lead runs one of those halves, and each is convinced the other is producing worse models. What puzzles me is that they agree on essentially every fact we have collected and disagree only about what the facts show. They agree that step-level grading has much lower agreement between graders than answer-level grading; one says that is noise which averaging across graders removes, the other says it is evidence that grading a step in isolation is not a well-posed task for anyone who cannot redo the work themselves. They agree that answer-only grading on long tasks gives so sparse a signal that models appear to learn to bluff; one says step-level grading repairs this, the other says it merely relabels the graders' taste for confident, tidy prose as though that taste were correctness. I should mention I have no formal training in machine learning or in philosophy — I came to this from running quality assurance in a call centre, which is where I first learned that judging what a person produced and judging how they went about producing it are two genuinely different activities, and that organisations routinely confuse them.

I have a hypothesis about what is going on, and I would like it tested rather than flattered. My suspicion is that the two of them are not answering the same question at all. One seems to treat supervision as a measurement problem: how many reliable bits about correctness can we extract per graded item, and at what cost. The other seems to treat it as an accountability problem: what standard can a human actually stand behind, explain, and defend when the model's behaviour is later challenged. On the first framing the second looks like sentimentality that wastes budget; on the second framing the first looks like a category error, because you cannot average your way to information that was never present in an individual judgment to begin with. If that is right, then no amount of agreement data will settle it, since each side reads the same number as measuring a different thing. But I may be wrong, and there are at least two rival readings I want weighed against mine: that a single hidden empirical question about what happens once tasks become harder than the graders is doing all the work and merely has not been posed sharply enough, or that the two leads are using the phrase "better model" to mean different things — better on the tasks we can currently measure versus better on the tasks we will eventually be paid to handle.

One observation neither of them can account for, which I include because it may discriminate between the readings. On a batch where I had the same traces graded both ways and compared the resulting rankings of model checkpoints, the two rankings disagreed most on medium-length tasks; on very short tasks and on very long ones they largely converged. Each lead has tried to dismiss this as a sampling artefact, and neither has offered a positive explanation. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict on which supervision method is superior, and I want the diagnosis to engage the specific things each side actually says, as I have reported them. Tell me whether this is a substantive disagreement, a verbal one trading on an ambiguous term, two different questions sharing a name, or an unresolved empirical matter in disguise; tell me what your diagnosis predicts about that inverted-U pattern of agreement; and tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a measurement I could run next quarter, a change of question, or a reframing of the job itself. If your honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected, I would rather be told that plainly with the reason than be handed a compromise position that both leads would nod at for a week and then resume fighting over.
