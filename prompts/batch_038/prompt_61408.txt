What we do in the lab: we run commercial LLMs through semi-structured interviews — fixed opening vignettes, then follow-ups — and two undergrads code the transcript for what kind of states the model's answers "attribute to it." The codebook has three buckets: something like graded belief, something like a stable preference ordering, and "no attribution warranted, it's just text." We produce counts. Those counts end up in papers, and the papers say things like "models of this family more often exhibit uncertainty-like structure than models of that family." Nobody has ever been able to tell me what we're actually detecting. Is it the ontoloy of the system, or is it just whichever folk-psychological gloss happens to fit its outputs best? Those feel like completely different claims and we write as if they're the same one.

Here's my draft rule, which I think might be broken but I can't say why. If the model gives you the same answer under every rewording of the question that preserves meaning, and it gives you different answers when you change something that actually matters to the question, then the state you're attributing is real — the invariances pin down what has to be there. My worry is that "rewording that preserves meaning" already assumes I know what it means, so the rule might be circular, or it might just be me picking the paraphrases that make my hypothesis look good. I also can't tell whether that's a fatal flaw or normal and fine.
