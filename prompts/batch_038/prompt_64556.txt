I spent thirty-eight years as a reliability engineer, most of it producing life-extension assessments for plant that had already run past its original design life: turbines, pressure vessels, the steel and concrete of ageing infrastructure. Last month I turned seventy-one. Within a fortnight I had been asked to step down from two boards, and a cardiologist had walked me through my own hazard curve in the flat, unhurried voice people reserve for other people's equipment. My son, who is better at gestures than speeches, sent me Cicero's essay on old age — the one in which Cato the Elder, in his eighty-fourth year, answers two much younger admirers who have been wondering aloud how easily he still carries the weight of his years. I have read it twice. Parts of it genuinely moved me, and parts of it struck me as a maintenance argument in a toga. That combination is why I am writing to you.

Let me be precise about what I want, and about what I can supply. I have no philosophical training and very little Latin. What I am fluent in — fluent enough that it is the language I think in rather than one I translate into — is the vocabulary of reliability and asset management: hazard rates and the bathtub curve; degradation mechanisms as distinct from sudden failures; rated capacity, demonstrated capacity, and derating; redundancy and single points of failure; safety-critical versus function-critical failure modes; condition-based monitoring; remaining useful life; the distinction, which governed most of my working days, between an item's design life and what the evidence about its actual condition licenses you to claim for it; and the standing rule that the honest response to an unavoidable degradation mechanism is not to deny the mechanism but to change what you ask of the component.

Please take Cicero's case and restate it in that vocabulary, charge by charge: the withdrawal from active affairs, the weakening of the body, the loss of pleasures, and the proximity of death; his underlying contention that these are mostly complaints about a man's character rather than about his age; his roll-call of old men who went on governing, writing, and farming; his treatment of appetite as a tyranny worth being emancipated from; and his closing argument that death is either nothing to us or a passage to something better. Where he is in effect making an empirical claim — about which capacities degrade, at what rate, and with how much scatter between individuals — say so, and say it in my terms. Where he is making a claim about what a person is for, or about the standing owed to age in a republic of patrons and clients, do not quietly convert it into a claim about function.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Three headings:

1. What translates cleanly. Claims of his that come out as recognisable statements in my framework, with the correspondence spelled out rather than waved at.
2. What translates only by distorting one side or the other. I suspect his recommendation that older men move from physical to deliberative work is a derating argument; I also suspect that derating concedes a loss of function, whereas he insists nothing of value has been lost. Tell me whether that suspicion is correct, and whether the distortion lies in him or in my vocabulary.
3. What has no expression at all in reliability terms. I would guess that his account of honour and deference, and his final move about the soul, land here. I want to know whether they land there because my framework has a real blind spot — because it can only treat a person as an asset whose worth is a function of continued operation — or because those portions of his case were never arguments and lose nothing by being exposed as such.

Two further constraints. First, flag every point at which the translation tempts you to smuggle my assumptions in and present them as Cicero's; wherever he would object to the reframing itself, say so and say why, since an asset-management idiom already assumes that the question is what a thing is good for, and I do not believe he accepts that framing without qualification. Second, tell me plainly which of his four replies a competent engineer would now judge sound, which are empirical claims my framework could in principle test and might well falsify, and which survive only because they are doing rhetorical rather than argumentative work.

Please do not send me references, a reading list, or anything I would have to look up. I would much rather have your most honest reconstruction, including its uncertainties, than a bibliography I cannot check.
