I spent thirty-five years pulling sediment cores out of lakes and a few coastal basins, and only since retiring have I gotten into the books about how societies come apart — the Maya, Easter Island, the end of the Bronze Age over in the eastern Mediterranean. Honestly the prose is better than most of what I read in my own field. But I've hit a wall and I can't tell whether its me being slow or whether there's a genuine muddle in there.

Here's the thing. In my world we talk about regime shifts constantly: a clear-water lake with plants flips to turbid and algal, and it doesn't flip back even after you cut the phosphorus loading, because it's now sitting in a different basin of attraction with hysteresis holding it there. We even have early-warning signals — critical slowing down, variance and autocorrelation creeping up before the flip. That vocabulary feels obviously relevant to "collapse," and the popular books lean on it hard, resilience and tipping points and so forth.

Except the moment I try to make the analogy precise it seems to break, and I can't articulate where.

With the lake I know it's the same lake before and after. Same hole in the ground, same water, same coordinates. The state variables changed; the system didn't. What is doing the job of "the same lake" for a society? When the southern Maya cities get abandoned and the monuments stop going up, there are still Maya people, still speaking Mayan languages, still farming a lot of the same land, just organized differently and mostly invisible to me. So what exactly is the thing that collapsed? Is there a principled way to pick it out, or are we just choosing a level of description and then reporting that the thing at that level died?

Second problem, and this one I feel in my bones because I've spent my life with dating error bars. The archaeological record is sampled about as badly as the fossil record: we get the stone, the elites, the monuments, the stuff that preserves, and we resolve events to the century when they may have taken a generation. In paleontology there's a name for the trap — Signor and Lipps, I think — the point being that the last fossil is never the last individual, so a gradual disappearance gets read as a sudden one and vice versa. How much of "abrupt collapse" could be an artifact of that? And does it even make sense to ask, given that we can't just go get more cores?

Third, and this may be the real crux. One of these authors — Tainter, if I remember right — insists that collapse just means a rapid, significant drop in sociopolitical complexity, not a catastrophe, and that for the individual people involved it can be a perfectly rational preference, shedding a bureaucracy that stopped paying for itself. That's a completely different claim from the moral freight the word carries when a journalist says a civilization collapsed. So is "collapse" a descriptive term with an operational definition, or is it more like "weed" — no such thing botanically, just a plant growing where somebody doesn't want it?

Can you explain what's actually going on here? I don't want a tour of who said what or a list of books to chase down. I want the conceptual joints: whether the ecology vocabulary can be rescued for societies, and what it costs to rescue it; what would have to be true, or measured, to distinguish "society X collapsed" from "society X was succeeded by something differently organized that the record describes with different words"; and whether there's a fact of the matter about the Maya collapsing or whether the question is malformed in some interesting way. Use my lake wherever it helps. I'd much rather understand this in terms I already trust than learn a fresh jargon — though if the jargon turns out to be necessary, tell me which pieces are load-bearing and which are decoration.
