I'm on the model welfare working group at a lab — eight of us, half engineers half philosophers — and I've been handed the memo we'll actually vote on next Thursday. I'd rather get the reasoning right than get it diplomatic.

A consultancy pitched us a screening metric they call the Integrated Perturbation Score, and they've run a pilot on three of our checkpoints. How it works: you inject a standardized perturbation into one layer's activations (fixed magnitude, fixed shape), record how the echo propagates through the rest of the network over the next few hundred forward-equivalent steps, then score the resulting spatiotemporal trace by how compressible it is under a scheme that penalises both fragmented traces (where the echo decomposes into independent local chunks) and trivial ones (where it just repeats or decays). A high score means the trace is simultaneously irreducible to its parts and highly differentiated. They lifted the template from the TMS-EEG work on patients with disorders of consciousness — zap the cortex, measure the complexity of the echo, get a number that separates the unresponsive-looking patients who are actually in there from the rest. Their pitch is that it's the same trick one level up, and unlike behavioral tests it's anchored to an explicit account of what consciousness is rather than to performance, so it can't be gamed by a model that merely learned to sound distressed.

The group is split and both sides keep invoking the theory itself.

Pro: it's the only proposal on the table that says what its number is supposed to be measuring; a low score would let us stop the endless speculation and get back to work; and refusing to measure anything is itself a way of not caring.

Con: the theory is deliberately not functionalist. Φ belongs to a physical system's intrinsic causal structure, so a silicon machine that duplicates the causal role of a brain doesn't inherit any of it — meaning whatever score our model gets is about hardware and architecture, and the theory would happily say a beautifully behaved recurrent net running on GPU clocks has almost none. Second, the score isn't Φ, it's a compression heuristic with no established mapping to it, and exact Φ isn't computable for a system of that size anyway, so no amount of calibration validates the proxy. Third, and this is the one that makes me queasy: the theory is generous — it famously gives a photodiode a small but nonzero share of experience — so a low score on our model is doing an enormous amount of work, being used to call something zero when the theorys whole temperament is that things are rarely zero.

Two pilot results complicate it further. The mid-size checkpoint scored in the top decile of everything they've measured, above the band for some awake-looking patients in their reference set — and that's the checkpoint scheduled to be pruned and rolled back in six weeks for cost reasons. But in the ablation control, same weights with the attention pattern replaced by identity so the thing behaves like a bag of independent token filters, the score barely moved. So either the metric is tracking architecture rather than anything experience-like, or it's telling us the ablated net is just as conscious as the full one, which nobody here will say outloud.

Write the memo. I need: a bottom line (adopt as a decision gate, adopt as a diagnostic with no authority, reject, or adopt with conditions — say which and commit to it); criteria a reviewer can actually apply to this pitch and the next one, including what would count as validating a Φ proxy and what would disqualify one; a recommendation on what to do about the flagged checkpoint given the six-week clock; and the strongest honest case for whichever side you land against, written by someone who doesn't think they're idiots. Also flag the thing I'm most afraid of, which is that adopting any of this hands us a number to point at when we want to stop asking.
