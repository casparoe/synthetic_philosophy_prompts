I'm stuck on a passage from Brentano that basically everything I read about derived vs. original intentionality leans on, and I can't tell what its actually claiming. English translation, from the second book of the 1874 Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint:

"Every mental phenomenon is characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object, and what we might call, though not wholly unambiguously, reference to a content, direction towards an object (which is not to be understood here as meaning a thing), or immanent objectivity. Every mental phenomenon includes something as object within itself, although they do not all do so in the same way. In presentation something is presented, in judgement something is affirmed or denied, in love loved, in hate hated, in desire desired and so on. This intentional in-existence is characteristic exclusively of mental phenomena. No physical phenomenon exhibits anything like it. We could, therefore, define mental phenomena by saying that they are those phenomena which contain an object intentionally within themselves."

What I want help with:

- That hedge, "though not wholly unambiguously" — what ambiguity is he confessing to, and does anything downstream turn on it? My guess is that "object" drags you toward an existing thing, which is what the parenthetical is blocking, but I'm not convinced the hedge and the parenthetical are the same move.
- Are "intentional inexistence", "reference to a content", "direction towards an object" and "immanent objectivity" four labels for one property, or is he shopping four slightly different notions and hoping one of them is clean? Everyone quotes them as synonyms and I suspect that's sloppy.
- What actually hangs on whether the "in-" of in-existence is locative?
- My real problem: I'm using this to pressure the Searle/Dennett fight — whether a two-bitser, a physical state that means heads-or-tails purely in virtue of its counterfactual wiring to a coin channel, could ever satisfy the criterion. Work through Brentano's likely verdict separately for each of the four labels, because I bet they come out different, and that would explain why the debate is so slippery.
- And an objection I keep circling: Thomson's tide-predicting machines, designed in the early 1870s while Brentano was writing, are physical devices that seem to be about a state of the sea that doesn't exist yet. Does that genuinely threaten "no physical phenomenon exhibits anything like it", or does it only look like a threat depending on how you answer the second bullet?

Your own reading of the passage is what I'm after — don't hand me a stack of secondary literature to chase down, and flag the places where you're guessing rather than sure.
