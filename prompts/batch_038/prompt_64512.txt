I am assembling a two-week unit for my graduate seminar on the ethics of war, and I have paired G. E. M. Anscombe's 1956 pamphlet *Mr. Truman's Degree* — her protest against Oxford's conferral of an honorary degree on Truman — with Christopher Nolan's *Oppenheimer*, which nearly all of the students will already have seen. Before I assign the film I want to know whether it can bear Anscombian weight, or whether it quietly installs the outlook she is attacking.

Let me state her position as I read it, so that we are not talking past one another. Her objection to Hiroshima and Nagasaki is not that the bombings produced bad consequences, or that the numbers came out wrong; it is that the order was a choice to kill the innocent as a means to an end, and that such a choice is murder whatever the tally. What makes the degree a scandal, on her account, is that it is a public act: to honour a man for that August is to announce that a pair of massacres belongs among his achievements. She is not a pacifist, and she is careful to separate merely foreseeing that one's action will certainly kill innocents from choosing their death — while adding that an unscrupulous failure to examine the alternatives can convert the former into the latter. She also insists that spectacular wickedness does not make an otherwise mediocre person impressive.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than confirmed, is that the film substitutes attribution for permission: its moral energy goes into whose hands the blood is on and into how the deed feels to the man who did it, rather than into whether the deed is of a kind that may never be chosen. Think of the Oval Office scene in which Truman extends his own hands and takes the deed onto himself, and then disparages Oppenheimer once he has left the room; the gymnasium address that collapses for him into visions of peeling skin and stampede; the fact that the film never once enters either city, so that the bombings exist only as names on a target list, a magazine cover, and a physicist's hallucinations; and the near-zero probability of igniting the atmosphere that Oppenheimer and Einstein agree they can live with.

Against that frame, I want the following. First, take each of those four episodes in turn and say whether it presses Anscombe's question or answers a different one. Second, tell me whether the film's moral grammar remains consequentialist even when it condemns — whether the indictment is ultimately that the calculation was bad rather than that the calculation was the wrong sort of thing to make. Third, and most important for my purposes, tell me where the film resists her: whether its displacement of the bombing by the hydrogen bomb and the security hearing changes the subject in a way that costs her anything, and whether anything in it engages the concession she makes about foreseeing and about unscrupulous inquiry into alternatives. Fourth, say what the film shows that her pamphlet's argument cannot show on its own. Finally, name the two or three sequences I should screen and what the students ought to be looking at inside them.

I want a reading faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of a doctrine, and do not soften the doctrine to make the film look better than it is. Describe scenes and paraphrase Anscombe; supply no invented dialogue and no invented quotations from either source.

Open with your bottom line in the first sentence — a single sentence stating whether the film is Anscombian, sentimental, or something else — and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of my question.
