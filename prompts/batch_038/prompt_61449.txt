I'm a novelist and I've got myself into a hole. My third book — out in the spring, so the hole is now load-bearing — is set in a Hebridean fishing village and the engine of it is a boat that gets rebuilt plank by plank over forty years while the owner's brother quietly bags every plank that comes off and rebuilds the entire original boat in a barn behind the chapel. It was supposed to be a comic business, a feud about a shed. It has turned into the spine of the novel, and I now have four characters who each have a different answer to which of the two boats in the harbour is the real one, and I've stopped being able to tell whether their disagreements are actually four different disagreements or one disagreement wearing four hats. I did a single philosophy module about twenty years ago and I remember the words endurantism and perdurantism and that it was the most absorbing thing I ever studied, but I dont trust my memory of the architecture any more, and I'm on a residency in a bothy with no signal for another three weeks, so whatever you write has to carry its own weight without me being able to look anything up. No reading list and no names of philosophers, please — I can't chase them and I don't want you inventing page numbers for me.

What I want is a decision tree, or rather a walk through one, that takes an ordinary reader from "honestly, they're both the boat, aren't they" to a named position on how things persist through time. Build it out of the logical space first rather than out of a list of isms: give me the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, and only then tell me which cells are actually occupied and by what. Nine or ten branch questions, five or six leaves. Every question has to be answerable by someone who has never heard the vocabulary, so it should be a concrete case or a plain claim — a thing wholly present at an instant, a person who is straight at one time and bent at another, two boats in one harbour — and not a bit of jargon in disguise. Don't ask me whether I accept temporal parts; ask me something I could answer over a cup of tea and only afterwards reveal what I've committed myself to. At least these have to be nodes somewhere along the way: whether a thing is ever wholly present at a moment or always spread out along time; whether change of properties requires having different bits at different times; whether "the same" can be relative to the kind of thing you're talking about, so that the same-boat-as-regatta-winner and the same-boat-as-property can come apart; whether two things can be in exactly the same place at the same time; and whether the whole quarrel might be verbal, with nothing really at stake. Each leaf gets its name and one sentence on the commitment that's most awkward for it to live with — the thing its defender has to keep changing the subject about. Then be honest with me about the cells nobody occupies: which combinations are ruled out as incoherent, which are merely ghastly to hold, and which are just unexplored and might be worth a novel. And flag every pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely seperate, because that's the bit I most need: specifically, whether the stage view and the worm view can be told apart by anything my characters could say out loud, and whether an endurantist who explains away the hard cases with counterparts is doing anything other than the stage view with better manners. Tell me too what the tree looks like if I cut out the two-things-in-one-place branch entirely — is it a shorter tree, or a broken one?

The reason I need this rather than an essay is my four people. The old owner says the harbour boat is his boat because it's the one that's been in the water and working every season and he doesn't give a fig that not one plank is original. His brother says the barn boat is the real one, because it's made of the real stuff, and that the thing in the harbour is a perfectly good new boat built out of new wood. The insurance surveyor says neither of them is asking a question with an answer until they say what they want it for — same boat for the salvage claim, different boat for the regatta entry, and she's tired of both of them. And the young maritime historian who narrates it says the lot of them are talking nonsense, that each boat in front of them is only a slice of a much longer thing that neither of them will ever point at. At the end of your walk, tell me which leaf each of the four has actually landed on, and whether any two of them are standing on the same leaf without realising it — that's the chapter I haven't been able to write. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables; I'll be reading it in bed by a lamp and I want to be able to follow the branches by the shape of your sentences, so just say "if she says yes to that, go on to ask her..." and so on. And in British English throughout, please — I wince at every z in an -ize ending.
