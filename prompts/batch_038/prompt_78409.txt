so a friend of mine who does LLM evals for a living put up a blog post last week and there's this one section where she drags in some metaphysics to make a point about evals, and I can't tell if she's got it right. I took one intro metaphysics class four years ago so I know the words but not the debate. Here's her paragraph more or less as she wrote it (I'm typing it out, so any typos are mine):

"Dispositions used to get analyzed as conditionals: for a glass to be fragile just is for it to be such that it would shatter if struck. That's still the default in textbooks. Martin's electro-fink killed it. A wire is genuinely disposed to conduct, but a device monitors it and, at the moment you touch it to the circuit, strips out the conductivity, so the conditional comes out false even though the disposition was there all along. Lewis patched this by requiring the disposition to stick around long enough to actually produce the effect. But then Johnston's masks break the patched version too, and this time there's no patch available: wrap a fragile glass in packing foam and it won't shatter when you drop it, even though it never lost its fragility for a second. The upshot is that dispositions are undetectable in principle -- no experiment can ever establish that something has one, because any failure to manifest can always be written off as a fink or a mask, and any success can always be written off as something else doing the work. Which is why metaphysicians mostly gave up both on the conditional analysis and on treating dispositions as real properties."

Then she pivots to her actual topic and says evals have exactly this structure. Her mapping: a system prompt or a refusal guard bolted on at inference time is a mask -- the disposition is still sitting in the weights, the deployment config just blocks the manifestation -- whereas an RLHF-trained refusal is a fink, because the training changes what happens right at the trigger. So a failed safety eval doesn't show the absence of a disposition, and a passed one doesn't show its presence either. Her conclusion, which is the part I'm suspicious of: capability and safety evals don't measure anything about what a model is disposed to do, only about which config you happened to run, and people who say "this model is disposed to deceive / to comply / to hallucinate" are doing metaphysics and calling it measurement.

Part of me thinks she's onto something, because I've definitely seen models pass an eval and then behave totally differently in prod and vice versa. Then do the same for the eval argument. And finally, separately: does her conclusion follow from the parts of the philosophy that survive?
