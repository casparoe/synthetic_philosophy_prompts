I chair the standing review panel of a mid-sized research institute: twenty-three members, mostly working scientists, who vote by simple majority on a single binary question — whether a submitted result should be treated as established. In eight years we have cast roughly nineteen hundred ballots. Our founding charter justifies the procedure explicitly on the Condorcet jury theorem, and for the past three years the panel has been arguing about that justification without anyone moving an inch. I want a diagnosis of the argument rather than a verdict in it, and I am not after a survey of the literature or any references: work from what the four camps actually say in our meetings, as I have set them out below.

The expansionists hold that the charter's reasoning is sound and that the panel's problems are problems of scale. Individual competence above chance is, they say, an empirically respectable estimate for most of our question types; the independence clause is an idealization, but violations merely slow convergence rather than defeat it, so the correct remedy for our errors is a larger and more numerous panel, perhaps with the voting threshold left untouched.

The correlation skeptics reply that this gets the theorem exactly backwards. Because our members share training, literatures, preprint feeds and professional incentives, votes are strongly positively correlated, and they maintain — with an argument they consider decisive — that under such correlation majority accuracy need not rise with panel size at all, and may fall. For them, size is not the lever; decorrelation is: staggered independent assessment before any discussion, sealed ballots, and recruitment against shared intellectual lineage.

The strategists think both of those camps are modeling the wrong thing. Real jurors, they say, do not vote sincerely on private signals; they vote with an eye to how others will vote and to what the consequences of being on the losing side will be. On their picture, aggregation happens through equilibrium behavior under the voting rule, and what determines accuracy is incentive design and the sequencing of the vote, not any ex ante distribution of individual competence. They regard the whole jury-theorem framing as a category mistake.

The constitutivists deny the premise all three share. For a large share of our questions, they argue, there is no fact of the matter, fixed independently of us, about whether a result is established; our verdict partly constitutes what established means, since it controls hiring, funding and citation practice. On this view the theorem is not false but inapplicable, and the other three camps are merely disputing the accuracy of a measurement with no fixed target.

There is one structural feature nobody has analyzed. A five-member screening committee sees every submission and forwards a subset to the full panel, so all of our votes are conditioned on having survived screening.

Finally, the numbers that keep getting invoked. For 214 decisions an independent later check eventually existed — a replication, a retraction, an unambiguous subsequent finding — and the majority verdict matched it in 146 cases. The average individual member, scored against the same checks, was right about 62 percent of the time. The expansionists say 62 percent vindicates the competence premise; the skeptics say the gap between 62 and 68 is proof of near-total correlation. Everyone also concedes that cases acquire later checks mainly when the panel acted on them, and nobody agrees about what that does to the sample.

My own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the impasse is largely an equivocation on competence, which is being used now for the marginal probability that a member's vote matches a checkable truth and now for a conditional quantity — how a member votes given what others have seen, or given that the case was screened in — and that the expansionists and skeptics would largely agree if each were forced to say which of the two they mean. A second, deeper equivocation may run through the word correct.

What I want from you is the diagnosis, argued rather than listed. Sort the four positions into genuine contradictions, merely verbal disputes, and cases of two groups answering different questions under one heading, and say which pairs cannot both be right. Test my equivocation hypothesis: does it explain the stalemate, or is something else doing the work? Interrogate the arithmetic — is 62 percent individual accuracy alongside 68 percent majority accuracy at twenty-three voters even coherent with the skeptics' story, and if so, what would the residual correlation have to be, and is that independently plausible, or does the discrepancy point somewhere else, such as at the screening step or at the way the 62 percent was computed? Identify any hidden empirical question our own ballot data could settle, and say precisely what we would have to estimate and whether a sample selected in that way can bear the weight. Then tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a formal result, a measurement, a change of question — or, if you conclude that the charter is asking the theorem to do something it cannot do in principle and that no progress should be expected, argue for that instead.
