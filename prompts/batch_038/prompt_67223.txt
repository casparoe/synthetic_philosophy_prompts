I am three years into a novel set in a river valley where the local language has no written form, no dictionary, and no missionary grammar — perhaps nine hundred speakers, most of them past fifty. To avoid writing a book about a place I had invented from the outside, I have been spending four or five months a year there assembling the language myself, from nothing: I sit in kitchens and on riverbanks, I watch what happens, I point, I ask, I write phonetic approximations in a notebook. I now have some eleven hundred entries.

The notebook has stopped being an asset and has become a trap, and I do not know how to get out of it. To record what a word means, I must assume I already know what the speakers believe about the thing the word is used for. To record what they believe, I must assume I already know what their words mean. I go around this circle again and again, and each time around I seem to add a little of myself and lose a little of them.

Three things have brought this to a head.

First: there is a word — call it naya, though nothing in my spelling is right — which my steadiest informant, a woman of about sixty who has been extraordinarily patient with me, glosses as something like the respect a person is owed. I wrote that down. Watching how it is actually deployed, though, I find it tracks something narrower and stranger: it surfaces almost only where someone has fed you, housed you, or pulled you out of the water, and where the debt has not yet been discharged. People owed ordinary deference — the priest, the oldest man in the village — do not reliably get naya. When I put the discrepancy to my informant she was unmoved and told me a long story about her grandmother, at the end of which I understood less than when I began. I cannot tell whether her gloss is a translation or a theory about her own language, and I have no way to ask her the difference.

Second: there is a sentence I hear in a certain context near the hill above the cemetery which, by my notebook, amounts to a claim that a named ancestor is presently alive and living inside the hill. Everyone present holds it true, including two men who service diesel generators for a living and have been to the city many times. My first instinct was that my translation must be wrong — that they cannot mean what my glossary makes them mean. But my only evidence for mistranslating is that the belief seems impossible to hold, and I could not say what else might count as evidence. So I am settling what they believe by settling what they say, and I do not know what would make that honest rather than merely comfortable.

Third, and most unnerving: the notebook is wrong in ordinary conversation constantly, and nobody minds. A fisherman will use a word that by my entries belongs to a different thing altogether; everyone follows him; someone takes the word up and uses it differently again three sentences later; a woman will open a sentence in a way my glossary forbids and land somewhere I could not have predicted, and the table laughs in the right place. In those exchanges I am not consulting my entries at all. I am doing something else, doing it successfully, and I cannot say what I know when I know it. It makes me suspect the eleven hundred entries are the record of a language nobody speaks.

A friend who trained as an anthropologist tells me to stop worrying. There is, she says, no fact of the matter about which of my competing glossaries is correct, since more than one could be made to fit everything I will ever observe, and they would not agree about what the valley's sentences mean; on her account I am not discovering their language but selecting one. She means this as a comfort. I find it alarming, and I notice she offers no way to distinguish a good selection from a self-flattering one.

Concretely, I have a chapter to revise in which a character says the hill sentence to a child, and I must decide whether to render it in English so that she asserts something my readers will take to be flatly false, or to adjust the words around it so that she says something defensible and the scene forfeits its strangeness. I would rather not be advised to consult a linguist or to spend another season there; I have done both and neither touches the problem. What I want is your judgment about whether the circle I have described can be broken from inside — by a procedure I could actually follow with a notebook, without a speaker's help — and if it cannot, what it is that I am doing well in the conversations where the notebook fails. I want to know whether the fact that one glossary lets me anticipate what people will say next in situations they have never been in is evidence that it is right, or only evidence that it is mine. And tell me plainly if you think my friend is correct that there is nothing here to be right about, since that would change how I write the book, and I would sooner learn it from you than from the reviews.
