Background first, because it explains why I'm asking you and not a professor. Thirty-eight years in a low-temperature physics lab, retired four years ago, and I've fallen into philosophy the way other retirees fall into golf. I started with Pascal for sentimental reasons — the man took a barometer up the Puy de Dôme and built a calculating machine to help his father with tax accounts, so I came for the scientist and stayed for everything else. The everything else being the political fragments. The justice-and-force ones have got me by the throat and I cant shake them.

Here's the position I've ended up accepting, and what I want is a map of where it's exposed. My reconstruction — tell me straight if I've mangled it — is that Pascal isn't just saying the strong win. It's a two-level claim: "Il est juste que ce qui est juste soit suivi : il est nécessaire que ce qui est le plus fort soit suivi," and then the punchline, "ne pouvant fortifier la justice, on a justifié la force." Justice is endlessly disputable; force isn't disputable at all. So you can never in practice hand power over to justice — instead you relabel whatever force has already produced as just, and then obedience looks principled rather than coerced. Add the fragment where he says it's dangerous to tell the people the laws aren't just, because they obey only while they believe they are, so you have to tell them to obey because these are the laws. And add the pensée de derrière business: the crowd honors the great for bad reasons, the half-clever despise them for good reasons, the genuinely clever honor them again for a reason behind that. Same behavior at every level, opposite justifications.

Call it the sanctification model: no political order coheres by being right; it coheres by strength that has been taught to look like rightness, and the looking-like is load-bearing.

Now, what would have to be true for that to be false? Specifically:

1. Sort the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and label them. In my line of work we were trained to separate the result that kills the model from the one that just makes everyone uncomfortable at a seminar, and I want that separation here.

2. Pressure-test the counter-evidence I dug up myself, which I can't evaluate. States complying with adverse rulings from international tribunals that have no way to make them comply. Leaders with the power to stay who leave because a rule says so. Election losers conceding. Constitutional conventions nobody can enforce and nobody much likes. My worry: each of these might be exactly what the sanctification model predicts one time-slice later — force did the work originally, belief is the residue. Is that reply a real explanation or a shrug?

3. The unfalsifiability question, honestly. Between level one (force), level two (opinion), and level three (the thought behind the thought), it looks like every possible outcome can be narrated. What observation could the model not accommodate? And if the answer is none — is that a defect, given that this may be a lens for making political order intelligible rather than a hypothesis about it? We had a word for theories like that in my field and it wasn't a compliment, but I suspect the comparison to physics is itself sloppy here. Tell me where its sloppy.

4. What a serious Pascalian would concede — not what critics say, but what a defender grants as counting against him. I suspect the honest answer runs through the theology: there is a true justice for Pascal, it's just unreachable, so the model is a claim about our situation and not about justice itself. Does that rescue it, or move the unfalsifiable part upstairs? Does the order of charity escape the force/justice dialectic or merely relocate it?

5. Rivals that make different predictions. Hobbes looks adjacent but I don't think he's identical. Hume on opinion and the many governing the few seems closer. And the little discourses on the condition of the great published after Pascal's death under his name, which split standing-by-convention from standing-by-nature, read to me as a different account of why we bow. Where do these actually diverge — what would one predict and the other not?

6. The self-application problem. If every normative order is sanctified strength, then his own advice (obey the established laws, since civil war is the worst evil) is just another move in the game, and his telling us so is a performance the model says can't be performed. Fatal, awkward, or dissolved by the two-level structure?

Finally, name your bet: the single thing most likely to break it, and the single thing its defenders will keep saying no matter what happens. Don't send me a reading list — no web access, and I'd rather you reason it through than point me at books. And if my reconstruction turns out to be a mid-century French reading dressed up as a lab result, say so in the first line rather than the last.
