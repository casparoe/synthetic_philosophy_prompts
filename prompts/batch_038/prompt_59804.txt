I am a staff engineer at a mid-size infrastructure company, and I own the internal standard for how we write the causal-analysis section of incident postmortems. I have no formal philosophy training, so please define any term of art the first time you use it, and do not include references or a reading list — I want something my colleagues can actually run, not a survey.

Here is the problem that motivated this. Two informal camps have formed on our review board. The first wants causal claims justified by counterfactual dependence: a condition counts as a cause of an incident if, had it been different, the incident would not have happened. The second objects that this licenses an explosion of causes — every guard that failed to fire, every absent safeguard, every ambient fact about the world — and prefers something closer to the interventionist picture, on which a cause is a variable whose value could be changed by an intervention that leaves the other relevant variables fixed, in a way that reliably changes the outcome across a range of background conditions. In practice both camps publish nearly identical English sentences, and we cannot tell whether we are disagreeing about anything.

**What I want:** a decision procedure, runnable in a 90-minute postmortem review by an engineer with no philosophy background, that converts raw incident material (timeline, telemetry, change records, human recollection) into a defensible, explicitly scoped list of causal claims. Specifically:

1. The steps in order. For each step, state the input it needs and whether that input comes from telemetry, from documents, or from a human judgment; what counts as a sufficient answer; and the output it produces. Flag which steps could later be automated from telemetry, because we intend to build tooling on top of whatever we settle on.
2. The first substantive step should concern the choice of variable set — which events and states get to be variables at all, at what granularity, over what time window — and it should say honestly whether that step is mechanical or a judgment call, and what makes one variable set better than another rather than merely convenient.
3. A rule for promoting absences (a disabled circuit breaker, a missing rate limit, an unmuted alert, an unclosed ticket) to causes, and a rule for demoting them, since otherwise everything is a cause and the report says nothing.
4. A rule for preemptive structure — the trigger that fired, with a second sufficient trigger queued behind it — and a rule for overdetermination, where two independent defects each suffice.
5. A fixed sentence grammar for the published report: a rigorous version and a plain version an executive can read, plus what must be attached to every claim (which intervention was considered, what was held fixed, the range of conditions over which the claim is supposed to hold, and a confidence statement).
6. The points at which the procedure must stop and hand the question to a person, because what remains is about responsibility, acceptable risk, or values rather than about facts.

**Then run it on three incidents.**

- **Incident A.** A canary deploy of our payments service introduced a slow query path. Upstream callers retried without jitter. The circuit breaker that would have contained the storm had been disabled eleven days earlier for a load test, and the re-enable ticket was never closed. Two further facts: a batch reconciliation job scheduled for 03:00 would have tipped the same subsystem over on its own within roughly four hours; and the on-call engineer's documented runbook step — failing traffic over to a second region — succeeded, but doubled retry load and extended the outage by about forty minutes because of an unrelated connection-pool default in that region.
- **Incident B.** An expired TLS certificate on an internal endpoint and a stale DNS TTL pinning clients to a decommissioned host; each was independently sufficient for the outage; both were corrected in the same change; and the alerting rule that should have caught the expiry had been muted during an earlier migration and never restored.
- **Incident C.** A database migration, reviewed and approved by our architecture board and executed exactly per the approved plan, caused six hours of downtime because of a latent assumption about row-lock behavior that no test covered. The plan was reasonable given everything known at approval time. The only thing the analysis seems able to implicate is the board's decision two years earlier to standardize on that migration tooling. I expect the procedure's output here to annoy people, and I want it anyway.

**Finally**, tell me where the procedure will misfire, what it cannot do at all, and — the part I care about most — whether the dispute between the two camps actually changes the text we publish in more than a handful of cases. If for most incidents both produce the same sentences, say so plainly and characterize the residue: the kinds of incident where the choice of account decides what gets written.
