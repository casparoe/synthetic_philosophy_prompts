So, six weeks from now I'm giving the closing talk at a writing-and-computation colloquium at a state university, which seemed like a great idea in an email and now seems like a mistake. I've got one novel out, one that sold okay, no training in philosophy, and nobody in that room is a novelist. I'm the novelty act.

The room, as far as I can tell: mostly cognitive scientists who study reading and creativity, two philosophers of art (at least one a hardline formalist who thinks bringing up the author's intentions is a rookie move), a clutch of MFA students who draft with a model and will take this personally, and one editor from a mid-list press who only cares about deadlines and unit sales.

Here's what I'm arguing, roughly. Readers don't value a sentence mainly for what it says or how it sounds — they value it as the survivor of a refusal. It beat the eleven versions the writer could have written instead, and part of what reading is, is silently rebuilding those near-misses and the person who turned them down. I'm calling that the shadow of the discarded draft. Machine-drafted prose doesn't have one. Not because its dumber, plenty of it is perfectly good — but the alternatives it beat were never refused by anybody, so that part of the reading act has nothing to hold onto. Which gives me a conclusion that's narrow and kind of mean: a generated passage isn't automatically bad writing, it's just unreadable as choice, and choice is most of what we mean by style.

The skeleton I'll actually give them: if the shadow theory is right, two identical paragraphs should feel different the moment you learn one was chosen and one was sampled; readers do report that shift, and criticism has a whole vocabulary for it; so the value isn't sitting in the paragraph, it's in the reader's reconstruction, and a reconstruction needs a reconstructor.

I want the Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the talk. Give me the seven or so questions that specific room will actually ask — not the ones the internet would give me, not the ones a philosophy department would — ordered by how much they'd hurt. Put each one in the mouth of whoever's likeliest to ask it, so I can hear the difference between the psycholinguist version, the formalist version, and the wounded-MFA-student version. For each: two or three sentences I could plausibly say out loud, plus one honest line about where that answer is soft. Somebody will bring up the studies where readers couldn't tell generated text from human text, so take that one head-on instead of steering around it. Then tell me what to give away in the first two minutes so I'm not defending things I don't believe, what I can't concede without the whole talk falling over, and the one reply that has to land or I'm done. End with the thing I'd rather not be asked at all, and why it's worse than everything above it.
