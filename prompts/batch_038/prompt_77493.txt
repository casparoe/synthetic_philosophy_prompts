I retired five years ago after thirty-eight years in structural biology — most of it spent on enzyme kinetics, the last decade directing a laboratory of some twenty people — and I have taken up philosophy with the same plodding habits I brought to the bench: reading slowly, annotating heavily, and trying to state another person's argument in my own words before objecting to it. My former institute has asked me to convene a monthly series in which a scientist and a philosopher are seated together and required to disagree productively in public. Last month's pairing went badly, and I have been made responsible for producing the paper the two of them ought to have written jointly. I had read Strawson's 1962 British Academy lecture, Freedom and Resentment, three times beforehand and annotated it as though it were a laboratory protocol, which probably prejudiced me in his favour. One of my speakers is a Strawsonian about moral responsibility: he holds that resentment, indignation, gratitude and moral blame constitute the framework within which the very idea of responsibility has its life, so that determinism has nothing to overturn. The other is a sceptic of the familiar kind: she holds that once an agent's conduct is fully explained by her circumstances, her temperament and her history, nobody ever deserves blame or praise in the basic sense, and that the Strawsonian has simply changed the subject. Two hours passed with the sceptic accusing her opponent of redefining responsibility into safety, and the Strawsonian accusing his of demanding a warrant that could only be issued from outside the practice whose warrant is in question — a demand he regards as unintelligible. Both are old friends of mine; neither will sign anything the other has drafted.

I should like you to draft the joint statement that such an adversarial collaboration ought to end in, written so that each of them could put a name to it without feeling described in the other's vocabulary. It should open with a preamble of a paragraph or two that both can sign, then a numbered list of the claims they genuinely agree on: what the reactive attitudes are and what distinguishes them from mere aversion or mere approval; the conditions under which we already, and uncontroversially, suspend them — the very young, the gravely mentally ill, the compulsive, the temporarily distraught, the victim of extraordinary strain; what determinism is and is not being claimed to show; and what the objective attitude amounts to, including the fact that it is a resource we already deploy locally rather than a general policy anyone has ever managed to sustain. Then a table of the disagreements that survive scrutiny: for each one, the claim as the Strawsonian would phrase it and as the sceptic would phrase it, each in wording its own author accepts, together with a statement of what would settle it. Then the cruxes — I want them identified as such rather than buried in the prose — and for each crux an argument or case that both parties accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance, so that at least one of them is on the hook if the case comes out the other way. Where you judge some part of the dispute to be merely verbal, say which part and why, and say what is left once the words are pinned down. Please do not dissolve the disagreement into agreeable vagueness: where the two cannot sign the same sentence, that is itself a finding, and I want it stated exactly. About fifteen hundred words for the statement itself.

I had better supply you with my own material, since I would rather the cruxes be tested on cases than on slogans. Three from my working life, which I ask you to use for at least some of the cruxes — and to treat sceptically, if you think any of them is rigged in one side's favour. First: over several years a technician in my laboratory falsified instrument-calibration records, endangering work that later fed into clinical decisions. When it surfaced I felt an indignation of a quite particular kind — not merely a wish to be rid of the man, but the sense that he owed an account to identifiable people. I have never been able to explain to myself why, if I also believed his conduct to be the inevitable upshot of his temperament and his circumstances, that attitude should have been withheld from him. Second: a former colleague with whom I had a wretched dispute over authorship later developed a progressive neurological illness. My resentment did not argue itself out of existence; it faded as my picture of him changed, much as alarm at a noise in the building fades once one learns where it comes from. I want both parties to say whether that fading is best read as the reactive attitudes correcting themselves in the light of what is true of him, or as psychological weather with no bearing on warrant at all — and to commit to a prediction about what else we should expect to find if their reading is the right one. Third, which I have taken to calling the persistence datum: I have held determinism to be true, or near enough, since I was an undergraduate, and it has made no impression whatever on my gratitude, my indignation, or my conviction that people owe one another things. I can adopt the objective attitude toward particular people on particular occasions, but I cannot hold it, and in seventy-three years I have not met anyone who could. Is my persistence something the sceptic must explain away as an irrational residue of our evolutionary history, or does it show that the reactive attitudes were never the sort of thing a metaphysical thesis could have dislodged? If, after working through these three, you think the real crux lies elsewhere — my own suspicion is that it concerns whether a complete causal explanation removes the warrant for resentment or merely gives us leave to set resentment aside in particular instances — say so plainly, and put that disagreement into a form both of my speakers would recognise as theirs.
