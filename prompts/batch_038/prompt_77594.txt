I teach a graduate seminar on the philosophy of social science, and this term our unit is the long-running dispute over whether the world is becoming less violent. I want to pair the readings with Kubrick's 1971 film of *A Clockwork Orange*, with Burgess's novel close at hand for the ending the film does not use, and I want a serious piece of criticism that treats the film as an argument about measurement rather than as a parable about free will.

The reading I need should work through the conceptual machinery the debate actually depends on and show what the film does with each piece of it. What counts as a violent act: is the Ludovico treatment itself an entry on the ledger, or only the blows Alex can no longer deliver? Whose suffering counts as harm when the person harmed is the one we have classified as a perpetrator? Rates or absolute numbers: the Minister's case at the demonstration is a per-capita promise about future offending, but the film's Britain never discloses a base rate, and we are given no denominator at all. And is there a fact of the matter independent of the metric, or is the film's central political event precisely the state's choice of a metric — one on which its own intervention cannot appear as violence, and one on which Alex's testimony about his own condition carries no evidential weight?

Please take the causal-inference problems seriously too, because I think they are where the film is most quietly interesting. Alex is a volunteer who takes the treatment to shorten a fourteen-year sentence, so assignment is not random; the demonstration audience is handpicked; the two droogs who beat him are now on the police payroll, which relocates violence into an institution whose acts are recorded differently; and in the novel's closing chapter Alex simply grows out of it at eighteen, which is a maturation confound that would have produced the same apparent decline with no intervention whatsoever. The film's ending — the aversions gone, the fantasies back, the Minister posing with him for the cameras — implies relapse instead. Tell me which of the two endings gives the better case study, and what each one says about whether a measured decline in violence can ever be attributed to the policy announced alongside it.

I want the criticism to be faithful in both directions. The film is not primarily about statistics, and an honest reading should say where this lens starts to distort it: where Burgess and Kubrick are concerned with moral agency, aestheticized cruelty, and the state's use of persons as instruments, and where forcing those concerns into a debate about operationalizing violence flattens them. Where the film genuinely resists the reading, say so and say why.

Concretely, I need: three or four scenes you would screen and discuss, with what each one is supposed to establish; an account of which side of the decline debate the film ends up supporting, or whether it undercuts the terms of that debate altogether; and a closing section on what the film contributes to the philosophy of measurement that an argument alone could not — what it is like to be a data point, and what it takes for a person's own report of his suffering to be admitted as evidence. No bibliography and no citations, please; I want the analysis, not a reading list.
