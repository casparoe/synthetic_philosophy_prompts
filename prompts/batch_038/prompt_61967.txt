Working on an essay about precommitment — Ulysses contracts, constitutional entrenchment, commitment devices, all that — and I want to run it through Memento, which I increasingly think is the best film ever made about commitment devices and nobody ever says so.

The setup I want to test: Leonard's whole apparatus (the tattoos, the Polaroids with captions on the back, the motel routine, the notes to self) is a stack of precommitments authored by a self who won't be around to honor or reconsider them. But the standard philosophical picture assumes the bound agent can still recognize the constraint as his own and is merely too tempted or too weak to release it. Ulysses hears the sirens and begs to be untied — he knows exactly why he's tied up, and has pre-instructed the crew to ignore him and bind him tighter. A constitution binds a legislature that can read the text, argue about it, and in principle amend it. Leonard gets neither. He can't recognize the commitment as his, can't tell whether the conditions that justified it still hold, and has no realistic route to revoking it.

So call it the recognition condition: an earlier self's commitment carries authority over a later self only if that later self could in principle see the commitment as answering to reasons it is still in a position to assess. Two things I want from you. First — is that condition real, or a philosopher's tic? Does a Ulysses contract that fails it stop being precommitment and become something closer to manipulating a stranger who happens to share your body? There's an obvious counter: the entire point of a mast is that you don't get to deliberate at the crucial moment, so maybe non-recognition is a feature and Leonard just dramatizes what that feature costs. Second, the ending makes it worse, because Leonard knowingly authors a commitment whose content he has good reason to think is false and rigs the record so his future self can't undo it — and he does this while misremembering why he made earlier commitments at all, which is the Sammy Jankis material. What does self-deception-as-precommitment do to the account? Does it break the analogy with entrenchment, or is it what entrenchment looks like from the inside?

Then pull it outward to the constitutional case: the people actually bound by a text mostly can't recognize it as expressing anything they'd endorse — the dead-hand problem — and amendment is the institutional version of untying yourself. Where does Memento give that debate something it doesn't already have, and where does the comparison collapse because Leonard is one body and a polity is millions of disagreeing agents? Sammy's story also seems worth its own paragraph: it reads as a precommitment that failed for purely informational reasons, and its murky how much of it Leonard invented, which might matter for whether a commitment can be authored by a self that is lying about why it made it.

No citations or reading list, please, and don't quote the film — paraphrase. What I actually want is an argument about whether the recognition condition earns its keep, plus an honest account of where Memento resists the analogy instead of just illustrating it.
