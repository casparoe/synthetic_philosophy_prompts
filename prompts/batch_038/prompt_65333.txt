You are advising me in the capacity of a moral philosopher who also knows comparative neuroanatomy. Think carefully before you draft; if it helps, spend three or four lines first identifying what is actually at stake conceptually in the proposal, and only then produce the document.

I sit on the animal welfare panel of a national research funding body. We set the tiering that determines housing, handling, and endpoint requirements for funded work involving animals. The current draft of our revised charter sorts protected species by encephalization quotient — brain mass relative to the brain mass predicted for an animal of that body size — on the stated rationale that a more encephalized brain is likelier to support the kinds of experience that matter morally. Two members want it adopted as written because it is computable from published anatomical tables, and our remit requires any criterion to be 'objective and verifiable from published data.' Three members object. Their objections, in brief: birds pack neurons at far higher density than mammals, so volume and neuron number come apart sharply, with some parrots and corvids reaching primate-range forebrain neuron counts in brains the size of walnuts; elephants have brains several times the mass of ours yet land no higher than chimpanzees on EQ and well below several dolphin species, so the ratio and the absolute mass tell opposite stories; and the capacities the charter actually invokes — the affective dimension of pain, fear conditioning, self-monitoring — are mosaic across lineages rather than moving together on a single scale.

Draft the memo I will circulate ahead of the vote. It should take a clear position (adopt, reject, or adopt with named amendments), give reasons a proponent of the proxy could actually answer rather than strawman, and either propose one alternative criterion that survives our remit or argue explicitly that none is available. Take the strongest case for the proxy seriously: that a rough but administrable line may be better than an ideal one nobody can apply, and that a panel which refuses imperfect criteria may simply end up tiering by charisma.
