I sit on my university's Intellectual Property Advisory Panel, a five-member body that determines ownership of and responsibility for disputed research outputs under the institution's IP policy. A case reaches us on Thursday, and I should like to circulate a draft opinion to the other members beforehand. I would be grateful if you would write it.

The facts, as I have assembled them from the disclosure file. A postdoctoral fellow in computational linguistics spent fourteen months fine-tuning a mid-sized language model on a digitized corpus of nineteenth-century periodical literature that the university licenses from a commercial vendor. The license permits use for non-commercial research and prohibits "the creation of derivative works that embody or reproduce a substantial portion of the licensed corpus." The model is now the principal asset of a company the fellow has incorporated with two outside investors, and the vendor, having learned of this, asserts that the trained weights are such a derivative work. It demands either withdrawal of the model from commercial use or a perpetual royalty-free license to the company. The vendor's technical consultant reports that he elicited some forty passages of twenty words or more from the model that match the corpus nearly verbatim, several of them containing distinctive typographical artifacts of the vendor's own scanning and normalization work; the corpus runs to several million words. Our general counsel advises that the contractual language is genuinely ambiguous, that the question is unsettled here, and that the panel's view of the merits will largely determine whether the university resists the claim, concedes it, or seeks terms of settlement.

A second question has been bundled with the first, and I think the panel will talk past itself unless they are separated. Our policy vests ownership in the university where an output was created with "substantial use of university resources." The corpus was acquired by the library; the compute was partly the department's cluster and partly hardware the fellow purchased herself; much of the work was done outside contracted hours. The technology transfer office wishes to claim the model on that footing. The fellow replies that a licensed corpus is no more a resource consumed in the making of the model than a library book is a resource consumed in the making of a monograph, and that reading a book does not make its owner a part-author of what one learns from it.

Please draft a memo of roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words recommending a decision. It should: state plainly what the panel is actually being asked to settle, distinguishing the vendor's question from the transfer office's question; say what turns on the claim that a trained model embodies or contains the text it was trained on, and offer the panel a way of adjudicating that claim that does not simply inherit folk pictures of how such systems work; address the analogy to a human researcher who reads the corpus and writes a competing book, identifying where it favors the fellow and precisely where it stops serving; take a position on whether the forty verbatim fragments matter, and why, given that they are individually trivial and collectively negligible in proportion; propose a rule the panel could apply to the next several cases of this kind rather than to this one alone; and give the vendor's strongest consideration its full weight before explaining whether it carries the day. Close with the recommendation and any conditions attaching to it.

Do not supply case citations, doctrine, or a bibliography; counsel handles the authorities, and what the panel needs from me is the reasoning.
