I lead a small team that maintains an internal LLM-based code review bot. A chunk of my job is measuring whether the bot is actually any good, and I've become convinced our measurement is broken in a way I can't quite articulate.

Here is the setup in detail. We stand up a second model as a judge that scores the bot's review comments. To convince ourselves the judge was trustworthy, we ran it over a few hundred pull requests where we "already knew" the bot's verdict was correct — except that in practice "already knew" usually meant the judge's score matched what the bot had said, or a teammate skimmed the diff and agreed. Our headline number is an agreement rate between the bot and the judge. The judge itself was prompted with examples drawn from cases the bot handled well. So the gold standard is mostly either the thing under test or something nearly as cheap and unverified as the thing under test.

A colleague summarized this by saying our validation is circular: we used the instrument to validate the instrument, so the study carries no information. Separately, I've been reading around a worry in epistemology that seems related but not obviously the same — that you cannot establish a source is reliable by relying on that very source, because then knowledge of its reliability comes too cheaply, since the check would have come out fine no matter how bad the source was.

I would like a careful comparison of those two diagnoses, treated as two distinct ideas rather than one problem wearing two hats. Specifically:

1. What is each complaint actually asserting goes wrong? For the measurement case I take it to be something about evidential independence or identifiability: the data simply cannot discriminate a good instrument from a bad one. For the epistemological case I genuinely cannot tell what the target is — whether the inference is invalid, whether it is valid but fails to transmit warrant, whether it yields knowledge that is somehow defective, or whether it yields perfectly good knowledge and the real complaint is an objection to a theory of knowledge rather than to the practice. Please lay out which of these the worry amounts to, and explain why the difference matters for someone in my position.

2. Do the two diagnoses actually coincide on my setup? I want cases where they come apart in each direction: ways our harness could be defective that the epistemological worry does not capture at all, and ways a reliability belief could be objectionably cheap even when the measurement methodology is technically clean. My hunch is that the human spot-checks bear on the second kind of case but not the first, and I would like to know whether that hunch survives scrutiny.

3. What follows for repair? My instinct is that I can buy my way out of the measurement problem — pay an engineer to label two hundred diffs carefully, add static-analysis signals as a second source, measure against downstream outcomes like whether the flagged code later produced a bug — and that enough independent signals eventually make the whole enterprise non-circular. Does the epistemological worry license the same move, or does it imply that pooling many fallible checkers, none of which I have independent grounds for trusting, still leaves me with something cheap? If the two cases get different answers here, explain what makes them come apart, because from where I sit they look structurally identical.

Please do not send me a reading list or citations; I have no way to chase them and they would just be noise. I want the reasoning developed in your own terms. You may name a position or a school of thought where that is the most efficient way to locate it, but the argument has to stand on its own without my looking anything up.

I would also ask that you be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely disputed among people who work on the topic. If the answer to one of my three questions depends on taking a side in a live debate, tell me which side you are assuming and sketch what the opposing side would say instead. I would much rather be told that something is contested than be handed a confident answer that turns out to be one faction's view.
