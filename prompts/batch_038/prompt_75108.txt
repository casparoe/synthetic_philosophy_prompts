I chair the membership committee of the Riverbend Community Land Trust, a resident-governed limited-equity cooperative of forty-four homes on the edge of a mid-sized city. I'm a retired school principal, not an economist, and I've taken this job for the last four years mostly because nobody else wanted the paperwork. Under our occupancy rules, every household admitted from the waiting list must complete four hundred hours of documented "sweat equity" before it can sign its occupancy agreement and collect keys: framing and drywall on its own unit and on neighbours' units, landscaping, snow clearance, hauling, and sitting on the maintenance or finance committee. The hours must be spread over at least fourteen months, they expire if unused, and they can be neither transferred nor bought out. When construction runs ahead of the hour ledger — which happens most summers — the site coordinator assigns make-work to keep households accruing: re-sanding fence panels already sanded, repainting the tool shed, sorting fasteners by size. The full board of eleven votes on the rule in three weeks, and two proposals are before it.

The first comes from Marcus, who has been a member for twenty-six years and helped pour the original foundations. He says the resale cap makes our homes the best bargain in the county, so people apply for the bargain rather than for the community, and the four hundred hours are the only thing standing between us and a membership of quiet free-riders: an applicant can charm an interview panel, he says, but nobody can talk their way through four hundred hours of July roofing. He is unembarrassed by the make-work and says it is precisely the point — what matters is not what gets built but that the household pays. The second proposal comes from Dana, who joined nine years ago. She argues the requirement measures available time and physical health rather than commitment, and so screens out exactly the households we say we want: single parents, night-shift nurses, applicants with disabilities, applicants in their seventies. She notes that our waiting list has fallen from ninety-six households to thirty-four in five years, and that the last two admission cycles drew nine and seven applicants respectively for six homes. Worse, she says, the signal has already decayed from the inside: one household in the last cycle logged ninety hours of "site cleanup" that three neighbours report was performed by the applicant's brother-in-law's landscaping crew, and two of the three households with the highest logged hours in the past five years have attended no monthly meeting since moving in — one of them is now subletting without permission. Dana proposes replacing the four hundred hours with sixty hours plus a three-thousand-dollar payment into the maintenance reserve, waivable on documented hardship. One board member who happens to be a fair-housing attorney has flagged the disparate-impact question, but I would rather the memo engage that concern on its merits as one consideration among several than treat it as a compliance issue.

My daughter is in an evolutionary psychology program and sent me some reading after I described this fight to her, and it introduced me to costly signalling and the handicap principle: the idea that a trait can be credible precisely because it is burdensome to carry — the peacock's tail is the standard illustration — and, critically, that the burden has to fall harder on the low-quality signaller than on the high-quality one for the signal to remain honest. Please draft the written recommendation I will circulate to the board before the vote. It should endorse one of the two proposals or a third option of your own design, and give reasons the losing side could actually answer rather than a summary of both. It should say what would count as evidence that an hours requirement is doing signal work as opposed to merely filtering for free time, and it should address head-on whether our four hundred hours satisfy the condition about who bears the cost. Treat seriously the possibility that the hours do something other than screen — that an initiation this demanding may generate the commitment it claims only to detect, which would make Marcus's rule worth keeping even if it screens badly, and which would be something the handicap model as such cannot explain. Say what to make of the make-work, and whether Dana's cash payment sharpens or destroys the logic of the requirement it replaces. Lead with your bottom line — what the board should vote, in the first sentence — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Aim for roughly nine hundred words, and close with a short numbered list of the criteria the committee should apply whenever it revisits membership requirements in future.
