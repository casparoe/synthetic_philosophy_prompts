so I've been asked to write down, in one place, what I actually believe about when consent counts as valid, for a workshop where the other people present are going to be paid to take it apart. I'd rather find the cracks now then find them in the room. Here's my package, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

1. Consent has three ingredients and I think you need all of them: the person has to be adequately informed, they have to be free of coercion, and they have to be competent. I don't think any one of these can do the work of the others — information without freedom isn't consent, freedom without information isn't either.

2. Lies only void consent when they're about something material. And what's material isn't read off some objective list of Important Facts — it's indexed to what the person consenting actually cares about. Two people can be told the same lie and only one of them has had their consent voided, because only one of them would have gone along otherwise.

3. That said, I still want a line between a lie about what the act *is* and a lie that just makes the act look more appealing. The doctor who tells a patient that an intimate examination is clinically necessary when it's actually for his own gratification — there was no consent there at all. The man who implies he's single and independently wealthy in order to get a date, when he's neither — her consent stands, even though she would tell you afterwards that his money and availability were exactly what she cared about most.

4. Threats void consent; offers made into an already-bad situation don't, because the offerer didn't create the badness. So the boater who says "ten thousand dollars or I keep going" gets valid consent from the drowning swimmer, while the person who says "pay me or these photos go to your family" gets nothing. My gloss on the difference is that coercion makes you worse off than you'd otherwise be, and a hard bargain doesn't.

5. But I also think those hard-bargain cases are wrongfully obtained. Taking advantage of someone's desperation is a wrong even when it produces valid consent, and I'd want remedies for it — rescission, price caps, whatever.

6. Voluntariness comes in degrees. I'm pretty sure there's no bright line between the person who decides under mild social pressure and the person who decides with a gun in their face.

7. Validity doesn't. There's no such thing as 70% valid consent, and I don't want a sliding scale in law or policy. Either it was voluntary enough or it wasn't.

8. Competence is task-specific and time-specific. The same person can be competent to agree to a filling and not competent to sell their house, and can be competent in March and not in November.

9. I'm strongly in favour of advance directives binding later on. If someone decides, lucidly, that they don't want to be fed artificially, that decision should govern even once they can no longer make decisions of that kind — including the case where the later person is demented but contentedly accepts food when it's offered and would, if you asked them, say yes please.

10. And also: a refusal expressed right now always trumps an authorization given earlier. Nobody should be pinned down by a past version of themselves against their present will.

11. I'm an anti-paternalist — consenting adults can agree to things that are simply bad for them, and "you'll regret it" is never a reason to void. The one exception I allow is consents that would destroy the person's capacity to consent in the future. People tell me that's paternalism wearing a disguise. I half-suspect 6 and 7 don't quite fit together either, though I couldn't say why.

Can you audit this for me? I want to know which of these genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do, and I want them ranked by how bad the conflict is rather than listed as a flat inventory of worries. For each real conflict, tell me the cheapest way to repair it and what I lose by making that repair. Then tell me which single change would fix the most, and — separately — whether any item on this list is doing covert work for another one, i.e. looks like an independent commitment but is actually being smuggled in by something else I said.
