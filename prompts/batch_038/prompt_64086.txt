I sit on my faculty's research conduct panel. The matter before us next Tuesday concerns a paper published two years ago in a reputable journal whose central argument — a compact one, to the effect that a familiar form of explanation covertly presupposes what it was supposed to explain — had been sketched aloud by a postdoctoral researcher at an informal reading group some three years earlier. The paper reproduces no text from anywhere, because there was no text: the session was not recorded, the argument was never written up, and the only evidence is the recollection of the seven or eight people present. The author maintains that he arrived at the idea independently, and since the argument is short I find convergence quite plausible; but he was in that room. The published version adds a premise and draws a somewhat stronger conclusion than anything the complainant is said to have offered, so far as the witnesses agree. Our regulations define misconduct as the use of another person's work without acknowledgement and leave the word work undefined, so the panel must decide whether an idea delivered orally and never fixed in any medium is work at all.

I argued about this for most of last night with a friend who holds that the complaint only makes sense if one already thinks of ideas as property, and that in our discipline the currency is arguments rather than expressions, so nothing rivalrous was taken. I am not persuaded, but neither am I confident in the opposite view.

Please draft the written opinion I shall circulate to the panel before we meet: a clear recommendation on whether to find misconduct, on what remedy if any, and on how the panel should construe its own regulations in future cases. Give the reasoning each party could actually answer, engage the strongest case on whichever side you reject, and state plainly whether the wrong alleged, if it is a wrong at all, is a violation of a property right or something of a different kind entirely. Lead with your bottom line, then set everything out in continuous prose — no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables, just as one would write a letter — and use British English throughout.
