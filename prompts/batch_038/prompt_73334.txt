I am a third-year doctoral student in social history, and this academic year I sit on my county's Heritage Designation Panel as its early-career specialist member. The Panel meets on 14 October to decide the application from the town of Alderwick to enter the Alderwick Lantern Procession on the Living Traditions Register, which carries designation, a brass plaque on the market cross, and an annual maintenance grant of £4,000. The application asserts continuous observance since 1487. My own archival work — as yet unpublished and forming two chapters of my dissertation — establishes the following. There are scattered documentary references to a lantern procession between 1534 and 1841, after which it disappears from the record entirely. It was reconstituted in 1926 by the Alderwick Antiquarian Society, whose secretary, a solicitor, composed the Lantern Song that is now sung at the close of the procession in 1927, and designed the present costume of hooded bearers with blue sashes from a single line in a 1662 probate inventory listing a horn lantern and one blue cloth coat. Local press coverage of the 1926 revival explicitly promoted it as restoring a link with the town's Saxon forebears, a claim with no basis in anything I have found; and in the mid-1930s the procession was used for two consecutive years by a local branch of a far-right association, whose photographs from that period are the ones most often reproduced in the town's present promotional material. Since 1974 the procession has run every year without a break, is organised by two primary schools and a brass band, draws roughly 1,800 participants, and now carries the town's football club nickname, a brewery's branding, and a unit in the local secondary curriculum. Schedule 2 of the scheme requires a practice transmitted within a community without substantial interruption for not less than seventy-five years, whose present form is recognisably continuous with its earlier form; the 1926 revival clears the arithmetic, and the continuity clause is where the argument will be, though the clause was in fact drafted in 2009 to keep out commercial re-enactment ventures rather than to handle revivals. The Panel is split: two members would refuse on the ground that designation is a public assertion about the past and the Panel would be certifying a fabrication with a politically tainted revival history, while three would designate on the ground that the practice is alive, widely owned, and that every tradition on the Register was manufactured somewhere. I hold the casting vote and want to circulate a short note before the meeting.

Draft that note for me. It must recommend one course — designate, refuse, or designate subject to conditions — and any conditions must be things the Panel can actually minute and enforce: for instance the wording of the Register entry and the plaque citation, whether the 1487 claim is corrected on the face of the Register, whether the 1930s photographs are addressed at all, what the grant may be spent on, and whether the Panel publishes my archival finding, refers it elsewhere, or keeps quiet about it. Engage the authenticity objection at its strongest, which is not aesthetic but institutional: the Register is a public record and designation is a speech act, so tell me what exactly the Panel would be saying if it designates. Do not simply recite that traditions are invented or that communities are imagined; I need those ideas doing discriminating work here, for example by separating a revival that claims an antiquity it does not have from a new practice that makes no such claim, and by telling me whether the arguments that make national identity a constructed thing cut for or against designating a practice at town scale, where nearly everyone who counts as inside it can be named and counted. Take a position on the evidence problem: my findings are dissertation material that will not be publicly available until after my viva, the Panel is not the natural venue in which to break them, and two of the organisers have given forty years to the procession. Finally, state the precedent, since eleven other entries sit on the Register and I know of at least two that are revivals of the same shape, one of them designated in 2019 without anyone asking these questions.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with nothing before or after — no salutation, no framing paragraph, no sign-off, no bibliography. Item 1 states the recommendation together with any conditions. Items 2, 3, and 4 give reasons of a kind a colleague who disagrees could answer rather than dismiss. Item 5 states the strongest consideration against your recommendation and what it costs the Panel to override it. Name the scholarly literature only where a term is actually earning its place.
