Experimental Bell tests are designed, executed, and reported around a standard apparatus of named loopholes, and I want to understand what that apparatus takes for granted. The practice runs roughly as follows. A test of a Bell inequality is treated as licensing its conclusion — that the observed correlations cannot be produced by any theory satisfying a certain package of conditions — only if a short, enumerable list of auxiliary assumptions holds. Two of those assumptions have been handled as engineering targets. The first is that no signal travelling at or below light speed could pass between the two measurement stations within a trial; closing it required space-like separation between setting choice and distant outcome, which in turn required long-distance entanglement distribution and random setting generation fast enough to occur after the particles were in flight. The second is that the detected pairs form a fair sample of the pairs emitted; closing it required detector efficiencies above a threshold that took decades to reach. When both were finally satisfied in a single experiment, the result was received as settling something that earlier, individually loophole-ridden experiments had not, and the whole enterprise was retrospectively organized around that achievement.

The third assumption is handled in an entirely different register. The measurement settings at each station must be statistically independent of whatever variables determine the outcomes. This one is not treated as closable by better apparatus. The response has been to relocate it: random number generators driven by physical events with as small a causal past as possible — light from distant stars, and later from quasars — so that any hypothetical common cause linking the settings to the particles' hidden variables would have to lie in the overlap of their past light cones, deep in cosmic history. The achievement is then quantified in exactly those terms: how far back a coordination would have to reach, how much of the universe's history it would have to have been arranged in advance. Yet the discussion sections of such experiments typically concede that this cannot be pushed all the way, since a sufficiently determined common cause could be installed in the initial conditions themselves, and then proceed on the footing that the residual possibility is negligible.

There is also a downstream technology resting on the same correlations. Device-independent quantum cryptography and randomness certification derive their security from the violation of a Bell inequality rather than from a trusted model of the apparatus, and their security proofs assume independence between the local settings and whatever information an adversary holds — but here the assumption is a bounded quantity, so that a limited amount of dependence still leaves a usable amount of certified randomness.

I would like an account of what this whole pattern of practice presupposes, philosophically, rather than a summary of the physics.

1. Reconstruct the theory of experimental confirmation implicit in the loophole framework. What picture of the relationship between a hypothesis and its auxiliary assumptions makes it sensible to treat a Bell test as resting on a short, nameable list of separately relaxable conditions, each of which can be satisfied by a distinct technical achievement? Does the framework presuppose that the alternatives to quantum theory are exhaustively partitioned by that list, and if so, what would license that claim?

2. Explain the distinction the practice draws between an assumption that can be closed and one that can only be pushed back. Is this a distinction about engineering difficulty, about probability, about the modal structure of the assumption, or about something normative — a judgment about which assumptions an inquirer is entitled to hold fixed? State explicitly which of these the practice is committed to and which it merely happens to fit.

3. The freedom-of-choice assumption is quantified in spacetime terms rather than probabilistic ones: experimenters report how far into the past a common cause would have to be placed. What conception of evidential support does that mode of quantification presuppose? Is it coherent to say a hypothesis has been partially undermined by making its required explanation more remote, when its probability is not thereby altered on any standard account? If it is not coherent, what is the practice actually doing when it reports those numbers?

4. Diagnose the apparent inconsistency that puzzles me most. Two loopholes were treated as defects that, until remedied, blocked the inference entirely; the third is universally unremediated and yet is treated as licensing the inference now. If the unremediable assumption were false, it would undercut the conclusion just as thoroughly as either of the others. Does the practice therefore hold two incompatible standards for what counts as a satisfied assumption, or is there a coherent methodology — a default in favour of assumptions that make inquiry possible, say, or an asymmetry between what must be shown and what may be presumed — that reconciles them? Reconstruct that methodology as precisely as you can and say whether it is defensible on its own terms.

5. The cryptographic use treats setting independence as a continuous resource that can be partially spent, while the foundational use treats it as a condition the experiment either meets or fails to meet. Is the same assumption being deployed under two different logical types here? What would have to be true of independence for both uses to be legitimate, and does the tension between them reveal something about which of the two is the more fundamental commitment?

6. Identify which rival positions the practice quietly rules out by its manner of proceeding — superdeterminism, retrocausal accounts, and any view on which the settings and the hidden variables share a common origin — and say whether the practice rules them out as false, as untestable, as ad hoc, or simply as unworthy of experimental attention. Those are very different verdicts. Which one does the actual behaviour of the field amount to, and what does that behaviour presuppose about the standards a hypothesis must meet to be taken seriously?

7. Finally, suppose the community decided to treat setting independence the way it treats locality: as a substantive, contestable claim that the experiment is designed to probe rather than a background presumption. What would have to change in experimental design, in reporting conventions, and in the concept of a loophole itself? If nothing could change, what does that show about the status of the assumption within the enterprise?

Throughout, please distinguish sharply between what the practice strictly presupposes — what it could not proceed without — and what it is merely congenial to, and flag the places where its content is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible accounts of evidence or of explanation would equally license exactly what experimentalists do.
