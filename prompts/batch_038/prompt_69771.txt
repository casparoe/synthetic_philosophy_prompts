I am a retired soil chemist — thirty-eight years studying nitrogen cycling in cropped soils at a state experiment station — and since retiring I have enrolled in a continuing-education seminar in the philosophy of science at our local university's lifelong-learning institute, which meets on Tuesday evenings about forty minutes from where I live. I confess I came to philosophy late and still find the vocabulary slippery: I have picked up terms like auxiliary hypothesis and, I believe, a protective belt, but I could not say with confidence what work they do when an actual case arrives. Last week our assigned reading was Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population, and two members of the seminar — Miriam, who teaches secondary-school economics, and Geoffrey, a retired newspaper editor with a strong taste for economic history — had a sustained argument that the rest of us could not settle. I took notes because I wanted to think it over afterwards, and the exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Miriam opened that Malthus is simply refuted. His claim was that population, unchecked, multiplies geometrically while the food supply grows only arithmetically, so wages and living standards must be driven back to subsistence; instead the world's population has risen roughly eightfold since 1800 and average real incomes by a much larger multiple. A prediction of that clarity, contradicted by two centuries of data, is false, and the only remaining question is how interesting a false prediction is.

(2) Geoffrey replied that this mistakes a failed prediction for a falsified theory. What Malthus described was a mechanism — population pressing on the food supply, held down by mortality and by the reluctance to marry without means — and that mechanism worked correctly until its conditions were dismantled from outside: industrially fixed nitrogen, mechanization, new crop varieties, the global grain trade, and above all a fall in fertility as incomes rose, which is the reverse of the relationship he assumed. The honest description, he said, is that the domain of applicability ended rather than that the theory was shown wrong, and he offered the analogy of calling Newtonian mechanics refuted by the precession of Mercury's orbit.

(3) Miriam said that is precisely the rescue she was worried about. On Geoffrey's account no outcome could ever have counted against Malthus: had incomes stayed flat he would have been vindicated, and since they rose he is merely "right about the regime that no longer obtains." She added that the escape mechanism is worse than an unanticipated condition, because Malthus did count moral restraint among the checks on population but insisted it would rarely bind the labouring poor — so what actually happened was not that the world supplied new circumstances around his theory but that it supplied a check he had written off as practically inert. If you may swap the mechanism and keep the name, she said, the sentence "Malthus was right" stops being a claim about the world.

(4) Geoffrey answered that he had in mind a narrower and defensible claim: for the whole of recorded history up to roughly 1800, population grew while output per head stayed flat, which is what Malthus predicted and what nobody before him had explained, so the departure is a two-century exception to a ten-millennium regularity. Being right about the default state of the world is not nothing, he said, and science has a recognized category for the situation — Wegener's continental drift, dismissed for want of a mechanism and vindicated later; Mendel ignored for decades and then recovered. A theory can be true of the world's baseline regime even while some historical contingency suspends it.

(5) Miriam's last move, which Geoffrey did not answer before we adjourned, was that he had retreated to a claim assembled after the fact. The theory as published predicted subsistence-level wages indefinitely; what Geoffrey defends is that same theory plus a proviso — unless something large changes — where the list of qualifying somethings is drawn up only once we know what happened and can always be extended by one more item, be it fossil fuels, nitrogen, trade, contraception, or next time perhaps fusion or ore brought back from asteroids. The reductio of his position, she said, is that it would equally rehabilitate the resource-exhaustion forecasts of the 1970s and every dated peak-oil projection, all of which their authors could defend as sound about the underlying geology and merely premature about the calendar.

The seminar leader has asked us to return next week with a verdict, and I should like yours first. Please write as a philosopher of science who also has a working command of economic and demographic history, and think the exchange through carefully before drafting — in particular, establish for yourself at each point which of the two is making a substantive claim and which is only relabeling, and whether the "right but early" formulation retains any content that survives Miriam's fifth move. Do not split the difference out of politeness and do not hedge; where one of them is simply wrong about how scientific theories are tested, say so.

Format: give me exactly five numbered items, one for each of the numbered moves above, and nothing else — no introduction, no summary, no closing paragraph. Each item should be one or two sentences and must state (a) a grade for that move on a five-point scale running from flatly wrong, through weak, even, and defensible, to entirely correct, and (b) the specific reason for that grade. If a move turns on something neither Miriam nor Geoffrey mentioned — a distinction they both skipped, or a feature of Malthus's argument or of his successive revisions that bears on who is right — put that observation inside the same item rather than in a separate remark.
