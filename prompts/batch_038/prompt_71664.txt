Three of my novels are built on real lives, and on the strength of them the obituaries desk of a metropolitan daily has asked me to run a Saturday-morning session for its staff and freelance writers — twelve of them, plus two copy editors, in a conference room with a whiteboard and eighty minutes. They are accomplished prose professionals with no classics behind them. I have not assigned anything beforehand and will not.

The single idea I want them to leave holding is this: that a narrated life does moral work on a reader not by exemplifying but by comparing — that Plutarch's paired Lives and the saint's life are two different instruments making two different demands on the reader, and that the modern obituary sits unhappily between them. I intend to reach it by way of the preface to the Life of Alexander, in which Plutarch explains that he is writing lives rather than histories and that a slight thing — a remark, a joke — will often disclose a man's character better than a battle in which thousands die; and by way of the contrast with hagiography, where the suppression of flaw is not a lapse in honesty but a deliberate design, the saint being offered as a type to be imitated rather than a character to be adjudicated.

Plan the session for me. Do not deliver it.

The dependency structure: what must be in place before what, and what breaks if I invert the order. My instinct is to begin with the anecdote and work outward to the pairing, but I suspect this hands them the instrument before they know what it is for.

The carrier: I had settled on the Nicias and Crassus pair — an Athenian undone by hesitation at Syracuse, a Roman undone by appetite in Parthia. Tell me whether that is the right horse for eighty minutes or whether another pair would serve better, and what the chosen case must have, and must conspicuously lack, in order to do its work.

The misconceptions: they arrive with the desk's standing rule that an obituary is not a eulogy, and they are proud of the divorce and the lawsuit they left in, so they will assume that admitting the flaw is the whole of the moral business and that Plutarch is merely their own house style in a toga. I also expect that they will take the telling detail for colour, a garnish on the résumé; that they will read a comparison as a ranking, the point of a pair being to establish which man was better; and that they will hear hagiography as simple bad reporting, leaving them already on the right side of the distinction I am trying to draw. Tell me which of these to head off, at what point in the sequence each must be met, and whether there is a likelier one I have not thought of.

The check: something for the last ten minutes that would show me the idea has landed rather than merely charmed them, and that they could attempt without having read a line of Plutarch.

The omissions: what I should decline to touch at this level — I assume the dispute over whether the Lives are history or literature, the ethics of the Moralia, and the English reception through North's translation — and what I ought to say about why I am declining.

Please do not send me a bibliography or a list of things to read.
