Every spring my department awards two twelve-week research grants — teaching release, with a 7,000-word paper due at the end, judged on whether the question is both open and finishable in the time. I intend to apply with an idea I have not yet managed to state cleanly, and I would like you to write the proposal for it.

The idea, such as it is. Newcomb-type problems and the Death in Damascus case are usually read as two exhibits in a single trial: does a rational agent choose by causal expected utility or by evidential expected utility? I suspect that framing hides a prior question neither side has squarely asked, namely whether an agent can change how predictable she is, and at what price. In Newcomb-type settings predictability is rewarded — the agent who could credibly have become a one-boxer is the one who wins. In Damascus predictability is fatal. Yet the standard theories evaluate acts given the agent's type; they seem to lack a natural way of evaluating the purchase of a type.

The second half of the idea is that infallible prediction is not one thing. Reliability could rest on at least four distinguishable mechanisms: a deterministic world whose prior state the predictor knows; a predictor that tracks dispositions and character rather than particular acts; a prediction that is self-fulfilling, in that the announcement itself is what brings about the predicted act — arguably what happens in Damascus, since the traveler flees because Death spoke; and a backtracking case in which the prediction was already made true by facts about the past. Whether unpredictability is available for purchase at all, and whether buying it changes anything, appears to vary across the four. My conjecture, which the paper would test rather than assume: part of the apparent standoff between causal and evidential decision theory here is an artifact of leaving the mechanism unspecified, and once it is specified either the two theories agree — so these cases stop being counterexamples to either — or they disagree in a way that shows one of them cannot represent the value of being hard to predict even in the very situations where being hard to predict is the only thing that would save the agent.

Please write the proposal so that it contains: a precise statement of the question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer rather than inviting a survey; why the standard positions leave that question open, described as positions and arguments rather than as sources, since I will insert my own references; the method, laid out week by week across the twelve, saying explicitly whether the work is formal (an expanded act space, a decision matrix for each mechanism) or discursive, and why that rather than the other; why this route instead of the obvious alternatives of proposing yet another decision theory or trying to settle the causal-versus-evidential dispute head-on; what a successful result looks like, described concretely enough that a referee could check for it; what would count as failure, including the live possibility that the value of unpredictability is fully capturable by ordinary causal expected utility once the act space is widened, in which case there is no result at all; and a realistic scope, naming what gets cut if the project runs long.

No bibliography, and no invented references. Finally, step outside the proposal and give me your own assessment of its weak points: which of the four mechanisms, if it collapses into one of the others or cannot be specified without already presupposing one of the two decision theories, would take the project down with it; what argument would convince you the question is not genuinely open after all; and where you think my reasoning is most likely to fail. Say plainly what would change your mind about whether this is worth twelve weeks.
