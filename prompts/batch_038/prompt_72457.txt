I'm the student rep on my university's Information Governance Committee — PhD student, non-voting, but I've been asked to write up a recommendation before Thursday's vote and I know it'll basically set the terms of the debate. Here's the situation.

The administration wants to sign a three-year contract with a vendor for a "student success platform." The detail that matters: the vendor collects nothing new. Its a join. Badge swipes at the gym and the library, wifi access-point associations, LMS login timestamps and assignment submissions, dining-hall transactions, bursar holds, and the counseling center's appointment metadata (dates and durations only, never content). What comes out is a nightly risk score per student plus a dashboard for advisors. The provost has said at least four times, in slightly different words each time, that this isn't surveillance because "no new data is being collected — the university already lawfully has all of it."

Students can opt out. But the opt-out flag lives in the student record and renders on the advisor dashboard as "declined monitoring," and anyone who opts out is ineligible for the new retention scholarship, because that scholarship is administered through the platform. Two advisors have told me seperately, off the record, that they check in more on the students whose flag is set.

Two things made this go live. Last term the system flagged a student as being in acute crisis off her card-swipe pattern and a counselor contacted her family; she'd been working overnight shifts at a hospital. She's told me she now avoids the library after 9pm and has stopped going to the gym altogether. And the student paper ran an op-ed calling the platform "a social credit system with a wellness brand," which half the committee wants treated as a serious warning and half wants dismissed as hysteria. Meanwhile our exchange partner in Germany refuses to feed us equivalent data and instead runs what they describe as a purpose-bound model: every request for a student's records has to be tied to a written purpose the student has countersigned, the purpose expires at the end of term, and the student can see the entire request log. One member keeps saying that just proves the Germans are pathologically anti-data; another keeps invoking the German constitutional court's 1983 census ruling on informational self-determination as though it settles the question.

Can you draft the memo I'd circulate? I need a bottom line a committee can actually vote on — sign as proposed, sign with conditions you name explicitly, adopt the German-style purpose-bound model, or reject — and reasons the provost's office could respond to rather than privacy slogans. Two things I especially want handled honestly. First, the strongest case *for* the platform: the retention numbers are real, the students most likely to slip through are exactly the ones least likely to raise a hand, and "we already have this data" isn't a legally empty claim. I don't want that strawmanned, because the people making it aren't stupid. Second, the aggregation point — whether "nothing new is collected" actually neutralizes the objection, which is the part I can't think my way around, since intuitively the join is where the harm is but I can't say why that's not just a vibe.

Also tell me whether the social credit analogy earns its place in the memo or should be struck. My own instinct is that it overreaches and simultaneously undersells the thing, but I can't defend either half of that.
