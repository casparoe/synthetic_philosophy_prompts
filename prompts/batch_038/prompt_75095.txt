I'm building an interactive feature for the ideas desk of my magazine around a deceptively simple question: is the genome genuinely a code, a message, a set of instructions, or is that way of talking just a vivid metaphor that biologists find too convenient to give up? The philosophy of biology has produced a striking number of mutually incompatible answers, and I'd rather hand readers a diagnostic instrument than a glossary. What I want is something that sorts them by what they actually believe, not by what they call themselves.

Could you write me that instrument as a decision tree of about eight questions, running from commitments any well-read layperson can honestly assess to a named endpoint? A few constraints on how I'd like it built.

No node may ask for a label or a self-description. Don't ask whether the reader thinks DNA is a code, whether they are a realist about biological information, or which camp they belong to. Every question has to be a concrete scenario or a specific claim they can accept or reject: what would survive if a cell's cytoplasmic machinery were dismantled but its DNA left intact; whether a synthetic genome whose codons have been reassigned still carries the same instructions as its natural ancestor; whether a heritable epigenetic mark that alters a phenotype is information in the same sense as the base sequence that accompanies it; whether the egg contributes the same kind of causal contribution to the offspring as the sperm does; whether a perfect reader plus the sequence suffices to reconstruct the organism; whether information talk earns its keep once every causal interaction has been specified. I'd also like one question built on a case I keep coming back to in my reporting on genome synthesis: the same sequence sitting as text in a public database, versus sitting in a living cell — is the informational content identical in both places, present in only one, or duplicated?

Build the tree from the logical space first and the roster of positions second. State up front which axes you treat as independent — whether informational talk is literal or merely useful; whether any content is semantic in a sense tied to evolved function rather than to chemistry; whether information is a property of DNA specifically or of the developing system as a whole; whether Shannon-style measures capture what biologists mean by it; whether the concept does explanatory work that causal description cannot — then locate the positions at the combinations those axes permit. Tell me which combinations come out unoccupied, and diagnose each absence as incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored.

The leaves must include at minimum: a strong view on which the genome literally is a coded program whose semantics is fixed by the cellular machinery that reads it; a view on which genetic information is real but derivative, grounded in evolved function; a deflationary view that reduces it to measurable difference-making and treats the rest as metaphor; a purely quantitative view that takes the term to name a unit and nothing further; a view on which information belongs to the distributed developmental system with no privileged factor; and, if your space admits it, a position holding that the question is not well posed. Name each leaf by its content. Use the names of individual thinkers only where you are confident of the attribution, and give no citations, dates, or reading list.

Finally, flag every pair of leaves your questions fail to separate, and say what further question would separate them.

Deliver it as an ordered tree — question text, answer options, the branch each option leads to — followed by a short key listing the leaves, the path to each, and one sentence on the most awkward commitment each leaf carries. Assume a reader who is intelligent, has read popular science on genetics and gene editing, and has never taken a philosophy course.
