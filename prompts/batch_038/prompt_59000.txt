I'm 71 and six years retired from seismic hazard work — thirty-some years figuring out what shaking a structure has to be able to take, first for dams, later for plants. Since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy, mostly badly and mostly at night, and lately I've been reading the arguments about how much assurance you can get for a deployed learning system: the worst-case people versus the average-case people. Reading them back to back, I keep having the same irritated feeling I had in the eighties, when the deterministic-hazard crowd and the probabilistic crowd shouted past each other for a decade because nobody had ever written down what a hazard number was supposed to deliver. Each side was answering a different question and calling it the same question.

So before I read one more of these papers I want the spec. Write me the desiderata: the requirements an account of assurance for a learning system has to meet before I'd call it adequate, with the consideration behind each one, and a mark on each saying whether it's non-negotiable or just the taste of a particular camp.

Here's what I'd want you to work through, imported from my old life — tell me honestly which of these are real requirements and which are artifacts of a field where the adversary is a rock that doesn't care what you think about it:

- We never designed to one level. There was a shaking level you had to keep functioning through because it's expected in the life of the place, and a much rarer level where you were allowed to stop, provided you stopped into a safe configuration. Does an assurance account have to be two-level like that, and is "fails into a bounded state" a separate requirement from "holds up"? I think it is and I want to know if that's me.
- Beyond the design basis, the code doesn't ask you to survive. It asks you not to have a cliff — no mode where a modest overrun of your assumptions produces a wildly disproportionate consequence. Can you make "graceful degradation when your own assumptions turn out to be false" into a coherent requirement, or is it self-undermining, since if the assumptions fail the guarentee is void by construction? If that pair can't both be satisfied, say so plainly. That's the part I most want.
- Every return period I ever signed my name to assumed a stationary ensemble. The ground doesn't reorganize itself because you built on it. Here the thing being assured about is inside the environment, learns from it, and moves the distribution it's supposed to be measured against. Does an adequate account have to take a position on that feedback, or can it get away with treating the world as a fixed sampler?
- Averaging only means something if you get to keep playing and can absorb a loss. My field had failures that were not like that — one shot, no recovery, no second sample. Should "no averaging across a loss you can't come back from" be a hard requirement, even in cases where the expected value is perfectly well defined?
- The numbers had to be auditable by somebody who didn't like you and didn't work for you, and the interesting disasters happened when the number was defensible but the phenomenon was outside the class of phenomena the number had been derived from. So: does the account have to state its own provenance — what sort of situations its conclusions were built from — and does it have to survive checking by a hostile competent reader?

Then, briefly, run the usual contenders against the list: worst-case/adversarial robustness, statistical guarantees of the probably-approximately flavor, interpretability-based assurance, formal verification, and structured red-teaming. A few lines each is plenty, and a table is fine. I don't want a reading list or citations — I can't look anything up and I don't want to guess at page numbers, so keep it to reasoning I can check myself. And tell me which single requirement on your list knocks out the most candidates, because that's the one I'll go argue about at the cafe.
