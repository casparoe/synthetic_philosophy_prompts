A twelve-month postdoctoral fellowship at a center that pairs philosophy with security studies asks for a research proposal of roughly 1,500 words. I have the raw material but not the framing, and the framing is where the intellectual work here lives, so please treat what follows as material to be organized, narrowed, and sharpened rather than as prose to be polished.

My idea, stated badly on purpose so you can see where it needs help:

The rationality of nuclear deterrence is almost always analyzed as a problem about intentions. An agent forms an intention to retaliate; forming it is rational because it deters, yet executing it would be irrational or impermissible; the familiar moves are to render the threat conditional, to insist that forming an intention and acting on it must be evaluated separately, or to argue in the style of the toxin puzzle that the separation cannot be sustained in exactly the case where deterrence needs it. Brinkmanship is then modeled as a competition in demonstrated willingness to run risks, including Schelling's idea of a threat that deliberately leaves something to chance.

My suspicion is that all of this assumes something questionable about the object being threatened with, namely that a deterrent threat is the expressed intention of an agent who could still decline at the moment of decision. Actual command arrangements are built to defeat precisely that feature. Launch authority can be delegated in advance to officers who will have no one to consult; warning postures compress decision time to minutes; and one nuclear-armed state is widely reported to have fielded a retaliatory system designed to function even if its leadership has been destroyed. When execution is distributed across personnel, procedures, and machines arranged so that they cannot reconsider, the threat stops behaving like a promise and starts behaving like something else — closer to a trap one has installed and warned others about, or to a standing hazard under one's control but not under one's moment-to-moment discretion.

The question I want the proposal to be about: is that difference load-bearing, ontologically and morally? Two parts. First, does pre-delegation and automation change what kind of thing a deterrent threat is, such that the intention-centered paradoxes stop applying to it — or apply to it in a worse form, since the agent has now arranged for the objectionable act to occur without a further act of will? Second, if the threat comes to resemble an installed hazard rather than a promise, does it thereby become more credible but less attributable, and what follows for who may permissibly install it and for whether the standard verdicts (permissible because it works and has never been executed; impermissible because the intention behind it is corrupt) retain any grip?

Please produce the proposal with the following in it:

1. A statement of the question precise enough to admit of a determinate answer, and precise enough that a reader could say what would settle it. My formulation above is vague about what it means for something to "change kind"; propose a criterion, and tell me if you think the criterion should be a metaphysical one, a normative one, or a decision-theoretic one.

2. An account of why the existing positions leave the question open rather than answering it. Work through the conditional-intention reply, the separate-evaluation reply, the lesser-evil defense of deterrent policy, and the revisionist suggestion that the whole puzzle dissolves if we evaluate standing policies instead of intentions. For each, say whether it actually engages the distribution of execution authority or simply assumes a unitary decider and therefore begs the question.

3. The method, laid out across the twelve months and specific enough to be checkable. My instinct is to build a small taxonomy of threat-structures — promissory, delegated, procedural, automated — and run each of the standard paradoxes against each structure to see which survive, supported by two or three constructed cases. Tell me whether that is the right method or a better one, and say explicitly why the obvious alternatives would not do the work: a purely game-theoretic model, a historical study of command and control, and a straight applied-ethics essay about the permissibility of automation.

4. A description of what success looks like — a claim specific enough that a well-informed opponent would have to engage it rather than restate their position — and an explicit statement of what would count as failure. I want at least one failure mode described concretely rather than gestured at. For instance: the taxonomy may collapse, because every one of the four structures reduces from the adversary's point of view to a probability of retaliation, which is all the strategic analysis ever required, in which case the distinction is real but inert. If you think that collapse is likely, say so and explain why the project is worth funding anyway.

5. An honest assessment of scope. What would have to be cut to finish in a year, and whether the empirical side can be used responsibly by a philosopher without becoming amateur history.

Two constraints on the writing. Do not relitigate the general permissibility of nuclear deterrence; assume that debate sits where everyone agrees it sits and go at the narrower question. And do not include a bibliography, a reference list, or a literature review organized by citation — I will supply citations myself. Naming positions, arguments, and thinkers where it aids clarity is welcome.

Write in American English. At the end, add a short paragraph stating how confident you are in the framing claims the proposal depends on — that the question is genuinely open, that the promissory-versus-trap contrast is not already standard in this literature under another name, and that the project is tractable in twelve months — and single out the one point you are least sure of, explaining what would change your mind about it.
