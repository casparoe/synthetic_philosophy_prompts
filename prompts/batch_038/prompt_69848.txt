I am drafting the opening chapter of a general-audience book on holding people responsible, and the analogy I use to explain hypocrisy is borrowed from litigation. A plaintiff may be entirely correct that the defendant breached a duty and still be turned away for want of standing: the case is not lost on the merits, it is never heard. I tell readers that hypocritical blame fails in the same way — the content of the complaint may be sound, but the complainer lacks the authority to press it, so the charge is thrown out before it is reached rather than answered. A friend who practices civil litigation read the draft and objected that I had borrowed the wrong courtroom: standing, she said, is not the thing in law that bars a claimant on account of the claimant's own conduct, and by reaching for it I have attached my moral thesis to a doctrine that cannot carry it.

Please audit the analogy.

1. Give the mapping explicitly, as a table: what in the moral case plays the part of plaintiff, defendant, court, injury, filing, dismissal, and the rule that supplies the standing requirement — and mark any cell where nothing in the moral case plausibly fills the slot.

2. Take up my friend's objection. Is legal standing the right source domain for the defect hypocrisy is supposed to be, or is there another part of the law that bars a party because of that party's own conduct and fits the phenomenon better? Name it, state what it requires, and say whether borrowing it in place of standing changes the verdict on hypocritical blame — for better or for worse.

3. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the argument I am making from the ones that do not. I expect the absence of a clerk, a docket, a judge, and an enforceable judgment to be listed against me; tell me which of these are fatal and which are cosmetic, and what makes the difference between the two.

4. Identify what the analogy smuggles in about blame that I have not argued for independently — in particular whether it presupposes that blaming is a proceeding conducted before some forum rather than a demand addressed directly to the person blamed, and what survives of my chapter if I deny that presupposition.

5. End with a verdict: does the claim I wanted — that hypocritical blame is barred rather than merely impolitic — survive the audit? If it survives only under a narrower description, give me that description in a sentence I could actually print. And if some other analogy would carry the same point with fewer imports, propose it and say where it in turn breaks down.
