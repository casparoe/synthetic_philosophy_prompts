I'm prepping a session for a history-of-econ reading group and I want to run sixteenth-century Castile through Hume's essay "Of Money" rather than through the crude quantity theory everybody retroactively hangs on him. Here are the facts I'll hand the group, so you don't have to go looking: American silver (Potosí first, then Zacatecas) came in through Seville under a crown monopoly and a 20 percent royal cut; Castilian prices rose something like four- or fivefold over the century on Hamilton's classic index, which is itself contested; the population roughly doubled in the same stretch; Philip II suspended payment on the crown's debt in 1557, 1560, 1575 and 1596; and a huge share of the metal never stayed put, going straight out to Genoese bankers on asientos, to the Army of Flanders, to imported cloth and grain, and eventually round to Manilla. Meanwhile the crown leaned harder on copper coinage, the alcabala, and juros annuities sold to investors.

So map the case onto what Hume actually says. He calls money not one of the wheels of trade but "the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy," insists that prices are "always proportioned to the plenty of money," and separately argues that manufactures "gradually shift their places," flying off from the countries they have already enriched toward cheaper ones. Then there's the companion claim — an inflow of specie helps industry in the interval before prices catch up. Which of those does Castile support, which does it strain, and which does the essay simply have no vocabulary for? I especially want to hear what his framework does with the debt and the suspensions, since nothing in there looks like a theory of sovereign credit, and whether a flow mechanism built on relative prices still does any work when the metal is leaving for fiscal reasons instead of commercial ones. And is the interval idea even testable in an episode that runs a hundred years, or can you always stretch it to fit? Also, given the policy he recommends in that same essay — a public bank that locks up everything deposited and never returns it to circulation, plus no expansion of paper credit — what would he have made of the Habsburgs' financing?

Bottom line I'm after: does Spain vindicate Hume, is it an anomaly he'd have to patch, or is it a case his theory was never built for? Then tell me what would change your mind about that verdict, and where you're least confident — including any of the numbers above you'd want to correct rather than quietly accept. American English, please.
