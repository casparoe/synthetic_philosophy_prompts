Setup (its not the usual puzzle): a driver plans at the highway entrance to take the second of two exits that look identical. Out at the first → stranded, 0. Past the first, out at the second → home, 4. Past both → off the end of the road, 1. Between exits he has total amnesia: at each one he cant tell whether he's already passed one, and remembers nothing about what he did there. All he can fix beforehand is a policy — continue with probability p. Plan-optimal p is 2/3, expected payoff 4/3, and on the obvious calculation his credence at an exit that its the first is 3/5 (or is it? that presupposes a rule the theory has to earn). But the p inside that credence is the p he's evaluating, so the at-the-exit calculation loops. And "continue with prob 2/3" means something different if he flips fresh at each exit than if he flips once up front and sticks to it — different payoffs, 4/3 vs 2/3 — and from inside he cant tell which he's doing.

Im writing the framing section of a survey on choice under imperfect recall, so I want the spec before I wade into proposed fixes. Write the desiderata any adequate decision theory has to meet here, and on the family this is an instance of: agents whose earlier selves get wiped, repeated choices with no memory, committees that take turns deciding. Which cases it must get right, which distinctions it must not collapse, with the consideration behind each. Mark what's non-negotiable and what's one camp's pet constraint — I suspect both "must pick a pure strategy" and "the at-the-exit choice must match the plan" are contestable, but argue it rather than assume it. Then: is some subset jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list is itself a negative result? Which single requirement knocks out the most candidates? And if your list ends up treating this as a self-locating-belief problem in the Sleeping Beauty mold, say so plainly and name the extra machinery that drags in.
