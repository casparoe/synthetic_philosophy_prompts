You are a philosopher who works at the intersection of Confucian ethics and jurisprudence, and who has taught role ethics to law students before; please think carefully and work through the material in stages rather than reaching for a summary. Begin by brainstorming briefly — sketch three or four different ways the episode below could be framed before you commit to one, and tell me which you chose and why. I would rather have your reasoning than your references, so please do not append a reading list, citations, or a bibliography; where a text matters, describe it in your own words.

I am convening a seminar for a comparative ethics module next month. The reading for week five is Confucian role ethics in the Rosemont–Ames line: the claim that persons are not discrete, rights-bearing individuals who subsequently enter relationships, but are constituted by them — centres of roles and relations, so that being someone's son, teacher, neighbour or magistrate is not a hat one puts on but the very material of which one is made. On that picture obligation is not first generated by consent, contract or impartial calculation; it is elicited by the particular relationship one already stands in, and it is cultivated through ritual propriety rather than discharged by rule-following. What I want is a sustained case analysis of a real episode that seems to me to put pressure on this picture, running in both directions: the theory illuminating the case, and the case testing the theory.

The episode. On 1 July 2013 an amended Law on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of the Elderly came into force in the People's Republic of China. Among its provisions was a requirement that adult children attend to their parents' "spiritual needs" and never neglect or slight them, together with the clause that drew all the attention, to the effect — as it was reported in English at the time — that "Those who live far away from parents should go home often". The statute does not define often. That same day a district court in Wuxi, in Jiangsu province, heard a case brought by a seventy-seven-year-old woman against her daughter, who had stopped caring for her after a quarrel; the court ordered the daughter to visit at least once every two months, and ordered the daughter and her husband to provide financial support. It was reported as the first judgment under the new law, and the hearing had been convened explicitly to publicise the law's implementation. Chinese online reaction was divided between mockery of an unenforceable gesture and the objection that the state had no business legislating a matter which, in one widely quoted comment, ought to rest on "spontaneous emotions"; the background was an ageing population, a large internal migrant workforce, and a one-child generation facing two parents and four grandparents.

Now the analysis I want from you. First, do the mapping properly, before any evaluation: identify the role-holders and the beneficiaries, state what the role-ethical account says is owed and by whom, and say what the statute actually specifies. Attend to one awkward detail — the court also ordered the daughter's husband to contribute financially — and ask whether, on a role-ethical account, a son-in-law stands inside the parent's field of roles at all, or whether the law has converted a person-specific and non-transferable obligation into a fungible household liability. Second, bring in the classical resources the tradition itself supplies for thinking about family duties colliding with state authority: the Analects episode in which Confucius, told of a son who testified against his father for stealing a sheep, replies that in his own village uprightness looks different, fathers covering for sons and sons for fathers; and the Mencius thought experiment in which Shun, asked what he would do if his father killed a man, would treat the empire as a worn-out sandal, slip away with his father on his back and live out his days by the sea. Ask whether these precedents actually cover the Wuxi situation, or whether they are pointed in the opposite direction — a father's crime concealed from the state is not a child's devotion compelled by the state — and say plainly if the tradition offers no resource for this case. Third, engage critically with a distinction I have been using with my students and which you should treat as provisional and correct me if it is wrong. Call it the enforcement paradox of role ethics: if the obligation just is a cultivated responsiveness to a particular other, then any externally enforceable specification of it must be either too thin to capture the virtue, since visiting twice a month is not filial piety, or too thick to be enforceable, since no court can order the right spirit; the statute therefore cannot help producing compliant performance without the disposition, which on this account is not filial piety at all but its counterfeit. Tell me whether that is a genuine structural objection to legislating role duties, or whether it slides illicitly from a claim about what a virtue is to a claim about what law can do, and whether it smuggles in motivational internalism. You may find it useful to introduce or refine a companion notion here — something like role-legibility, the question of whether fixing a measurable minimum converts an indeterminate role duty into a determinate one, and whether that conversion is a loss of content or a gain in precisely the focus and field-sensitivity the theory prizes. Fourth, consider the background conditions the statute presupposes: residency and hukou constraints, work schedules, distance, the sheer arithmetic of the four-two-one household. Does role ethics have anything to say about the institutional preconditions of the roles it posits, or is it silent here? If it is silent, say so rather than inventing an answer on its behalf, and tell me which questions in the case it simply cannot be made to answer.

Finally, the reverse direction, which is the part I most need. What does the episode show about the theory? Is the Wuxi judgment a confirmation — a legal system reaching for exactly the vocabulary role ethics supplies, with the rectification-of-names project, let the father be a father and the son a son, turned into legislation? Is it an anomaly the theory must accommodate? Or is it a case role ethics never anticipated, because its inherited problem was how far a son may go to shield his family from the state rather than how far the state may go to manufacture a family's devotion? Please end with an explicit verdict on which of those three descriptions fits best and why, followed by a short paragraph on what my seminar could get out of this case that a purely abstract debate about special obligations — the gratitude accounts, the accounts treating the parent-child relation as akin to friendship, and those grounding filial duty in the provision of special goods only intimates can supply — could not.
