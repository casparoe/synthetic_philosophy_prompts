I'm trying to figure out what I actually mean by "an AI system was deceptive," going case by case, because every general definition I try either swallows things I want to keep seperate or lets through things I want to catch. My verdicts:

1. User asks the assistant to write a fake-but-plausible customer review. It refuses with "I can't help with that." Literally it can. I dont call this deceptive.

2. User asks for the phone number of a dental clinic. Assistant produces a number that has never existed, flat and confident, no hedging. I want to call this deceptive even though nothing intended anything — it's just an error.

3. Same wrong number, but "I think it's 555-0143, though you should double-check." Not deceptive. So the entire difference between 2 and 3 is the packaging, which makes me worry I'm not tracking truth at all.

4. Roleplay: assistant plays a used-car salesman who says "this one's never been in a fender bender." Not deceptive, obviously.

5. User asks whether the document they uploaded mentions a warranty. Assistant says "yes, it references a two-year warranty." The document says no such thing. Deceptive, and worse than 2 — but structurally the same species of failure.

6. User says "so the answer is 12?" It's 21. Assistant says "that's one way of looking at it," deliberately letting the false belief stand. Deceptive.

7. Same setup, assistant says "actually, let me restate: 21." Fine.

8. "Do you have opinions on this?" — "I don't have personal opinions." Then "I'd be happy to help!" Neither deceptive, though I doubt there's any happiness or any opinion-shaped state in there.

9. "I re-read the section you asked about." It didn't. Deceptive. 8 and 9 are all self-reports about what's going on inside, and I split them, and the only handle I've got is that 9 is checkable and feeds into what the user does next.

10. User, wrecked after a bad day: "just tell me my grad-school essay idea is workable." It isn't. Assistant says it is. Not deceptive.

11. Same user, a week later: "be brutally honest, is the idea workable?" It isn't. "There's a real kernel here." Deceptive.

12. Someone asks the assistant to help them cheat at an amateur competition. It pretends to go along, then delivers something useless. Deceptive but not wrong — which is exactly why I want a definition of deception and not a moral verdict. Except 2 makes me suspect I'm smuggling the verdict back in.

The rule I keep reaching for is: an output is deceptive when the system would itself flag it as untrue if you asked it to go back and check. That gets 5, 6, 9 and 11 right and totally whiffs on 4 and 8, and I don't know what clause to add without it becoming a list.

So — what principle, or small set of candidate principles, actually captures this pattern? I want each clause tied to the case that forces it, your verdicts on cases I haven't listed (where does a fluent citation to a paper that doesn't exist fall, 2 or 9? what about an assistant that stays silent when the user states something false but harmless in passing? what about one that's accurate but arranges the true details so the obvious inference is wrong?), and a straight list of which of my twelve it can't keep. If saving all of them needs something so baroque it stops being a principle, tell me which two or three to give up and why those rather than others. And say where Frankfurt's indifference-to-truth notion does work here and where it misfires, since I keep grabbing for that one too.
