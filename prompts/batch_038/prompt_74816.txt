I've been running a little online reading group for about two years now — eleven of us, mostly people who work with their hands or are retired, nobody with a philosophy degree including me, I manage a hardware store. Every couple of weeks I post a question, everybody writes a few hundred words, and then I'm supposed to give feedback, which is the part I'm bad at because half the time I can't tell whether someone has actually got it right or just sounds confident while typing. We're three weeks into a run on joint action — how it is that two people can do something *together* rather than doing the same thing at the same time next to each other — and I've been reading around Bratman, Searle, and Margaret Gilbert, and honestly thier differences mostly blur together for me.

This week's question was mine and it came out of something that actually happened. Last month I helped a customer get a sleeper sofa up three flights, and partway up I realized the guy's "friend" wasn't helping at all, he was standing there holding a flashlight and nodding, and it turned out he'd been given twenty bucks to show up so it looked like a two-person job. So here's what I posted to the group:

QUESTION: Two people are moving a piano upstairs. In the first case they're genuinely doing it together. In the second case the exact same physical movements happen, but one of the two has been paid by a third party to look like a participant and never forms any attitude about the joint activity at all — the other one is deceived. (a) On Bratman's account, on Searle's, and on Gilbert's, what makes the first case joint action and the second one not? (b) Separate case: two movers each intend that they move the piano, but each privately plans to do as little as possible and hopes the other carries it. The piano gets moved. Did they act together, and does your answer change depending on whether it got moved? Finish by saying which of the three accounts you think does best overall and why.

And here's Ray's answer, roughly as he submitted it. He writes in one long block so I've split it up, otherwise it's his words:

"I'll take a run at this. Bratman's idea as I understand it is that there's no group mind and nothing extra over and above the individuals, just a certain way their individual planning hangs together. Each intends that they move the piano, each intends to do his bit in a way that meshes with the other's subplans, they're responsive to each other as they go and adjust, and each knows the other is so intending. He's got the painting example where two guys paint the same house but each on his own plan and they just happen not to bump into each other, and that isn't shared activity.

So for the paid fake I think the answer is clearly no, and the reason is that Bratman builds in common knowledge of each other's intentions. The fake has none and the deceived one has a false belief, so the structure can't interlock and you've just got two men lifting. Searle is different. For him the we-intention isn't made out of an I-intention plus a belief about what the other fellow will do, it's a primitive kind of attitude that sits in one person's head, with the form I-intend-that-we-do-it. That means Searle doesn't require the fake to have anything going on inside him at all, which I'd call an advantage, since in real life you generally can't check. So the fake case doesn't damage Searle, it damages Bratman, and Searle comes out ahead on it.

Gilbert goes a third way. The two of them create a plural subject, and the way that happens is that each expresses readiness to be jointly committed in conditions where that's common knowledge between them, and once it's in place neither one can rescind it on his own, which is where the standing to rebuke comes from — you're not nagging him, you're holding him to something he's a part of. In the fake case nobody expressed readiness and there was no common knowledge of any such thing, so no plural subject, and the deceived man's complaint is that he was lied to, which is a different sort of complaint entirely and I think Gilbert can say that cleanly.

The slackers are the hard one. Each intends that they move the piano and each intends to contribute as little as he can, and by luck it gets moved. I think this shows Bratman's account is too demanding, because there's plainly a sense in which those two did it together, they just each hoped the other would carry it, and any account that can't call that joint action is missing something. It also puts Searle ahead again, since all Searle needs is the attitude in each head and both of them have it in good faith. Gilbert would say the plural subject exists, both are obligated, and both breached, which to me sounds exactly like a bad day at work, so she's not wrong, but she doesn't explain why the piano moved. On the last part of the question, whether it matters that it got moved — I'd say it shouldn't matter, intentions are intentions, but I admit his talk about the activity having to be explained by the meshing plans makes me less sure."
