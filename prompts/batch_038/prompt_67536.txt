My history teacher has this tiny podcast (like 40 listeners, mostly his old students) and next week we're interviewing a philosophy prof whose new book is about Berkeley's economics — not the idealism stuff everyone knows, the money stuff, mainly from The Querist. She told him she'd only do it if a student asked the questions, so that's me, and she made me read like thirty of the queries beforehand.

Her argument as far as I can follow it: Berkeley doesn't think money is wealth at all, it's a counter for keeping score — one of the queries asks whether the true idea of money "be not altogether that of a ticket or counter" — and real wealth is labour and industry, with money mattering only insofar as it "stirreth up industry." She says this is a genuine theory and not a slogan, that it falls straight out of his attack on abstract ideas plus the bit in Alciphron where he's perfectly happy using words like force with no clear idea attached to them, that his plan for a national bank is basically paper money that isn't redeemable in silver but secured on the subscribers' land, and that he's miles ahead of Locke, who kept insisting the silver content of the coin is what matters.

Can you write me seven or eight questions, each one two sentences max, and each with a follow-up aimed at the answer I'm pretty sure she'll give? Apparently she's very good at turning a hard question into another pitch for the book, so the follow-ups need to close that door. Nothing she can answer by summarising her chapters, and nothing that assumes the listener already knows Berkeley's vocabulary.

I definitely need ones on:

- the tension I keep tripping over. If money is only a ticket or counter, why does he also ask whether "less money swiftly circulating" is in effect equivalent to more money slowly circulating, and say the "wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit"? That sounds like the amount and the speed of the money really does causal work — i.e. a quantity theory, which is kind of the opposite of "it's just a counter." What do I fire back if she says the quantity only ever matters relative to the volume of trade?
- the land backing. If the notes are secured on land, isn't their value anchored to a real asset at exactly the point where her anti-commodity reading needs it not to be? And if she says the pledge is just theatre to build public confidence, then what is confidence doing inside the theory — a psychological fact or another sign relation?
- the Alciphron analogy. I want a question that makes the comparison with force pay rent: what does treating money as a sign with no idea behind it let Berkeley explain that a commodity theorist can't? Follow-up if she says the analogy is just an illustration of his nominalism.
- that query about the "denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone." Is he proposing fiat money, or just saying bookkeeping survives the metal going away? Related: does something have to make the tokens go — the state, the law — given his bank is private subscribers pledging land?
- Ireland. Absentee landlords, money draining across the channel, people working hard and staying poor anyway. Can a pure token theory explain why losing counters is a catastrophe instead of a printing problem? Follow-up if she says the real issue was circulation and credit, not the metal.
- one closing question asking her what in the Querist would make her admit she's over-reading him.

Then at the end, can you say how confident you are that the Berkeley details baked into these questions are actually right, flag anything you filled in yourself rather than something Berkeley clearly says, and single out the one question you're least sure puts real pressure on her view — like the one she'd swat away easiest.
