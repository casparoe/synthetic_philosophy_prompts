I am a senior at a fairly small public high school, and I run our philosophy club, which meets on Thursdays for thirty minutes in a classroom that has a whiteboard and nothing else: no projector, no handouts, no laptops. Next week, at my teacher's suggestion, I have agreed to lead the session on Hempel's raven paradox. There will be about fourteen students, mostly juniors. None of them has taken a logic course, and none has met probability beyond the fractions and coin-flipping our algebra classes require. Two of them are quick and will push back hard on anything that smells like a trick, and one of them has already read about the paradox somewhere online and knows the punchline about the green apple, which he will be strongly tempted to blurt out in the first two minutes. What I want from you is a plan for running the explanation, not the explanation itself. I know the material well enough to talk about it; what I cannot see from the inside is the order it has to go in and where it will break.

So, first, the sequence. Tell me what has to be in place before anything else can be said, and why each piece depends on the ones before it. In particular: should the generalization be one the students produce themselves, or handed to them, and does that choice change anything downstream? When should the equivalence condition come in relative to the intuition that a nonblack non-raven tells us nothing about ravens, and which of the two should the students be permitted to want to give up first? I have a suspicion that if they get to reject one too early, the rest of the session has nothing to do, but I cannot tell whether that suspicion is right or whether I am just protecting my own favorite part.

Second, the example that carries the whole thing. I would rather not run it on birds at all, partly because the club already contains a shoe from the lost-and-found and somebody will wave it around regardless. Tell me whether an ordinary inanimate classroom object can actually bear the weight here, what the example has to have and what it has to lack for the reasoning to go through, and if a shoe will not work, what will. I would also like your judgment on whether the demonstration should be something we physically do, such as drawing slips of paper from a bag, or something I only draw on the board, since doing it seems more engaging but may let them focus on the mechanics of the drawing rather than on the inference.

Third, the misconceptions, listed in the order I should expect them and with the exact point in the sequence at which each one should be headed off. The ones I can already predict are: that confirmation just means making something more likely, so of course a white shoe counts and there is no paradox at all; that a shoe cannot be evidence about ravens because it is not a bird; that the whole thing is a trick because there are vastly more nonblack objects than ravens, which I suspect is a real reply rather than a mistake, so tell me how much of it fourteen-year-olds can be given with no probability at all; that the paradox shows logic is broken; and that it shows science is arbitrary, since anything confirms anything. Add the ones I have missed, especially any that my own explanation would create rather than the students bring in. And tell me what to do with the boy who knows the ending, in a way that does not simply silence him.

Fourth, a question of my own that I would like you to rule on rather than indulge. I have become convinced that what is really going on has to do with our sense of surprise, and with what we expect an observation to look like, rather than with confirmation as such, and I want to close the session by saying so. Tell me whether that belongs in thirty minutes at this level, where it would have to sit if it does, and whether introducing it any earlier would collapse the sequence I have just asked you to build. If you think I should keep it to myself, say so plainly and tell me what to put in its place.

Fifth, a check. Give me one question I can ask in the final three minutes whose answers will separate the students who understood the problem from the students who were merely entertained, and describe what a good answer and a bad answer would each sound like, including the answer that sounds right but is not.

Finally, tell me what to leave out and to announce that I am leaving it out: the probabilistic treatment, the role of background knowledge, the question of whether we should keep or abandon the intuition about nonblack non-ravens. I would much rather say openly that we are stopping short than pretend to a resolution I cannot support.

Please write all of this as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, and no separate sections marked off from one another. I would also ask that you not direct me to any books, articles, videos, or names of specific philosophers' solutions beyond the ones I have mentioned myself; I intend to prepare entirely from what you write, and a list of sources I have not read would only tempt me to invoke authorities I could not explain if anyone asked.
