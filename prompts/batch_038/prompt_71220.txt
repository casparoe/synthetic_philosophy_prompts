Color phi has sustained an argument that has gone nowhere for more than thirty years. Flash a blue dot at the top of a screen and, a fraction of a second later, a red dot below it, and subjects report a single dot traveling between the two that changes color halfway along — a change that, on any physical description of the stimuli, is not yet available at the point where it is reportedly seen. Two accounts compete. The Orwellian says the brain writes the record first and revises it once the second flash arrives. The Stalinesque says there was never an unrevised record: the trajectory is assembled before it becomes anything the subject can report. The pair was introduced precisely to argue that they are empirically indistinguishable and that the question of when the change entered experience is badly posed; yet the argument has carried on as though a fact were at stake. My working hypothesis is that what divides the camps now is not the neurophysiology but a disagreement about what a perceptual report is supposed to be accurate to — an episode with determinate temporal boundaries, or nothing over and above the report itself. Test that hypothesis. Say where the fault line really lies, whether postdictive results of the flash-lag and cutaneous-rabbit kind could ever break the tie or merely push it upstream, whether an account on which experiences come with duration already built into them escapes the dilemma or relocates it, and what would count as progress here: a measurement, a distinction, or a demonstration that the question was never well-posed.
