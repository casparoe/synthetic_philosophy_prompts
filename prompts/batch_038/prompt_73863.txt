I'm a journalist covering science and ideas — mostly the border zone where psychology, law and moral philosophy slop into each other — and I'm working on a long piece about why Norway's handling of its worst mass killing turned into a global argument about what punishment is *for*. I've got the reporting; what I need is someone to do the conceptual work properly with me, because my editor is going to ask hard questions and I don't want to hand-wave.

The episode, in case your recall is fuzzy on the details: July 22, 2011, a bomb in Oslo's government quarter and then the shootings at the youth camp on Utøya, 77 dead. The trial ran through spring 2012 in Oslo District Court. The thing that ate the whole trial was not whodunit — he admitted the acts from the start, and pleaded not guilty anyway on the grounds that he was acting out of necessity — it was whether he was sane. Two court-appointed psychiatric teams produced flatly contradictory reports: the first said paranoid schizophrenia, the second said not psychotic and criminally responsible. The verdict in August 2012 found him sane and guilty, and imposed 21 years of preventive detention with a minimum term of 10 years — and, as I understand it, extendable in chunks of up to five years at a time, indefinitely, for as long as he's judged dangerous. Two smaller details I want in the analysis because I think they matter: one of the lay judges had to step down on the second day after it emerged he'd argued for the death penalty on Facebook the day after the attacks, and the defence called witnesses including a well-known Islamist and a far-right activist specifically to establish that political extremism isn't a mental disorder. Later, if I remember right, he sued the state over his prison conditions and won in part at first instance.

So: run this through the theories of punishment, and do it in both directions.

First, the mapping, done carefully rather than impressionistically. What does a retributivist say the sentence should have been — and is the real problem that 21 years is wildly disproportionate to 77 murders, or that proportionality has an upper bound set by dignity and the expressive aims of the state, so the number isn't the scandal? What does a consequentialist say — is the indefinite tail just incapacitation being honest about itself, and is that a point in its favour or a confession? And when do the two theories actually come apart here, versus when are they observationally equivalent? Because my suspicion is that this sentence is consequentialist content in retributivist packaging, and I want you to tell me what specific event would make the packaging visibly tear — him becoming genuinely non-dangerous at year 11, him still being dangerous at year 21, something else.

Second, the inversion, which is the part I find most interesting. In a normal trial the defendant bids his desert down and the state bids it up. Here the defendant bid up: he wanted to be held responsible, and the insanity finding was the outcome he was resisting, with his own lawyers arguing sanity. I've been kicking around a term for this — call it a *desert bid*, meaning a party's attempt to move the court's attribution of responsibility in one direction or the other. Take that seriously as an analytic device. Does a penal system's willingness to accept an upward bid *from the offender* — to let his own claim to be a responsible agent carry weight — count as a virtue, a bug, or an accident of this case? Should the offender's view of his own desert have any standing at all, and do retributivism and consequentialism give different answers to that? I'm thinking of the old argument that there's a right to be punished, that treating a person as a patient when he demands to be treated as an agent is a kind of demotion — say whether that holds here or whether the bid is better explained by something with nothing to do with punishment theory at all, like the fact that his whole project required an audience that took him as an actor rather than a symptom.

Third — and don't fudge this — what does the episode show back about the theories? Which one does the case actually favour, if any? Is the Norwegian settlement (determinate-looking sentence with an open-ended tail, plus a dignity floor enforced by the prisoner's own lawsuit) a principled hybrid, or an evasion of the question the two theories disagree about? And what should the lay judge's recusal do to anyone who leans on punishment as the community's voice — the fact that the person who most loudly wanted to speak for the community's outrage had to be filtered out of the mechanism for expressing it seems like a real datum, not a curiosity.

Don't give me a survey and don't give me a reading list — no citations, no bibliography, I can look people up myself. I want argument and a verdict at the end, even a qualified one. As long as it needs to be and not a word longer; two thousand-ish is probably the sweet spot.
