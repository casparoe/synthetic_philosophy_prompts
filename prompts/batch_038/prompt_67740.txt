Please write a parody of a mandatory corporate e-learning compliance module, presented as it would appear on screen. The issuing body is invented — call it the Meridian Institute for Reciprocity Governance — and the artifact is "Module 4: Gift or Bribe?" of its Global Reciprocity Integrity Standard, deployed word-for-word at every site of a multinational. Target length 800–1,100 words. Use no real organizations, statutes, officials, or named individuals, and include no bibliography, further reading, or references.

Structure it the way the module would actually run: a welcome screen stating the purpose and the completion deadline; a numbered set of definitional clauses; a decision flowchart rendered as indented conditionals; four to six knowledge-check scenarios, each with three answer options and the auto-generated feedback the learner receives for whichever option they pick; one sidebar box; and a closing certification screen.

The target is not any particular country or culture. It is the universalist assumption built into the compliance genre: that the difference between a gift and a bribe is fundamentally a matter of paperwork, that it can be operationalized as a monetary de minimis threshold plus a disclosure form plus the absence of a pending matter, and that the resulting test travels unchanged across societies. The module must never break character, never wink, and never concede a relativist point; it stays cheerfully and bureaucratically certain throughout.

The humor should come from getting the concepts right rather than from exaggerating them. Build the piece around the following, without announcing any of them as themes:

- The module claims to define corruption as a violation of the duties attaching to a public office, while in practice it keeps falling back on what its own auditees think is improper, and it has no way at all to express the third candidate definition — harm to the public interest.
- Its central requirement that a permissible gift be "free of any expectation of return" is self-undermining: a transfer with no expectation of return is not a gift in most of the settings it audits. Let the module's own clauses collide with the idea that giving, receiving, and reciprocating are obligations, and that the person who cannot reciprocate becomes a client rather than a peer.
- Its bright-line rules fail on incommensurables: the animal, the funeral contribution, the patron who pays a relative's school fees, the marriage transfer. Require a declared value in a single hard currency and show what that does to the item.
- Its "no pending matter between the parties" test fails on a twenty-year relationship, in which nothing is pending precisely because everything is.
- It recognizes only holders of state office, so the elder, the party cadre, the union delegate, and the mother-in-law are invisible to it — as is any patronage network that runs outside the state.
- Its de minimis threshold auto-clears the small payments that speed up routine procedures, while the far larger transfers it encounters — sponsorships, board seats, fellowships, consulting fees — get classified as private-sector arrangements and marked out of scope.
- One scenario should show the module certifying a genuinely coercive exchange as fully compliant, because it was logged, disclosed, denominated, and signed off by legal; another should show a patronage machine that functions as the only reliable distributor of wells, jobs, and insurance in its district, which the module condemns for a reason it cannot state.

The satire should be fair in both directions: a reader should come away unsure whether the module's impersonal office is a moral achievement or a local and recent anthropological oddity, and equally unsure whether the alternative it condemns is a bond or a racket. Do not resolve either tension.

The module's voice is second-person, upbeat, and litigious, with the flat affect of instructional design. End with a final line beginning exactly "Verdict:" followed by a short phrase giving the compliance determination the module has computed for the learner.
