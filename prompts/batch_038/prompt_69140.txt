Just got back from three hours in the pub with Dan, who's doing a masters in logic, and I've completely lost the plot. We were meant to be going over the seminar reading on conditionals — Adams, Lewis's triviality results, Edgington — and instead he walked me into an argument for a conclusion I'm sure is false but cant block, and then he ran the same argument again for counterfactuals and it got properly ridiculous. Here's his chain as best I can reconstruct it; he sketched it on a beer mat and I've tidied it up.

1. Indicative conditionals embed. They get negated ("it's not the case that if she applied she'll get it"), they occur under "probably", they can be the antecedent of another conditional, and they turn up in modus ponens and modus tollens arguments everyone agrees are valid.
2. Only something with truth conditions can do all that. If "if A, B" expressed no proposition at all, there'd be nothing for "not" or "probably" to operate on, and there'd be no fact of the matter about whether the modus ponens inference is valid.
3. So indicative conditionals have truth conditions: they're true or false.
4. "If" is a connective joining two sentences. By compositionality, the truth conditions of "if A, B" are fixed by the contents of A and B plus the meaning of "if" — and "if" means the same thing in every indicative conditional.
5. So whether "if A, B" is true is settled by whether A is true and whether B is true. There's nothing left in the sentence for it to depend on.
6. Given that, only one truth-function is available. It must be false when A is true and B is false, or modus ponens fails. It must be true when A and B are both true, or "A, and if A then B" would be a contradiction. And it can't be false whenever A is false, or a conditional would settle the truth value of its own antecedent, which it plainly doesn't — "if she applied, she'll get the job" tells you nothing about whether she applied. Those three constraints leave exactly ¬A ∨ B.
7. So indicative conditionals are material conditionals, and P(if A, B) = P(¬A ∨ B).
8. Adams' thesis says P(if A, B) = P(B | A). The two coincide only in special cases — trivially when P(A) = 1 — and not in general. Concretely: 200 applicants, 40 got interviews, 12 of those interviewed got offers, and nobody got an offer without an interview. "If an applicant was interviewed, they got an offer" has conditional probability 12/40 = 0.3, but read materially its probability is (160 + 12)/200 = 0.86.
9. Adams' thesis is supposed to be the one thing the data support — it's what explains why we assert some conditionals and not others. But (1)–(6) look forced. So Dan's verdict: Adams is wrong, assertibility isn't probability, and we should be Griceans about the lot — material truth conditions plus a pragmatic story about why the odd-sounding ones go unsaid.
10. Then he ran the identical argument for counterfactuals. They embed too, they're built out of two sentences by a connective, they're false when the antecedent is true and the consequent false, and a counterfactual can't be refuted merely by its antecedent being false. So counterfactuals are material as well — which makes every counterfactual with a false antecedent true, including "if Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, the moon would have been made of cheese" — and the entire closest-worlds industry was a detour.

Its that last step where I want to say something has gone wrong, but the trouble is I can't find a place to put my finger on where the earlier steps weren't already doing the damage. If (1)–(6) really force the material reading for indicatives, what's supposed to block it for counterfactuals other than that the result is horrible? My instinct is to keep (1), (3) and Adams' thesis and sacrifice something in the middle, but Dan says the middle steps are just compositionality, and that denying them means giving up on having a semantics at all. He also floated a fallback I don't know what to do with: keep material truth conditions, but insist the assertibility rule is conditional probability even inside embeddings, so that "probably, if she applied, she'll get it" comes out as something like the probability that she gets it, supposing she applied. If that's coherent, what work is the truth condition of the embedded conditional doing there, and what happens when the two rules give different verdicts about the same sentence?
