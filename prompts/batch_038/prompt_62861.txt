Hillsborough, and whether Fricker's two categories can actually hold it.

Some facts up front so you're not guessing: 15 April 1989, FA Cup semi-final at Hillsborough, 94 Liverpool fans crushed to death in the Leppings Lane end with three more dying later (the toll was eventually revised to 97). Four days on, the Sun runs a front page headlined "The Truth" claiming fans had picked the pockets of the dead, urinated on police officers and beaten up a PC giving the kiss of life — claims that later turned out to trace back to a Sheffield agency whose material four South Yorkshire Police officers had originated. The Hillsborough Independent Panel, reporting in 2012, found 164 statements from officers on the day had been flagged for substantive amendment, with self-critical passages taken out before the accounts went into the official record. First inquests returned accidental death in 1991; a fresh jury only concluded unlawful killing in 2016.

What I want is an analysis that runs in both directions, theory onto case and case back onto theory.

First, the mapping, done properly. Who is the speaker and who the hearer at each stage — individual survivors, the bereaved families as a bloc, the police as an institution? Where exactly is the identity prejudice, and is "football fan" or "Scouser" even a social identity in Fricker's sense, or is it a stereotype the police had a positive interest in generating rather than merely inheriting? Does the Sun front page count as testimonial injustice at all, given the paper wasn't so much disbelieving a speaker as manufacturing one?

Second, and this is the bit I actually care about. I've been using a label for the 27 years afterwards and I want you to attack it — call it narrative displacement. The thought: the familes were never hermeneutically marginalised in the standard sense, because they had a fully articulate account of the crush in their own words from week one. What they lacked was any institutional setting in which that account was the default. The record had already been filled with a rival description, so their testimony didn't attract a credibility deficit so much as get treated as a challenge to a settled fact, and had to be re-argued from scratch every single time. Is that a genuinely third wrong, distinct from testimonial and hermeneutical injustice, or is it just testimonial injustice with institutional power sitting behind it? Make the case and then give me the strongest version of the other side.

Third, be disciplined about where the framework goes quiet instead of making it speak. The altered statements look like the hard case: a theory built around a hearer's prejudice against a speaker, applied to a body that was speaker, hearer and record-keeper simultaneously.

No bibliography, no citations, nothing to look up. Around 1200 words. And finish by telling me which part of my framing you think is just wrong.
