I teach a fourth-year seminar on the philosophy and sociology of work — unofficial title, The Right to Be Idle — and its always a bit of a downer in the last session before reading week. We spend three weeks on Weber's Protestant Ethic, including the argument about whether iron cage is remotely what Weber actually wrote, which the students have strong feelings about; then the French 35-hour week and the RTT days that came out of the Aubry laws; then Japanese karōshi and karōjisatsu, and the fact that death by overwork is an administrative category there with a workers-comp route and an annual government report attached to it. We seperate out Keynes's 1930 essay on the fifteen-hour work week for the very end, which is nominally the optimistic note and never lands as one.

I want to open that session with a joke, because the mood after the karōshi material is grim and I don't want to just walk in and say "so, discussion questions." But it has to be an actual joke about this material. I'm not interested in a sociologist-walks-into-a-bar, a pun on work ethic, or anything that plays just as well to a room that has never read Weber — the punchline should turn on something structurally distinctive about the ideas. Some seams you could use: that the 35-hour week was pitched mainly as an employment measure and in practice produced work intensification plus extra days off rather than idleness; that innerworldly asceticism makes leisure itself feel like theft from the calling, so the guilt survives the theology; that you can die of overwork and have it certified, while there is no comparable category for anything caused by too much free time; or — this is my own half-formed view and you're welcome to attack it rather than use it — that the four-day-week movement is the Protestant ethic with the theology left running in the background, rest framed as recovery so you can work better afterwards. Pick one seam per joke and don't let all five land on the same one.

Two things on tone. The target should be the ideology, the statistics, or me — never the dead workers, and the two students in the room who have genuinely worked sixty-hour weeks (one of them ran a konbini night shift in Osaka for three years) should not feel like the butt of anything. Also, a couple of colleagues sometimes sit in and are easily offended, so keep it clean. Give me exactly five candidate jokes as a numbered list, each two or three sentences long, setup and punchline, with no introduction, no notes on which one is best, and no explanation of why any of them are funny — I'll pick, and I'll take the risk myself.
