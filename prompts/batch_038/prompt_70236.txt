I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, but reading about pain asymbolia started an argument I cannot get out of. It runs as follows.

(1) What makes a pain bad for the person who feels it is that it feels bad — its unpleasantness.
(2) Patients with pain asymbolia, typically after lesions of the insular cortex, report pains they can localise and rate for intensity as well as anyone else, yet they are not bothered by them: they describe them calmly, sometimes with detachment, and do not withdraw.
(3) So the sensory character of a pain can be fully present while its unpleasantness is absent.
(4) If unpleasantness can come and go while the feeling itself stays the same, then unpleasantness is not part of what it is to feel a pain; it is a separate state — a reaction to the pain.
(5) But a mere reaction to something cannot make that thing itself bad.
(6) So no pain is bad in itself; only the minding of it is.
(7) Hence a person rendered wholly indifferent to pain would lose nothing that matters, and if that could be done safely it ought to be offered to everyone; likewise, an analgesic given to someone in agony improves nothing about their experience, only their behaviour and their prognosis.

I cannot accept (7). My father was in considerable pain during his last months, and nothing about that period suggests the relief he was given was merely cosmetic. If I have to give something up, I would rather abandon (5) than (2), since (2) at least looks checkable. Which step is the weak one, and why did it seem acceptable when I wrote it? What does each of the main accounts of unpleasantness — as an imperative or command to stop, as a representation of badness, as an intrinsic hedonic quality — pay in order to reject that step? I would also like to know what (2) actually licenses on the empirical side, and whether a repaired argument still delivers a conclusion worth having.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of the question.
