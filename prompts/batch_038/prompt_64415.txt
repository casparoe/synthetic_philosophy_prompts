So I'm partway through my second novel and there's one scene I keep walking around without going into. I want to hand it to you cold and see what comes out.

Backstory you'll need. Ilse Renner, 62, built her career on one paradigm: somebody sits in a chair with an EEG cap, watches a dot sweep round a clock face, flexes a wrist whenever they feel like it, and afterwards reports where the dot was when the urge to move hit. You average a couple hundred of those trials backwards from the muscle twitch and a slow negative ramp shows up, starting roughly half a second before the movement, while the reported urge lands only about a fifth of a second before it. Ilse spent thirty years telling packed rooms that the ramp means the brain had begun the act before the person knew they were choosing it. It made her. She doesn't think its exactly wrong, but she knows the version she said out loud was flatter and more certain than what the data can carry.

Now her postdoc, Duy, has gone back through the same recordings with a much less romantic analysis, and what he finds makes the ramp look like a trick of the averaging. If the underlying activity is noisy, and you only keep the trials where the noise happened to climb high enough to trigger a movement, then lining all of those up on the movement and averaging manufactures a smooth ramp out of pure jitter — nothing decided anything, the ramp is just the shadow a threshold leaves when a random walk crosses it. Separately, in the cancellation version of the task, where a tone sometimes tells the subject to abort, people can still stop if the tone comes early but are more or less helpless if it comes late, which suggests there's a genuine point of no return in there, and that the stretch before it is open rather than already written.

Then the letter. A defence lawyer wants Ilse as an expert witness in a case where a man killed his brother-in-law in a parking garage, and what he wants her to testify to is that the defendant's brain had set the act in motion before he was conscious of it. Ilse knows the honest version of her own science won't hold that weight. She also knows that saying so on the record means saying the ramp didn't mean what she said it meant for three decades.

One more thing I want folded in, obliquely, not explained: the old claim in her field that conscious experience gets referred backwards in time — that a touch felt half a second after it lands on your skin gets dated, subjectively, to the moment it landed. Ilse finds this both absurd and, on bad nights, accurate about her own life.

What I actually want written:

One scene, 1600–2000 words, close third person on Ilse, running in real time in the EEG room while a subject is in the chair and the dot is still sweeping. The lawyer's letter should be physically in the room — under the gel tub, on the cart, wherever. Duy is there for part of it.

Formal constraint: no chapter breaks, no timestamps. Divide the scene into sections marked only by where the clock dot is when each one starts — the position described, not named — so the reader gets the same reconstructed, unreliable sense of when things happened that the subjects do. If that's disorienting, good, that's intended, but the story itself has to stay legible.

Hard rules:
- Nobody explains the theory to anyone who already knows it. The noise-and-threshold idea has to arrive as something Duy *does* — rebinning trials, deleting a beautiful average, drawing a ramp that turns out not to be there — never as a speech.
- No real researchers, no paper titles, no journals, no citations, no bibliography. It's a novel.
- Keep the numbers to the two timings I gave you. If you want a third, don't.
- Include at least one moment where a character states a reason for what they're doing that the reader can tell isn't the real one. That's the theme of the whole book and I dont want it announced.
- Ilse has to answer the lawyer's question out loud, in the room, even though he isn't there. What she says should cost her something visible.
- End on something in the room — an object, a sound, the cap coming off — not on a thought or a closing interior summary.

I don't want a tie at the end. I want the reader to be able to feel which of the two readings of that squiggle Ilse needs to be true, and to suspect it isn't the one she'll testify to.

After the scene, give me four or five sentences max on which reading you ended up weighting toward, and what you had to bend or fudge to make it work as fiction. And if anything I've told you about the science feels thin or stale, flag it — but don't go looking anything up, just tell me where it wobbled.
