I've got two people in my department who've been going in circles for three years about risk: one is a bog-standard expected utility maximizer who thinks any rational preference ordering over gambles has to come out of a probability-weighted sum of utilities, and the other is a Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility theorist who thinks the global shape of a gamble — how likely you are to end up badly off, not just how likely each prize is — is a legitimate input to rational choice. They've agreed to do an adversarial collaboration but neither will draft anything, so I'm going to hand them a joint statement and make them red-line it. Write me that draft.

What I need in it: the claims both camps can sign without wincing; then the disagreements stated precisely enough that each side recognises its own position in the wording, in their own preferred terms rather than the other side's; then the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions where settling it would actually move at least one of them; and for each crux, a case or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance so we can check later who moved and who just reworded thier position. If some chunk of the argument turns out to be merely verbal — about what the word rational is doing, or about whether separability across states is constitutive of rationality or just a substantive constraint on it — say which chunk and why.

To keep this from turning into a seminar summary, build the cruxes out of a family of cases I made up. Call it the zero-dial. Fix three prizes: nothing, $2,400, and $2,500, with utilities u(0) = 0, u(2400) = 0.8, u(2500) = 1, so both sides are arguing about the same arithmetic. Left act L pays $2,400 for sure. Right act R(w) pays $2,500 with probability exactly one third, nothing with probability w, and $2,400 with the remaining probability two-thirds minus w, where w runs from 0 to two-thirds. So at w = 0 there's no catastrophe branch at all, at w = 0.01 you're sitting on the familiar Allais-style common-consequence case, and at the far end w = two-thirds the right act is a bare one-third shot at the top prize.

Here's what I want them to sign or fight over, and I want you to actually do the algebra rather than gesture at it. First: does each theory predict exactly one flip point in w, where the agent switches from R to L, and where is it? I get w = 1/12 on the expected utility side, and on the other side something like 0.8·r(1−w) + 0.2·r(1/3) = 0.8, where r is the risk function — check that, I'm not certain I've set up the rank-dependent weighting right. Second, and this is the crux I care most about: given that r is only required to be increasing with r(0)=0 and r(1)=1, it looks to me like for any observed flip point below the expected utility one you can find a convex r that reproduces it exactly. So is r independently pinned by something else — other choices, a normative argument about what attitudes a person may permissibly have — or is it a free parameter that buys fit at the price of testability? Get both camps to say what would count as r being independently constrained, and what they'd each predict about a subject whose utility numbers are already fixed by risk-free certainty-equivalent questions and who then gets run across the whole dial.

Third crux: bundling. Take one zero-dial problem and an independent coin flip and present them as two separate choices, then as a single combined act. Does each camp's theory permit the subject's ranking to flip between the two presentations? Expected utility should say never, because the ranking can't depend on what happens in states that are the same either way; I think the other side has to say a flip is possible for some r, and if they say it isn't, they need to explain why not. Have each side state the direction of the flip they expect.

Fourth crux: the sequential version. Put the agent in a two-stage problem where the zero-dial choice is made now and a further choice comes up only after w resolves, and where the plan they'd commit to at stage one is not what they'd choose at stage two. Both camps should agree on a concrete case of that shape, and then pre-register whether they think such an agent can be pumped for a sure small loss, and if so whether that's a reductio or a price worth paying.

A few things they should be able to sign and I want stated plainly: the dispute isn't about what the probabilities are; concave utility over money buys you some risk-aversion but can't buy you the Allais pattern; expected utility is the special case where the risk function is linear, so the two theories aren't rivals about every piece of data; and a theory that can fit any pattern isn't thereby vindicated, the burden is on out-of-sample prediction.

Don't give me a bibliography, don't cite papers or names of commentators, don't tell me where to go read about it. I want the argument itself. And format: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter. Signpost in the sentences themselves if you need to, and switch into the two voices where they diverge, but keep it readable as one document that both of them could put their names to.
