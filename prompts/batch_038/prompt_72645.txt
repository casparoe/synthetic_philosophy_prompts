I'm giving a talk in about three weeks at one of those interdisciplinary workshops — philosophy of neuroscience bolted onto a computational psychiatry series — and it's the first time I'm presenting this stuff outside a philosophy department, so I'm honestly kind of dreading the Q&A. Act like a philosopher of mind who works on the debates about computation and explanation in neuroscience and who has sat through a lot of brutal question periods, and prep me. Before you write anything, spend a short paragraph brainstorming out loud about who in the room has what motive to attack which part of my argument, then give me the actual sheet.

Here's the thesis, roughly: (1) computation, in the sense that matters, is just what mechanisms do — a system computes when its physical properties are organized so that they systematically realize a function, and the description at that level is medium-independent in the ordinary way; (2) the dynamical-systems attack on computationalism — the line that runs through van Gelder's mid-nineties paper on the Watt governor and the timedynamics people who came after it — doesn't actually establish a rival picture of the mind. Trace those examples through carefully and what you get is continuous, non-symbolic, non-representational computation, not no computation. On my view they win the expository fight and lose the metaphysical one; (3) the risky part, and where I expect trouble: what makes a system computational is its functional organization, not its substrate's energy profile or how wet it is, so a noisy, analog, metabolically expensive brain is a computer, and calling it something else is aesthetics rather than science. I'm not claiming it's a Turing machine, and I want to be clear in the talk that I'm not.

The room: about two thirds systems and circuits neuroscientists — big multielectrode recordings, people who fit RNNs to neural data and then argue about whether the RNN is doing the explaining. They say the word computation forty times a talk and mean by it something like whatever the circuit does between input and output, so my thesis sounds to them either trivially true or like I'm handing them a term of art they don't need. What they actually care about: does the computational level earn its explanatory keep, does it constrain experiments, does it predict anything a purely dynamical description would miss, and is any of this falsifiable. There's one postdoc who works on metastability and timing and is allergic to any account that implies the brain has a clock or a clean input/output boundary. Then two philosophers: one sympathetic to dynamical and enactivist critiques who will go at me on homunculi, semantic bootstrapping, and how I individuate computations without already assuming the thing I'm defending; the other works on mechanistic explanation and will want to know whether my view is any different from the standard mechanistic account or just that account with a new label.

What I want back is a prep sheet, not a defense. Separate out the two or three that have real force from the ones that only sound bad in the moment. Then tell me the one reply that has to land for the whole talk to survive the afternoon, and the single question I'm hoping nobody asks, with the best answer I've got for it in case they do.
