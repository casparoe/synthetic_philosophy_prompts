I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on the political economy of European decarbonisation, hosted by a policy school. The thesis is narrow and deliberately provocative: that Germany's decision to shut down an existing, licensed and largely amortised nuclear fleet was a substantially larger setback for European emissions reduction than the failure to commission new reactors, and that the underlying error was to conflate the defensible case against building new large reactors with the case against running the installed ones to the end of their technical life.

My argument has three steps, and I want the objections aimed at them. First, on the counterfactual: in the hours that matter, the marginal generation that filled the gap was not solar and wind but lignite, hard coal and, before that, gas; the relevant comparison is therefore between a running fleet and what actually displaced it, not between a running fleet and a renewable buildout that was proceeding under separate subsidy legislation regardless. Second, on cost: a plant with sunk capital should be assessed on short-run operating cost and capacity factor, not on a levelised figure that imports the financing and construction risk of new builds at Vogtle, Flamanville or Hinkley Point. Third, on institutions: the liberalised German market had no mechanism for socialising the residual cost of lifetime extension, while the French model does; my claim is that this asymmetry, rather than any technical verdict, explains the divergence between the two countries, and that the divergence should not be read as evidence about the technology.

The room is about twenty people. Most are energy economists working on cost-effectiveness and market design. Two are power-system modellers who run continental high-renewable scenarios and are used to defending them. One is a regulatory lawyer specialising in state aid. One is a planning analyst from a transmission system operator who works on redispatch and north–south congestion. There are graduate students. No nuclear engineers. Nobody is reflexively hostile, but several hold that the phase-out was at worst a rounding error because renewable deployment was never in fact crowded out.

Prepare my question-period sheet. Give me the eight to ten objections this particular room will actually raise, in roughly the order I expect them, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it — the modeller's objection will not sound like the economist's, and the state-aid lawyer will attack a different step from either. For each: a two-to-three-minute reply, an explicit note on where that reply is soft or rests on a counterfactual I cannot observe, and what I should concede before I argue. Separately, name the objection I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the one my argument can least absorb. Finally, identify the single reply that must land for the thesis to survive the session, and tell me plainly if I do not currently have one.

Where an objection or reply turns on a quantity — French fleet availability in the year of the stress-corrosion outages, German power-sector emissions in the last years the plants ran and the years since, the gap in carbon intensity between the two systems, the order-of-magnitude cost of an extension retrofit — give me the figure you are confident in and flag the ones you are not. I do not want a bibliography or a source list; I want to know which numbers I can say out loud without being corrected from the floor.
