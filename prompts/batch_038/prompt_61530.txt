For several years I have convened a graduate methodology seminar, and the topic most resistant to progress has been the standing dispute about thought experiments — intuition pumps, in Dennett's term. The two camps are now thoroughly familiar to one another. On one side: a pump is a legitimate instrument, and sustained reflection on a case whose parameters have been deliberately varied can yield knowledge of what is possible, of what follows from what, and of where a concept ceases to apply; Galileo's two stones tied together, which generates a contradiction inside Aristotelian physics without any new observation, is the stock exhibit. On the other side: the conviction such cases produce is a psychological effect of vividness, narrative detail and strategic omission, not a record of modal insight, and the exhibits offered in defence are simply the ones whose conclusions their offerer already accepted. Both sides have said this for two decades, at increasing length, and neither has budged.

What puzzles me is a pattern that recurs in every case the seminar has taken up — Searle's Chinese room, Block's nation-sized implementation of a mind, Chalmers's zombies, Davidson's Swampman, and the Gettier cases in their successive refinements. Each exchange collapses into the same pair of moves. The sceptic says the case has not really been imagined: that one has pictured a man in a room without picturing the competence the description entails, or a billion people without picturing the organisation that would constitute a mind. The defender says it has been imagined perfectly well, and that the intuition is plain. There is no agreed procedure for settling which of them is right about the imagining; and — this is the part I find striking — the two sides' catalogues of successes and failures never intersect. Each maintains a list of pumps that misfired, each list tends to contain precisely the examples the other side counts as triumphs, and no criterion of misfiring is ever applied to one's own favourites.

My working hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that the dispute is therefore not about the epistemic standing of intuition at all, but about who bears the burden of showing that an imagined case is faithful to its description: a procedural question wearing the costume of a substantive one. A second, related thought is that part of the impasse is verbal, since 'intuition' is used on one side for a distinctive seeming with a phenomenology of its own and on the other for any judgement short of explicit argument, including ordinary counterfactual reasoning. And two influential philosophers — Williamson and Cappelen, on rather different grounds — have argued that the picture of philosophy as intuition-mongering is a misdescription of the practice, which would leave both camps arguing against a caricature.

I want a diagnosis, not a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word 'intuition', a dispute about standards of evidence, or two questions run together under one heading — a normative one about whether a pump can justify belief, and a descriptive one about what actually produces the conviction? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, and if so what would an experiment bearing on it look like? Please engage with what each side actually argues: I do not want a survey of the positions, and I do not want to be told whether thought experiments work.

Then tell me what would count as progress, given your diagnosis — a distinction, a piece of empirical work, a formal result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected and explain why. And if the reflexivity in the pattern above is real, so that each side presses its case using the very instrument under dispute, say whether that makes the impasse structural, and whether any position remains available to a participant that is dialectically neutral.
