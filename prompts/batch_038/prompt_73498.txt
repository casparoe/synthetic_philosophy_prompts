So here's where I'm at. Fourth year, dissertation on Hume's epistemology of testimony, and I've talked myself into a fairly specific reading of the miracles section that I need someone to try and break.

My position: the hard reading — but not the standard a priori version. I don't think Part I's maxim is a conceptual claim about what miracle means, and I don't think Hume is smuggling in a definition that rules miracles out by fiat. What I think is that the maxim is in-principle because of the *structure* of the evidence. A law of nature, for Hume, is established by firm and unalterable experience, and a huge chunk of that experience is itself testimony-mediated: other people's observations, records, reports we've found reliable in the past and then absorbed into our baseline. So when testimony is offered against a law, it's being weighed against a baseline it partly constitutes. There's no independent evidential standpoint from which counter-testimony could pile up until it outweighs the law, because whatever you add to the counter side is already baked into the pro side. The bootstrapping never terminates, and that — not the definition — is why the balance can never tip. Part II then isn't a grudging concession that testimony could in principle suffice; it's a survey of why the actual historical record is as bad as it is, which is perfectly consistent with the balance being structurally untippable.
