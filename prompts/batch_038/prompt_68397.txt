I'm in a reading group on dynamic inconsistency and commitment devices, and last week a colleague circulated a two-page note arguing from the absent-minded driver to a design principle for autonomous systems. The setup is the familiar one: the driver gets home by leaving the highway at the second of two indistinguishable exits, where exiting at the first pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, and driving past both pays 1; because of his absent-mindedness he cannot tell the exits apart when he reaches one and does not remember having passed the first. Ex ante, the best he can do is to continue with probability two-thirds at each exit, for an expected payoff of four-thirds — but once he is actually at an exit and reasons from where he finds himself, that same arithmetic no longer recommends the same behaviour, and the plan comes apart under his own interim reasoning. My colleague's claim is that all of this is the Odyssey in a decision-theory costume: the ex ante mixed plan is the rope and the mast, the siren's song is the seductive local calculation that will tempt the agent once he is in the fog, and the moral is that a self who cannot be trusted at the moment of action must be bound in advance by a self who can see the whole map. From this he draws a practical conclusion: systems that operate under intermittent loss of state — long-horizon agents whose memory or telemetry drops out mid-task — should have their plans fixed ex ante and should be denied any authority to revise them on the fly, since revisory authority is exactly what the ropes exist to remove, and an agent granted it ends up with the driver's payoff of one rather than four-thirds.

I'm sympathetic but uneasy, and I suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can bear. Ulysses, as I understand the story, is a case about a change in what he wants: lashed to the mast he keeps his memory, knows precisely which moment of the voyage he is in, can feel that the ropes hold, and hands execution of his plan to sailors who are not tempted at all and who are not the person making the choice. The driver's trouble, by contrast, is not that his desires shift — he wants the same four-thirds throughout, and only loses track of where in the sequence he has got to. Nor is it clear that the rope exists in his case: the planning moment is not one he will ever occupy again, and there is no crew to whom the mixed strategy can be delegated, so he must execute the plan himself at exactly those junctures where he cannot tell whether he is executing it for the first time or the second.

Could you audit the analogy for me? I would like an explicit correspondence table — what in the driver problem plays the role of Ulysses at the planning moment, the sailors, the ropes, the sirens, the hearing of the song, the loss of self-control, and the return home — with a mark wherever the driver case simply has no counterpart. Then tell me which of the disanalogies are load-bearing for my colleague's design conclusion and which are harmless decoration; what the analogy quietly imports from the Odyssey (a two-self picture of the agent, weakness of will, an executor with intact memory, a binding that can be inspected, a single episode of temptation) that the driver problem lacks; and whether the conclusion survives once those are on the table — is denying revisory authority to the in-the-moment agent really what the driver case recommends, and does it transfer to the intermittent-memory systems he has in mind? If the analogy fails where it matters, say what a better one would be, something that keeps the agent's desires fixed and destroys only his knowledge of which moment he is in, or explain why no replacement is needed. Please open with a verdict of no more than two sentences on whether the analogy can carry the conclusion, hold the correspondence table to seven rows or fewer, and keep the whole response under 700 words.
