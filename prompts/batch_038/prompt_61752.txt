I help run a fortnightly journal club that's about half physicists and half philosophers, fifteen of us, and we argue bad. We've spent the last few weeks on laws of nature and I've noticed something annoying: at least four people have said "I'm a Humean" and I'm now pretty sure they don't mean the same thing by it, while one guy who insists he's a full-blooded realist about laws keeps giving answers to concrete cases that sound Humean to me. I want to build a diagnostic quiz that sorts us by what we actually say about cases rather than by what we call ourselves, so nobody gets to self-report a label. I'd like ten questions, each a short scenario with two or three answer options, and a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the real positions — best-systems Humeanism and its pragmatist/improved variant, necessitation between universals, dispositional essentialism, the primitivist view on which laws genuinely govern and aren't reducible to anything, the natural-necessity / stability account, and the sceptical option that says there are no laws at all. No question may require knowing a label to answer it, and please don't use the words "supervene" or "ontologically prior" in the question text — half my group will just pick whichever option sounds more dignified.

Rather than ten unrelated thought experiments, I want everything to live inside one invented universe so nobody can pattern-match from a textbook case. Call it World W: a closed, finite universe of about 10^20 events containing two kinds of thing, which I'll call motes and knots, with no observers until very late in its history. Stipulations I'd like you to mine for questions:

1. Whenever a mote meets a knot it hands over exactly half of what I'll call its charge. This holds in every single event in W's whole history — call it the Halving Rule. Had W contained twice as many motes, it would still have held.
2. Every knot is the same colour as every other knot, but only because of one particular very early event that could easily have gone the other way; nothing in the Halving Rule rules out two-coloured knots.
3. There's a generalisation that holds throughout the first 10^19 events and then fails once, near the end of the history — and the failure is itself something the best simple systematisation of the total history predicts and assigns a probability to.
4. There's a regularity that is exactly true of the entire history but is so gerrymandered that writing it down takes more symbols than writing down the whole history does.
5. W's dynamics are time-reversal invariant, and its history begins in a fantastically special, low-entropy arrangement. There's also a fact near the very end of the history that seems to explain some earlier goings-on better than the initial arrangement does.
6. W contains one genuinely indeterministic process: which way a mote scatters. The actual long-run frequency is 50/50, but the best simple systematisation of the whole history assigns it 0.3.
7. There's an arrangement that never occurs in W but which an outside intervener could have produced, and if it had been produced the Halving Rule would have been violated.

The deliverable, in British English please: the ten questions with their options, then the scoring key. For each option say which position or positions a person holding it is most likely committed to, and be explicit where one answer is shared by several positions — that's the point, I want to see the overlaps. Then flag every pair of positions the quiz genuinely cannot separate, and tell me what an eleventh question would have to look like to separate them; don't paper over it if two views really do give identical answers everywhere. I'd also like you to point out which cells of the logical space come out empty — say, someone who thinks laws are necessary connections between universals but denies that laws explain their instances, or someone who's a Humean about laws but not about chance — and diagnose for each whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's just thought to defend it. One more thing: my friend maintains that a Humean can accept every counterfactual the necessitator accepts and differs only in the story about why. Put pressure on that in at least one of the ten questions, or tell me straight that it can't be done. Finally, mark which questions are doing real philosophical work and which are mostly artefacts of my stipulations, since I made those up on the bus and some of them are probably load-bearing in ways I didn't intend. It needs to be runnable in about thirty minutes. I'm not after a reading list or any citations — just the instrument.
