I cover science and ideas for a general-interest mag, and I've got a feature due next week on the "farming was a disaster" debate. The first half of the piece is just the standard case — Sahlins' original affluent society, Lee's work-week numbers from the Kalahari, the Cohen and Armelagos paleopathology volume, Diamond's 1987 Discover essay — and my editor's fine with that part, so don't spend much time there. What I need you to look at hard is the second half below, which is my own argument and which nobody will have seen before.

Read it as a hostile-but-fair fact checker. Three things specifically:

1. Anywhere I've got the anthropology wrong, or where I'm leaning on a figure that's been revised since it was first published.
2. Whether the middle move actually works — the averages-vs-totals bit. Does the conclusion follow, or am I just renaming the disagreement?
3. Which paragraph is weakest, and whether cutting it makes the piece better. I suspect it's the last one but I can't tell anymore.

DRAFT, second half:

Everyone frames this as a fight about how well the average person lived. Foragers, on the usual telling, worked maybe fifteen to twenty hours a week, ate a varied diet, and stood a few centimeters taller. Then came the cereals, and with them cavities, enamel defects, shrinking stature, epidemic disease in permanent villages, and the first serious concentrations of wealth. Farming made the typical human worse off for most of history.

My claim is that the average is the wrong statistic, and that both camps have quietly agreed to use it anyway. Agriculture did two things at once: it made the median life worse and it made the total quantity of human life enormously larger, maybe a hundredfold in a few thousand years. So the real disagreement isn't about the bones. Its about an unstated choice in population ethics. If you only care about how things go for the individuals in the story, farming looks like a catastrophe. If you add up wellbeing impersonally across everyone who ever lives, farming looks like the best thing that ever happened to us.

And that second view is the repugnant conclusion arriving ten thousand years early. A crowded planet of stunted, overworked, decayed farmers can outscore a sparse planet of well-fed foragers purely on headcount. If I'm prepared to say agriculture paid off on the total view, I have to be prepared to say the same about a future with billions of people whose lives are barely worth living. Most people who like farming are not prepared to say that — which is why they retreat to averages. But averages are the anti-farming side's home turf.

Which leaves the move I actually want to end on: the objection that there was no mistake because there was no decider. Nobody chose the Neolithic. It accumulated over generations among people who could not possibly have predicted the dentistry. Mistake implies an agent and an alternative, and there was neither. My reply is that this cuts both ways — if you can't call it a mistake, you can't call it a triumph either, and the progress story my readers assume goes with it. And the reason it felt like no choice is that it was a ratchet: once the population had been pushed up to what cultivated land could carry, going back meant starving most of those people. So the transition wasn't a decision badly made, it was a change in the feasible set that made the old option unavailable. Not welfare — lock-in.

My worry, which is why I'm asking, is that the population-ethics paragraph and the lock-in paragraph are pulling in different directions and I can't tell which one the piece is supposed to be about.
