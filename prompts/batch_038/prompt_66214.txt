I do long-horizon forecasting for a living — political stability, big infrastructure, that kind of thing — and I've realized my intuitions about which reference class to use are a total mess. Here are my honest verdicts on a bunch of cases. I want to know whether there's a principle underneath them, and if there isn't, which ones I have to give up.

1. A friend asks me, half joking, how long their marriage will last. I refuse to answer with the divorce base rate. Not (only) because it's rude — because I genuinely think that number is the wrong number for them. Ask me the same thing about an anonymous row in a dataset and I'll happily give the base rate.

2. Will this particular tunnel/bridge/rail megaproject come in over budget? I narrow hard: same country, same decade, same procurement type. "Every megaproject ever built" feels like a useless class to me here.

3. Will a foreign military intervention that started this year achieve its stated aims? I go the other direction and want the widest class I can assemble — every intervention, everywhere, every era. Any narrowing feels like it's just where the cherry-picking happens.

4. A pharma company says their phase-2 asset is unusual and shouldn't be scored against the phase-2-to-approval rate for its therapeutic area. I don't buy it. They stay in the area-level class.

5. A founder says don't put me in the all-startups class, put me in the class of startups at my funding stage, in my sector, with my founder history — and I do buy that. Thats structurally identical to 4 as far as I can tell, and I rule differently.

6. A regime that has already lasted 40 years: I condition on that. The class is "regimes that reached 40," not "all regimes." Same for a marriage already at 20 years. Same for a company with 10 clean years and no product recall — except actually no, for the recall one I want to say the 10 clean years carry no information, because there's no reason the hazard should be falling. I can't say why I flip there.

7. How long does the current great power's primacy last? I want to say "all hegemons in history," but I would never pool agrarian empires with industrial ones for anything else I forecast, and I can't articulate what's different about this one.

8. On timing: I think you're supposed to fix the reference class before you look at the case-specific details, blind, so you can't shop for a flattering class. But in case 4 the therapeutic area is only identifiable after a lot of inside-view work on the molecule. So I break my own rule there and feel fine about it.

9. A class of 3 that matches tightly beats a class of 400 that matches loosely. I also refuse to run a forecast on any class with fewer than about 15 members. I say both of these out loud, in the same meetings.

The closest thing I have to a principle is: narrow when the narrowing is forced by information you already had before examining the case, widen whenever narrowing amounts to saying this case is special. Please test that against all nine and repair it if it's salvageable, because it clearly doesn't handle 6, 8 or 9.

What I want back: one account of correct reference-class selection, stated as an actual decision rule rather than vibes, with the clause that does the work in each case flagged; an honest list of which of my verdicts it cannot save and why; and its verdicts on two cases I havent thought about — (a) whether a specific person is alive in 30 years, where the tight class is "people with their exact condition and history" and the loose one is "everyone their age and sex," and (b) whether a brand-new technology with no precedent gets widely adopted within 20 years, where every candidate class is picked by analogy and the analogy is precisely what's in dispute.

Bottom line in the first sentence — does such a principle exist or not — then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my cases back at me.
