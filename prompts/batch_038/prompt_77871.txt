I work in the history of ideas and have never found a term for a move that recurs constantly in writing about peoples. Someone attributes a national trait — thrift, reserve, pride — and when you produce behaviour that cuts the other way the trait simply absorbs it: the same word now covers the opposite conduct, and the writer registers no adjustment, because the word was never really tracking conduct. This is not ordinary vagueness, and it is not the stock objection that stereotypes are unfalsifiable, since the same writer cites evidence readily enough whenever the behaviour falls on the side they like. It looks more like a term with a fixed charge and a floating extension: which deeds count as instances is settled by whether the writer wants to approve or disapprove of the people concerned, and it is the valence, not the description, that stays put. Hence one adjective serves as praise in one chapter and condemnation in the next over conduct that has not changed — saving and deferring to one's elders is civic-mindedness in one people and timidity in another. Is there an established concept for this? Essentially contested concepts and thick ethical concepts both come close, and neither quite does it. If nothing fits, could you define a term sharply enough to exclude ordinary vagueness, Barnum-style generality and a plain double standard, and say where its boundary cases lie?
