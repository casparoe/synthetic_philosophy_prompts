Longtime LW/SSC lurker-turned-arguer here — been reading this stuff since the Overcoming Bias days, and I have slowly mutated into the guy who argues against his own tribe in public. Next Thursday I'm doing a 25-minute talk at a big-city rationalist meetup's philosophy night. About 40 people: mostly EA-adjacent software folks and a handful of grad students, three of whom do actual decision theory or formal epistemology, two psychologists who study charitable giving empirically, and zero Williams scholars. Most have read the Sequences and something by MacAskill, a couple did a phil undergrad and have hazy memories of Jim and the Indians. The room's native moral vocabulary is expected value, scope insensitivity, moral uncertainty, and shut-up-and-multiply.

The thesis: I want to argue that Williams's integrity objection — specifically the one-thought-too-many move in the wife-in-the-fire case — is not the intuition pump this audience assumes it is. My version, in five steps:

1. The standard rationalist read is that Williams is reporting a squeamish intuition about the output of a case and dressing it in phenomenology, and that the intuition evaporates once the numbers get big. I grant that reading is correct about how Williams gets *used*.
2. But the real target isn't the output, it's the architecture. Maximizing agency requires that every commitment you hold be held conditionally — at every decision point the calculus can call it in. I'm calling this the readiness condition, and its cost the readiness tax.
3. A specific class of goods is partly *constituted* by the absence of that standing readiness: trust that survives bad news, being the person someone calls at 3am, love that isn't a revocable lease. Not because the agent is ignorant or irrational, but because the other party's response is keyed to a disposition that has to be real, i.e. not up for recalculation.
4. So the readiness tax can't be entered as a line item, because registering it requires the stance that destroys the good. It's a standpoint-relative blind spot, not a weighing error.
5. Hence the debate isn't intuitions vs. numbers. It's whether global E-maximizing is a dynamically stable thing for a creature like us to be — and whether going indirect repairs it. I claim it doesn't, because adopting indirect consequentialism is itself one more act of the same readiness.

I also run a case I made up for the talk: a pill that permanently deletes your standing readiness to reconsider your central commitments at the next decision point. You'd be a much better friend and a much worse responder to large distant numbers. Take it or not? I predict most of the room says no, and I want to be ready for what that "no" costs me.

To be clear about what I'm not claiming: I'm not positing agent-relative reasons as fundamental, not defending deontological constraints, and not saying numbers never win. Someone will absolutely try to force me into "so you're a deontologist in denial" and I need a clean answer.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense. The eight-ish objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they do to the argument above (not by how fun they are), each phrased the way *they'd* phrase it, using their vocabulary, so I recognize it when it lands. Then a ~90 second reply for each, an honest flag on where the reply is soft, and a mark of which ones have genuine force versus which are a motte-and-bailey I can swat in a sentence. Also: two concessions I should make in the first five minutes to defuse the dumbest version of the fight, and the one question I'm praying doesn't come — I have a guess, something about a person with genuinely awful projects getting the same deliberational protection, but tell me if there's a worse one hiding.

And no reading list, no citations, no references — I want stuff I can carry in my head backstage, and I don't want you guessing at page numbers.
