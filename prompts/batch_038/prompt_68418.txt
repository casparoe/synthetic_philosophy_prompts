**Context.** I am grading the problem sets for an upper-level seminar on value pluralism and political bargaining. Below are the question as it was set and one student's response. I would like a rigorous assessment of the response on its philosophical merits. There is no need to supply references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want an evaluation of the reasoning.

**Question as set**

Consider two agents — they might be parties, advocacy organizations, or ministries — whose final values conflict: the first ranks liberty above all, the second equality. Neither misunderstands the other's commitments, and neither is willing to revise or abandon its own. Yet the two may enter a standing arrangement of mutual concessions, in which each sometimes lends its support to measures that its own values rank poorly, in the expectation that the other will do likewise elsewhere.

(a) Explain how such an arrangement can serve each agent's own values without either agent changing what it cares about. What feature of the two value systems, and of the set of decisions before them, generates the gains?

(b) In a competitive exchange economy a voluntary trade is a Pareto improvement: both parties are better off, and, on welfarist assumptions, the resulting allocation is better in a single shared sense. Do the gains from mutual moral concession have this structure? Explain how they resemble, and how they differ in kind from, the gains from market exchange.

(c) State one condition under which the arrangement fails to yield gains for at least one of the agents, and explain why it fails in that case.

**Student's response**

(a) What makes the arrangement possible is that the two value systems, although opposed at the level of final ends, are not equally engaged by every question that comes before them. Some issues bear heavily on liberty and only marginally on equality; others bear heavily on equality and only marginally on liberty. Each party can therefore concede on the issues where its own end is barely at stake in return for support on the issues where its own end is deeply at stake, and over a sequence of decisions both end up with more of what they most want than either would have secured through permanent stalemate. The logic is that of vote-trading, transposed from bargaining over interests to bargaining over values. Neither party converts, dilutes its commitments, or comes to see anything worthwhile in the other's ends: the gain is generated by the structure of the issue-set rather than by any change in what either cares about.

A further condition is nevertheless required before the arrangement can be rational for either side. Concessions are made one at a time, and on any given occasion what A gives up produces nothing that A itself values: the benefit of A's support for B's measure falls entirely within B's value system. A can of course expect support in return, but that expectation can motivate A only if A is able to register the exchange as a gain for itself, and for that A must be capable of counting B's getting what B wants as a good of some kind. So each party must assign at least a small weight to the other's ends — a thin layer of overlapping concern — and it is this layer, rather than the mere geometry of the issues, that ultimately makes moral compromise pay.

(b) The gains do not have the structure of a Pareto improvement in an exchange economy. In the market case welfarism supplies a single standard, individual well-being, in terms of which both parties' gains are measured, so the trade is not merely better for each of them but better in one common sense. Here no such standard exists. A's gain is denominated in liberty and B's in equality, and there is no vantage point from which the two can be summed, weighted, or traded off against one another. Each party's improvement is an improvement only relative to its own ordering, so the package is Pareto-superior merely in the deflationary sense that neither party ranks it below the stalemate. There is also a structural difference in what is being exchanged: market trade transfers separable private goods, which the buyer consumes and the seller no longer has, whereas what these parties trade are positions on a shared public outcome to which both remain subject and of which neither becomes the owner.

(c) The arrangement fails when the issue-set lacks complementarity of the required kind. If liberty and equality are engaged in the same direction on every question that arises — if each issue that engages A's end heavily also engages B's end heavily, and each issue that engages A's end marginally also engages B's end marginally — then there is no issue on which either party can cheaply purchase the other's support, and the stalemate returns. A related failure obtains if the interaction is genuinely one-shot, since the concession is made now and the return promised later, and once the final decision has been taken no party can be held to its side of the bargain.

In structure, what I have described is Rawls's overlapping consensus: parties holding incompatible comprehensive doctrines converge on the same set of political arrangements, each endorsing them from within its own outlook.
