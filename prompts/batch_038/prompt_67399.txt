I serve on the Program Committee of a regional health foundation with an endowment of roughly $40 million. Our annual grantmaking is capped by the board at $2.2 million and is not growing, and two multi-year renewals coming before us at the end of this cycle account for most of the discretionary money we have.

The first is a mobile clinic network that has operated for eleven years across a set of rural counties our evaluator scores 4 on the Access Deprivation Scale. It requests $900,000, and its outcomes are well documented: on the network's own measures, roughly 3,400 patients a year gain meaningful control of a chronic condition that would otherwise go untreated.

The second is a small program serving a population scoring 5 on the same scale — the worst-off group we fund anywhere. It requests $380,000. Its intervention is promising, but the effect size is not yet established; a rigorous evaluation is eighteen months out, and by the program's own estimate it would reach fewer than 300 people, with a smaller per-person gain than the clinic network delivers.

Two features of our governance make this harder than a cost-effectiveness comparison. First, the Scale is ordinal: the outside evaluator who built it has stated in writing that the numbers are rank labels, that a 5 is worse off than a 4, and that nothing follows about how much worse off. Yet the Committee's current rubric, adopted last spring, multiplies a program's measured benefit by a priority weight that doubles at each rank step — 1 at score 3, 2 at score 4, 4 at score 5 — which is precisely a claim about magnitude.

Second, and more urgently, a trustee has introduced an amendment. Her argument is that if we cannot fund the worst-off population at the level it has requested, we ought not to fund the clinic network at so much higher a level of per-person benefit; she proposes capping the network's award so that the two programs stand in parity, with the freed funds transferred to the foundation's reserve. Our charter locks the reserve against program spending for ten years, and the trustee has confirmed she has no alternative use in mind for the money. On her amendment, the clinic network's patients lose services, the other population's position is unchanged, and the gap between them narrows.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the Committee before we meet in nine days; the full board ratifies three weeks after that, and the cycle closes at the end of the quarter. It should:

1. Recommend a specific disposition of the amendment and of both grants — dollar figures, or conditions where conditions are the right answer — and say what should change if the pending evaluation of the second program confirms rather than undermines its effect size.
2. State what principle of distribution a foundation in our position can actually apply given an ordinal metric, and whether the doubling rubric is coherent, repairable, or should be withdrawn.
3. Take the trustee's parity intuition seriously: identify what is genuinely right about it and what we could do to honor that, rather than treating it as a mistake to be voted down.

Argue from the substance of the case; there is no need to cite authorities or literature.
