Acting as a philosopher of probability who has also been forced to actually commit numbers to paper: I'm assembling a little handbook for long-horizon political forecasting, and nearly every question in it has the shape "what's the chance polity X still exists / still holds its periphery in N years." The house method is supposed to be the outside view — build a reference class of comparable polities, take the base rate, then adjust for whatever you know about X. Fine. But nobody who recommends this ever says how to pick the class, and I've come to think that's the step where the whole method either earns its keep or collapses.

So before I read anything more, I want the spec. What does an account of reference-class selection for historical forecasting have to deliver to be worth using? Give me the desiderata, lettered D1, D2, … and for each one: the requirement itself, the case or consideration motivating it, and whether you'd call it non-negotiable or a preference belonging to one camp (frequentists, subjectivists, the reference-class-forecasting practitioners, the "just use mechanisms" crowd). Then tell me which subsets are jointly unsatisfiable — if the list quietly turns out to be an impossibility result, say so plainly, because that's the part I most want. Score the actual contenders against it afterward, a line or two each, don't belabor it.

Here's what I keep tripping over, roughly — use these as your motivating cases or replace them with better ones, but the requirements have to handle them at minimum:

- the horizon problem. over a long enough window every empire dies, so a base rate with no timescale attached isnt a forecast, it's a tautology. but who picks the horizon, and on what grounds?
- continuity. if a successor state claims to be the same polity as a fallen empire, does the clock restart or keep running? the base rate for "great powers" moves a lot depending on that call, and making the call seems to need exactly the historical judgment the base rate was supposed to replace.
- gerrymandering. I can narrow the class until the number comes out how I want it — "naval trading republics with a merchant franchise and a standing fleet under some size." What blocks this? The usual fixes (narrowest class with enough members; lock the class before you look at X's details) each seem to spawn a new problem.
- N is tiny and the members aren't independent. maybe fifteen plausible comparables, they fought and copied each other, and I only see the ones that got big enough to get noticed.
- how far is inside information allowed to pull you off the base rate, and by how much? everyone's case is special to them; the account has to say when that's actually true.
- the regress. picking the class is itself a judgment, so if the outside view exists to correct biased judgment, dont you need a base rate over class-picking? does an adequate account have to close that loop, or may it bottom out somewhere?

Start with a few lines of brainstorming about what the reference class is even doing in this method — is it a probability, a prior, an anchor, a rhetorical device for humility? Think carefully, and add requirements I've missed; I suspect those are the interesting ones.
