ok, this is for a novel im working on and i need a chapter that's nothing but a found document, so i want you to write the document.

The document is an advice column in a free weekly in a mid-sized English town. The columnist is a woman in her late sixties who took it over eighteen months ago when the previous columnist died — he was a retired Methodist minister, plain-spoken, kind, actually answered things — and nobody at the paper has yet realised that she is not a minister and does not intend to answer anything. She taught philosophy of religion for thirty years and her head is entirely full of the later Wittgenstein on religion: the lectures he gave in Cambridge around 1938, the Phillips-style reading of them, and the sixties charge (Nielsen, you dont need the name in the piece, just know the objection) that this reading leaves religious belief unintelligible.

What i want: three letters and three replies, plus the editor's two-line footer at the bottom. 700-750 words total. The letters should be real small-town letters — a woman asking whether her brother's atheism means he cant be godfather to her son; a man who has stopped believing since his wife died and wants to know whether he has lost something or found something out; a sixteen-year-old girl asking, quite politely, whether there is any evidence for any of it. Warm, badly punctuated, not stupid.

The replies have to be funny because they're right, not because they're mean. Run the actual moves: a religious belief isn't a hypothesis about the future, so an ordinary doubt doesn't contradict it; contradiction here doesn't do the work contradiction normally does; the belief is a picture that regulates the whole of a life rather than a claim waiting on evidence; you can't be argued into it, but if you had it, it would show; the gulf between believer and sceptic isn't a difference of opinion about a matter of fact. She never names a philosopher or a book, and she never once uses words like meaning, language-game or form of life as terms of art. The joke is that she has internalised all of it and it comes out as advice about casseroles and about whether to keep going to the church fete.

Then the trap. The girl's letter is the one where a straight answer is available and would be kind, and the columnist declines to give it — the reader should feel the cost of that refusal without anyone mentioning it. And in the first two letters there should be one question with nothing to do with religion at all, something checkable — will the column run next week, is the parish hall free on the 14th — and she should dissolve that one in exactly the same way, so a reader can see this is a philosophy she is doing and not a neutral description of what other people mean.

Finally the footer. The editor has been appending one plain helpful sentence to each column since month two. By the third week her sentence has become indistinguishable from the columnist's reply, or possibly better than it, or possibly revealed as the same move wearing a cardigan — your call, but it has to be two lines and it has to be the funniest thing in the piece.

The target is the style of discourse, not belief and not believers. I want a Wittgensteinian to read it and laugh and feel fairly treated, and i want the standard objections — that a belief nothing could contradict isn't a belief, that this is non-cognitivism in a nice coat, that the philosopher who dissolves the question has definitly taken a side — to be felt in the structure rather than stated anywhere. Nobody in the piece ever says the word fideism.

And please no quoting Wittgenstein. If you put his words in her mouth you'll get them wrong and so will i. Paraphrase, or invent something of her own that sounds like him.
