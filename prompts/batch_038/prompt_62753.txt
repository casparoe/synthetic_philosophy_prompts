I am a doctoral student in philosophy of science, and I have been asked to open our department's reading group on scientific realism with a piece of satire that will amuse people who actually work on the pessimistic meta-induction rather than a general philosophical audience. Please write a mock peer-review report on a manuscript whose thesis is nothing more than the pessimistic meta-induction itself, submitted to an ordinary philosophy journal as though it were a routine empirical study: past theories that were strikingly successful in prediction and application have overwhelmingly turned out to be false, therefore today's successful theories are probably false too.

Use the standard apparatus — summary of the submission, recommendation, numbered major points, a brief list of minor points, and a closing declaration of interests — and keep the register impeccably courteous and administrative throughout. The humour should come entirely from the reviewer behaving like a perfectly normal referee: requesting the dataset and the coding criteria, asking how the reference class of past theories was selected and whether it is representative, wondering whether a theory that survives in corrected form within its successor counts as a default or as a partial repayment, noting the manuscript's silence on the obvious rival explanation of predictive success, and flagging as a possible route to revision the more recent worry that the historical record concerns well-confirmed alternatives nobody at the time conceived of. Familiar cases such as caloric or the luminiferous ether may be mentioned; do not name any real philosopher, paper, or journal, and include no bibliography or reading list. The reviewer should be a committed scientific realist, scrupulously fair and generous where praise is due, and should never once notice that anything unusual is happening — a reader should be able to tell both that the objections are real ones and that the reviewer is anything but neutral, without the piece ever winking. Around 600 words, in British English.
