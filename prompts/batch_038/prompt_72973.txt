Spent about three hours last night arguing with a friend who does defense-side antitrust work, and I still don't have an answer for him. I'd been saying that some of the explanations we write at work are genuinely about reasons that nobody has — a system did X because X would have scored better on the thing it was being optimized against, and that "because" is doing real work even though no meeting, no memo, and no single head contains it. Call it a free-floating rationale, or reasons without reasoners, whatever you want to call it. He says that's just a category error with a nice name: there is always a person somewhere who picked the metric, picked the features, picked the deadline, or picked not to look, and if you can't find them you haven't looked hard enough. He says "the system learned to do it" is the most expensive sentence in the English language, and that everyone who writes it is either confused or hiding. I think he's wrong at least some of the time, but I couldn't show him where, and honestly I'm not sure I could show my own committee either.

Here's why it matters for me. I run a two-person internal review function at a mid-size online marketplace — me (ex-risk analyst, no philosophy training) and one engineer who's borrowed half-time — and we do a written post-mortem roughly once a month whenever one of our automated systems produces an outcome that generated complaints, or that a regulator might plausibly ask about. We get four to six hours on a case, we interview maybe six people, and the deliverable goes to a three-person risk committee that signs off on it. These write-ups are discoverable, so wording matters. We keep falling into exactly two failure modes, sometimes in the same document: one is scapegoating whichever junior engineer happened to be holding the pen when the thing went out, and the other is writing a version of "nobody chose this, the model converged on it" that is technically defensible, costs the company nothing, and lets a whole series of actual decisions vanish. I want the second one to stop being free.

So: write me a procedure I can actually run on a case, not an essay about the topic. I want numbered questions in the order I should ask them, what counts as an adequate answer to each one, and what evidence would let me move on. It needs to help me sort out which kind of no-reasoner story is actually in play, because I suspect there are several and they don't have the same moral or legal status — something being selected among variants by an optimizer seems different from slow accumulation over years where each step was unremarkable, which seems different from an aggregate of hundreds of individually reasonable small choices, which seems different from a gap somebody deliberately declined to fill. Include a test I can apply to a sentence that has a reason but no subject, to tell whether it's a legitimate explanation or an alibi. Mark clearly where the procedure hands the call to a human because it's a values question rather than a factual one — naming an individual, how much residual risk to leave on the table, whether to say the word intent anywhere — and be honest about which of your steps are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: a recommender that quietly shifted traffic toward a small set of very high-frequency sellers over about nine months; an eligibility model that turned out to lean heavily on a feature strongly correlated with a protected class, where the analyst who added it knew the correlation and said so in a review comment; and a repricing tool that drifted into near-identical prices with our main competitor, where a regulator has asked whether there was an agreement and where "nobody decided it but there's a rationale both sides' systems converged on" might be true and is also possibly the single most dangerous paragraph we could put in writing. At least one of these should come out against me — I want to see the procedure say the free-floating story fails and that somebody's reasons are doing the work. Finish with what it definately can't settle, and how I would notice if my team started using the procedure as cover rather than as analysis.
