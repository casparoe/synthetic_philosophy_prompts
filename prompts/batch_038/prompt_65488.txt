A friend of mine has an argument that the statistical-mechanical account of time's arrow eats itself, and I can't find the exit. Roughly this:

(1) The microscopic laws are time-reversal invariant, to a very good approximation.
(2) So nothing in the dynamics picks out a direction; the whole asymmetry has to come from a boundary condition — call it the Past Hypothesis, that the early universe sat in a wildly low-entropy macrostate.
(3) Now take any time t and ask which history is most probable given only that the macrostate at t is what it is. Because of (1) the answer is symmetric: a low-entropy moment is overwhelmingly likely to be a local dip, with entropy higher on both sides of it.
(4) And by (2) that's all there is to go on — the Past Hypothesis says that some moment is low-entropy, it doesn't say which moments count as early. So (3) applies to now, and entropy was higher the further back you go.
(5) Which makes the things we call evidence about the past — memories, fossils, tree rings, the CMB — spontaneous orderings rather than records, on exactly the same footing as the fake "records" of a future nobody's had.
(6) But the Past Hypothesis is itself believed only on the strength of those traces.
(7) So the account destroys the evidence for its own central postulate.

He says the obvious repair — conditionalize on the present macrostate and run the dynamics back — is circular, because running it back already assumes there's a back to run to. I'd much rather kill (3) or (4) than (1) or (2), but I can't see how to do it without smuggling in the direction I'm supposed to be deriving. Which step actually breaks, why did it look fine, and what does the repair cost?

And if you think I've set this up wrong — if one of those steps isn't a real step, or if this is a well-worn objection with a well-worn answer and my friend is just dressing it up — say so plainly rather than humouring me.
