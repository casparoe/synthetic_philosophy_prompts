I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about futarchy, and he kept returning to the same analogy: a prediction market is simply an opinion poll in which respondents stake money on their answers. The sampling frame becomes whoever chooses to trade, the stated opinion becomes a wager, the tally becomes the clearing price, and polling error becomes the profit awaiting anyone sharp enough to correct it. He uses this to insist that every objection to futarchy is secretly an objection to polling — that markets cannot be "manipulated" any more than a poll can be biased by respondents lying, that a thin market is just a small sample, and that a wrong price is a wrong estimate that gets competed away. Since, he says, we already govern on poll-grade information about economic effects, moving the same questions to bettors is a strict upgrade rather than a novel risk, and the case for futarchy inherits the case against polling sceptics.

Please audit the analogy. Give me the correspondences explicitly, then separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten his conclusion from the ones that are merely picturesque, and say whether the claim that futarchy is better polling survives once they are on the table. I want particular attention to the places where the market is not measuring the thing at all — where the price helps bring about what it predicts, where the payoff depends on how the question is resolved and who resolves it, and where only the confident and the solvent get to answer. If the analogy must be replaced rather than repaired, tell me what should replace it.
