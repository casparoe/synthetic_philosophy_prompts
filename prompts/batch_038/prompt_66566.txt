I'm two years into a PhD in ancient history — my thesis is on honorific decrees and elite self-presentation in the Greek East under the empire, so Plutarch keeps getting waved at me by my supervisor as the thing I ought to be reading him against. I've also been co-teaching a survey that runs out into late antique saints' cults, which is not my period at all. Last week our reading group took up a recently published trade-ish book on the history of life-writing, and the introductory chapter makes an argument that I found persuasive on the first pass and slightly wrong on the second, but I can't tell which of my two reactions is the ignorant one. Here's the chapter's argument, condensed and put in my own words but with nothing added or dropped:

"Plutarch is where the West gets the idea that a life is a moral instrument rather than a record of events. He tells his reader at the outset of the Alexander that what he is offering is a life, not a history, and that what actually discloses a man's character is rarely large — a remark, a joke, how someone conducts himself when nobody consequential is watching — whereas battles with tens of thousands dead tell you almost nothing about a soul. Hagiography is the same genre re-pointed: instead of an exemplar for civic life you get an exemplar for religious life, but the machinery is identical, so select the edifying episodes, compress the chronology, suppress what doesn't serve the portrait, and you have either a Plutarchan life or a saint's life. Which is why both are equally useless as evidence about their subjects and equally good as evidence about their audiences: they tell you what the people reading them valued, not who the person was. The one genuine formal difference runs in the opposite direction from the usual assumption — Plutarch at least paired a Greek with a Roman and then set the two side by side in a comparison, so he had a built-in control on how exceptional his subject was. Hagiographers had no comparative apparatus whatsoever, and therefore no way of knowing whether their saint was unusually holy or merely conventionally pious."

Can you go through this claim by claim and tell me what's solid, what's a defensible simplification for a general reader, and what a specialist would push back on? Specifically I'd like: (1) whether the "life, not history" business supports the inference the chapter draws from it, given how much campaign narrative Plutarch actually includes; (2) whether "invented" is fair in the first sentence, or whether there's a prior tradition of ethically-shaped life-writing he's working inside; (3) whether "same genre, same machinery" holds up for hagiography — I have a vague sense the saint's life is doing something structurally different, not just swapping the domain of virtue, and I'd like to know what that difference actually is (who the agent of the excellence is, what the reader is supposed to do, what the text is for in a cult); and (4) whether that last formal claim about comparative apparatus is right or whether hagiography has its own ways of calibrating degrees of holiness. Then tell me whether the two conclusions follow from the corrected premises: that both are equally useless as sources for their subjects, and that Plutarch's self-limitation gives him an evidential advantage the hagiographer lacks.

My supervisor wants me to cite Plutarch for how provincial elites wanted to be seen; the chapter would have me say Plutarch is only evidence for Plutarch's audience and nothing more. Those feel like they can't both be wrong, but I also can't see how to hold them together — is that a real tension or am I just confusing two different questions?
