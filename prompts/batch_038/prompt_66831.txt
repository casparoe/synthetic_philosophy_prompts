A chapter of a trade book about trust summarizes the epistemology of testimony as follows:

"Philosophers divide into reductionists and anti-reductionists about testimony. The reductionist line, which goes back to Hume, holds that a hearer's belief is justified only if she has evidence, independent of testimony itself, that this particular speaker is telling the truth on this particular occasion; since collecting such evidence usually means asking other people, the view implies that we can never rationally trust a stranger. The anti-reductionist line, which goes back to Reid, holds that belief in testimony is justified a priori, meaning we are entitled to it independently of all experience: nature installs in us a principle of veracity licensing us to accept what we are told, and the license is unconditional, since any attempt to check it would itself have to run through testimony. Both sides grant that testimony yields knowledge only when it is reliable; what they disagree about is how reliable it actually is. Reid's side looks better placed, because nobody in fact checks, and young children believe whatever they are told."

I am inclined to accept the ending, since I do mostly believe what people tell me without investigating, but I grew uneasy at the sentence claiming that both sides grant everything turns on reliability. Please go through the passage claim by claim: what would a specialist in social epistemology accept, what would they regard as a simplification that changes the position being described, and what would they reject outright? I would especially like to know whether the reductionist really demands case-by-case evidence about the speaker at hand, what the technical label "a priori" is doing in the anti-reductionist's mouth as opposed to its everyday sense, and whether the appeal to children and unreflective adults supports the conclusion the chapter draws from it. Finally, once the passage is corrected, does anything substantive remain of the case for anti-reductionism, or does the correction leave the two sides arguing only about how often testimony happens to be true?
