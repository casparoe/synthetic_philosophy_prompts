Question 3 (25 points). Consider the following case, a version of Parfit's hitchhiker example, which we discussed in lecture. You are stranded on the shoulder of a road through an uninhabited desert, with no water, no phone, and no prospect of another car for days. A driver stops. He says he will take you into town, sixty miles away, only if you promise to pay him one hundred dollars once you arrive. He adds three things: that he is an unusually reliable judge of whether a promise is sincere; that he has no way of enforcing the promise afterwards, since he is a stranger who will never see you again and there is no one to appeal to; and that if he judges your promise insincere he will simply drive on. Assume that once you are in town, handing over the hundred dollars costs you and benefits no one, that you would rather lose the money than die in the desert, and that the case is one-shot — there is no reputation to build and no further interactions with anyone who knows about the promise.

(a) [10 points] State what evidential decision theory recommends at the roadside and what it recommends once you are in town, and state what causal decision theory recommends at each of those two moments. Explain the reasoning that generates each recommendation, and explain why the case is taken to be a problem for causal decision theory rather than for evidential decision theory.

(b) [8 points] Explain how the case is meant to support the thesis of rational irrationality. Be explicit about what is claimed to be rational, what is claimed to be irrational, and why the two are supposed to come apart.

(c) [7 points] Compare the case to Newcomb's problem. Is the disagreement between the two theories here the same disagreement as the one in Newcomb's problem? Explain, and say what turns on your answer.

Student's answer:

(a) The two theories agree on the facts of the case and disagree about what to do with them. At the roadside, evidential decision theory conditionalizes on each available act. If you make a promise the driver accepts, you end up in town; if you refuse, or make the kind of promise he can see through, he drives off and you stay where you are. Since arriving in town and then losing a hundred dollars is vastly better than dying of exposure, promising has much the better evidential expected utility. The theory also gives a recommendation for later, and this is the part people find strange. Once you are standing in the town square, paying the hundred dollars is strong evidence that you were one of the people the driver brought in, while refusing to pay is evidence that you were not; so paying still has the better evidential expected utility, even though you already know perfectly well that you are in town. On this way of reasoning you pay in order to be someone who got a ride. Causal decision theory instead asks which act causally brings about the better outcome. In town, the ride is finished: paying causes the driver no benefit and you no protection, since nothing enforces the promise and you will never see him again, so paying causes a loss of a hundred dollars and nothing else, and the theory says not to pay. At the roadside it says the same thing prospectively. You will not pay later, so any promise you make now is a promise you will break; and since the driver is an excellent judge of sincerity, he detects exactly that. A causal decision theorist therefore cannot produce a promise this driver will accept, and is left in the desert. That is the sense in which the case counts against the theory: its recommendations are self-defeating, and an evidential agent placed in the same situation comes out far ahead. The parallel case we read alongside this one is Kavka's toxin puzzle, where the reward attaches to the intention rather than to any act the agent goes on to perform, and where the reward is handed over before the act falls due.

(b) The general idea Parfit is pressing is that rationality does not require every act of yours to be the maximizing act. It can require you to be, or to become, the kind of agent whose acts are not each maximizing. In this case the act of handing over the hundred dollars is irrational in the act-by-act sense, since it buys nothing at all, but having a motive that produces that act is rational, because agents with such a motive get rides and agents without one die in the desert. He calls this rational irrationality, and it is the same point he makes about belief in Reasons and Persons, where he argues that it can be rational to cause yourself to hold a belief your evidence does not support because holding it makes things go better for you. What the driver detects is precisely a belief of that kind. The hitchhiker has to believe that he will pay, and because he knows that once he is in town he will have no reason at all to pay, that is a belief he cannot support on the evidence available to him. So the case supports the thesis by showing that an agent does better by managing his own beliefs than by reasoning correctly at each step of the decision.

(c) Structurally this is Newcomb's problem with the boxes replaced by a ride. The driver is the predictor, your promise is your choice of boxes, and his belief about your sincerity plays the part played by the contents of the opaque box. As in Newcomb's problem, the payoff-relevant belief is fixed before the act that is supposed to bear on it: the driver has already made up his mind by the time you are deciding about the hundred dollars in town. So the disagreement between the theories here is the same disagreement, and the defender of causal decision theory has the reply Lewis gives in Newcomb's problem. The evidential recommendation looks attractive only if you allow backtracking conditionals, on which your paying now would mean that the driver formed a different belief earlier; once you restrict yourself to non-backtracking conditionals, paying has no causal effect on anything and the appearance of rationality evaporates. On this reading what is really at stake in the hitchhiker case is not whether causal decision theory is self-defeating but which semantics for counterfactuals we should accept, and a defender of the theory who is content with Lewis's account has nothing to fear from Parfit's example.
