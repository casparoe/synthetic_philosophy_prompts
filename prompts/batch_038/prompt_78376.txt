**Licensed Intoxication: Wine as an Audit Technology in the *Laws***

**Abstract.** The wine legislation of the *Laws* is normally read as a programme of habituation: the city permits controlled drinking so that citizens will practise resisting pleasure and thereby acquire the capacity to resist it. This paper argues that the primary function of the supervised symposium in the first two books is not training but testing. On the reading defended here, the Athenian Stranger treats intoxication the way a regulator treats a controlled trial: as a means of generating information about character that ordinary conditions cannot supply, at low cost and without irreversible harm. The paper sets out the distinction between inoculative training and diagnostic exposure, collects the institutional evidence for assigning the symposium to the second category, answers the habituation objection, and draws out what the diagnostic reading implies about Plato's view of the visibility of virtue.

**1.** The opening book of the *Laws* turns on a complaint about Spartan and Cretan education. Those regimes, the Athenian argues, organise everything toward courage, but courage of a lopsided kind: they train citizens to stand firm against pain and fear while leaving them untrained against pleasure. His illustration is of a fighter permitted to use only one hand, who is then expected to compete against an opponent who has both. A training that covers only half of the motivational field produces a citizen who is, in effect, half-formed, and the deficiency is invisible in peacetime.

**2.** To make the deficiency vivid the Athenian runs a thought experiment. Suppose there existed a drug that induced fear. One could administer it in graded doses to groups of citizens, under supervision, and simply observe who remained steady and who did not. The city would learn who was courageous without fighting a battle, without risking anyone's life, and without the moral cost of exposing men to genuine danger for the sake of finding out about them. The information would be cheap, safe, and immediately useful for the assignment of offices and honours. No such drug exists. Wine, however, is a drug with the contrary effect: it produces confidence and strips away shame. The Athenian proposes that the city exploit this fact in the same spirit, running the experiment in the opposite direction.

**3.** My central claim is that the point of this passage is informational rather than developmental. What the thought experiment establishes is that character can be known cheaply and safely under artificially induced stress; what it recommends for wine is that the city acquire the same knowledge by the same means. Note what the passage does not say. It does not say that drinking the fear-inducing drug would make the subject braver. It says the city would *find out* who was brave. If we carry that structure across to wine, as the Athenian explicitly invites us to do, the conclusion is that supervised drinking yields knowledge of self-command, not self-command itself.

**4.** The institutional machinery in the second book supports this. Wine is forbidden to the young altogether; permitted in moderation, without drunkenness, to men below a certain age; and permitted to intoxication only to those past forty, and then only at gatherings presided over by a sober senior official appointed for the purpose. Read as pedagogy, these rules look like a graduated curriculum with an unusually late completion date. Read as a testing regime, they look like something else entirely: an eligibility threshold, a dose ceiling, an independent monitor who is not himself under test, and a public setting attached to the city's choral performances so that the results are witnessed. On this reading the presiding officer is not a host but an examiner, and the symposium is not a school but an inspection.

**5.** The structural parallel I have in mind is the modern licensing regime: a legally carved-out exception zone in which a risky substance may be consumed only under supervision, within limits, by those who qualify, with an official present whose job is to watch rather than to participate. I want to be careful here, because the parallel may mislead. A licensing regime is normally justified by harm reduction, and nothing in the *Laws* suggests the Athenian is worried about the health of drinkers. If the analogy is to be kept at all, it should be kept as a way of naming the structural features in paragraph 4, and explicitly disclaimed as a claim about rationale.

**6.** The second book also treats wine as a remedy given by Dionysus against the sourness of old age, and connects this to a specifically civic problem: the elderly are ashamed to perform in the choruses, and so withdraw from the shared musical life of the city. The remedy is therefore targeted at a failure of participation. My suggestion is that the symposium does double duty at this stage: it reveals which of the older citizens have withdrawn, and it supplies the condition under which they can be brought back into performance. Diagnosis and repair are two phases of one institution, and the repair depends on the diagnosis having been made.

**7.** The obvious objection is that the text also says, in so many words, that practising resistance to pleasure makes one better able to resist it, so that the habituation reading is not merely compatible with the passages I have used but explicitly asserted by them. My reply is that repeated successful testing produces a second-order state: the tested citizen comes to know that he has held firm under a condition designed to make him fail. That is a cognitive acquisition, and it is not the same thing as the conditioning of the appetitive part of the soul that habituation is usually taken to involve. I therefore do not deny that the symposium has training effects; I deny that training is its primary function in the argument of the *Laws*.

**8.** The upshot is a claim about the visibility of virtue. On the diagnostic reading, Plato holds that character is not reliably legible under ordinary conditions, and that a city which cannot induce controlled stress cannot allocate office on the basis of knowledge rather than reputation. This brings him close to the contemporary situationist claim that behaviour tracks circumstances more than stable dispositions, though the difference matters: the situationist wants circumstances to *replace* dispositions in our explanations, whereas the Athenian wants circumstances to be *used* to reveal them.

**9.** If this is right, the wine legislation is the most permissive feature of a document with a deservedly austere reputation, and the permission is epistemic rather than hedonic. The city licenses intoxication because it wants to know something it cannot otherwise learn.
