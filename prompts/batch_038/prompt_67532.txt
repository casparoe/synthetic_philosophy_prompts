ok so I'm a junior and I'm on my school's peer mediation committee — student-run, we handle stuff like rumors and stolen hoodies and people being horrible in group chats so it doesn't go straight to suspension. The program's whole pitch, on the poster in the counseling office and in the training we did in September, is basically "guilt is healthy, shame is toxic." Guilt is about what you did, shame is about who you are, and our job as mediators is to get people to the first one and keep them out of the second one. Nobody on the committee has ever questioned this, it's just the air we breathe in that room.

But something's bugging me and I cant tell if I'm being dumb about it. Half of what we actually do looks exactly like shame machinery. The entire point of a resolution circle is that the person says what they did out loud, in front of the person they hurt, plus two mediators and sometimes a teacher. We make them write an acknowledgment of harm that gets read to the group. There's a resitution list. We deliberately build a situation where your standing in front of other people depends on how you handle the next ten minutes. If shame is the emotion about how you look to others, then we're manufacturing it on purpose and calling it guilt because the script used the right words. Either the poster is wrong about what shame is, or it's wrong about what we're doing, or the two emotions aren't actually seperable the way the poster assumes.

Can you take that slogan apart for me? I want the different precise claims it could be making, laid out as a menu — is the guilt/shame split about object-vs-self, or about private-vs-audience (real audience? imagined audience? does it matter if anyone actually finds out?), or about whether the thing can be repaired vs whether it can only be re-granted by other people, or about whether the feeling tracks a real moral fact or just your social standing, or something else I haven't thought of? Tell me for each one whether it's a platitude, whether it's an empirical claim about what these feelings actually do to behavior, and whether it's just false or way stronger than the poster has any business being. Then — this is the part I actually care about — for each reading, say whether it makes our program coherent or makes it self-undermining. I want to know whether there's a version of "we convert shame into guilt" that's a sensible goal for a small institution like a school to set for itself, or whether a group can't really choose which of these emotions its members feel, it can only choose the furniture and hope.

And don't just hand me the menu and stay neutral. Give me your best verdict on which reading is the right one and which the poster is probably leaning on without knowing it, and tell me what would change your mind — what argument, or what kind of evidence from actual cases, would make you flip one of those verdicts. Also flag where you think *I'm* reasoning badly, because I suspect I'm sliding between "shame is about an audience" and "shame is bad" and treating that slide as if it were a discovery.
