I am in the second year of a philosophy PhD, and for the past month my seminar on the structure of normativity has been circling the distinction between right-kind and wrong-kind reasons for attitudes. I follow the standard setup well enough. Some considerations bear on whether an attitude is fitting or correct: that a novel is genuinely funny is a reason to be amused by it, that a colleague's conduct was cruel is a reason to disapprove of it. Others bear only on whether having the attitude would be good, useful, or prudent, as when a demon threatens to torment you unless you admire him, or when you are offered a reward for believing something improbable. The second sort seems to be a reason in some sense, and yet not the kind of reason that could make the attitude appropriate; the two familiar diagnoses are that wrong-kind reasons are state-given rather than object-given, and that they are considerations which cannot figure in the reasoning characteristic of the attitude in question. A classmate of mine is content to leave the matter there. I am not, and I suspect the distinction is being asked to carry more weight than it can bear.

Make the case, as directly and as strongly as you can, for the thesis that wrong-kind reasons are genuine reasons for the very attitudes they bear on — that the demon's threat is a real reason to admire the demon, not a reason to want to admire him or to bring it about that you do — and that the feeling that such considerations are of the wrong kind is an artifact of assuming that every reason for an attitude must be explained by whatever makes that attitude fitting or correct. I want this set out as an argument rather than as a tour: identify the premises you are relying on, flag the step that does the real work, and say which premise a theorist who grounds normativity in fittingness will reject and on what grounds. One escape route is closed by stipulation. You may not reclassify the problematic considerations as reasons to intend, want, or try to have the attitude, since I think that move only relocates the difficulty: intending to admire the demon is itself an attitude with its own conditions of correctness, and the threat looks like the wrong kind of reason for that as well. You can assume fluency in the technical vocabulary — fittingness, correctness conditions, buck-passing accounts of value — and I have no need of references or pointers to the literature.

Then test the thesis against a case of my own, which I will call the endowed chair. A donor of otherwise unimpeachable standing announces that she will fund a professorship only if the faculty members who appoint its holder come to genuinely admire her; she is explicit that she wants the admiration itself rather than declarations of it. You already know everything about her work and conduct that makes her admirable, and the announcement adds nothing to your information about those achievements, though it may well tell you something about her character. Press the case in both directions. Is the announcement an object-given or a state-given consideration, given that it is a fact about her rather than about your psychology? Does it give you a reason to admire her, a reason not to admire her, or both at once? And if your thesis implies that one and the same fact is simultaneously a reason for and a reason against the identical attitude, explain why that is a tolerable consequence instead of a reductio. Finally, address the objection from involuntariness — that you cannot admire the donor for the sake of the chairmanship, so the consideration cannot function as a reason at all — and tell me whether that objection shows these considerations are not reasons, or only that they are reasons you cannot act on. If the latter, say whether reasons one cannot act on form a coherent category, and what independent pressure there is to admit them, such as reasons bearing on the past or reasons one is permanently cut off from ever knowing. Close by stating plainly what the thesis costs: which intuitions about admiration, belief, and fear you are asking me to surrender, and whether anything worth keeping goes with them.
