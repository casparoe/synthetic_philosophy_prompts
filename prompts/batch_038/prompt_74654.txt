I am a claims adjuster by trade, and for two years now I have been auditing a Thursday-evening reading group that meets in the back room of a public library; I go for the arguments rather than for the reading. Last week the group took up the claim that no person could have originated from a different egg and sperm — the doctrine that came out of Kripke's lectures and is usually called the necessity of origin — and two members, whom I will call Peter and Nadia, spent most of the evening on it. I took notes as they went, because at nearly every turn both of them seemed to land something, and by the end I could not have told you who was ahead. I am deliberately not saying which way I lean, because I do not think I lean anywhere; what I want is an umpire's account of the exchange as it actually happened.

Here is the sequence, as near as I can reconstruct it. (1) Peter opened by saying the doctrine cannot be argued for, only asserted: ask whether you could have come from a different sperm, and the essentialist answers no, but when pressed for the reason, all he produces is that whatever came from a different sperm would be a different individual — which is the conclusion dressed up as a premise. (2) Nadia replied that the charge is empty until Peter supplies a competing account of what makes an individual the same across possibilities, since a principle of individuation is not rendered question-begging merely by being fundamental; on her telling Peter had only complained that the essentialist's answer was too basic, and that is not an objection. (3) Peter accepted the challenge and gave one: identity is fixed by continuity rather than by gamete provenance, so you could have come from a gamete pair differing by a molecule here or there, provided the resulting life was continuous with the one you actually lead. (4) Nadia said this is a sorites in waiting — if one molecule's difference is admissible so is two, and iterating lands Peter on the claim that he could have come from the gametes that in fact produced his brother, which she took to be a reductio of any tolerance principle about origins. She added that Peter was sliding between numerical identity and qualitative similarity, and that granting him an almost indistinguishable counterpart is not granting that the counterpart would have been him. (5) Peter said he would restrict the principle rather than abandon it: alternative origins are admissible only where the alternative gametes are not already spoken for, where they would not otherwise have gone into making anybody, so the brother case never arises. He then turned the tables. Exactness, he said, is the more desperate position, because it makes whether you exist at all hang on a single molecule; because it must be applied to the egg and the sperm as well, on pain of a regress into their own origins; and because it leaves the doctrine silent about the situations where the question is a live one, such as a clinic deciding which of several embryos to transfer. (6) Nadia answered that the restriction concedes the substance, since on Peter's own view origin settles identity whenever nothing else has gotten there first, so origin is doing the individuating work after all; that exactness is a price worth paying, because without it there is no principled way to track an individual from one possibility to another, and there is nothing peculiar about the demand, any more than there is something peculiar about the fact that nothing can be almost identical to the number seven; and that Peter still owes an answer about the brother, because a restriction engineered to dodge one counterexample is not an account of anything.

Peter's parting shot was that a doctrine which will always describe the product of an alternative conception as somebody else has been insulated from refutation by definitional fiat, and is therefore a stipulation about what counts as being me rather than a discovery about persons. Nadia's parting shot was that Peter's tolerance principle is ordinary vagueness promoted to metaphysics, and that vagueness belongs to our descriptions, not to what they describe. Neither budged.

Could you adjudicate? Please do not give me a survey of the literature or a list of things to read — I want your judgment on these arguments as they were made. Please organize the reply under the following headings, in this order. "Bottom line": about 100 words, saying who is ahead overall and on which single point the outcome turns. "Move by move": about 900 words, rating each of the six numbered moves on a five-point scale of your own devising, running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with three to six sentences of justification per rating; where a move is right in part and wrong in part, say which part earns which rating, and flag any premise the other side left unchallenged but should not have. "Who is right about what": about 250 words, separating the sub-claims the two of them actually agree on from the ones they genuinely dispute, and identifying which of the disputed ones are load-bearing for the doctrine. "What both of them missed": about 300 words, at least three items, and for each, whether supplying it would have changed the outcome and in whose favor. "The argument they should have had": about 150 words, restating the disagreement in the sharpest form you can manage and saying whether you think that version is resolvable. Please take a stand at every step; a verdict of "both have a point" is the one answer I could have supplied myself.
