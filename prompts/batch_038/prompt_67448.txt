I was recently appointed to a role in which I approve country-level programs for a development funder, and last month I signed off on a grant whose conditions amounted to telling a sovereign ministry how it must run its own health budget. In preparing an internal session on the intellectual history of that kind of authority, I went back to John Stuart Mill — partly because of his India writings, and partly because he spent roughly three decades in the East India Company's service, rising to a senior post drafting dispatches to India, which is recognizable as an ancestor of the job I now do. I am not interested in denouncing him and not interested in defending him; I want to know how much of his machinery still turns.

Below are five justificatory moves that recur across those writings — the remarks on backward societies and the limits of the liberty principle in the introductory chapter of *On Liberty*, the chapter of *Considerations on Representative Government* on the government of dependencies by a free state, and the essay on non-intervention. I have stated each in my own words; please work from these formulations rather than from memory of the texts.

1. **The capacity precondition.** The principle of liberty governs only those societies that have reached the point at which free and equal discussion can actually improve them. Before that threshold is crossed, despotism is a legitimate mode of government, provided that its end is the improvement of the governed and that its means are justified by actually effecting that improvement.

2. **The guardianship analogy.** Ruling a dependency is relevantly like educating a child not yet capable of self-government: free institutions are the best education for a people able to use them, and a superior power may therefore be entitled to construct those institutions from above rather than wait for them to emerge.

3. **Output legitimacy as fiduciary substitution.** Whether a government is good is settled by the security, impartial justice, and material improvement it produces, not by whether the governed chose it; a small body of well-informed administrators may know a people's interests better than the people does. The trustee's judgment of the beneficiary's interest stands in for the beneficiary's own, with no standing mechanism by which the beneficiary can overrule it.

4. **The self-help argument.** A people's freedom is durable only if it is won by its own exertion, so an outside power that removes a domestic despot on the people's behalf deprives them of the habits and resolve that freedom requires — subject to the qualification that assistance to a population already actively resisting may tip a balance without appropriating the struggle.

5. **The corruption-of-the-ruler argument.** Exercising arbitrary power over others degrades the institutions and character of the ruling people itself; a free nation therefore has a self-regarding reason to keep its dependencies under public, parliamentary, argument-exposed administration, and the requirement of justifying one's decisions to a distant and skeptical readership is itself a discipline that improves the quality of rule.

Please score each of the five on two axes, from 0 to 10:

- **Survivability** — how much of the argument still stands today as a non-question-begging case for asymmetrical authority between political communities, once the nineteenth-century vocabulary of civilization and backwardness is stripped away. A 10 means the argument retains independent content that a contemporary critic would have to answer; a 0 means what remains is a restatement of the conclusion, or a claim we now reject on empirical grounds.
- **Load-bearingness** — how much of Mill's overall position depends on it. A 10 means the rest of the structure collapses without it; a 0 means it is ornament.

Then rank the five by survivability, and add: one sentence per item explaining where it lands; identification of the single item sitting in the high-load-bearing / low-survivability quadrant, with an account of what its removal costs the system; a note on any pair that you judge to be one argument wearing two costumes, merging them if so and adjusting the ranking; and, for whichever item you score highest on survivability, the strongest objection available to a defender of self-determination and your verdict on whether the item survives it.

Some constraints. Around 700–900 words, with a compact table of the two scores and the rank before the prose. No bibliography, no secondary literature, no page numbers, and no quotations from Mill — paraphrase. Commit to numbers rather than hedging into the observation that all five are compromised in different ways; I can reach that conclusion myself. Finally, keep the scoring of the argument's logical structure strictly separate from the scoring of its historical consequences: what the Company actually did is evidence about the world, not a premise about the validity of any of the five, and I do not want the numbers quietly driven by the latter.
