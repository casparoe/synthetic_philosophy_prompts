Third year in a world/global history program here, and I've come away from the last two workshops convinced that half my field runs on two sentences that nobody ever unpacks. Last Thursday it was a postdoc responding to a chapter draft on pre-colonial trade in the western Indian Ocean. The draft leans pretty heavily on customs registers and shipping lists kept by European companies, because that's where the numbers are, and he waved it off with "history is written by the victors" — his point being that you can't read Indian Ocean commerce off a European ledger and say anything about what the people actually in it were doing. Fine, maybe. But ten minutes later the chair used the *same* slogan in the opposite direction, to swat down a comparativist in the room who kept pushing GDP shares and real-wage series: no account of the global past comes from nowhere, so the comparativist's "let's just look at the numbers" isn't the neutral referee he thinks it is. Same sentence, two completely different jobs, and I couldn't tell whether anyone noticed.

The other one, "there's no view from nowhere," I associate mainly with a philosophy book title from the eighties that people in my department cite without having read, and it got deployed at least three times last term, usually to end something rather than start it. So here's what I want: lay out the distinct claims each of these slogans can be making, as a menu rather than picking a favourite and explaining it. For each reading, give me the strongest version of the claim, the weakest, and what it actually entails — and then tell me which readings would genuinely license the postdoc's dismissal of the ledger evidence, which would genuinely license the chair's dismissal of the comparative series, and which do neither no matter how confidently they're said. I care about the whole spectrum: the ones that turn out to be platitudes about selection and emphasis, the ones that are really claims about archives rather than about truth, the ones that are semantic or conceptual claims about words like "rise" and "backwardness" smuggling a trajectory in through the vocabulary, the ones about who gets taken seriously as a knower, the ones that look like they're about evidence but are actually genetic-fallacy moves, and any that are just false. Say which ones self-undermine — if every account is a view from somewhere, why should I accept the slogan that says so — and where the two slogans overlap versus where they're doing different work.

A few candidate readings of my own, offered only so you can tell me they're wrong or redundant: something about the composition of the surviving archive, something about the direction of fit between a generalising vocabulary and the cases it was trained on, and something about whether explaining why a belief arose ever bears on whether it's true. Format it as a numbered list I can literally bring to the next workshop, with a one-line note on each of which use it supports. Please don't put anything in quotation marks that you're reconstructing from memory — if you can't state a source's wording exactly, paraphrase it and say you're paraphrasing, because I'm going to be reading this next to people who will absolutely call me out on a fake quote. And be honest about your own epistemic footing: give me a rough sense of how confident you are in the menu overall, and single out the one reading you're least sure either exists as a real position or is distinguishable from its neighbours.
