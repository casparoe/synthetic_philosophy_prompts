I help out on Wednesday nights at a chess club for middle schoolers in the back room of my library. I'm not a strong player myself, maybe 1200 online on a good day, so mostly I set up boards, keep score sheets, and drive kids home. Two of the other volunteers, Dana and Marcus, got into a long argument last month about how you should judge a kid's game when you go over it with them afterwards, and it never got resolved. I'm just the bystander here — I honestly couldn't tell you which one of them I'd have backed, and I've been turning it over in my head since. Here's roughly how it went, in order:

(1) Dana said you should grade the moves, not the result: if a kid played sensible chess and lost to a lucky trap, praising them for the loss anyway teaches them nothing and makes them scared to play. (2) Marcus said there's no such thing as grading a move apart from its result — a "good move" just means a move that helps you win, so process grading is outcome grading with extra steps, except the outcome is being guessed by a tired adult at a glance instead of measured, so it's the same signal but noisier. (3) Dana said that's wrong on the facts: you can see a move is principled without playing it out — develop pieces, don't hang material, have some idea behind the move — and besides, the kid has no way of knowing which of their forty moves lost the game, so a bare win/loss gives them almost nothing to change next week. (4) Marcus said the engines prove his point, because when an engine evaluates a position, that number is basically a prediction of who's going to win, so even the most sophisticated "position judgment" anyone has is still an outcome forecast in disguise. And he added that when you tell a kid their plan was great in a game they lost, you're training them to make moves that look like good chess to you, and looking right to a fallible judge is a different skill from winning — some of these kids will get very good at playing impressively and losing. (5) Dana said the fix for a bad judge is a better judge, not handing the whole job to the scoreboard, and anyway results are gameable in the other direction: a kid can beat up on weaker opponents with cheap tricks, post a great record, and build habits that fall apart the second they meet real resistance. (6) Marcus said sure, but the answer to that is lots of real games against real opponents over years, which averages the cheap tricks out — not substituting his eyeball for the standings. Dana shot back that a kid here plays maybe thirty serious games a year, so "let the results average out" means giving a twelve-year-old feedback that is mostly noise for a decade while they're still forming their habits. Then the pizzas showed up and it stopped.

One thing worth mentioning: Marcus said at one point that this is exactly the fight people have about training AI systems — checking and rewarding each step of the reasoning versus only checking whether the final answer came out right — and Dana said the comparison is bogus because a model can be run and scored millions of times at no cost while her kids get thirty games a year and one volunteer with a ratings app. That's part of what I want you to look at.
