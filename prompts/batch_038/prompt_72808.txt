Help me with something for my personal identity seminar. I need an example — an original one — of a case that exposes a real conceptual problem for Buddhist no-self reductionism, and it has to be a case my students havent met a dozen times already. So: no chariot, no candle flame passed from wick to wick, no teletransporter, no prince and cobbler, no brain split between two skulls, no ship of Theseus. All burned.

Here's the constraint that makes it worth assigning. Build a small concrete scenario — a person, a promise or a debt or a particular memory, and some stretch of time in which the psychophysical stuff gets rearranged — and then show me that the two Buddhist options come apart on it. By the two options I mean, on one side, the mainstream Abhidharma reductionists, for whom a person is nothing over and above a convenient way of talking about a causal series of aggregates; and on the other the pudgalavadins, the minority school Vasubandhu keeps having to attack, who held that the person is real but inexpressible: you cannot say it is identical to the aggregates and you cannot say it is other than them either. Most stock examples are useless for teaching this, because both camps say the same thing about them and nobody learns anything. I want a case where the inexpressible-person move actually does work — where it licenses some verdict, some prediction, some assignment of responsibility, that mere designation-for-a-series does not.

Then in a couple more paragraphs, name the conceptual issue that falls out of your case for each side. I already know the usual suspects: for the reductionist theres nobody left who is the bearer of the earlier commitment, and for the pudgalavadin inexpressibility looks like a dodge that smuggles a self back in through the back door, especially once you need the person to be the same across lives for karma to be coherent. Dont just hand me those two in generic form. Tell me precisely what goes wrong in the scenario you built, and where the pressure lands hardest — on the ontology, or on the semantics of person-talk, or on the ethics of holding someone to a promise.

And say which of the two problems you think is worse, but flag it as your own judgment rather than the settled view, because I'll be telling them it's contested.

Continuous prose please. No headings, no bullet points, no bolded anything, no tables — just paragraphs, the way youd write me a letter. And no citations or reading list at the end; I'm not looking for references, I'm looking for a case I can drop onto a handout.
