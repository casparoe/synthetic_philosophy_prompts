I have no academic training whatsoever: I left school at sixteen, spent thirty-one years as a heating engineer, and came to philosophy late and entirely on my own, through public libraries and second-hand bookshops. Six years ago I started a small subscription reading group. There are eleven members now, mostly adults who, like me, never studied the subject formally. This week's question concerned levels of description and causation. I built it around a toy world whose vocabulary I borrowed from Douglas Hofstadter's "careenium" and "simmballs"; the reconstruction below is mine rather than his, and I stripped it of everything I could not state confidently.

What follows is the question exactly as the members received it, and then one member's answer exactly as it was submitted. I have not marked it, and I am deliberately not telling you what I make of it, because I want your reading rather than an endorsement of mine.

QUESTION AS SET

Part One. Here is a world. Its fundamental physics consists of very simple particles — call them careens — which do nothing but move and collide according to fixed local rules. Under some conditions they clump. Suppose that in one large region of careenium, clumps form, drift, merge, split, and set off further clumping elsewhere, and suppose that the only level at which the behaviour of that region can be stated compactly is the level of clump-patterns — call them simmballs — rather than the level of individual careens. Now suppose that in a more elaborate version of the world a simmball reliably triggers a large-scale rearrangement of careenium some distance away: a limb-like projection swings, a valve closes, a second region begins clumping in a new pattern.

Someone objects as follows. "The simmball moved the limb. That cannot be right. What moved the limb was careens hitting careens; and if I were given the complete careen-level history of that world, I could in principle derive everything that happened, including the limb's movement. So either the simmball is not a real cause, or else the limb moved twice over, for two unrelated reasons."

(i) Set out as clearly as you can why the complete low-level account seems to leave no causal work for the simmball-level account. Name the assumption or assumptions that generate the pressure, rather than describing the pressure in general terms.

(ii) State what options are open to someone who wants to hold on to both claims — that the careen-level account is complete, and that simmballs genuinely cause things — and say what each option costs.

ANSWER SUBMITTED

The objector's worry comes from three ideas working together. First, completeness: if you had the whole history of the careens — which careen hit which, and when — then everything else in that world is fixed by it, the limb's swing included, and there is nothing left over for anything else to do. Second, exclusion: if an event already has a sufficient cause at the careen level, then nothing distinct from that cause can also be a cause of the same event, unless we are prepared to say the effect happened twice over. Third, a ban on systematic overdetermination: two independent causes coinciding once is tolerable, but coincidence on every single occasion is not, and here the coincidence would happen every time. Put those three together and the simmball has nowhere to stand. The careens are already doing all the work, so the simmball is either not a cause or a redundant one. This is essentially the shape of the exclusion argument associated with Jaegwon Kim, transferred from minds to clumps.

One point about the wording of the objection. The objector says that from the complete careen-level history they could "in principle derive" everything. That is an epistemic claim, about what could be worked out. What the argument needs is the metaphysical claim that the careen-level facts suffice for the later facts, whether or not anyone could ever derive them. Otherwise the argument would be about the limits of derivation, and simmball-level talk might be indispensable for creatures like us without that telling against its being causally idle. The premise should be restated as sufficiency, not derivability.

So far as I can see there are four routes out.

(1) Identity. Say the simmball just is a certain pattern of careen-clumping — not something extra alongside it. Then there is only one cause, described twice, and no overdetermination to ban. The cost is real. The same simmball-pattern can be built out of different careens in different runs of the world, so the pattern is not identical to any one arrangement of them; at best you get token identity, a single event here under two descriptions, and never type identity. But the identity route is also what rescues downward causation, which is the other thing people want from simmballs. Once the higher level just is the lower level arranged a certain way, there is no longer any pushing between levels to worry about. Downward causation and the exclusion problem dissolve together, in one move, which is why I think this route is worth more than it looks.

(2) Deny exclusion. Keep the simmball as a genuinely distinct cause and deny the principle that a sufficient lower-level cause crowds it out. The cost is that you then owe an account of why this is not overdetermination; you cannot wave at it. You have to say what realization is, such that a realized cause and its realizer do not compete, and that is a piece of philosophy rather than a stipulation.

(3) Deny completeness. Let the simmball-level facts make a difference to how the careens go, so that the careen-level history was never sufficient after all. The cost is that the careen level stops being fundamental, which is more or less the one thing you wanted it to be, and you still have not said how or where it fails.

(4) Concede the objection. Keep completeness and exclusion, and say that simmballs are real but never causes: they are how the clumping looks from a long way off, and nothing at that level does anything. I do not think this is genuinely available to someone who takes the simmball level seriously, though. If a simmball made no difference to anything, then nothing said at that level could come out true or false, because there would be nothing for it to be answerable to. The whole level would be, strictly speaking, nonsense, and we would not be arguing about it. So (4) is not a live option and can be set aside.

The best route is (1), with (2) as a fallback for anyone who cannot live with token identity alone.
