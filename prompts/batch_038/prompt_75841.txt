I teach a seminar on the ethics of public office and I'm building a session around the Lockheed affair of 1975–76, because it's the one episode where the same set of payments got four completely different verdicts in four countries, and I want the concepts put under that kind of pressure. I don't want the usual culture-relativism-versus-universalism boilerplate from either side. I want the mapping done properly first.

Facts, so you don't have to guess, though flag anything you think I've garbled: a Senate subcommittee under Frank Church established in late 1975 and early 1976 that Lockheed had paid something like $22 million to foreign officials to move aircraft sales along. In Japan the money went through a private fixer, Yoshio Kodama, to get All Nippon Airways onto the TriStar instead of the DC-10 it had options on, with roughly $3 million reaching Kakuei Tanaka's office; the order was announced in October 1972; Tanaka was arrested in 1976 and, as I remember it, finally sentenced in 1983 to four years — but for failing to declare the money under foreign-exchange reporting rules, not for bribery, because nobody could pin a specific official act to the payments. He stayed free on appeal until he died in 1993. In the Netherlands the payments touched Prince Bernhard, who was inspector general of the armed forces and a figure in a lot of semi-official institutions; a commission of inquiry found he'd taken money but concluded, roughly, that it hadn't bought him, and he was stripped of his military functions rather than prosecuted. In Italy the allegations reached former ministers and forced President Giovanni Leone to resign in June 1978. In West Germany the claim that Franz Josef Strauss and his party had taken money over the Starfighter purchase was never corroborated and was dropped. The FCPA followed in 1977.

So: work through each country and tell me who the office-holder actually is and what office it is. That's where I think it gets interesting, because two of these cases strain the concept. Bernhard had no procurement authority at all — under an office-centered account, where corruption is a violation of the standards attaching to a public role held for public purposes, which role got corrupted, and does the answer change if the payment bought access and standing rather than a decision? And Kodama held no office whatsoever; he was a private intermediary with underworld ties. Does an account that defines corruption in terms of public office have anything to say about money that moves through a private broker into a private airline's purchasing decision, where the airline is regulated and effectively state-steered? Say plainly if the answer is no.

Then run the competing accounts against the four outcomes, one at a time. The public-interest account. The office-centered/fiduciary account, including its bribe-versus-illegal-gratuity distinction, which turns on whether a payment buys a specified act or just goodwill. And the opinion-relative account — Heidenheimer's scheme, if you know it, where an act is black if both elites and the public reject it, white if both tolerate it, grey if they split. Use that scheme if you have it, and tell me if my description is off. I also want the gift reading taken seriously rather than dismissed: the anthropological point that a gift creates an open-ended obligation to reciprocate rather than purchasing a named act, so that calling it a bribe may misdescribe the transaction — and then tell me where that reading is doing real analytic work and where it's an alibi that any well-paid lobbyist could recite.

Two more things. Which country does each account condemn, which does it let off, and where is it silent? I want the silences named, not papered over. And on the grease argument — that these payments oiled up sclerotic bureaucracies — test it against the ANA case specifically, where there were two credible sellers and a competitive choice, versus the Starfighter case, which was a politically driven NATO procurement. Does the grease defense survive either one?

Finally, the reverse direction: what does the spread of verdicts do to the theories? Four legal systems, one set of payments, four outcomes. Is that evidence that corruption is indexed to locally recognized standards for an office, or evidence that verdicts track prosecutorial capacity and who's vulnerable — a head of state's consort and a party leader being rather harder to touch than a fixer? And when Congress responded by exporting American standards to American firms' conduct abroad, was that a discovery about what corruption is, or a jurisdictional power move wearing a moral discovery's clothes? Argue it, and tell me which account you think comes out best and what it still can't handle.

No bibliography, no reading lists, no citations — I can't check them and neither can you. Reason it out, and mark the places where you're reconstructing rather than remembering.
