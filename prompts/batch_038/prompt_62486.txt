So — I'm the faculty member on our university's AI systems review panel. Six of us: three philosophers, two from IT governance, one lawyer. We do exactly one thing. Every system the administration wants to deploy gets stamped either TOOL or AGENT. The AGENT stamp means it has to have a named human principal who's accountable for it, a tested off-switch, and a hard cap on how much money or energy it can commit between reviews. TOOL means none of that. It sounds trivial right up until you notice that nobody on the panel agrees about what the word means and we've mostly been deciding cases by vibes and then rationalizing afterwards.

Track record so far, all six unanimous, for what that's worth:

1. Room scheduler. Constraint solver, runs once a term, produces a draft timetable; the registrar reads it and clicks approve before anything is published. → TOOL
2. Endowment execution algo. Adaptive — it revises its own order-slicing strategy during the day based on realized market impact. Places binding orders, no per-order approval, just aggregate risk limits. → AGENT
3. Student advising chatbot. Frozen weights, generates text, can't touch a single university system, a human reads everything it produces. → TOOL
4. Microgrid controller. RL, keeps updating in deployment from realized prices, bids into the wholesale power market and dispatches batteries. The developers admit it sometimes does things they didn't anticipate. Interrupting it mid-cycle can damage equipment, so there's no clean pause. → AGENT
5. Payroll. Fully deterministic and auditable, stages every transfer, but nothing leaves the building until a human controller signs off on the cycle. → TOOL
6. Admissions screener. Statistical model, frozen for the cycle, scores applicants; a human commitee makes every actual decision, and we noted on the record that you could swap in a different scorer without changing who decides. → TOOL

Now the new one. It comes up for a vote Thursday and it's a mess.

A deterministic bid advisor for the same power desk. Hand-written optimization, no learning whatsoever — the developers actually supplied an argument that it does exactly what the spec says and nothing more. It emits a recommended bid every five minutes into a queue. It never touches the market itself. But sitting on the other end of that queue is a relay, a dumb forwarder with no discretion at all, itself classified TOOL years ago under something like the payroll precedent, which submits whatever lands in the queue to the wholesale market automatically. Nobody reads the recommendation before it goes out; a human reads the log the next morning. And obviously bids that clear are binding, and the other side of that market is full of people who adapt to us.

So neither component is an AGENT by anything we have ever said, and yet the pair does, causally, exactly what the microgrid controller does, minus the unpredictability. I've started calling this the composite problem and I want your read before Thursday.

What I want from you:

(a) Which criteria actually survive the six precedents? Panel members have variously muttered "does it act in the world without a human approving each act," "is it still changing after deployment," "does it transact against other agents who adapt to it," and "does it have ends of its own that could come apart from ours." I'm fairly confident at least one of those four is already dead on the existing record — tell me which, and which precedent killed it, and whether any of them can be patched into something that survives.

(b) For each criterion that does survive, what does it say about Thursday's case? My suspicion is they split down the middle.

(c) What should we vote if we care about being consistent with ourselves rather than with our intuitions about this particular machine? Name the closest analog among the six and be honest about where the analogy breaks.

(d) What principle are we adopting with each possible vote? My worry: TOOL commits us to scoring components one at a time, which means anyone who wants to dodge the AGENT label just splits their system into an advisor and a relay. But AGENT invites the reductio that the thermostat down the hall is an agent too, since it also closes a circuit nobody approves each time. I'd like that reductio actually answered, not waved at — including whether "the panel labels systems, not architectures" is a real principle or just a way of not answering.

(e) If the record genuinely underdetermines it, say so plainly, and then tell me the two cheapest further cases we should queue up next: one that would seperate the surviving criteria on the AGENT side from each other, and one that would seperate the surviving criteria on the TOOL side from each other.

Please be explicit about how confident you are in the prediction you land on, and single out the one step in your reasoning you're least sure of — I would much rather know where you're guessing than get a smooth writeup. And whatever else you do, make the very last line exactly one of these two, so I can drop it straight into the minutes:

Verdict: TOOL

Verdict: AGENT
