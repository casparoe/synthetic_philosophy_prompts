so my aunt Miriam died in February — she was 84, a high school biology teacher in a small town, and I'm giving the eulogy at her memorial on Sunday. Roughly eighty people. About half of them are family, her old teaching colleagues, and the local foray club: no philosophy, no patience for abstraction, they want to cry and eat potato salad. The other half are people from the county mycological society and a couple of archivists from the university library's special collections, plus three of her former students who are now actual biologists. The reason that second group is there is that Miriam kept, for something like forty-five years, a card index of every single mushroom walk she ever went on. Nine shoeboxes, maybe six thousand cards, and — this is the part I need you to get right — organized by *walk*, not by species. So one species shows up in forty different places, filed under a date, a weather note, who was with her, what the oaks were doing that year. Her own private notation: a slash for "spore print taken," a small circle for "ate it, fine," a triangle for "ate it, regretted it." Pencil, always, so she could amend, and honestly the amendments are most of the interesting content. Two years ago she couldn't get out into the woods any more, but she could still correct her grandkids' identifications from the cards over the phone, and she was faster than the field guide apps.

Here's the thing I want the eulogy to actually be about, and I'll say it bluntly because I've been reading philosophy of mind on and off since I was ninteen and I know exactly how this is going to sound at a funeral: I'm a committed externalist about minds, and I think Miriam's capacity to know a mushroom was never located entirely in her skull. It was distributed across her head, the card file, the pencil, and a few specific woods she walked on a cycle. The file wasn't a record of her knowledge, it was part of the knowledge, and it qualified for exactly the reasons the thought experiments say it should — she reached for it constantly, it was reliably there, and she took what it said on trust without checking, the way you take your own memory on trust. That last condition is the load-bearing one for what I want to do here. Because I arranged, last autumn, to donate the whole file to the university archive, where it will be re-catalogued by species name into a database, which is the correct and responsible thing to do with it and also, I think, the moment it stops being part of anybody's mind and becomes a source you consult and cross-check against other sources. Same cards. Different relation. Something real dies in that transfer, and I am the person who signed the paperwork. The eulogy has to be able to say that in front of the archivists without sounding ungrateful or resentful, and it has to say it to the family without turning into "she lives on in her shoebox," which she would have considered sentimental garbage. She was a materialist and an atheist, she thought minds were brains and said so loudly, and she would have found the whole extended-mind business ridiculous — which is part of why I don't want a lecture. No philosopher names, no paper titles, no quotations, no jargon; the words externalism, cognition, and artifact should not appear in the speech at all. If the philosophy people in the room can reconstruct the argument from the concrete details alone, and the family just hears something true and funny about their aunt, it's worked.

Hard constraints: nothing she'd have thought false — no watching over us, no reunions, no "her spirit." It should run about five minutes, so 650–750 words. It has to contain at least two things that make people actually laugh, and the humor should come from her and the cards, never from the theory. It should not end on grief; it should end on a request. Specifically I want the closing to be a small, doable ask — one card, one walk, taken up by somebody in the room in the right way, trusted rather than researched — because that's the only form of preservation I actually believe in, and it's also the only comfort I can offer without lying. And please don't let the ask sound like an instruction manual or a call to action from a conference talk.

You're a philosopher of mind who has also given far too many funerals and is good at both — write like someone who has read the arguments and knows that none of them are the point at a graveside. Think carefully about the failure modes before you start: the biggest ones are the speech turning into a stealth seminar, the ask turning into a gimmick, and the family being told that their grief is smaller than it feels because part of her survives in paper. Begin with a short plan — six to eight bullets — that maps the shape of the speech and says, for each section, what it's doing and what it's specifically not allowed to say; then give me the eulogy itself.
