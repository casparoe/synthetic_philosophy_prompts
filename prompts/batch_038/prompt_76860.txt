Two people at the pub-thing I go to on Thursdays got into a proper argument over a made-up case one of them had sketched out, and the rest of us mostly just ate nuts adn listened. The case isn't real but the disagreement was, and it never got settled, so I'm hoping you can.

The setup: a coastal region of a country holds a referendum on independence. 54% yes on an 82% turnout. The national constitution says such votes are only advisory, but the government has publicly promised to honour this one. Inside the region, an inland valley district votes 71% to stay in the old country. The valley has two big hydro schemes that would generate something like 40% of the new state's electricity, and it makes up about 15% of the territory. It was folded into the coastal province in the 1890s by an administrative reorg done for railway reasons — before that it sat in a different province entirely. People there speak a dialect that isn't the coastal language, and the independence campaign has promised a language law making the coastal one the only official language. Three weeks after the vote, the valley's district council announces it wants to hold its own referendum on staying behind.

Marta says the valley can go. Priya says it can't. Roughly how it went:

1. Marta: self-determination cuts both ways or it isn't a principle. If the region gets to leave because the people who live there said so, the valley gets to leave the region for the same reason. You can't say "the people who live here decide" on Tuesday and "the people who drew the map in 1897 decide" on Wednesday.

2. Priya: there's a disanalogy. The region is a would-be sovereign state with a government, a territory and institutions, and the normal practice is that a new state inherits the administrative boundaries it already had — which is the only thing that stops every border being reopened each decade. A valley is a district of a region, not a people. If any district that voted the other way can peel off, nothing is ever final and every referendum just moves the fight one level down.

3. Marta: that practice is a convenience for avoiding wars between states, not a moral fact, and it's precisely what's in dispute here. The valley says its own border was drawn by exactly the same kind of administrative fiat — a railway reorg nobody in it was consulted about. You can't treat inherited lines as sacrosanct when those lines were themselves inherited from paperwork.

4. Priya: then you're arguing from history, and history never runs out. Inside the valley are two towns merged into it in the 1970s, and one of them would vote to go with the new country. Run your principle all the way down and every level has a minority that wants out, forever. A principle that never terminates can't be an action-guide, and you have to stop somewhere — the region is the smallest unit here with any plausible claim to be a demos.

5. Marta: infinite regress isn't an objection, it's a description of what happens when nobody actually wants to leave. Most minorities inside minorities are perfectly happy. The valley isn't, and not for fluffy reasons: it goes from being the overwhelming majority of its own district, with a council that works in its dialect, to roughly an eighth of a new country that has already promised a single official language. That's a change of status, not a preference.

6. Priya: notice you've moved. You started with "the majority of the people who live here decide" and you've landed on "serious grievances justify exit." Those come apart, and the second is much harder to run. If grievance is the trigger, you owe a hearing to the 29% of the valley who want to be citizens of the new country — they lose their vote and their passport without anyone asking them. And to everyone else in the old country, who never got a say in the region's referendum but would lose 15% of the territory and the dams. If the principle is that all affected people get a vote, the region's own referendum was illegitimate too.

7. Marta: that proves too much. Any secession affects everyone in the state, so "all affected" hands the status quo a permanent veto and calls it fairness. But — fine, I'll give you that the valley's claim isn't automatic either. The honest answer sits in the middle: the valley stays, its language gets entrenched, it gets a fixed share of the dam revenue and a guaranteed second vote in fifteen years. The new state has to buy the valley's acquiescence rather than inherit it.

8. Priya: and there you've given up the thing you started with. If it can be bought off with revenue shares and a deferred ballot, it was never a right, it was a negotiating position. Rights don't have prices.

They shook hands and changed the subject. Can you go through it move by move — for each of the eight, say whether it lands, half-lands or fails, and why — and then tell me who came out ahead overall, and what neither of them said that they should have? I'm not after names of books or theorists or anything like that, I just want the arguments judged on what's actually in them.
