You are a formal epistemologist who has taught graduate seminars on rational disagreement for many years and who is unusually good at designing diagnostic instruments for debates. The context. I convene a reading group on whether two ideally rational agents can genuinely agree to disagree. The attendees range from people who have never seen the formal theorem to two engineers who work on whether separately trained prediction systems, fed the same corpus, can justifiably keep diverging outputs. I want a sorting instrument — a decision tree with a scoring key — that locates each attendee in the logical space by what they say about concrete cases, never by which label they find attractive. No question may ask whether the respondent accepts the common prior assumption, and no question may name a theorem, a paper, or a school of thought; every node must be a scenario or a plain-language question that can be answered without knowing where it leads.

The deliverable: ten to twelve numbered questions, each with two or three answer options. Your nodes must between them probe at least these fault lines, in an order you choose: whether the space of states can be taken to be finite; whether each agent's knowledge can be represented as a partition, so that agents know what they do not know; whether the two reported probabilities are common knowledge or merely known to each other to some finite depth; whether "they told each other everything they know" is even well defined when there are propositions neither agent has conceived of; whether a prior is the sort of thing that could be rational or irrational at all; whether priors are fixed prior to any experience or are themselves shaped by experience; whether updating is strict conditionalization on evidence the agent can state as a proposition; whether credences must be precise; and whether agreement is what repeated exchange of reports would converge to rather than what the agents currently report. At least two questions must concern non-human agents — for instance, two prediction systems trained on the same data but with different architectures — where the respondent must judge whether "same evidence" is determinate at all.

Requirements on the leaves: at least nine, and they must include (a) the view that the description is impossible as stated, so the case has been misdescribed; (b) the view that a common prior is a harmless modeling convention with no epistemological upshot; (c) the view that heterogeneous priors are rationally permissible; (d) the view that what looks like a difference in priors is really a difference in experience; (e) the escape through ambiguity or imprecise credence; (f) the escape through unawareness; (g) the escape through mutual knowledge falling short of common knowledge; (h) the escape through infinite state spaces or the failure of countable additivity; and (i) the dynamical view on which the pair would converge under continued exchange of reports, so the case as posed is unstable.
