ok so I'm in a 19th-century philosophy seminar right now and we just did Kierkegaard's Two Ages: A Literary Review (the 1846 one, the review of Gyllembourg's novel), specifically the part where he goes after "the public" and the press. Separately I'm in a creative writing workshop, and for the next one we get to bring in a story that's basically smuggling an idea from another class in through the fiction. That's what I want to do here but I need the story actually written, because I can describe the idea way better than I can plot a scene, apparently.

The bit that got me isn't the famous stuff about the crowd or about this being a reflective, passionless age — it's the narrower claim about the press. Roughly as I understand it: the press is the thing that manufactures the public, and the public is an abstraction that never actually exists in any room, because no two people in it are the same person in it. And because everything is said anonymously and endlessly, and nobody ever has to answer for anything, authority dissolves — there's no one left to be for or against, just commentary on commentary, and people end up as spectators of their own lives instead of actors. Also relevant: the leveling thing, where it's not that a mob beats you down, it's that everything gets quietly averaged until nobody can tell where anyone stands.

Here's the setup I want, and this part is mine, not Kierkegaard's:

The last newspaper in a small college town, a weekly that ran for like 140 years, folds in March. Within a week an anonymous hyperlocal forum appears. It has no masthead, no editor, no letters column, no bylines at all — one moderator, who enforces exactly one rule: no names, ever, not even your own. The moderator deletes posts that break it and otherwise does nothing. And here's the thing that has to drive the story: the forum never takes a position on anything. It has no opinion. It's purely a place where opinions happen. But within two months everyone in town is saying things like "the forum is against the new gym" or "you know what the forum thinks about the interim dean," as though there were an editorial board behind the curtain, and people start changing how they behave on the basis of that. The phantom has a will but no body, and nobody can be for or against it, and when someone tries to argue with it, there's literally no one there to argue with.

What I want from you: a short story, call it 1,300–1,900 words, that enacts that idea instead of explaining it.

Constraints, which I'd rather over-specify than get back an essay with dialogue in it:

1. No character may mention Kierkegaard, or say the words "the public," "the press," "leveling," "reflection," or "abstraction" as terms of art. Not one. If the philosophy can't survive being turned into a bake-sale dispute and a school board meeting, I don't want it in there.
2. Give me a single point of view — someone inside the town who's figuring this out slowly, not someone who already gets it. A person who's a little complicit would be ideal. Maybe the moderator, maybe the last reporter from the dead weekly, maybe someone who's been quietly enjoying it.
3. I need at least one scene where somebody tries to hold the forum accountable in a direct way — addresses it, demands an answer, writes an open letter to it, shows up somewhere to confront it — and it goes wrong in an instructive way rather than a dramatic one. The failure should be almost anticlimactic. That's the point.
4. At least one character who has a real conviction about something concrete and keeps postponing acting on it, not from cowardice exactly, but because of what they anticipate will be said. Make the postponement feel reasonable to them each time.
5. No villain, and nobody delivers a speech that sums up the theme. The moderator in particular shouldn't turn out to be secretly orchestrating anything — it's much worse for my purposes if they genuinely are just enforcing the no-names rule and going to bed at ten.
6. The ending can be bleak or not, I don't care, but it can't be a clean victory or a clean warning. Either someone commits to doing something anyway with no guarantee it won't just get absorbed into the discussion, or they don't and we don't get told that this is sad. Leave it standing there.
7. Tone: dry, a little funny, small-town specificity — the kind of comedy that comes from everyone being extremely sincere in service of a thing nobody built.

One thing to be careful of: the easy version of this story is "social media bad, townspeople are sheep," and that's not the idea at all. The idea is that the forum is not deceiving anyone and is not powerful in the way a tyrant is powerful — it's that it's nothing, and yet it's the only thing in town that seems to have a will. If the story makes the forum into a manipulator with intentions, I've failed my seminar paper, basically.

Also small thing: I don't need you to tell me afterwards what the story means or how it maps onto Two Ages. Just the story. If you want to add anything, one line at the end with a title you'd give it is fine.
