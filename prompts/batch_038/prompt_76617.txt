I spent six years writing model checkers: tools that evaluate temporal-logic formulas over a structure consisting of states plus an accessibility relation between them. In that setting "possibly P" means nothing more than "some reachable state satisfies P," and nobody on my team ever asked what the states were. We never needed to. Reading the metaphysics of modality on my own now, I find that the question we never had to ask is the question the field is about, and that the candidate answers differ so much in kind that I don't yet know how to compare them. Before I take a position on whether Lewis-style realism about concrete possible worlds is worth believing, or whether some cheaper substitute does the same work, I want the acceptance criteria written down — the specification, not a review of the products.

So: what must an ontology of modality deliver in order to count as adequate? Give me the requirements list, and for each requirement the concrete case or consideration that motivates it. State those cases as specific sentences or scenarios rather than as slogans, and where you can, state them without modal vocabulary — an acceptance test that quietly presupposes the thing under test is not a test.

Two further things I want for every requirement on the list.

First, label it interface or implementation. An interface requirement concerns what the theory owes us as output: truthmakers for our modal claims, correct verdicts on particular cases, some account of how we could come to know any of it. An implementation requirement concerns the machinery used to pay: concrete worlds, sets of sentences, essences, dispositions, whatever else. My suspicion, formed from watching people argue past one another in this area, is that several of the most famous objections are implementation complaints wearing interface clothing. Sort them out and say which is which.

Second, mark whether the requirement is non-negotiable or a preference of one camp. I particularly want your judgment on two points: whether a theory is obliged to analyze modality without using modal notions anywhere in the analysis, or whether that demand is itself camp doctrine; and whether the theory owes us anything at all about claims concerning particular individuals — that this specific bridge could have collapsed — as opposed to claims about how things generally might have been, de re versus de dicto, if I have those labels the right way round.

Then tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidates, and whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. If some subset is, that is the part I most want: which requirement has to go, what giving it up costs, and which one the field usually sacrifices.

One possible requirement I want your reasoning about rather than your guess at: in model checking, two properties that hold at exactly the same states are the same property, and that is perfectly acceptable for verification and plainly wrong for belief reports. Must an ontology of modality do better — must it separate possibilities that come out identical under a worlds-based semantics? If yes, say what it costs the theory; if no, say what the theory is thereby conceding it cannot handle.

Finish with a short pass over the main contenders: the concrete-worlds view, at least one substitute that builds worlds out of something less extravagant, and whatever you take to be the strongest alternative that does not traffic in worlds at all. One paragraph each against the list. No reading list, please — I'll track down sources myself.
