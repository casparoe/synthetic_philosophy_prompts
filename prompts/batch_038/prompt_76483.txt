So, two months into a new job as assistant curator at a house museum — the estate of a guy who made his money in textiles in the 1890s — and the board has hired consultants to redo the interpretive plan so it actually deals with his record: the strike-breaking, the company housing that was segregated by desgin, all of it. Two of our volunteer docents, both in their seventies, have been giving the same tour for about thirty years, and they are taking this personally. One cried in the staff meeting. The other sent me a four-page email arguing that the house was a home and not a courtroom. And I've been driving home each night rereading Ishiguro's Remains of the Day, which I first read in high school and had totally misremembered as a quiet love story, and I can't stop seeing our docents in Stevens — and myself in, well, I'm not sure who I'm in, which is part of why I'm asking.

Could you read the novel through Nietzsche's Untimely Meditation on history and life (whichever title you like — use and abuse, advantage and disadvantage, doesn't matter to me)? I don't want a summary of the essay, I've read it. I want the three modes applied to the book specifically. So: is Stevens an antiquarian at all? He reveres Darlington Hall, but he doesn't seem to care about the place or the lineage the way Nietzsche's antiquarian cares about his town and his ancestors — what he reveres is a conception of dignity and the great butlers who came before him, which feels to me more like monumental history turned inward and pointed at a profession instead of at a people. Does Nietzsche's trichotomy have a slot for that, or is the novel exposing a genuine gap in it? And then the part I actually care about: Stevens's whole narration is a defence of Darlington against the charge of being a Nazi sympathiser — the conference, Ribbentrop in the evening, the two Jewish housemaids sent away, the libel suit that finished him — and by the seafront bench at the end he has lost the argument with himself. Is that critical history working on a man, or is it the demonstration that critical history is unavailable to someone who *is* the thing on trial, since Nietzsche's own worry was that we're descendants of what we condemn and Stevens has literally no self left over after the verdict? I also can't tell what to make of the bantering. He resolves to go home and practise being funny for Mr Farraday, and I can't work out whether that's what Nietzsche means by plastic power — healing wounds, rebuilding broken forms out of one's own shape, growing out of oneself — or just a smaller, sadder, better-lit version of the same servitude, or a third thing the essay has no name for. Please say which and say why.

Two more things, and don't soften either. First, where does the reading strain? The book is narrated by a self-deceiver, so the past it hands us is corrupted at the source before anyone gets to judge it, and I don't think Nietzsche ever imagined an agent with no usable evidence about his own life — tell me what Ishiguro shows there that the essay's framework can't handle, and where you'd have to distort the novel to keep the Nietzsche going. Second, the uncomfortable one: Nietzsche thinks too much history kills life and he has real praise for the unhistorical, for the animal that can forget and sit inside its moment. By that standard, are my docents healthier than I am? Is "a home, not a courtroom" a defensible Nietzschean position rather than just denial, and if it is, what does that imply about the interpretive plan I'm helping write — because I suspect the board is doing critical history in bad faith for a press release and the docents are doing antiquarian piety in good faith, which feels like the wrong way round. No need to quote the book at me, I don't have my copy with me, and I don't want page numbers. Just tell me what you think.
