I lead the adult formation series at my congregation, and next month's ninety-minute session has been given to me on the question of whether religious arguments belong in political debate. Nothing has been assigned and I cannot assign anything; the room will hold perhaps thirty people, roughly half of whom suspect that this topic is a courteous way of telling them to keep quiet, and roughly half of whom regard the question as already settled against them. I have a whiteboard and no handouts. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation.

The live case I intend to build on is local: our town council is deciding whether to renew the contract under which a faith-based charity runs the municipal overnight shelter, on terms permitting the charity to apply a religious test in hiring and to decline to house unmarried couples in the same room. Both camps in the room have a stake in it, and both have reasons.

What I am asking for:

1. The order of the pieces, with the dependency structure made explicit — what has to be in place before the next thing can do any work. In particular, tell me whether the fact that reasonable people persistently disagree about the good life must be established before any claim about legitimacy can bite, or whether opening there is a trap that hands the first half of the room a reason to tune out.

2. A verdict on my carrying case. Can the shelter contract sustain ninety minutes, or must it be replaced or supplemented? State what a case for this idea has to have and what it must lack in order to work; my hunch is that it must be a case in which the religiously grounded position is not obviously repellent and the secular opposition is not obviously neutral, but I want your specification rather than mine.

3. I have seen this taught two ways: through the metaphor of translating religious reasons into public ones, and by opening from church–state separation. I think both are wrong. Tell me which is more damaging and why, and whether a third framing should open the session instead.

4. A misconception map. Mine so far: that the view amounts to a ban on religious speech in politics; that it is establishment-clause material rebranded; that secular reasons are neutral and so the justificatory burden falls on one side only; that supplying public reasons is a form of disguise or bad faith; that the whole apparatus applies exclusively to believers. Add the ones I have missed, and for each say whether it must be headed off before it forms or after, at which point in the sequence, and by what move.

5. Someone will say that the rule is not neutral either — that insisting on public reasons is itself one contested view of how to live, no less comprehensive than the ones it excludes. Say where that objection belongs in the sequence and what can honestly be done with it in the time available.

6. Scope: it may be that a municipal service contract is not the sort of question this ideal governs at all. Tell me how to handle that live possibility without the session collapsing into a seminar on where the line gets drawn.

7. A closing check — one question I can put to the room whose answers will show whether they have grasped the difference between a reason being religious in its origin and a reason being religious in the sense that only those who share the faith could accept it, as distinct from merely feeling better or worse about their own side.

8. What to cut. Of the original position, the overlapping consensus, the burdens of judgment, the internal debate about how inclusive the ideal should be, and the standing criticisms of it, say which must enter anyway as a one-sentence premise and which can go entirely, and what I should say out loud about the omissions.

Please commit. I want the choices made — the case, the order, the framing, the placement of each misconception, the closing question — each with the reason you settled on it, not a menu left for me to weigh. Where a competent alternative plan would genuinely diverge from yours, flag the one or two such points and say which version you would run and why. And if you think my diagnosis of the room, or my candidate case, is mistaken, say so plainly rather than building the plan around it.
