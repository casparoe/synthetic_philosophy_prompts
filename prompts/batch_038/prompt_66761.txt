I teach a graduate seminar on population ethics and the long-term future, and this year two of my students have independently written papers concluding, from the simulation hypothesis, that we should stop taking the far future so seriously. They arrive at the same place by what look to me like different routes, and the seminar has degenerated into a trading of intuitions rather than an argument. I want to give the class a disciplined instrument for comparing such arguments, and I intend to use my own list as the exercise. The setting is Bostrom's simulation argument (2003) in its trilemma form: either almost no civilizations at our level survive to a posthuman stage capable of running high-fidelity ancestor simulations, or almost none of those that survive are interested in running them, or else almost all observers with experiences like ours are simulated. Let us use "fanaticism" in the technical sense the seminar has adopted: a willingness to accept large and certain present costs in exchange for small probabilities of astronomical far-future value. The question I want each route scored on is narrow — whether accepting the third horn of the trilemma lowers the expected value of far-future-focused projects relative to near-term ones.

Here are the five routes I have assembled, in my own formulations rather than anyone else's. First, the runtime route: a simulation's remaining duration is fixed by its creators' purposes and resources rather than by physics or by our own prudence, and a project of historical or sociological interest is plausibly closed down once the interesting part is over, so the far future actually available to us is short and the astronomical scale on which fanaticism depends is simply not there. Second, the redundancy route: if we are simulated, there are presumably vast numbers of simulated civilizations at roughly our stage, and nothing one of them does can change what the others do, so our marginal contribution to the total value at stake is negligible and we should stop treating our branch as special. Third, the extrapolation route: a simulation need only be rendered in the detail its inhabitants require, so our grounds for assuming that nature's regularities persist unchanged into regimes no one has yet probed are weaker than we suppose — and long-range forecasting is precisely such an extrapolation. Fourth, the complicity route: if we inhabit an ancestor simulation, then bringing about a vast simulated future means creating astronomically many beings who exist for someone else's purposes and can be terminated at will, so the far future looks less like a prize to be maximized than a wrong to be avoided, and accelerating toward it is reckless rather than obligatory. Fifth, the witness route: if the third horn is true, then some civilization did survive to a posthuman stage, did so at least once, and had both the means and the interest to run simulations of the kind we appear to be in, which makes the simulation hypothesis evidence that the transition we most fear is traversable, and so raises rather than lowers the probability that our own descendants get there.

For each of the five, please assign a signed score on this scale: +2 strongly dampens fanaticism, +1 mildly dampens it, 0 makes no difference to the choice between far-future and near-term projects, −1 mildly inflames it, −2 strongly inflames it. "Dampens" is to be read strictly as the comparative claim above, not as "is worrisome" or "raises interesting doubts." Return exactly five numbered items, one per route in the order given, each stating the score followed by one or two sentences of justification, with no introduction, no summary, and no closing remarks. If you judge that two of my routes are a single consideration wearing different clothes, say so inside the relevant item rather than in a separate note; and I would much rather have five committed scores than five hedges, since the point of the exercise is to make the class argue about where the numbers go. For what it is worth, my own inclination is that the extrapolation route is a wash and the witness route is the only one with real force — but I suspect I am rationalizing, because I find the simulation hypothesis philosophically absorbing and would like it to have practical consequences. Please do not include references or a reading list; I will supply those myself.
