I am in the second year of a master's programme that combines modern European history with philosophy of the historical sciences, and this term's seminar on historical explanation has produced a standoff I would like to resolve on paper before our last session. Half the room treats the psychology of hindsight bias as a standing indictment of narrative history; the other half, most of whom have actually spent time in state archives, thinks that literature is being read by people who do not know what historians do all day. We have been asked to stage the disagreement rather than re-litigate it, and I have volunteered to write the script. What I want is a dialogue of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words between two equally formidable people who disagree about whether narrative form in historical explanation is an epistemic virtue or an epistemic hazard. The first is a historian of late-communist Central and Eastern Europe whose working life has been spent in party-committee minutes, internal economic reports and secret-police files. Her view — I have been calling it record-constrained narrativism, a label I invented for the handout, so feel free to use it or replace it — is that a narrative is not an ornamental frame around an independent list of causes but a claim about how the surviving record came to exist: a good account implies things about which documents ought to be there, in what order, with which silences and which internal contradictions, and it can therefore be embarrassed by the archive. On this view, conditioning on a known outcome is legitimate in history for much the reason it is legitimate in geology or in a homicide investigation, and a strong sense that events were coming is sometimes a discovery about the world rather than a symptom in the discoverer. The second character is a researcher in judgment and decision-making who has run experiments in which trained historians were shown pre-crisis assessments and then asked, after learning the outcome, what they would have predicted beforehand. His view is that felt coherence is manufactured by machinery tuned for tracking other people's intentions and for justifying outcomes to an audience, not by machinery tuned for recovering causal structure, so a story's ability to absorb surprises is evidence about the storyteller. He should press two specific objections: that her archival test cannot fail, since a missing document is always explicable as destroyed or as never written, and that the files she works in were produced by officials who were already narrating their own durability to one another, so conditioning on the archive and on the outcome counts the same story twice.

The spine of the piece should be this stipulation: the two of them agree entirely about the psychology. The historian grants that knowing an outcome inflates people's retrospective estimates of how likely it was, that the effect appears in professionals and not only in undergraduates, that it survives being warned about, and that her own judgment is not exempt; she disputes only what follows from it. That is deliberate, because I do not want the standard script in which the historian simply denies the experiments — the interesting question is whether the psychology licenses the scepticism. Then I would like the case they argue over to be the collapse of the communist regimes in Eastern Europe in 1989, useful precisely because in 1988 essentially no professional forecaster gave the sequence that followed any serious probability, while a few years later the same events were routinely described as overdetermined; his opening move can be that the profession's memory of 1989 is now a story it tells about itself. About a third of the way through, please shift them onto a much older and much thinner case — the disintegration of the Late Bronze Age palace systems in the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE — where there is barely any record left to constrain anything, and let that shift actually move the argument: I want to see whether record-constrained narrativism survives in a low-evidence setting or has to retreat to a weaker claim, and whether the sceptic gains anything there that he did not already have. Neither of them should win. End on a precisely stated crux that they cannot resolve, and make it a crux I could hand to my seminar as a discussion question in its own right.

A few further requirements. Do not let either character become a strawman: each should get the argument the other would most dread, and each should answer what was actually said rather than a generic version of it, so I would like at least three points where one of them visibly revises or narrows a formulation in response to the other, even where the conclusion does not change. Please include no citations, no bibliography, no named monographs and no invented study titles; if the sceptic appeals to an experiment, have him describe it in his own words, and if the historian appeals to archival practice, have her describe the practice rather than cite it. Keep the register formal and the stage directions minimal. Finally, and importantly for how I intend to use this: after the dialogue, add a note of about 200 words separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on these questions, so that I do not present a disputed position to the seminar as though it were agreed. I would like to know which of the moves your characters make reflect real consensus — about the robustness of hindsight bias, say, or about whether counterfactual reasoning is a legitimate historical method, or about whether narrative form carries explanatory content at all — and which are live disputes, including disputes about how the question ought to be framed in the first place. Where you are unsure, say so plainly instead of smoothing it over, and flag anywhere the dialogue takes a liberty with the ideas or leans on a contested reading of them for the sake of the exchange.
