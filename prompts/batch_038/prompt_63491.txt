You are a professional philosopher specializing in social epistemology, and you have been retained as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration between two camps that have spent a decade arguing past each other over the epistemology of conspiracy theories. Your task is not to referee the dispute and not to summarize the literature, but to produce the single document the collaboration should have yielded: the one both camps could put their names to. Think carefully and at length before writing. Begin with a private brainstorming section, clearly marked as such, in which you list the points at which you believe the two sides are merely disagreeing about words, about burden of proof, or about which sample of cases is representative; then commit to the formal draft, revising whatever your brainstorming shows to be unbalanced.

The two camps are as follows.

**Camp A (structural skeptics).** They hold that allegations of large-scale covert coordination carry a defect that is not merely a function of how much evidence happens to be available in any single case. Their reasons: such hypotheses are, in practice, adjusted indefinitely to absorb counterevidence, so that the absence of the expected leak is itself explained as competence or as complicity; they posit a unified, monolithic agent where events admit of fragmented and mundane explanations; they are advanced disproportionately by people with little access to the primary records and much access to one another; and the institutional accounts they displace have, on the whole, a track record produced by internal auditability and by competitors with an interest in exposure. Camp A therefore thinks a defeasible presumption in favor of the institutional account is earned rather than assumed, and that this presumption is defeated case by case by the kind of documentation that eventually surfaced for the FBI's COINTELPRO, the CIA's MKUltra, the Tuskegee syphilis study, Iran–Contra, and the tobacco industry's decades-long coordination around the manufacture of doubt.

**Camp B (particularists).** They hold that conspiracy is an ordinary causal mechanism, routinely available to actors with resources and something to hide, and that any prior penalty on conspiracy-shaped hypotheses is a bias in favor of the powerful. Their reasons: the very cases Camp A cites as exceptions were themselves called conspiracy theories by respectable commentators while they were true but undocumented; the demand that an allegation be supported by leaked documents misunderstands what an allegation is for, since the point of asking is precisely to trigger the investigation that would produce documents; the diagnostic markers Camp A relies on were shared by the true cases before exposure, so they cannot be doing the epistemic work Camp A assigns them; and the correlational psychology of conspiracist belief, whatever it shows about believers as a population, cannot make any individual belief less likely to be true. Camp B thinks the label "conspiracy theory" now functions mainly as a conversational stop sign, and that Camp A's presumption survives only because it is never stated precisely enough to be tested.

Draft the joint statement in four sections.

**I. Shared commitments.** Numbered claims, each phrased so that a committed member of either camp could sign it without embarrassment. These must be substantive rather than methodological platitudes; they should include what makes an allegation conspiracy-shaped at all, what the historical record of documented covert programs establishes and what it does not, what the psychological literature on conspiracy belief can and cannot bear, and the possibility that each camp's favored policy of belief produces characteristic errors in both directions. If you cannot find genuinely shared ground in an area, say so explicitly rather than manufacturing thin agreement.

**II. Residual disagreements.** A table with these columns: a neutral label for the disagreement; the disagreement stated in wording Camp A's representative would accept as a fair rendering of their own view; the same stated in wording Camp B's representative would accept; and your judgment of whether the disagreement is substantive or at least partly verbal, with a one-sentence reason. Aim for five to eight rows. Exclude disagreements that amount to differences of emphasis, rhetorical register, or which cases each camp likes to discuss.

**III. Cruxes.** For each crux give: the sub-question, stated so that settling it would move at least one camp; the test, dataset, thought experiment, or argument both camps would agree in advance bears on it; each camp's prediction, recorded before any result is known; and what outcome would actually change each camp's mind, including, where applicable, the frank admission that no outcome would. Include at least the following four, and add any I have missed.

1. The concealment–sample problem. Exposed conspiracies are necessarily a biased sample, since successful concealment is by construction unobserved. What, if anything, follows about base rates from a sample selected on exposure? Note that both camps accept the relevance of actuarial leak modeling of the kind David Robert Grimes published in 2016, which estimates how long a secret survives as a function of the number of people in on it and calibrates on cases that did come out. State each camp's reading of what such a model can and cannot license, given that its calibration set has the same selection bias.
2. Whether the markers Camp A treats as diagnostic can be shown to separate warranted from unwarranted conspiracy beliefs when applied *ex ante*, to cases whose outcomes the drafter does not already know.
3. Whether testimony from institutions deserves a defeasible presumption, and whether that presumption is uniform across kinds of institution or varies with the institution's exposure to rival scrutiny.
4. Whether a definition of the target term can be given that is neither self-sealing (builds in falsity) nor so broad that it covers every claim about covert coordination, and how much of the decade-long dispute would evaporate if it could.

**IV. Drafter's note.** A short passage in your own voice identifying the single disagreement you judge most productive, and stating what would have to be true for one camp to be substantially right.

Constraints. Write in extended prose and tables, not bullet fragments. Extend equal charity: any formulation you would reject as a strawman of one camp is disqualified. Do not adjudicate, score points, or add a concluding verdict on who is right. Do not supply a bibliography, footnote apparatus, list of references, or named attributions beyond the single study mentioned above; everything else must be argued from the structure of the positions and from historical cases you describe in your own words, without quotation. Where you suspect a claim cannot be signed by both camps in identical wording, present it twice, labeled as contested wording, rather than smoothing it into something vacuous. Target roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words.
