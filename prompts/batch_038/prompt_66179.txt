Hi — I'm a PhD student in public policy (transport planning mostly), and to pay the bills I've been working part-time at a national infrastructure funding body for the last two years. I've gotten stuck on something in their appraisal rules and I can't work out whether it's philosophically coherent or just a fudge everyone has agreed to live with. Sorry, this is long. I don't need references or a reading list — I roughly know where the ideas come from, I just want the conceptual lay of the land.

Here's the practice. When a project sponsor submits a cost estimate, they sign it personally as their own best estimate of what the thing will cost. Then the guidance says: before that number goes anywhere, add an optimism-bias uplift. It's a fixed percentage that depends on the category of project — non-standard buildings get the biggest multiplier, standard buildings and roads get less, and so on — and on how far the project has gotten through the approval stages. Early stage, big uplift; the more the design is firmed up and the more of the identified risks have been retired, the smaller it gets. Crucially, the uplift money isn't added to the sponsor's budget. It's held centrally by the funding department as a reserve, and the project has to ask permission to draw on it case by case as problems actually materialize. And the sponsor who signed the original number is the one whose career takes the hit if the project overruns.

Two things I've watched people do with this, which is where my confusion starts. First, since the uplift is a percentage of your own submitted number, the cheapest way to reduce how much gets held back from you is to submit a higher base estimate — so a rule whose entire purpose is to fight optimism quietly rewards the sponsor who pads on purpose. Second, people fight hard over which category their project gets sorted into, because the category sets the multiplier, and they always fight for the one with the smaller uplift. Nobody thinks this is cheating; it's just how you work the form.

So what does this apparatus presuppose? Start with the status of the signed number. It doesn't seem like it can be the sponsor's actual belief, because the system treats it as something that has to be mechanically corrected by a table and at the same time as something the sponsor is personally answerable for. Is the practice assuming the inside estimate is a belief, a bargaining position, a performance of confidence, or a piece of evidence about the project? Those can't all be true at once, and I don't see which one the machinery needs.

Then there's what it assumes about probability itself. The table looks like it's saying the right number for a single project is just a function of where it sits in the distribution of similar past projects — as if the individual case has no seperate fact of the matter beyond the group. But if that were really the view, why require an inside estimate at all, and why make the uplift proportional to it rather than flat? Multiplying by the sponsor's own figure seems to concede that the inside view carries information, while the existence of the table says it can't be trusted with any. Is there a version of this that resolves the tension, or is the practice just holding two incompatible pictures of what a project-level probability is?

Related: where is the bias supposed to live? One reading is that it's in the planners' heads, a cognitive distortion you debias with a corrective factor. Another is that its not anybody's fault — the projects that reach approval were selected precisely because their estimates looked good, so the reference class is adversely selected and no individual forecaster is biased at all, they're all just the survivors. Do those two readings license the same machinery? I'd think the second one would demand something pretty different, especially about the sign-off ritual and the career consequences, which only make sense if there's an individual who could have done better.

And what about the asymmetry — overruns get punished, underruns basically don't get rewarded, so the incentive structure leans toward pessimism even though the stated goal is accuracy. Is the practice committed to thinking a falsely optimistic estimate is worse than a falsely pessimistic one of the same magnitude, and if so is that supposed to be an epistemic claim or just a claim about who eats the cost?

Tell me what the practice strictly requires versus what it merely happens to fit well with. My suspicion is that it's much more underdetermined than people assume and that several quite different epistemologies would license exactly the same form, which would mean the form isn't evidence for any of them. And then tell me what would actually have to change if you replaced the fixed table with something like having sponsors report a full distribution and scoring them on calibration afterward, or with a market over final costs. Would either of those be a more honest version of the same idea, or a different idea entirely that smuggles in its own set of commitments?
