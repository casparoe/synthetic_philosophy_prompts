Six months ago I took over the strain-improvement programme for a two-member synthetic consortium that we run in serial batch culture, and I must now defend a manuscript whose central claim my own coauthors cannot agree about.

The system is simple. Strain A hydrolyses a polysaccharide and releases soluble sugar; strain B converts that sugar into the product we sell and in doing so removes a metabolite that inhibits A's growth. Neither performs well alone. We propagated ninety-six replicate communities for eighteen months under two regimes. In the first, at each transfer we ranked the replicates by product titre and passed the top quartile intact, preserving each community's composition and floc structure. In the second, we pooled all ninety-six replicates, homogenised the pool, and drew the next inoculum from it, so that between-community differences were erased before propagation and only per-cell representation across the pool determined what carried forward.

The regimes diverged as the textbooks predict. Whole-community transfer raised titre by roughly forty per cent; the pooled line lost about a third of that gain and drifted toward a B-dominated community that overconsumes sugar and stalls. Two features of the result are awkward, however. First, most of the gain in the first regime came from communities that collapsed below our culling threshold and were never transferred at all — differential community death rather than differential community reproduction, and nothing in that regime produces an offspring community in any recognisable sense, since a transfer is a volumetric sample and not a propagule carrying a developmental programme. Second, the phenotype disfavoured under pooled transfer is not a distinct genotype: B switches reversibly between a productive and a fast-growing state, so even the individual-level units are not stable objects. Running our own frequency data through contextual analysis compounds the problem, because depending on how the partition is drawn the same numbers can be read as higher-level selection or as a cross-level byproduct of lower-level change.

Then the referee reports arrived, and their dispute is a replica of a stalemate that has run since Williams in 1966. One referee holds that we have demonstrated genuine community-level selection, invoking the distinction between multilevel selection where fitness attaches to individuals in a group-structured environment and multilevel selection where groups themselves have fitness and leave group offspring. The other holds that we have demonstrated nothing of the kind: the same data can be repartitioned entirely at the level of individual cells with no loss of content, the higher-level description is bookkeeping, and Dawkins, Hull, Sober and Wilson, and Okasha have traded versions of this charge and this reply for decades without anyone moving.

I do not want a tour of that literature. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, and my hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, not a conclusion — is that it is not about any empirical or mathematical fact, since both camps accept the same population-genetic results and can translate each other's models into their own idiom. Rather, the camps mean different things by selection: for one it is a causal relation between an entity's properties and its representation in later generations, for the other it is a way of partitioning observed change, and the partitions are causally neutral. On top of that I suspect a second, unspoken disagreement about whether reproduction must be a property of the candidate unit at all, which is precisely what makes my first regime so hard to classify.

Is that diagnosis correct, or is it too charitable? Tell me whether this is a substantive disagreement, a verbal one turning on the word selection, two questions answered under one heading — one about what causes change, one about how to describe it — a dispute about which entities are eligible to bear fitness, or a question that is ill-posed rather than merely difficult. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims, and say what would count as progress on that picture: a distinction, a theorem, a formal result about the equivalence or non-equivalence of the partitions, an experiment of the kind I have described run differently, or a change of subject. If your view is that no progress should be expected, argue for that. Finally, given your diagnosis, is there a fact of the matter about which of my two regimes involved selection at the level of the community, or is my choice of description free once the frequencies and the titres are reported honestly?
