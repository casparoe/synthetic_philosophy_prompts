Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words, confined to a single location and a single night shift, in which the following piece of reasoning supplies the plot. The reasoning must be legible to a reader who has never encountered it, but it should be carried by two people arguing about what to do next rather than by narration addressed to the reader.

**The setup.** A storm-surge barrier stands between the sea and a low-lying town. The operator on watch trusts the authority's hydraulic model, but she has adopted a standing hedge against the possibility that the model is wildly wrong: her belief about any event is not a single number but a family of numbers, every member of the family built as ninety-five parts the official model and five parts sheer possibility — for any way the world might be, some member of the family spends the entire five percent on it.

For the event she actually cares about tonight, the inner gate seal failing under the surge, the model gives two percent. Her family therefore spreads her belief from 1.9 percent to 6.9 percent. She and the officer she is relieving both work by an agreed trigger, arrived at over years of arguments about what a hollow alarm costs against what an unwarned flood costs: the siren pays for itself once the chance of failure passes one in ten. At 1.9 to 6.9 percent, no member of her family reaches the trigger, and both officers agree the answer tonight is no siren.

**The observation.** Sometime after midnight, a redundant telemetry channel runs its scheduled self-test and returns a code that the manual identifies as a fault in the testing apparatus itself, not in anything the channel measures. Under the model, that code appears on about one night in a hundred, and it is unconnected to the condition of the seal. Everyone in the room knows this. What makes the night turn is that her five percent allowance for arbitrary error includes members of the family for which no such guarantee holds: conditioning on an event the model calls one-in-a-hundred shrinks the denominator of the mixture to roughly six percent, so the member that spends its five percent on the worst story now puts the failure of the seal at about eighty-four percent, while the member that spends its five percent elsewhere drags the lower bound down to about three tenths of one percent. Her belief about the seal has gone from 1.9–6.9 percent to 0.3–84 percent on news that means nothing. The same hedge that was supposed to protect her against being wildly wrong is precisely what lets a meaningless code move her this far, and the story should let at least one character see that.

**The divergence.** The two officers now part company, because they hold the same family of beliefs but act on it by different rules. One works by worst case: what governs is the most dangerous member of the family, so eighty-four percent is far past one in ten and the siren is now required — though an hour ago the same rule forbade it. The other works by a rule of elimination: an option is ruled out only if the alternative beats it at every member of the family. Before the code, not sounding beat sounding at every member, so the rule was decisive; now the interval straddles one in ten, sounding wins at some members and not sounding at others, and the rule simply stops speaking. A piece of pure noise has converted one officer's settled refusal into a command and the other's settled refusal into silence.

Give the officer who will not sound the siren a real argument rather than a bad one — for instance, that a rule which acts on the top of the interval will empty the town for a burnt-out lamp and a jammed printer, and that three hollow alarms in a season guarantee nobody comes for the fourth, so the worst-case rule is not cautious but reckless across a year even if it looks prudent across one night. Let the other officer answer, and let the answer be that this is the night.

**Constraints.**

- The code must remain meaningless. Do not redeem it: no later discovery that the test apparatus was in fact picking up something real, no ambiguous flicker in the final paragraph that lets the reader suspect the alarm was justified after all. The weight of the story rests on the widening being genuine while its cause is empty.
- Do not let the night referee the argument between the two rules. End before the surge crests, or otherwise leave it impossible to say who was right.
- Keep the technical vocabulary out of the story entirely. Words like imprecise probability, credal set, dilation, maximin, and admissibility should not appear, and no character should be shown deriving a formula for more than a sentence or two. The numbers should surface as things people say aloud, write on a board, or refuse to look at.
- Give the control room a body: weather on the windows, machinery noise, bad coffee, the particular boredom of a shift that had already decided nothing was going to happen.
- A decision theorist reading it should recognize exactly what has happened to the operator's beliefs and exactly why the two rules come apart; a reader who has never heard of any of this should simply read a night shift coming apart between two people.

The barrier and the storm are a suggestion rather than a requirement. If you relocate the story, keep the three load-bearing pieces — a precise model, a fixed allowance for the model being arbitrarily wrong, and an observation that is improbable under the model and empty of information — and keep the resulting numbers in the same relation to one another.
