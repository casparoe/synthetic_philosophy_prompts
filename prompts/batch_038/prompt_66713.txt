I'm in a formal epistemology seminar and we just spent two weeks on imprecise probabilities, so I'm trying to figure out what I actually think by just writing down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases. Here they are:

1. A coin whose bias nobody has ever checked and that I have zero information about: I refuse to say 0.5 for heads. I also refuse a bet on heads at even odds, and a bet on tails at even odds. Refusing both doesn't feel irrational to me, but a sharp 0.5 agent seems stuck taking at least one.
2. Same coin, except I've watched it come up heads three times. Now I'll take the bet on heads at even money. So the mushiness is suppose to shrink as evidence comes in.
3. An urn with 90 balls, 30 red, the other 60 either black or yellow in a split nobody tells me. Bet on red vs. bet on black: I take red. Bet on red vs. bet on yellow: I take red again. Black and yellow are perfectly symmetric so I've got no more reason to favor either, and I still don't want either one. But I also think somebody who'd pay $10 to dodge that ambiguity is being dumb — I just can't say why.
4. A case where I learn something that has literally nothing to do with the question and my range for the question gets wider instead of narrower (our prof called it dilation). Verdict: bug, not feature. You shouldn't ever get less confident for free.
5. A poll comes out using a sampling method I don't understand at all, and my range for the result widens a lot. Verdict: that's fine, maybe even correct. Cases 4 and 5 are the pair I can't reconcile — both are "learn something, get less precise," and I want to call one broken and the other right.
6. Is a pile of 5,000 grains a heap? My credence is mushy here too, but it feels like a different animal: the mush is in the words, not in my evidence. And whether the keys are in the kitchen — I haven't looked, my range is wide, and here I think I'm criticizable for it, unlike the coin where I genuinely have nothing to go on.
7. A doctor with a genuinely wide risk estimate has to decide whether to operate. I don't think she's irrational for acting as if the risk were one number, even though I don't think her belief state should be one.

Is there a principle — or a small handful of candidates — that gets all or most of these? I'd want it stated properly rather than gestured at, with each clause tied to the case that motivated it. Then tell me what it predicts for a case I didn't list: learning the result of a medical test whose sensitivity and specificity are only known to lie somewhere in a range. And be honest about which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I half-suspect it's 4 or 5, but maybe it's actually 6.
