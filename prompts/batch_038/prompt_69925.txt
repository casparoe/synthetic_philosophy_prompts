Here's roughly what he said — I'm paraphrasing from memory but I'm confident I've got the substance and the order of his points:

"Zorn's lemma says that any partially ordered set has a maximal element, i.e. a biggest one. That sounds obviously false, because the natural numbers in their usual order have no biggest element, but the hypothesis saves it: the lemma only applies when every chain has an upper bound, and the naturals fail that, since all of N is a chain with nothing above it. Zorn's lemma is basically a corollary of the axiom of choice, a weaker and handier version of it, which is why textbooks state it instead. And it can't be proved — Gödel showed back in the 30s that its independent of the standard axioms — so the theorems that lean on it, Hahn–Banach, Tychonoff, 'every vector space has a basis,' are really just things we've collectively agreed to pretend are true. The clean demonstration is Banach–Tarski: Zorn's lemma is what lets you cut a ball into five pieces and reassemble them into two balls each the same size as the one you started with. That's a flat-out contradiction, so Zorn's lemma is false, and that's why some mathematicians refuse to touch it. The consolation is that it doesn't matter, because anything about finite or countable objects can be done with no choice at all — Zorn only shows up in weird corners of functional analysis."
