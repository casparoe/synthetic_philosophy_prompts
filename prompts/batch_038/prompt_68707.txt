I'm in the second half of a seminar on historical judgment and foreign policy — the weeks devoted to how analogies get used and abused: Munich 1938 read forward onto later crises, the domino theory read onto Vietnam, and the Thucydides trap, with its count of sixteen rivalries between a rising and a ruling power of which twelve ended in war. Our instructor's line is a psychological one: analogies mislead because people retrieve the source case that suits the conclusion they already wanted, and then reason from it. I tried to write down a sharper, non-psychological version of the worry, and what came out seems to prove far too much. Here it is. (1) A case-based argument from a source S to a target T is cogent for a given person only if that person can identify which of the respects in which S and T resemble each other bear on how T turns out. (2) Which respects bear on T's outcome is fixed by which properties produce that outcome, not by which resemblances strike us as vivid. (3) So to identify the relevant resemblances is to identify certain properties as producing an outcome in T. (4) But a property produces T's outcome only if it is in fact operative in T; and if one knows which outcome-producing properties are operative in T, one knows, or all but knows, how T will turn out. (5) Therefore whoever deploys the analogy either already knows how T will turn out — in which case the analogy does no work — or does not know it, in which case she cannot say which resemblances are relevant, and the analogy is arbitrary. (6) Nothing in this turns on the peculiarities of international politics: the identical dilemma presses on the medical student reasoning from a textbook case to the patient in the clinic, and on any generalization from a sample to a further instance of it. (7) Hence case-based reasoning is never cogent, and the familiar policy analogies fail not because history is an especially resistant domain but because they instantiate a form that cannot work anywhere.

I'm attached to (2) — I don't want relevance to collapse into what happens to strike us — and I'm confident I don't want (7), yet I can't see which step to drop without giving up something I care about. My suspicion is that something slides between (3) and (4): in (3) relevance looks like a claim about causal structure that can be known in general terms, while in (4) it has quietly become a claim about the particular course of events in T, and knowing the mechanism surely doesn't require knowing the outcome, any more than knowing that friction converts kinetic energy into heat requires predicting where the car will stop. But if that is the leak, tell me what (3) or (4) would have to become for the argument to fail, and whether the repaired premise is still strong enough to sustain (1) — because a notion of relevance thin enough to be knowable in advance of the outcome may be exactly the notion (2) forbids.

If instead the argument is valid and the real question is which premise is least costly to abandon, walk me through the options and price them: what does rejecting (1) commit me to about when an analogy is licensed, what does rejecting (2) commit me to about relevance, and what does accepting (5) but refusing (6) require — some principled reason the dilemma bites in history but not in medicine, or the reverse? And please tell me plainly if the honest verdict is that (1) and (2) jointly impose a standard that no ampliative inference can meet, in which case I have produced a skeptical argument about induction wearing a historian's hat, and I would rather hear that now than defend it in seminar. What I most want to know is whether there is a version of the case against historical analogy that keeps the psychological point my instructor makes — that the source case gets chosen to fit a preferred conclusion — without needing a premise that would also condemn the clinic, the courtroom, and the sample survey.
