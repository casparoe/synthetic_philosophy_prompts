I teach a consciousness seminar and I'm trying to get my own verdicts straight before I inflict them on the grad students. Case by case, what I actually think:

Sperling's partial-report setup — array flashed, tone says which row to name, subject nails the cued row and says she saw the rest too but it faded before she could say it. I think there was phenomenology of the whole array, briefly, whether or not any of it was ever available.

The usual ten-minute drive home: she arrived, steered, braked, noticed the roadworks, could have answered questions about all of it — and I think there was nothing it was like for those ten minutes.

The fridge hum: I only notice it once it stops, and I'd say I was experiencing it faintly the whole time. But the observer in the counting/gorilla study who misses the gorilla: I'd say she didn't experience it at all. Same inattention, opposite verdicts, and I can't say why.

Blindsight: the patient denies seeing anything, guesses well above chance, and when pushed describes something like a hunch arriving in her head. I want to say there's an experience there but not a visual one, and I can't cash out that difference.

Split-brain: word to the right hemisphere, the mouth says nothing was seen, the left hand finds the object behind the screen. I say there was an experience that nobody owned up to.

The command-following patient, years past diagnosis, who lights up appropriately when asked to imagine playing tennis. I say, strongly, experience.

Cerebellum: enormous processing, real deficits when it's damaged, and I feel no pull whatever toward thinking its conscious or that it contributes anything to what it's like to be me. I know the comeback will be that it isn't recurrent or integrated enough, but that smells like a principle fitted to the verdict after the fact.

My own half-formed guess is that experience goes with content poised to be used in reasoning and report — which immediately gets the commute and Sperling backwards, so I need help. Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate ones, that captures these? State it precisely, say which case each clause is earning its keep on, and give me its verdict on one I didn't list: a masked prime that steers my behavior but that I could not have reported even had I tried. Then be honest about the price — which of my verdicts your best principle has to overturn, or if you'd rather save all of them, what the extra machinery costs. And tell me where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail: which single consideration, if I pressed it, would make you switch to a different principle?
