I'm a third-year clinical psychology doctoral student, and next week I have to present my theoretical orientation at a lab meeting where half the room does computational modeling of affect and the other half does qualitative work on emotion discourse. To keep the session from dissolving into an argument about vocabulary, I've tried to write down in advance everything I'm actually committed to about what emotions are, rather than gesturing at a general outlook. I'd like an audit of whether the package hangs together before I walk in with it. I'm not after a survey of the literature, and I don't want a defense of whichever view you think is correct; I want you to treat the list as a single position and tell me where it breaks.

Here is the list, with the reasons I hold each item, since the reasons matter for the audit:

1. Emotions are at bottom evaluative stances with propositional structure: to fear is to represent something as dangerous, to be indignant is to represent a situation as containing an undeserved slight. I hold this because emotions are the kind of thing that can be fitting or unfitting, and because we sometimes argue people out of them with evidence.
2. I also hold that this representational character is perceptual rather than judgmental. Emotions are perceptions of value, on analogy with seeing that a surface is red. And I hold the stronger version: perceiving something as dangerous is a way of taking it to be dangerous, of endorsing that content, which is precisely why I think the perceptual model keeps everything the judgment model wanted about responsiveness to reasons.
3. Separately, I hold that what makes an episode emotional rather than merely evaluative is that it is felt. There is a bodily-felt character to being afraid, broadly Jamesian in spirit, and an episode that lacked anything of the sort would not count as an emotion however accurate its content. My working assumption is that this felt character is constituted by registration of peripheral change — heart rate, muscle tension, visceral shift — rather than by any purely central process.
4. On kinds, I'm a naturalist: I don't think the question of what fear is gets settled by competent speakers' intuitions or by conceptual analysis, and I think the categories psychology inherits are partly constituted by the concepts and norms a culture supplies, so that different languages and different labs carve the affective domain at genuinely different joints and none of them is the one true joint.
5. Case verdicts I'm unwilling to surrender: a fourteen-month-old who recoils and screams at an unfamiliar adult is afraid, full stop, and not merely in some proto-emotional or undifferentiated-distress sense; a dog that waits by the door for weeks after its housemate dies is grieving; a spider phobic who sincerely says she knows the spider is harmless but is nevertheless terrified has one emotion rather than a cool judgment plus a separate free-floating feeling; and a person with a complete high spinal lesion who reports vivid terror and vivid grief in the relevant situations is genuinely terrified and genuinely grieving — the interruption of the usual feedback loop does not, in my view, downgrade those episodes to something else.
6. A parity constraint of my own that I want treated as fixed for the purposes of the audit: whatever account I end up with must deliver the same verdict in the paradigm adult case and in the infant and dog cases. I'm not prepared to say that non-linguistic creatures have emotional surrogates.
7. Finally, I hold that emotions can be irrational, unfitting, and in a broad sense incoherent — the phobic's fear is a defect of some kind — while also holding that emotions are not under direct voluntary control and that having one is not by itself a ground for blame. I believe these are compatible, but I can't articulate why, and I'd like to know whether my confidence is misplaced.

Please work through this as a package rather than a menu. For every pair or cluster of commitments that genuinely cannot all be retained, give me the mechanism of the conflict — what specifically in one forces the denial of the other — rather than just naming the two views, and then rank the conflicts by severity with the most serious first. Separately, tell me which of the conflicts I've set up dissolve on inspection and why; I expect at least one of them looks worse than it is, but I don't want you to assume that before checking. For each real conflict, state the cheapest revision that would restore consistency and what that revision costs me in the work I actually use these commitments for: building coding schemes for observed affect, inferring emotion type from self-report, and making cross-species comparisons. Close by identifying the single change that would clear the most conflicts at once, and say plainly what I'd be left holding if I made it — I would much rather learn now that it forces me to abandon something I like than discover that at the lab meeting.
