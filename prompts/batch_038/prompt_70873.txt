I write literary fiction, and the next one is set around a salmon farm and a shrimp hatchery, which is why I spent last month tagging along at an aquaculture welfare workshop on the coast. On the final night two of the attendees got into it at the bar and I basicly transcribed the whole thing in my notebook, because it's exactly the argument I need to put in the book. Neither of them was a strawman, which is the problem.

Setup: Neil runs harvest operations for a mid-size salmon company that installed electrical stunning on its kill barges a few years back, replacing what used to happen, which was crowding fish out of the water and letting them suffocate for minutes. Dara campaigns on farmed fish and crustacean welfare for an advocacy org and thinks the whole enterprise shouldn't exist.

1. Neil opened by saying stunning is a real, measurable improvement — every fish his company kills now loses consciousness before bleed-out instead of asphyxiating, per-animal suffering has dropped sharply, and that shouldn't get waved away just because Dara objects to farming them at all.

2. Dara said that's the wrong unit of account. What matters isn't suffering-per-fish, it's total suffering, which is number of animals times how badly each one fares. His stunning made the product premium and marketable, output grew, and the company now kills considerably more fish a year than it did before the reform — so the total may well be up even though each fish's death got better.

3. Neil said that assumes demand is fixed, which it isn't, and if every farm stunned, the marketing edge would vanish. Then he said her logic proves too much: if welfare improvement can be bad because it grows the industry, then on her own account you'd want fish to suffer *more* so people would stop buying salmon, which is absurd.

4. Dara said she takes the reductio in limited form — she wouldn't campaign for more suffering, but declining to campaign for cruelty isn't the same as calling less cruelty progress. Her real point is that reformers don't get to label something moral progress when its aggregate effect is unknown or possibly negative. And his audit stops at the fence: on the shrimp side the animals get killed in ice slurry, which for a warm-water species looks grim; broodstock get their eyestalks ablated; the salmon are fed wild-caught fish plus wild-caught cleaner fish that die in droves and never appear in any welfare metric. For every animal he stunning humanely, several more died out of sight.

5. Neil said the feed point is about wild fisheries, not about whether stunning a farmed fish is good, and that she keeps sliding between "aquaculture is bad" and "welfare reform is bad," which are different claims. Then he pressed the measurement objection: you can't measure total suffering. Nobody can compare the experience of a stunned salmon to a suffocating one, let alone add shrimp to salmon to cleaner wrasse across species. If your preferred metric is unmeasurable in principle, the only thing anyone can act on is per-animal welfare — and that's the one number his company has actually moved.

6. Dara's last move: unmeasurable isn't unknowable. We know the direction of change in animal numbers and we can rank severities roughly, so we can bound the aggregate even if we can't calculate it. And "we can't measure that, so we act on what we can measure" is exactly the mechanism by which industries end up choosing the metric that flatters them.

They circled this loop twice more and I went to bed. I honestly have no idea who won.

So: go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step, not just overall. Rate each of the six numbered moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right — use something like −2 to +2, or whatever scale is cleaner for you — with a couple of sentences of justification per move. Then give me an explicit verdict on (a) whether per-animal or aggregate is the right unit of moral accounting here, (b) whether the fixed-demand reply in move 3 actually works, (c) whether the proves-too-much reductio lands or whether Dara's "limited form" answer dissolves it, (d) whether the fence/wild-feed scope point is a genuine objection to welfare reform or a change of subject, (e) whether the equivocation charge is fair, and (f) whether the measurement objection is decisive or just practically important. Then tell me what both of them missed — the argument nobody made that would have changed the shape of the whole exchange. And since I have to write these two as intelligent people rather than as positions, say whose view rests on the more fragile load-bearing premise, and name that premise.
