I'm a retired soil chemist, 33 years in a state lab, and since I stopped working I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the whole "did societies collapse or did they just change" business. Book group, mostly ex-engineers and one woman who teaches high school history, and we've been arguing since November. I've finally written down where I actually stand, and I'd like somebody who knows this literature to tell me whether the package hangs together before I read it out loud to these people. Here it is, roughly:

(1) Tainter is the right starting point — his book from the late eighties, where collapse is a rapid, significant loss of an established level of sociopolitical complexity.
(2) On that definition "collapse" is a value-neutral technical term, like "erosion" or "senescence." It measures structure, not whether anybody's life got worse, and it should not be confused with a verdict on a people.
(3) Easter Island is the case that made me suspicious of the standard story. I buy the revisionist line that the palm loss was substantially driven by introduced Polynesian rats and that the island never had the dense, monument-obsessed, resource-hungry population the ecocide version requires.
(4) I'm deeply skeptical of the simulation models people build to "test" hypotheses about past societies — agent-based models of Rapa Nui and the like. Too many free parameters, and the outputs mostly measure the modelers' assumptions about human behavior rather than anything about the past.
(5) What counts as a problem is set inside the society, by its own institutions and values; there's no exogenous list of problems against which you can score a polity. Complexity is always somebody's solution to something they themselves framed as a problem.
(6) Plenty of things called collapses weren't. The Maya are still here, and the abandonment of Cahokia looks to me a lot like a large-scale relocation, which for the families involved may have been an improvement.
(7) Where I land: "collapse" has a normative job baked into it that can't be scrubbed out by defining it technically. When it's applied to a society whose descendants object to the label, the word does political work whether the definer intends it or not, so I'd rather we stopped using it outside of very narrow technical contexts.

Three things I want out of you. First, go pair by pair and tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do — I expect at least one of the tensions I'm privately worried about is not a tension at all, and I probably can't see the one that is. Second, rank the real ones by severity rather than dumping them all on me as equally bad, and for each say what the cheapest revision is and what it costs me intellectually, because some of these I'd rather not give up. Third, and I mean this: if you think the whole exercise is confused, or that one of my numbered claims rests on a muddle about what a definition can and can't do, or that I've described a position nobody could coherently hold, say so plainly. I'd much rather be told the setup is broken than get a polite audit that quietly fixes my framing for me. And if you're unsure about a case fact — the rats, the population numbers, whatever — flag that it's uncertain instead of writing as though it's settled; I spent a career being careful about that and I notice when other people aren't.
