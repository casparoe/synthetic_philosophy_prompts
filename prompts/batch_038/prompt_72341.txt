I run product security at a mid-size fintech — ~400 engineers, we hold customer funds, so the regulators have opinions about everything. For a couple years now I've been getting a species of message that our incident response playbook has no slot for, and I've never seen anyone write up how to actually handle them in practice. I've read a lot of the old rationalist corpus, so I know the puzzles: counterfactual mugging, the XOR blackmail letter, the whole "should you pay the predictor" family. As puzzles they're fine. But I need something a junior analyst who's on call and has never thought about decision theory can run cold, on real money, in under an hour and a half.

The situation. Three to six times a year we get a message from someone claiming to have found a real vulnerability in our systems, with a working proof of concept, and the message has a prediction structure instead of the usual ransomware structure. The key difference: the harm they're describing is already done or already averted, by them, weeks before the letter arrived, on the basis of a prediction about what we'd do when it landed. Nothing we can do now changes which branch we're in. The only live decision is whether to wire the money — typically $25k, though the exposure if the bug is real and it's out there is more like $250k plus a disclosure obligation. And the reason this isn't a flat "never pay extortionists" is that these letters are batch-sent to a bunch of companies at once, the letter states a rule connecting *whether you got a letter at all* to which branch you're in, and there's a semi-public forum where recipients compare notes. So receiving one is itself information, and in principle the stated rule is checkable against data.

Two more things about us the procedure has to swallow. First: we maintain a public commitments page with dated entries — "we do not pay for undisclosed vulnerabilities", "we pay a fixed rate for anything that comes through the program", that sort of thing — including the date each entry was posted and last amended. It's archived, it's indexed, and these people read it. So what our policy *was* on the date they claim to have made the prediction is a lookupable fact, not a guess, and we cannot retroactively adopt a better policy and have it count for this letter. Second: atleast once, the "prediction" turned out to be a departed contractor with read access to our Jira. That's not a predictor, that's surveillance wearing a predictor costume, and it needs different treatment. I need the procedure to tell those apart, or to say plainly that it can't.

So: write me the decision procedure. Numbered questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which branches terminate in "do not engage", which terminate in "pay", and which stop being questions of fact and get handed to me or to general counsel because they're about values or legal exposure rather than anything anyone can look up. Flag — specifically, not vaguely — which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I'd rather have those marked than have the document look tidier than the reasoning under it. Assume whoever's running it has 90 minutes before the escalation SLA, and that anything involving money gets signed off by the CISO and GC together.

I also want you to tell me which distinctions from the literature actually have to survive being flattened into a checklist and which ones I'm allowed to throw away. My guess is that "are we choosing an act or a policy" and "is the correlation causal, evidential, or just them having read our Slack" both have to survive, and that most of the rest is decoration — but argue with me if that's wrong rather than nodding along.

Then run it on three cases:

**Case A.** Letter from a handle we recognize from the recipients' forum. Says they found an auth bypass in our partner-onboarding flow, confirmed with a PoC, and sent the same letter to eleven companies that month. Before sending anything, they predicted for each company whether it would wire $25k within seven days. Separately and independently of that prediction, they decided whether to hand the PoC and a write-up to an exploit acquisition firm — some got handed over, some didn't, and they say that decision had nothing to do with the prediction. Then the rule: they only mail a company if exactly one of those two things is true — either the prediction was "pay", or the PoC went to the acquisition firm. Never both, never neither. So a company holding the letter is either (predicted to pay, PoC not sold) or (predicted not to pay, PoC sold). No threats about the future, no promises to delete anything, no follow-up. Two forum members report outcomes: one paid and says nothing ever surfaced, one refused and saw that bug class used against them five weeks later. n=2, self-reported, unverifiable, possibly invented. What does the procedure do with that, and what does it say given that our commitments page has read "we do not pay for undisclosed vulnerabilities" since March 2023?

**Case B.** A research group with a real reputation posts a standing policy on their site, dated. Each quarter they pick three companies. For each, they predict whether that company, if asked, would hand over a month of unfettered read access to internal telemetry and answer questions honestly about a past incident. If the prediction is yes, they publish a favorable technical profile of the company and send the findings over early — and they never actually ask that company for anything. If the prediction is no, they ask, and the profile runs either way unless the company comes through. We get the ask. The prediction was obviously "no." Cost of coming through: about three weeks of a senior engineer, plus whatever honest answers about an incident two years ago do to our regulatory posture. Nothing in the letter suggests coming through buys us anything this quarter, since the good profile already went to someone else. What does the procedure say — and does the answer change if this group's predictions have been publicly wrong twice before?

**Case C.** A letter with no PoC attached, claiming active in-the-wild exploitation, sent from an address that traces to a jurisdiction where wiring the money would put us straight in front of sanctions counsel. The prediction language is near-verbatim from a template that's been circulating publicly for two years, and it references an internal ticket number that a departed contractor had read access to. This one should fall out of the procedure early. I want to see it say "this is not a predictor, stop doing philosophy and go do incident response" instead of grinding through all the branches — and if your procedure can't distinguish this from Case A in the first three questions, that's a design flaw, tell me so.

For Case A, at least one of the answers should be one I don't like. If the procedure never tells me to do something I find repugnant, I'll assume you reverse-engineered it from my preferences, and that makes it worthless to me.

Last thing: tell me what it can't do. Where it misfires, which counterparts it doesn't cover at all — state actors, exploitation already in progress, a demand tied to an ongoing intrusion — and what I should plan to revise after the first ten times through. And no bibliography, no reading list, no footnotes. I want the procedure, not the syllabus.
