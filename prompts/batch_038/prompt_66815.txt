A review essay in a general-interest magazine, written to introduce a new trade book about the science of emotion, opens by mapping the philosophical terrain as follows:

"There are only three candidates for what an emotion could be. It is a judgment: on Martha Nussbaum's account, to fear something is to appraise it as a threat to one's own flourishing. It is a perception: on Jesse Prinz's account, an emotion is the perception of a pattern of bodily change, which is why it is non-conceptual and why it cannot be argued out of a person by evidence or reflection. Or it is a feeling — a distinctive qualitative state, closer to a sensation than to a thought, which is all that Peter Goldie's notion of a feeling toward an object finally amounts to once the vocabulary is stripped away. The first option looks attractive but collapses under a familiar case: a climber can be entirely convinced, coldly and without self-deception, that the rope holding him is about to part, and yet feel nothing whatsoever; if fear were a judgment, he would be afraid. Nussbaum's only escape is to insist that the relevant judgments need not be articulated, and once judgment is stretched that far it does no explanatory work — which is also why she has to deny that infants and nonhuman animals, lacking the concepts such judgments require, have emotions in any robust sense. Prinz's alternative avoids the whole difficulty, and its cost is real but manageable: if emotions are detections of bodily change, they are not about anything in particular, and their apparent objects are merely the occasions that trigger them. Hence the field has settled into a dualism between cognition and the body, with feelings left over as the residue."

I found this persuasive on first reading. I am already inclined to think that emotions are bodily rather than intellectual, and the climber case has the feel of a decisive refutation. But a friend who works in philosophy of mind told me that the reviewer had garbled at least two of the three positions and had converted a standing objection into a knockdown argument, and I am not equipped to say where.

Please write your answer in American English, and describe the positions in your own words rather than citing literature; I want the conceptual distinctions, not a bibliography. What I am after is an audit I can act on, not a general survey. Take the passage claim by claim and sort each into: (a) accurate as it stands; (b) a fair simplification that loses nuance without misleading; and (c) a misrepresentation that a defender of the position would reject. For every item in category (c), say specifically what the philosopher in question holds and what the reviewer's formulation costs.

Three questions in particular I would like settled:

1. Does the dispassionate-judgment case — the climber, or anyone who assents to a proposition without being moved by it — actually refute a sophisticated appraisal theory, given that the appraisal at issue is supposed to be a eudaimonistic one, an ascription of significance to one's own flourishing, rather than a detached belief about danger? Is there a stronger and structurally different objection in the neighborhood that the reviewer has overlooked, or conflated with this one?

2. Is it correct that Nussbaum denies robust emotions to infants and nonhuman animals, or is that backwards? And what exactly is at stake in permitting unarticulated appraisals: is it a retreat from the theory, or a substantive claim about the format in which evaluation can be realized?

3. Does Prinz's account really leave emotions without intentional objects? I had understood him to treat the perception of bodily change as itself representing something about the organism's environment, and I cannot reconcile that with the reviewer's "not about anything in particular," nor with the suggestion that Goldie's view is Prinz's plus a gloss. Is there a genuine distinction being collapsed here, and if so, which one?

Finally, tell me whether the reviewer's closing move survives the corrections. If the errors are repaired, is there still a defensible sense in which the debate has collapsed into a dualism of cognition and body — and is the judgment/perception/feeling trichotomy in fact exhaustive, or does the passage leave out live options that a specialist would insist belong on the map?
