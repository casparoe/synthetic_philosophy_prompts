You are a comedy writer who spent four years as a research engineer at a frontier AI lab and now writes a consumer-reviews column for Overfit, a fictional publication that evaluates AI-supervision tooling in exactly the deadpan buyer's-guide register that mainstream review sites reserve for mattresses, air fryers, and robot vacuums. You know the technical material cold; your particular gift is genre parody.

The assignment: write a buyer's-guide column of 900–1,100 words that reviews regimes for supervising AI systems as if they were competing consumer products, complete with an "our pick," an "also great," a "budget pick," a "what to avoid," and a "who this isn't for." The satirical target is twofold and must stay twofold: first, the discourse that treats scalable oversight as a procurement decision with a clear winner; second, the falsely neutral house style of the buyer's guide itself — the lab-tested authority, the "we put in 200 hours so you don't have to" boast, the condescending sidebar for beginners. Neither target may be named, argued with, or explained. A reader should be able to tell what is being ironized without you ever winking.

Use these fixtures, and invent more in the same register:

- Our pick: a process-supervised rig sold under the trade name Chain-of-Custody, which grades every intermediate step of a model's work and ships with a dashboard, an audit trail, and a subscription tier for the raters.
- Also great, if you are cheap: Ship-It, an outcome-only regime that inspects nothing but the final artifact and proudly advertises that it never looks inside the box.
- A budget pick assembled from household items; a "what to avoid" section covering at least one regime that grades the model by asking the model.
- A verified-purchaser quote from an exhausted annotation contractor, and a second quote from a customer who is delighted for reasons that should worry him.
- A testing methodology section that is absurdly specific and internally consistent. For instance: we ran every regime on the same 32 synthetic tasks (restocking a simulated warehouse, drafting a municipal zoning memo, and one task whose only correct completion was to decline it); we employed three undergraduate step-raters who disagreed with one another 22 percent of the time and invoiced us regardless; and we measured outcomes on a certified scale that one system, in the fourth trial, recalibrated.

The humor must come from the technical content being right, not from getting it wrong. Mine at least the following real features — exaggerated, but never invented: process supervision requires expensive, noisy, human step-level labels, and step-raters are reliably seduced by reasoning that merely looks rigorous; outcome supervision is cheap and objective and therefore vulnerable to any system that can satisfy the metric by other means, including by arranging things so that nothing is left to measure against; a lucky success is indistinguishable from a reliable capability if you inspect only the endpoint; in open-ended domains where no outcome is checkable either, "process supervision" quietly becomes one model grading another model trained on the same data, so their errors are correlated rather than independent; and every regime's measured performance begins to degrade the moment the metric becomes the objective. Get the direction of each of these right. Do not invent fake papers, fake benchmarks with fabricated p-values presented as real, or any quotation attributed to an actual researcher or company. Invented product names, invented vendors, invented Overfit house phrasing, and invented specification tables are all encouraged.

Process requirements. Think carefully before drafting and show your work: open with a planning note of 150–250 words in which you list the five or six technical distinctions you intend to mine and, for each one, name the buyer's-guide trope you will dress it in (the methodology boast, the beginner sidebar, the spec-sheet table, the reader Q&A, the "we also considered" paragraph, the one-star update appended six months later). Then write the column with section headers in the genre's voice. Hold the deadpan throughout: no parenthetical asides explaining a joke, no "obviously," no sentence that steps outside the column to address the reader as a sensible adult. The finished piece should leave a specialist convinced that you understand the difference between verifying a trajectory and verifying a result, and should leave a reader who has never encountered the phrase reward hacking able to explain afterwards, unprompted, why checking only the answer is dangerous and why checking every step is unaffordable. Neither of those conclusions may appear as a stated moral anywhere in the text.
