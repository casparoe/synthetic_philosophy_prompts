I cover science and ideas for a magazine — mostly economics and physics, plus whatever weird corner of math is having a moment. I'm working on a piece and I want an actual stress test, not a summary of both sides.

Here's the view I've drifted into, and I'd like to know exactly how exposed it is: trading volume is a symptom of epistemic breakdown. The reasoning is the no-trade theorem (Milgrom and Stokey) bolted onto Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result. If everyone is Bayesian, everyone starts from a common prior, and the market's information structure is common knowledge, then nobody can profit off private information — the bare act of offering to trade gives away that you think you know something, so the offer gets priced in before anyone accepts it. Speculative trade should be close to zero. Instead trillions of dollars change hands every day, in currencies alone. So I've been writing that the volume is itself the evidence: the common-prior and common-knowledge assumptions are failing, at scale, and therefore the claim that prices are a superb information aggregator is much weaker than thier defenders let on. I've also been leaning on a distinction between plumbing volume (rebalancing, tax-loss selling, index flows, someone who needs cash for a house) and epistemic volume (people trading because they think they know better), arguing that only the second kind counts and that the second kind is embarrassingly large. Part of why I care is that this argument is doing a lot of work in my skepticism about prediction markets being treated as oracles, so I want to know whether the load-bearing wall is real.

What would count against it?

- What would have to be true for the position to be false? Which mechanisms can generate large volume among agents who are all perfectly rational and share a prior? For each, tell me whether it's merely logically possible or independently motivated and empirically supported.
- Where does my plumbing/epistemics carve-out break? If I have to concede that most turnover is mechanical, does the view survive in any interesting form, or does it collapse into "some people are overconfident" — which nobody disputes and which is compatible with everything the efficient-markets people say?
- The bit that worries me: Grossman and Stiglitz argued that if prices fully reflected available information nobody would spend anything acquiring it, so an informationally efficient market definately requires informed trading to keep happening. Does that mean my argument undercuts itself — that volume is what makes prices informative rather than evidence against it?
- Common knowledge is famously brittle; a small chance that someone doesn't know that you know can flip the whole result. Does that fragility rescue the standard story, rescue mine, or hurt both?
- What does my view predict that the rival doesn't, and what have we actually seen? I'm thinking turnover versus subsequent returns, volume clustered around announcements, whether the most active small traders underperform, how much prices move per dollar traded. Which of those, coming out the other way, should make me kill the piece?
- Which defeaters would a card-carrying rational-expectations person themselves concede as counting against the standard story, and which would they wave off as missing the point of the theorem?
- Honest verdict on falsifiability: if any volume confirms me and any absence of volume gets explained as illiquidity, have I insulated the view into meaninglessness? Sometimes a framing that can't fail is still doing real work. Tell me whether that's true here or whether I'm kidding myself.

Rank the whole thing from fatal to merely awkward. For what it's worth, I'd be most moved by a worked equilibrium model with a common prior, no liquidity needs, and no heterogeneous beliefs that still spits out realistic volume — so tell me whether that's buildable in principle, or whether the assumptions contradict each other.
