For several years I have kept a spreadsheet of my own small decisions — trades in a card league, which of two offers to keep chasing, whether to drive across town for a lower price — and the pattern that keeps showing up is that I finish a few dollars and a few hours worse off than I started, having said yes at every single step. When I described this to an online group I belong to, it became an argument that has now circled for a year with three camps and no one moving.

The first camp says the sequence is a money pump, that being pumpable is proof my preferences run in a circle, and that the leaking money is the demonstration rather than a side effect. The second says a pump only works on someone who cannot see the whole sequence at once: a person who can predict what she will choose tomorrow refuses the first trade, so circular preferences are not irrational, merely shortsighted. The third says both are scoring the wrong object — nothing is wrong with the ordering, only with an agent who cannot bind her later selves, and what leaks out is a fee for keeping the option to change her mind. Alongside this runs a smaller quarrel about whether attaching a cost to each step dissolves the pump or only slows it down.

My hypothesis is that the three camps are each evaluating something different — a preference ordering, a decision procedure, an agent extended through time — which is why no argument connects. Is that right? Tell me what kind of disagreement this really is: verbal, a concealed factual question about whether anyone can predict her own later choices, a clash of unargued intuitions about what counts as a loss, or a substantive matter on which one camp is simply wrong. Then say what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment — or why none should be expected.
