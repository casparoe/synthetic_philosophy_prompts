For eight years I have sat on the technical panel that decides which names enter our national inventory of protected taxa, and for most of that time I have been the member least at ease with what we were doing. I now want to know what principle, if any, my own voting record adds up to. Please do not recite the catalogue of species concepts at me; I have read it as carefully as anyone. What I want is reflective equilibrium performed on my verdicts: the principle or principles they commit me to, stated clause by clause, together with a candid account of the places where no principle will hold them together.

My votes, with the reasons I gave the panel at the time:

1. The ring of salamander populations encircling California's Central Valley: I split. The two forms that meet in the south behave as separate species where they overlap, and the existence of a continuous chain of intergrading populations joining them the long way round does not, to my mind, make the endpoints one species.
2. Polar bears and brown bears: two species, notwithstanding fertile hybrids in the wild, recent common ancestry, and gene flow in both directions.
3. Domestic dogs and gray wolves: one species. I cannot tell you why 2 and 3 differ, since both divergences are recent and both pairs hybridize where they meet. If anything the bears feel more obviously separate to me, and I cannot explain that except by body size and by how long we have all been used to the names.
4. The eastern North American coyote: a lineage in its own right and a nameable one, even though it is substantially a hybrid swarm of coyote, wolf, and dog ancestry. This sits badly with 3 and I know it.
5. Bdelloid rotifers: real species, in the hundreds, delimited by morphology and ecology and corroborated by discrete genetic clusters — in animals with no known males and no meiosis, some of which appear to acquire genes from one another without sex.
6. Bacteria: I will not say. I voted to retain Shigella as a separate genus although it falls inside Escherichia coli at several independent places in the tree, because clinical and public-health practice requires the name; but I would not defend the claim that bacterial species are units of the same kind as those in 1 through 5.
7. The European edible water frog: a species in its own right, although every individual is a hybrid of two other frog species and discards one parental genome in order to transmit the other.
8. The hybrid sunflowers that originated more than once from the same two parental species, breed true, occupy habitats neither parent occupies, and are reproductively isolated from both parents without any change in chromosome number: not species, in my vote, but stabilized hybrid derivatives. I now suspect 7 and 8 are inverted with respect to one another, since the frog lineage cannot persist unless both of its parents are present alongside it and the sunflower lineages can.
9. Two morphologically identical frog populations that never interbreed where they co-occur and are reciprocally monophyletic: two species. Two strikingly divergent colour morphs that form a single panmictic population wherever they meet: one species.
10. A stratigraphic series of fossils within one lineage: no species boundaries may be drawn along it. Two fossil populations at the same horizon, geographically separated and diagnosable: two species. Vertical cutting is convention, horizontal cutting is discovery.
11. Twice I have voted against all of the above because of consequences. I split a widespread amphibian so that one isolated and endangered fragment of it would become listable; and I voted against splitting a native duck whose remnant population is being absorbed by an introduced relative, on the ground that a split would hand the fisheries ministry an argument for managing the whole complex as a single abundant hybrid swarm. In both cases the biology was, so far as the panel knew, of exactly the sort I had decided the other way on other afternoons.

What I am asking for:

(a) State the principle, or the small set of them, that delivers as many of these verdicts as possible. For each clause, identify which of my cases motivated it and what the clause thereby rules out.

(b) Say whether this is one criterion with epicycles, or two distinct jobs being done by a single word — a claim about what the units of evolution are, and a rule about which names an institution should maintain. If it is the latter, state the rule governing when the naming consideration may override the metaphysical one, and tell me whether 11 is a legitimate application of that rule or a betrayal of it.

(c) Give your verdicts, with reasons, on three cases I have not listed: an island population that is diagnosable but has never had the opportunity to interbreed with its mainland relative, so that no test of reproductive isolation is available; a plant pair reciprocally monophyletic at nuclear loci but sharing chloroplast haplotypes freely; and a fungus capable of sex that nonetheless reproduces clonally in most of the populations we sample.

(d) Tell me which of my votes has to go. I would rather lose one or two verdicts than purchase all eleven with a disjunctive monster, so set out the cost of each option.

(e) Finally: is there any fact that could settle 3 against 4, or is the difference between them purely a matter of what I want the word to do?
