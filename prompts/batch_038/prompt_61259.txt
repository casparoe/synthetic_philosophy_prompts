I sit in on a philosophy-of-logic seminar at a university near where I live even though I'm not enrolled — they don't mind, I just never talk. Last week was on Fitch's paradox of knowability, the result that if every truth is knowable then every truth is already known, which is supposed to be bad news for verificationist/anti-realist accounts of truth. (Our professor mentioned in passing that the proof was actually supplied by an anonymous referee long before the paper came out, which I thought was a great story.) Afterwards two of the grad students — I'll call them Marcus and Dana — got into a long argument first in the hallway and then at the pub, and I've been mulling it over for days because I genuinely couldn't tell which of them was winning. Here's roughly how it went, with the moves numbered so you can take them one at a time:

(1) Dana opened by saying the paradox is wildly overhyped: all it establishes is that "every truth is knowable" and "some truth is unknown" can't both be true, so anyone who thinks there are unknown truths should just drop the first claim. Verificationism is refuted, case closed, move on. (2) Marcus said she was treating a diagnostic as a refutation. His point: the proof only ever uses three ingredients — that the knowledge operator is factive, that it distributes over conjunction, and that there's some possibility operator available to state the knowability principle. Swap in "is written down in some book," or "is believed by an omniscient being," or "is expressible in some language," and you get the identical collapse. So the paradox isn't about knowledge or about anti-realism at all, it's a triviality about a certain shape of operator, and Dana's case-closed attitude mistakes a schema for a discovery. (3) Dana said that's exactly backwards: if the argument goes through for every operator of that shape, it's not a technical artifact you can shrug off, it's a hard constraint that any theory pairing a factive conjunctive operator with a possibility claim has to respect. A theorem that general is a real result, not a curiosity.

(4) Marcus said fine, then restrict the principle. His own suggestion was to apply knowability only to what he called stable truths — ones such that if they're true at a time, they're true at every later time. The troublesome sentence, the one saying p is true and nobody knows it, isn't stable, because the moment somebody learns it it becomes false, so it never gets into the antecedent and the proof never gets going. (5) Dana said the restriction may block the derivation but destroys the theory it was supposed to protect: the anti-realist wanted truth to be nothing over and above what would be verified under ideal conditions, and if some truths are in principle beyond even ideal verification, she owes an account of what makes *those* true, and any such account is going to be a realist one. So Marcus saved the letter of the principle by giving up its entire point. (6) Marcus said he hasn't given up the point, because the truths he's excluding aren't worldly: a sentence like "it's raining and nobody knows it" is true in virtue of the weather plus a fact about our own epistemic bookkeeping, and only the weather part is what a theory of truth is in the business of explaining — knowability was always a claim about the worldly component. Dana said that concedes a whole domain of facts the theory doesn't cover, so it's no longer a general account of truth, which means the paradox was never a threat to begin with and the two of them had been arguing about nothing. Marcus said no, the difference matters a lot, because on his version the verificationist program survives in restricted form and on hers it's dead.

Can you adjudicate this properly rather than just picking a side? What I want: go through moves 1 to 6 in order, rate each one from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification for the rating, and say specifically who is right about the sub-points where they clash (e.g. in 2/3, is generalizing to other operators a reason to discount the paradox or a reason to take it more seriously; in 5/6, does excluding unstable truths actually cost the anti-realist anything). Then give an overall verdict. Two extra things I'd really like addressed. First, does the stability restriction in move 4 genuinely block the proof, or does the contradiction come back by another route — I have a nagging feeling you could cook up a sentence that is stable and still causes trouble, but I couldn't build one, and I'd like to know if it exists. Second, tell me what both of them are missing; I keep wondering whether it matters if the possibility in the knowability principle is read as epistemic rather than metaphysical, and whether the whole thing leans on classical assumptions about truth values that a verificationist would already be suspicious of. Please don't bother with the history or with who in the literature proposed which fix — just assess the arguments as these two actually made them.
