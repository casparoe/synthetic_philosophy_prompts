I chair the Clinical Safety Committee of a 600-bed regional hospital, and on Thursday we vote on the final text of a new incident-investigation policy. Three of its clauses have split the committee, and I have come to think the two camps are using the same vocabulary for two different things. I need a written opinion to circulate to members before the meeting.

The incident that prompted the policy: in March a patient undergoing radiotherapy received roughly forty percent more dose than prescribed, delivered to a small volume across five fractions, and now carries a permanent injury. The planning system flagged the discrepancy twice; on both occasions the physicist on duty dismissed the flag. That particular flag fires on approximately one plan in five in our unit and had been dismissed on every occasion in the preceding eighteen months without consequence, and her predecessor had described it, informally and in front of trainees, as noise. Nobody has been suspended. Our regulator requires the committee to publish an investigation report and to state whether any individual is being referred to their professional body.

The contested clauses are these:

1. The published report must contain no agent-locating description of the causal sequence: not that she dismissed the warning, but that the warning was dismissed. The drafting group's rationale is that any sentence with a person in the subject position smuggles blame back in through grammar alone.
2. Where the investigation finds that the conduct arose from a defect in the system rather than from the individual, that individual is exempt — the draft uses this word — from referral, discipline, and censure, and is instead entered on a retraining plan.
3. Every individual whose conduct features in a report must sign a closing statement acknowledging the harm done to the patient and to the patient's family, as a condition of the matter being closed.

Two members hold the opposing positions. Our consultant oncologist maintains that only a report which declines to identify a culprit will ever surface what actually happened, because the moment individuals appear in the text, colleagues start managing their testimony rather than their practice. Our patient representative maintains that a report which will not say who did what treats the patient as an input to a process rather than as someone to whom something was done, and that a retraining plan is a euphemism for a decision not to take the harm seriously.

The distinction I believe the two camps are collapsing is the one Strawson drew between an excuse and an exemption, and between standing in a relationship with someone and adopting what he called the objective attitude toward them — seeing them as something to be managed, retrained, or engineered around. Strawson allowed that this second attitude might be taken up toward a person on an occasion or across the board. The question I cannot get this committee to face is whether an institution can take it up domain by domain, confining it to incident investigation, without its altering how we stand to one another everywhere else in the building.

Please draft the opinion. It should:

- Set out that distinction in language a committee of clinicians, one hospital lawyer, and two lay members can follow without having read any philosophy, and then apply it clause by clause: for each of the three, say which of the two things the draft is in fact doing, and what turns on the answer. Pay particular attention to whether clause 2 does what its own chosen word implies, and to what clause 3 is demanding of the individual once clause 1 has stripped the agent out of the narrative.
- Take a position on the scope question rather than merely posing it, and say what would count as evidence on either side — including something in our own practices, such as those eighteen months of dismissed warnings and the informal instruction that made them routine.
- Recommend adopt, reject, or amend for each clause, and for every amendment supply replacement wording the committee could vote on directly.
- State the strongest case against your recommendation in terms the patient representative would recognize as a fair statement of her position, and say precisely what your recommendation concedes to it and what it refuses.

No bibliography, no reading list, and no further name-checking beyond the single reference above: if the argument cannot be carried in its own words, it is not one I can put in front of this committee. Aim for between 1,100 and 1,500 words.
