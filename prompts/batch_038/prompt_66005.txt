So here's a thought experiment I've been chewing on and I want someone to actually commit to an answer rather than hedge.

Frank Ramsey — the Cambridge guy, dead at 26 in 1930 — built his account of partial belief out of betting. Roughly: what you believe to degree p shows up in the odds you'd accept, and you can read a person's beliefs off their choices as long as those choices are internally consistent. A few decades later you get the results that seem to cut against that picture: rational agents who share a prior and who both know what the other believes can't agree to disagree, and two traders who differ only in information (same tastes, same attitudes to risk) can never find a mutually acceptable bet, because the mere fact that the other person is willing to take the other side is itself evidence you're supposed to update on.

Pretend Ramsey read that second batch of results. Does he come away thinking his betting analysis got vindicated, or gutted? Push on this specific worry: if belief just is a betting disposition, and it turns out that any two people whose beliefs are mutually known can't actually bet against each other, is there anything left for the betting talk to do? And then the harder case — suppose I'm more confident in a stock than you are, we both know that, and neither of us will trade. On Ramsey's account, what exactly is my extra confidence made of, if it can never cash out in an actual wager against you? Does it cash out somewhere else, against a bookie who doesn't know my mind, or is it just a disposition waiting for a situation that can't arise?

I want you to say what Ramsey would conclude, and defend it. It's fine to reach for other bits of his thinking — his treatment of consistency as something enforced on you by the possibility of being made a suckers of, his pragmatism, the way he wanted belief to be a map for action rather than a picture. If you think the honest answer is that he'd split the difference, tell me precisely where the split falls.
