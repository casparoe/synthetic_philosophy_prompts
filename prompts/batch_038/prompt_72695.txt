I am four drafts into a novel whose protagonist is an ancient historian, and I need help predicting what she would say about a story I have not yet let her rule on. Everything you need is below; I am not asking for sources, and I would rather you work from my summary than reach for anything you are not sure of. If a detail seems wrong to you, flag it — do not silently correct it.

Three scenes already in the book fix her verdicts, and I have written them so that a specialist reader could reconstruct her method.

First, on the war at Troy. She concedes the mound: a city at the right place on the right strait, burned and battered in the right century, and diplomatic letters from the Anatolian empire to the east naming a western client kingdom, and one of its rulers, in forms that many scholars have thought uncomfortably close to two Greek names in the poem. She concedes none of the narrative. She will not grant a single named agent, battle, or speech. But she makes one exception that her colleagues find inconsistent: she accepts certain objects and turns of phrase in the poem as genuine Bronze Age survivals — a helmet built from boars' tusks, described in exacting detail, of a type obsolete for centuries before the poem was being composed yet matching what has come out of Mycenaean graves. Her formulation is that oral singers could carry forward what things looked like and could not carry forward what people did.

Second, on the exodus from Egypt. She denies the event as narrated, and denies it for two independent reasons: a record-keeping state that logged the movements of small bands of foreigners left nothing about this one, and a generation spent crossing a desert would leave a scatter of pottery and campsites and left none. She nonetheless grants a people called Israel in Canaan by the late thirteenth century, on the strength of an Egyptian victory inscription that lists them as a people rather than a place, and she refuses to call the story a lie: on her account a founding story can be true of a society's formation while being false about an itinerary.

Third, on Arthur. A colleague presses her with the fact that three later sources agree on the name, list his battles, and even date the last of them. She denies the man anyway. Her reason is that the British monk writing within reach of the events names the great battle, dates his own birth to the year of it, and never names a commander; the name surfaces only centuries later, in a compilation with a list of battles and no witnesses. Convergence among late sources sharing an ancestry is not, for her, corroboration.

Now the case I have not written. In the scene, a geologist argues to her that the story of the island empire that sank into the sea in a single day and night, as told by a philosopher in a dialogue eleven centuries after the fact, is a garbled memory of a real catastrophe: an Aegean island whose Bronze Age town — multi-storey houses, running water, painted walls, a society at the height of its confidence — was buried under volcanic ash when the island blew itself apart. The eruption is dated by physical methods some century and a half earlier than the regional archaeological sequence implies, and that quarrel is unsettled; ash from it has been identified in cores across the eastern Mediterranean; one Egyptian inscription of roughly the period describes weather violent enough that readers have wanted it to be the fallout, though the connection is argued about.

Against this she has: that the entire textual tradition is one philosophical dialogue, in which the teller insists he is reporting a true account rather than inventing one, and supplies a chain of custody running from priests in the Nile delta to a visiting Athenian lawgiver and thence through two generations of his own family to himself as a boy; that no Egyptian archive records a great island empire or an Athenian victory over it; that the story sets the war nine thousand years before the lawgiver, which is out by a factor of five or six against the eruption; and — the point she will press hardest — that the story as told contains no volcano, no ash, no pumice, no darkened sky. The catastrophe in the dialogue is earthquake and inundation. If a memory had actually been carried from the eruption to the philosopher, she thinks, the eruption would be in the story.

Give me your prediction, in this order. Open with her verdict in a single sentence, stated as she would state it, in her register. Then: the candidate rules that are each consistent with all three settled verdicts, including the boar's-tusk exception, and which of them fire on this case and in which direction; which single rule you think is actually hers, and why it explains the exception better than the others; which of the three prior verdicts is the closest analog, and what happens if the analogies pull apart; a fourth case drawn from the real record of legendary claims that would separate the rules you have identified, together with what she would be forced to say about it under each; and finally, if the rules genuinely diverge here, which verdict would be the better scene — the one a reader who paid attention could retrodict from what came before, rather than the one I would prefer.

No preamble, no restating of my set-up, no bibliography.
