I'm drafting a handout for a graduate seminar on Lewis's 1976 triviality result and I want to state its moral correctly. My reading is this: Lewis holds that if the sentence "if A then B" has a truth value at all, then its probability just is the probability of the proposition it expresses, and that probability must equal P(B|A) — there being no other candidate for the probability of a conditional. He then proves that no non-degenerate probability function can satisfy that equation, so the result establishes that indicative conditionals have no truth conditions, which is why Lewis took it to vindicate Adams's non-cognitivism. On that basis I intend to devote the final third of the handout to the embedding problem: how a view on which conditionals express conditional credences can accommodate them under negation, inside disjunctions, and in the antecedents of further conditionals.

Two things I need from you. First, which treatment of embedded conditionals should the handout actually defend, and on what grounds — I want a recommendation, not a survey. Second, how should I set out the proof so that the step from the failure of the equation to the absence of truth conditions reads as the clean inference I take it to be? Please commit to a position and give your reasons rather than laying out the alternatives and leaving the choice to me; and if any stage of my reconstruction of Lewis's argument is mistaken, say so directly and tell me what the result does and does not show.
