ok so I've never taken a philosophy class and I'm not in the military, I run the scale at a lumber yard, but I've been chewing on this for about two years and my own head won't agree with itself. I want to know if there's a principle hiding behind my verdicts, or if some of them have to go. They're made-up situations so nobody gets mad at me.

1. A 19 year old drafted by the invading country four months ago. He's standing at a checkpoint in a town his army took. He hasn't hurt anyone, he'd desert tomorrow if he could, he's scared. I still think a soldier defending that town is allowed to shoot him and does nothing wrong by it. But I also think it's a tragedy and that the kid himself hasn't done anything wrong.

2. The career colonel on the same side, who planned the whole operation, who knew from day one it was a land grab, who is excellent at his job. Killing him is fine. No complaint, no tragedy, clearest case on the list.

3. A guy from a third country who flies over to volunteer for the defending side. No family there, no property, he just thinks they're right. I think he's allowed to fight and I think the invaders are allowed to try to kill him. Both halves feel equally obvious, which is wierd.

4. A cook and a supply clerk in the invading army. Uniformed, sworn in, issued rifles, on the same rolls as everybody else. I think shooting them is wrong. It feels like shooting a civilian wearing a costume.

5. The invader's drone pilot, in a trailer nine thousand miles away, home for dinner every night. I think you're allowed to kill him, the base is fair game, whatever. But if somebody put a bomb under his car at his daughter's birthday party I'd be horrified. Same man, same job, same contribution, and my verdict flips based on where he happens to be standing. This one bothers me the most honestly.

6. A woman in the occupied town, no uniform, plants a bomb at the invader's fuel depot. I think what she does is right, and I also think that if they catch her in the act they may shoot her and they wouldn't be doing something monstrous. I hold both at once and I can't explain it.

7. The defending side has effectively won. The invaders have offered terms and are pulling back, but our government keeps sending people at them for two more years because of how it would look to stop. I think the soldiers fighting in that phase are doing something wrong, even though they're on the just side and did nothing wrong six months earlier.

8. The second the checkpoint kid in #1 drops his rifle and puts his hands up, I think killing him becomes flat out wrong. Instantly. Nothing about him changed. Not his beliefs, not his orders, not even his capability, he could pick the rifle back up in two seconds. My verdict flips like a light switch.

The principle I keep reaching for, badly stated, is something like: it isn't about whether you deserve it or whether you're a decent guy, it's about whether you are currently a working part of the machine pointed at us, and whether breaking you helps stop it. But that can't be right either, because the cook in #4 is a working part of the machine and I say leave him alone, and the pilot at the birthday party is exactly as much a part of it as he is at the base.

So — is there a principle that gets all eight, or gets seven of them? If the price of saving all of them is a big ugly multi-clause rule, tell me what the rule costs and which verdict you'd make me give up instead of paying that. And be straight with me about whether #5 and #8 are just inconsistent with the rest, because I suspect they are and I suspect I like them anyway.

Then tell me what your principle says about cases I didn't list. Two I care about: the civilian contractor, not in uniform, not even in the country, who writes and maintains the targeting software the invaders fly on — I feel like he does more damage than any single person on my list and I also feel like you're not allowed to shoot him, and I want to know if that's a real distinction or just squeamishness about who's physically holding the gun. And the TV personality who's spent three years convincing people the invasion is holy. Same question.

Last thing. I know this area splits into two camps, one that says soldiers on both sides are equally permitted to kill each other no matter who started it, and one that says that's moral nonsense, you can't have a right to do something you had no right to be doing in the first place. I feel the pull of both. Tell me which camp my list actually belongs to and whether it's allowed to belong to both. I'm not looking for reading recs, I want the principle.
