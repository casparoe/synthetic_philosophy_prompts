Until three months ago I ran a regional operations team and never gave a thought to generational categories. That changed when attrition among employees in their late twenties roughly doubled within a single year and I was made responsible for the retention initiative that followed. One of the first decisions was whether the predictive model our analytics group is building should include a generation variable. I convened a working session with two people: Nadia, who leads people analytics for the division, and Grant, a professor of organizational sociology who consults for us a few days each month. They disagreed sharply and at some length. Since the decision falls to me, I would rather have the argument assessed than the mood of the room reported, so here it is as faithfully as I can render it.

(1) Grant opened by arguing that generations are not real kinds. The boundary years, he said, were fixed by journalists and market research firms rather than by any fact about the people; a boundary can be moved five years in either direction without anything visible happening in the data; and dispersion within a cohort exceeds dispersion between cohorts on nearly every attitude measure anyone bothers to collect. On his view the label is folklore that produces a spurious feeling of explanatory depth.

(2) Nadia replied that arbitrary boundaries are unremarkable: the poverty line, body-mass-index categories, the employee headcount that makes a firm legally small. All conventional, all useful. What makes a variable worth including, she said, is whether it improves prediction out of sample, and birth-year bands do in our historical data. She added that demanding more than predictive success before calling a category real is a philosophical preference, not a methodological standard.

(3) Grant's counter was technical. In any dataset, cohort, period, and age are linearly dependent, since cohort is period minus age. A difference between generations is therefore formally indistinguishable from an age effect combined with a period effect, and separating them requires assuming that one of the three is flat, which is exactly what is in dispute. So predictive success licenses no interpretation. Worse, he said, it licenses no projection: our twenty-eight-year-olds today have had a different period exposure from the twenty-eight-year-olds we will employ in ten years, so a cohort term fitted now may not travel.

(4) Nadia said she had no mechanism claim to defend. She could include age and calendar-year effects, let the cohort term absorb the residual, and treat survival of that term as evidence of a genuine cohort effect. She then argued that Grant's collinearity point proves too much, because it applies equally to every historical-exposure variable that sociologists themselves use, such as having entered the labor market during a downturn. If his argument eliminates the generation variable, it eliminates those too.

(5) Grant accepted the residual strategy but disputed its interpretation. If a cohort term survives age and period controls, he said, one candidate explanation is that the label has itself become causally active: people read descriptions of their own generation and adjust their expectations and self-presentation accordingly. A category that becomes real because it is applied, on his account, cannot support the projection a retention model requires, since targeting on it changes what it measures. Nadia answered that a looping effect is still a causal effect, and that real is real. Grant's reply was that it is real in the way a published market inefficiency is real: available now, exhausted once exploited.

(6) Grant then added that because the labels originate in advertising and popular journalism, they cannot track anything causal at all. Nadia said this step confuses the origin of an idea with its warrant, and Grant did not defend it.

Please work through moves (1) to (6) in order and say who is right at each, rating every move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification. Two points I would like addressed specifically: whether the "proves too much" reply in (4) actually succeeds, and whether (5) gives Grant anything he did not already have in (3). Then tell me what the two of them are jointly missing, meaning considerations neither raised that bear on whether the variable belongs in the model. Finally, once the weak moves are stripped out, say what the exchange establishes about my question. I am not asking how to weigh the two personalities, and I have no interest in a verdict on who came out ahead socially; only what survives as argument, and what, if anything, it implies for the variable.
