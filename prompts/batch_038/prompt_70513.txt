Our hospital group's ethics committee rules on two or three requests a month from clinicians seeking to be excused from some part of their duties: a procedure, a step in a protocol, an assignment to a particular patient. What we are actually deciding is whether an objection expresses a moral commitment we ought to accommodate or a bias we ought to refuse, and we have no method for telling the two apart. Our practice is a single test — if the clinician can state reasons, we accommodate; if the clinician cannot, we refuse — and I am confident it fails in both directions.

Write a decision procedure the committee can run to completion inside a ninety-minute meeting, for members who are clinicians plus two lay trustees with no training in philosophy. Give the questions in the order they should be put, state what a sufficient answer to each looks like, mark the points where the procedure terminates in a refusal, and mark the points where it stops because what remains is a question of values and must go to the board. Say explicitly which of the familiar tests you keep and which you discard — articulacy, consistency across cases, whether the judgment flips when some feature of the patient that is not morally relevant is varied while everything else is held fixed, the presence and intensity of emotion, survival of deliberate reflection, the clinician's own account of where the objection came from — and justify each choice.

Then run it on three requests. A surgical resident asks to be excused from a bariatric service; he reports a dread he cannot explain, and two staff members have separately reported remarks he made about patients' self-discipline. An intensivist asks never to initiate renal replacement therapy for patients with advanced dementia; his reasons are secular, fully worked out, applied without exception, and he has arranged coverage on every occasion. A nurse asks to be excused from a research protocol recruiting from a prison population; she is visibly angry, argues that consent cannot be free in custody, has never in fact declined an assignment, and reports no comparable objection when the same protocol recruits from a homeless shelter.

One of these three should come out in a way I will not like. Close by stating what the procedure cannot settle even when it is run correctly, and what we should do about that residue.
