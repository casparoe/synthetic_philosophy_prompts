I am preparing a handout for a reading group that sits across philosophy and the social sciences, where we have been working through David Lewis's game-theoretic analysis of convention in *Convention: A Philosophical Study* (1969) alongside population-dynamics accounts of how signaling and behavioral regularities get fixed in a group without anyone designing them. A recurring confusion in our discussions is that people slide between two very different claims: that Lewis's analysis tells us *what a convention is*, and that it *explains how conventions arise and persist*. The second claim looks shaky, because the analysis makes an epistemic state — mutual expectation, iterated up to common knowledge — central, whereas the dynamics that actually fix a regularity in a group seem to need nothing of the kind. I would like a single designed case that drives a wedge between the two claims and that I can put in front of first-year graduate students without any technical apparatus in the scenario itself. Please construct the thought experiment to the following specifications.

The case must satisfy all of these: (i) it is realistic and mundane, set in the present day, with a small population you can characterize member by member — my suggestion is a daycare room of about fourteen two-year-olds, but you may change the setting as long as it stays ordinary; (ii) the recurrent situation is a pure coordination problem, with at least two alternative regularities that would serve the participants equally well, and with everyone preferring coordination on either alternative to a failure to coordinate; (iii) there is no rule given in language, no adult instruction, no enforcement and no sanctions of any kind, and no agreement — the regularity is sustained by nothing beyond the participants' own behavior and their perceptual and imitative sensitivity to one another; (iv) the regularity originates in an accident whose history you can narrate in two or three sentences, and is then fixed by a transmission process you can describe; (v) the population is mixed in cognitive sophistication — a small minority of three children, on the verge of turning four, behave in ways a generous observer would read as attributing expectations to others (checking who is approaching, pausing, waving the other through), while the remainder show only first-order sensitivity to what others are doing right now; (vi) crucially, the case must be built so that the near-universal quantifier prefixed to Lewis's clauses is the hinge — on one natural way of fixing what proportion of the population counts as all but a negligible minority the analysis is satisfied, and on another it is not, and you must make the case turn on that; and (vii) the regularity must prove more robust than many adult human conventions in comparable settings, surviving a full year plus the turnover of most of the original cohort, so that the case exhibits durable stability without the iterated epistemic state the analysis treats as essential.

Please deliver, in this order: (1) the scenario, in plain prose, no more than three hundred words; (2) a compact statement, in your own words, of the clauses of Lewis's analysis that a behavioral regularity must satisfy, followed by a clause-by-clause audit of the case under two readings — the strictest, on which the clauses require genuine beliefs and higher-order beliefs, and the most charitable, on which dispositions to act and to react suffice — noting for every clause whose verdict differs between the readings exactly which fact about the case decides it; (3) the verdicts of two rival criteria, which you should state precisely before applying: a purely dynamical criterion, on which a regularity counts as conventional just in case it is a stable attractor of conformist imitation in a common-interest coordination game and could be displaced only by a coordinated shift, and a criticism-licensing criterion, on which a regularity counts as a norm just in case deviation licenses protest or repair from participants, including bystanders who are not themselves harmed; (4) an identification of the single feature of the case doing the most work, together with a removal test — add back one ingredient of your choosing (older children, an announcement by staff, a physically asymmetric tunnel, a vocal marker) and report whether the verdict under each criterion flips; (5) the strongest defense a committed Lewisian could make against your case, and an assessment of what it costs — in particular, whether retreating to a fully dispositional reading of the expectation and iterated-knowledge clauses leaves the analysis with enough content to exclude anything; and (6) a closing paragraph on what the case implies for the project of using Lewis's framework to characterize the evolution of norms. Two further requirements. First, the verdicts must be generated by the features of the case rather than stipulated: you may not assert that a participant lacks beliefs about others without giving the behavioral basis for that attribution, and you may not describe a preference without a situation in which it shows up. Second, if you judge that any specification cannot be met simultaneously with the others, say so explicitly and offer the nearest satisfiable variant instead of quietly dropping it. Finish by telling me plainly which of the three criteria, if any, you think tracks something philosophically important that the other two miss.
