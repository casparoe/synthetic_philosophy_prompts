DRAFT

§1. The shape of the debate. Moral particularism, in the form I want to address, is an argument from a thesis about reasons to a thesis about principles. The premise is holism: a consideration that counts in favor of an action in one case may count against it in another, or may count for nothing at all, and which of these happens is not fixed by anything intrinsic to the consideration itself. The conclusion is that there are no true moral principles, or, in the weaker and more common version, that principles are dispensable — at best crutches for the morally immature, at worst an obstacle to seeing the case in front of us. The generalist standardly replies by hedging: lying is pro tanto wrong, subject to defeating conditions. The particularist then presses a dilemma. Either the hedge is left open-ended, in which case the principle licenses no inference, because we cannot tell whether we are inside or outside the range of cases where it holds; or the hedge is completed into an explicit list, in which case the list is more informative than the principle it was meant to illuminate, and the principle has become a redundant label for a set of conditions we could have stated directly.

§2. A shared assumption. My claim is that both sides accept a requirement I will call valence fixity: a principle, to count as a principle, must state a context-invariant contribution. On this picture, a genuine generalization about reasons is one whose valence and weight are settled by the generalization itself, so that knowing the principle plus the non-moral facts puts you in a position to read off the reason. The particularist argues that valence fixity fails across the board; the generalist tries to restore it by enumerating the defeating conditions. I want to argue that the requirement is simply the wrong standard for what a generalization has to do, and that abandoning it does not resolve the debate in either direction so much as relocate it.

§3. The analogy. In the philosophy of science, the corresponding move was to stop asking whether a generalization is exceptionless and start asking whether it is invariant. Exceptionlessness turned out to be the wrong test of lawlikeness: the special sciences are full of generalizations with exceptions that nonetheless support counterfactuals, figure in explanations, and license predictions, precisely because their exceptions are not arbitrary. What makes such a generalization robust is that it continues to hold across a wide range of interventions on the other variables, and that when it fails, the failure is itself explicable by reference to interfering conditions that can be named and, in favorable cases, ordered relative to one another. A generalization with many exceptions can be more invariant than one with none, provided the exceptions are few in kind and the range of variations under which the generalization survives is broad. This is roughly the lesson of the intervention-based account of explanation and causation, and of the earlier debate about ceteris paribus laws in the special sciences.

§4. Applying it. Take the principle that lying is pro tanto wrong. The particularist points to the murderer at the door. Under valence fixity, that is a refutation. Under the invariance test, it is one data point about the intervention range. What matters is how the generalization behaves as we systematically vary features of the situation: the stakes, whether the lie protects or exploits, whether the addressee has any claim to the truth, whether lying forecloses a less costly alternative, whether the lie is part of a practice that only works if generally abstained from. If, across that range, the consideration continues to count against the action, and if it is silenced or reversed only under a small, nameable, and ordered set of conditions, then the generalization is highly invariant — plausibly more invariant than a number of generalizations that no one hesitates to call laws in biology. Moreover, if the defeating conditions are themselves systematically nameable, then the completed hedge is not the embarrassment the particularist's dilemma assumes; it is the content of the principle. The dilemma only bites if we expect a principle to function as a decision procedure. On the account I am proposing, principles are not decision procedures but claims about which inferences across cases are licensed, and licensing inferences is exactly what invariant generalizations do.

§5. A second argument: recognition. Holism is usually motivated by cases of switching, where the same consideration changes polarity as the context shifts. But describing a case as one of switching already presupposes that we can identify the consideration independently of its valence. To say that cruelty is a reason against in this situation and no reason at all in that one, we must be able to pick out cruelty as the same feature in both, and to do that in cases we have not already adjudicated requires general, projectable criteria for what counts as cruelty in the first place. The particularist's own argument therefore depends on a generalism at the level of feature-identification. If identifying morally relevant features is general while only their valence is contextual, then holism has shown something narrower than the conclusion drawn from it: that valence is not intrinsic. It has not shown that principles are dispensable.

§6. Upshot. On this picture, what principles do is license inferences: from one case to another, from another person's judgment to my own, from a case I have thought about at leisure to a case I am inside of and cannot see clearly. That is why moral testimony is not always defective, why we can learn from cases that are not ours, and why any view that denies the availability of such inferences owes an account of moral development. Holism about reasons may well be true. Particularism about principles does not follow, because the premise that would make it follow is valence fixity.

§7. Worries I already have. (a) Invariance comes in degrees and is assessed relative to some set of interventions we happen to care about; if the relevant intervention set is chosen by our interests, then the resulting principles may be interest-relative in a way that undercuts their normative authority. The particularist could grant everything in §4 and insist that I have described our habits of attention, not a norm. (b) The analogy may run out. Ceteris paribus generalizations in the special sciences are backed by mechanisms, which ground the distinction between interfering and non-interfering conditions. If there is no analogue in the moral case, then talk of nameable defeaters may be doing no explanatory work and merely deferring the problem. (c) §5 may prove too much. If identifying a feature requires general criteria, the same is true of aesthetic and perceptual properties, and particularists about those domains have managed without conceding principles; so whatever §5 establishes may be too weak to matter.
