I have been assembling cases for a seminar on dispositions, and I should like to know what principle my verdicts on them add up to, if there is one. The familiar material is Martin's electro-fink, Johnston's masked cases, Lewis's reformed conditional analysis with its requirement that the object retain the relevant intrinsic character, and Bird's objection that the interfering mechanism can itself be intrinsic. What follows are largely cases of my own construction, most drawn from reliability engineering and from the biochemistry of protein aggregation, together with the verdicts I have been unable to shake.

1. A pressure-relief valve calibrated to open at eight bar. A bolt-on actuator, added after manufacture, clamps the disc shut within milliseconds of any incipient lift. Verdict: the valve is disposed to open at eight bar. The disposition is genuine but masked; remove the actuator and it opens.

2. An otherwise identical valve in which the preventing mechanism is not an addition but the geometry of the spring itself: at eight bar the spring re-locks against its seat, so lift cannot begin. Verdict: this valve is not disposed to open at eight bar, and never was. Nothing is masked here; there is simply no such disposition.

3. A purified protein sample that aggregates at physiological temperature. A contaminating chaperone molecule, carried through the purification and present in the same tube, refolds the protein as fast as it begins to misfold. Verdict: the sample is disposed to aggregate; the chaperone masks.

Cases 2 and 3 are the pair I cannot reconcile. In both, the interferer sits inside the object as I would ordinarily individuate it, and in both it prevents the manifestation on the very trial in which that manifestation would otherwise have occurred. Yet I say the disposition is absent in the one and merely masked in the other. The difference I want to appeal to — that the spring's geometry is constitutive of what the valve is, whereas the chaperone is a hitchhiker — strikes me as doing no work at all; and if it does no work, one of my two verdicts is false.

4. An aircraft fitting whose corrosion-inhibiting coating has quietly delaminated. It is now disposed to crack on the next severe load cycle, and it will. Verdict: the disposition came into being at delamination, before anyone knew of it, and its existence is unaffected by discovery.

5. A redundant brake channel that would carry the load if the primary failed, except that a maintenance lockout switch has been left in the disabled position. Verdict: at the level of the channel, it is disposed to carry the load; at the level of the system, it is not. I want to know whether that is a difference in object or a difference in description, and whether one account can honor both.

6. A wire that a device forces to conduct whenever touched, whether or not the wire would have conducted on its own. Verdict: it is disposed to conduct, but the disposition explains nothing about what happens, and I do not think the device conferred the disposition.

7. Roughly two percent of a batch of capacitors fails at rated voltage, owing to occasional internal voids; any given unit either has a void or does not. Verdict: the batch has a disposition, in the sense of a rate, but I doubt that any void-free unit is disposed to fail, and I doubt that a voided unit fails at every application of rated voltage.

8. The case I keep returning to. A component with a self-diagnostic routine that enters a protective mode whenever it detects a test signal, so that any attempt to check for the disposition defeats it, while ordinary service is unaffected. Verdict: I think it is disposed — but I am uneasy. No test could ever reveal the disposition, and I am not sure that a disposition whose evidence is systematically unavailable is a disposition at all.

Two requests beyond the tally. First, state the principle, or the small number of candidate principles, as precisely as you can: I would like the clauses written out, the case above that motivates each clause, and the verdict each principle returns on every case as I have listed them. Second, tell me plainly which of my verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate and, where two candidates differ, what the difference costs. I should also like each candidate's verdict on a case I have not described: an object whose interferer is intrinsic and constitutive, but which is removed for service and then reinstated — does the disposition return, and on what grounds? Finally, if you think my engineering vocabulary, with its talk of latent failure and hidden function, is smuggling an operational notion of badness into the metaphysics, say so and identify which of my verdicts depend on the smuggling.
