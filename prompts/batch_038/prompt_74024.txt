Here are twelve cases concerning my own survival, together with my verdicts on them. I want to know whether any single principle accounts for all twelve, and what I must surrender if none does. I have assembled the set by recording what I actually judge rather than by reasoning down from a theory, and I accept that it may prove incoherent. I am not after a survey of the personal identity literature, nor for references of any kind — only a formulation, and its consequences.

1. My neurons are replaced one at a time, over several months, by synthetic components doing exactly the same work. I survive. This is the least troubling case I know.
2. The identical replacement is performed instantaneously: my biological brain is destroyed and a functionally indistinguishable synthetic one installed in my skull, in my body, while my heart goes on beating. I also survive — though I flinch at this description in a way I do not at (1), and I cannot justify the flinch.
3. The destructive teletransporter. I am scanned, my body annihilated, and an hour later a Replica wakes on Mars with my memories, my character, and the cut on my upper lip from this morning's shave. I survive.
4. The machine malfunctions and my body is not annihilated. I walk out of the booth on Earth; the Replica wakes on Mars all the same. He is not me — and if I am then killed in a road accident a minute later, that does not promote him.
5. My body is annihilated by the scan, the record is stored, and no Replica is built for two hundred years, when somebody switches one on. I survive. The delay costs nothing.
6. As in (5), except that the record is also used at the time of scanning to make a second, short-lived duplicate, which is switched off after a minute. The Replica of two hundred years later is not me.
7. A reconstructor who has never scanned me builds a person out of my correspondence, my publications and decades of close observation, so that his psychology matches mine exactly, with no causal link to my brain at all. Not me — and yet I cannot say why, since (3) appears to show that what matters is the resulting psychology rather than the route by which it arrives.
8. I wake in my own body with total retrograde amnesia: no memories, no projects, no recognisable character. I survive, and I would dread pain inflicted on that body tomorrow.
9. My body is annihilated and a Replica is built with my brain and my physiology but no psychology whatsoever — a blank wearing my face. Nothing survives.
10. My two hemispheres are transplanted into two bodies, and both recipients wake with my psychology and my memories. Neither is me, but this is not death either; and I would dread the operation considerably more than I dread ordinary survival. With ten recipients rather than two, I would dread it more still — which I cannot reconcile with the thought that what matters is equally present in each.
11. A Replica is built with all my settled dispositions, tastes, temperament and plans, but with every episodic memory erased. Another is built with all my episodic memories intact, a wholly different temperament, and none of my projects. The first is me, or at any rate the one whose survival would matter to me. The second is not.
12. A Replica is built with ninety-nine per cent of my psychology: everything is intact except my attachment to one particular person I love, including the sincere belief that I still love them. I have reversed my verdict on this case three times and cannot settle it.

My tentative principle, offered for you to break: what matters is the continuation of my settled dispositions and projects, not the persistence of my memories; and such continuation counts as my survival only where it is caused by the continued existence of the physical thing that carries those dispositions, and only where nothing else is continuing them at the same time. I suspect the second clause is smuggling in a bodily criterion by the back door, and I suspect that if it is, cases (2) and (3) are in trouble.

So, four requests. First: is there a single principle that delivers all twelve verdicts? If there is, state it clause by clause, identifying the case or pair of cases each clause exists to handle, and show that the clauses are more than the verdicts restated in a different vocabulary. Second: if there is not, give me the two or three nearest candidates and compare them on cost — how many verdicts each overturns, and how much machinery each needs in order to save them. Third: put whichever principle survives your own scrutiny to work on three or four cases of your devising, at least one of which makes my clauses pull against one another, and rule on them explicitly. Fourth: tell me which of my verdicts I ought to abandon, and say what is unreliable about the intuition that produced it — and tell me whether my quarrel with the Parfit-style view on which psychological continuity and connectedness are what matter, whatever their cause, is a substantive disagreement or merely a disagreement about what the word 'I' is tracking in cases such as (6), (7) and (12).
