I'm in an advanced-undergrad modal epistemology seminar this term (there's two PhD students sitting in, which is why the reading list is brutal) and for the last session we're staging a scripted debate instead of doing papers. I'm writing the script for another pair because one of them dropped, and the prof explicitly said no more zombie-argument reenactments, he's seen forty of them. So the setup: a modal rationalist — someone holding that ideal conceivability entails metaphysical possibility, roughly the two-dimensionalist package, Kripke-style a posteriori necessity handled by distinguishing what's true at a world considered as actual from what's true at it considered as counterfactual — versus a modal empiricist/skeptic who thinks conceivability reports at best on the shape of our own concepts and imaginative capacities, not on the modal structure of reality.

I invented a case for them to fight over, and I'd like the whole dialogue built around it rather than around the usual textbook examples. Here it is. Priya is a chemist who's known since her first year that water is H2O and has no doubt about it. She reports three things. First: she can conceive, in full positive detail, of a world microphysically just like ours in which the clear, drinkable liquid filling the oceans and rivers isn't water — she calls this easy, basically twin-earth reasoning. Second: after years of reflection she finds she cannot frame at all a world in which the clear liquid in the beaker in front of her is water but isn't H2O — not that she disbelieves such a world, but that she can't get the scenario to cohere, it slips apart whenever she tries to hold it. Third, and this is the part I actually care about: Priya, an only child raised by her mother with no evidence either way about siblings, reports the exact same kind of total imaginative failure for a world just like ours in which she has an older brother. She insists this is not a belief that she had none — she knows full well such worlds are possible, she says so out loud — but she simply can't build the conception anymore, hasn't been able to for years, and no amount of further reflection, information, or effort restores it. The scenario just won't assemble.

So: is report three a counterexample to conceivability as a guide, or does it just show the guide was never meant to be about actual humans' imaginings? Have both of them engage seriously with that. The rationalist has to say out loud what the idealization is doing, what constraints make an ideal conceiver well-defined rather than a get-out-of-jail-free card, and why the same idealizing move applied to report two isn't just special pleading. The skeptic has to offer a positive account of report one — not merely "conceivability is unreliable" — and has to deal with the awkward fact that their own deflationary move seems to dissolve their best datum too, since if conceivability only tracks conceptual competence then report three is unremarkable and report two tells us nothing modal either way. I also want a decent chunk of the dialogue spent on whether the disagreement is even substantive: does the rationalist's epistemic-possibility gloss make their thesis trivially true, and if it does, is that a bug or the whole point. Nobody wins, but end with each of them stating in a sentence or two what would actually settle the issue — some further case, some arguement, some experiment — and each explaining why the other's proposed test begs the question.
