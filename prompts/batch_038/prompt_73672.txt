In three separate debates about historical inevitability that I have followed closely over the past year — one on the Industrial Revolution in Britain, one on the collapse of the Soviet Union, one on the fall of the western Roman Empire — the argument took the same shape, and in each case I found myself unable to say what was going wrong. I am now writing a dissertation chapter on counterfactual method in economic history, and I need a term, or at least a precise diagnosis, for this shape.

Here it is. Someone asserts that a large historical outcome was inevitable. An opponent offers a counterfactual in which it did not occur, and offers it responsibly: the succession goes differently, the harvest holds, the fleet is not recalled, the reformist faction keeps power. The defender of inevitability does not contest the counterfactual's coherence, and often grants that the world described would differ enormously in its particulars. What they say instead is that the outcome would have arrived anyway — later, or elsewhere, or under another banner, or in somewhat different dress. The particular event the counterfactual removes gets replaced by the process it instantiated, and the thesis survives untouched.

Two features are what I want to capture. First, every one of these concessions is locally respectable. Distinguishing an event from the conditions that generated it is exactly what a careful historian ought to do, and no single step looks like a dodge. Yet after three or four of them the original claim is compatible with nearly any history one cares to describe, and nothing has been risked at any point. Second — and this is the part I have seen no one remark — the contingency side runs the identical maneuver in reverse. Tell a defender of inevitability that the process would have completed itself in the counterfactual world, and they will now insist that what happened there would not have been the same revolution, the same industrialization, the same collapse: it was led by a different class, it rested on different institutions, it lacked this defining feature. Fine-grained description protects contingency exactly as coarse-grained description protects inevitability.

So the dispute does not appear to be about the facts. It appears to be about how finely the outcome is specified before the question is asked, with both sides adjusting that specification in mid-argument and neither ever naming the parameter or defending their setting of it.

I can tell you what it is not, which is perhaps more useful. It is not No True Scotsman: no category is being kept pure against a counterexample, because the claim at issue is about how the world had to go, and what gets re-described is the outcome, not the extension of a predicate. It is not hindsight bias, which is a distortion in a person's confidence rather than a move in an argument. It is not plain unfalsifiability, since both sides do offer considerations and would presumably withdraw on some of them. It is not equivocation either, because nobody is trading between two senses of a word; if anything each side is holding to one sense while altering the granularity of the thing the sense picks out. And the standard complaint that counterfactual history is a parlor game is a charge against the whole enterprise, not an account of this particular dialectic.

What I want is the carving, not the label — though I do want to know whether a label exists. Please answer in the following sections, in this order:

**The phenomenon, precisely stated** (about 150 words). Restate the pattern compactly enough that I could use your version in a footnote, separating the two directions it runs in, and give the conditions a debate has to meet for the pattern to be present at all rather than merely for two people to talk past each other.

**Nearest established concepts** (about 600 words, the heart of the answer). Take at least four candidates — I expect these to come from the philosophy of history, from work on causation and explanation, and from the methodology of counterfactual reasoning — and for each say what the concept holds, how much of my description it captures, and precisely where the fit breaks down. If a candidate concept applies only to one of the two directions, say so.

**Best fit** (about 150 words). Which of these comes closest, and is there anything in the literature that names this outright? If you are unsure whether a named concept exists, say that you are unsure rather than inventing a citation.

**A name and a definition** (about 250 words). If nothing names it, propose one and define it in terms of conditions a debate either meets or fails to meet, and state explicitly what your definition excludes so that it does not simply become a label for "unproductive disagreement."

**Boundary cases** (about 300 words). At least three, with verdicts. Include: a case where re-specifying the outcome is the correct thing to do because the original claim mis-described what was to be explained; a case where a coarse-grained inevitability claim is a substantive empirical thesis about how many and how varied the counterfactuals are that lead to the same outcome-type, rather than a retreat; and a case where the fine-grained resistance is a real point. I want to know where the edges of the concept lie, not just its center.

**What would settle it** (about 150 words). What a disputant could do to turn the silent parameter into an arguable claim — what fixing the grain in advance would require, and what would count as a reason for one grain rather than another.
