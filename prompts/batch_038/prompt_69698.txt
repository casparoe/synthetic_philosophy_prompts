**The photocopier test**

There are two ways people talk about what might have happened to a thing. On one, objects are stuck inside their own world, and a claim like Amelia could have been a baker comes out true because in some other world there's someone a lot like Amelia who is a baker. On the other, the very same Amelia is literally there in lots of worlds, and one of those Amelias is a baker.

Everyone treats this as a deep disagreement, but I've started to think it might just be two ways of doing the bookkeeping, and I have a test for it.

Photocopy everything. Every world gets an exact duplicate; every duplicate world contains duplicates of all its contents; and you widen the counterpart relation so that each thing is also a counterpart of its own copy. Now ask what changed. Amelia could have been a baker — still true. Nothing Amelia actually did is different, no new possibilities turn up, no old ones vanish. The duplication is invisible from the inside. And notice the other theory puts up with duplication too: if two worlds are duplicates, the same Amelia is simply in both, and again nothing changes.

So both theories have this slack where you can multiply everything out and stay exactly where you were. That smells to me like the difference between them is the same kind of thing: redundant structure rather than a claim about the world. If two descriptions can be blown up into each other without altering anything you'd ever notice, they're describing one situation twice.

The best counter I've managed against myself: the counterpart side can say things the other can't. I read somewhere that on that view it's allowed for a single world to hold two people who are both counterparts of me, so there's a world where I run into myself and we get a coffee. The transworld person supposedly can't say that, because one object can't be in the same world twice. But I can just rerun the photocopier inside one world — stick two copies of Amelia in it and let them meet. Same trick, apparently. Except the two sentences then don't mean quite the same: mine is Amelia meets a copy of Amelia, theirs is Amelia meets Amelia. And I can't work out whether that leftover gap is a genuine difference between the theories or just more bookkeeping.

What I want comments on:

1. Does the photocopier argument do any actual work, or is there an obvious objection that wrecks it?
2. Where have I got either view wrong or badly oversimplified?
3. Is there any sentence at all that comes out true on one theory and false on the other — i.e. can the dispute be shown to be non-verbal rather than notational?

You dont need to send me off reading anything, I mainly want to know whether the reasoning holds up. British English please.
