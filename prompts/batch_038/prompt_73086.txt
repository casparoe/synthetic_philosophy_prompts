I am a junior in high school, and I run a small Saturday philosophy reading group with three friends and one very patient teacher who moderates but refuses to lecture. This month we are working through questions about blame: who is entitled to blame whom, and whether a hypocrite forfeits that entitlement. I have drawn the short straw and been assigned to defend the view that hypocrisy really does undermine standing, which is unfortunate, because after several weeks of arguing about it I suspect the opposite. I would like to practice before our next session.

Please play a professional moral philosopher who holds a firm and articulate version of the following position: standing to blame is not a separate possession that can be acquired or lost, so hypocrisy — of any variety — never defeats it. On your view, blame is legitimate when the moral claim it expresses is true and the blamer is responding to a genuine wrong for the right kind of reason; the blamer's own record bears at most on whether the blame will be effective, well received, or prudent, and never on whether it is permissible or apt. Think carefully before each reply and give me an argument rather than a slogan. Be sharp and patient, and feel free to concede subsidiary points — you may grant that hypocrites are often insufferable, that their blame is frequently self-defeating, and that hypocrisy is itself sometimes a moral failing — but never concede the main thesis. Stay in character and in the first person until I write "stop." Keep each reply to roughly two paragraphs so this feels like a conversation rather than an essay, and please give me no citations, reading lists, or names of philosophers unless I name one first; if you want to lean on a well-known argument, restate it in your own words. Before you take up my opening move, spend two or three sentences stating your thesis as plainly as you can, and add one sentence naming what you consider the strongest objection to it, since I want to be certain of exactly what I am arguing against.

Here is my opening move. My older brother cheated on exams for two years, was caught, and has genuinely reformed. Last week he discovered that our younger sister had copied a friend's homework, and he sat her down for a serious moral lecture in which he said explicitly that he had done the same thing, that it was wrong, and that his own history was precisely why he was telling her. He does not exempt himself from the norm, does not pretend to have satisfied it, and has no illusions about his past — so the account our group keeps reaching for, that the hypocrite's real offence is granting himself an exception he denies to others, simply does not touch him. Yet something still seems off in two ways I cannot yet articulate: he has never apologized to the people his cheating actually harmed, and the standards he now applies to her are ones he was content to live under for as long as they suited him. My question to you is therefore this. If your view is correct, then everything intuitive about the claim that he lacks standing reduces to a remark about tone and effectiveness, and blaming collapses into nothing more than asserting a true moral proposition loudly at somebody. Are you prepared to say there is no difference in kind between my brother's lecture and the identical words spoken by a teacher who never cheated, or are you letting a notion of standing back in through the side door?
