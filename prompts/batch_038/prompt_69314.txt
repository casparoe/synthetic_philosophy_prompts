I'm building a session on sphexishness — the digger wasp thing, where the wasp hauls her paralyzed prey to the mouth of the burrow, goes inside to check, and if you drag the prey a few inches while she's in there she hauls it back to the threshold and starts the whole routine over — and the case my students already have in their heads is completely played out, so I want one engineered to invert the lesson.

Build me two vignettes. Same unglamorous workplace, same job, same shift: pick something like a municipal permitting counter, a hotel banquet kitchen, a pharmacy tech bench, whatever you can make concrete. Person A behaves, from the outside, exactly like the wasp — cue-triggered, restarts from scratch when interrupted, won't improvise, re-does completed steps. Person B is the smooth one: adapts on the fly, absorbs every curveball, never repeats a step needlessly. The inversion I want: A is the agent with real flexibility, and B is the deeply sphexish one, because B's graceful adaptiveness is itself nothing but high-bandwidth cue-chasing — B has no policy, B is the situationist's picture of a person, spun out moment to moment by whatever the room throws at her.

Constraints, and these are the part that matters:

- One tight paragraph per vignette. No scifi, no brain imaging, no "unbeknownst to her, a module in her visual system" — everything has to be followable by a reader in real time from the outside.
- A's rigidity has to be something A would endorse and could drop at will, but you may not make it an obvious precommitment gadget. No locked safes, no burning bridges, no Ulysses-and-the-mast talk, no contract, no literal checklist taped to a wall. Get the same result by less cartoonish means.
- B has to be genuinely excellent at the job and to outperform A on every metric that appears in the story, so nobody can dismiss the case as "B is just sloppy."
- There must be one unexpected interruption that hits both of them, described in both vignettes, and their responses to it have to diverge. That shared moment is the whole evidential weight of the case, so design it deliberately rather than letting it fall out.

Then, after the scenarios:

1. Spell out what the usual test for sphexishness is actually keying on — restart-versus-resume, cue-versus-goal, or whatever you think is the real criterion — and say precisely why it hands back the wrong verdict on this pair.
2. Give me the best available move for a defender of the standard picture to dissolve the inversion. Not a strawman: the strongest thing they can say, e.g. that A's endorsement is just another cue among others, or that "having a policy" is doing no work here. Then say whether that move succeeds and why.
3. Name the single feature of the design that's load-bearing, and tell me what the case collapses into if I delete it.
4. Be straight with me about whether the two vignettes are actually observationally distinguishable from the outside, or whether I've just smuggled the verdicts in through the narrator's tone. If it's smuggling, tell me which sentence does it and how to fix it.
