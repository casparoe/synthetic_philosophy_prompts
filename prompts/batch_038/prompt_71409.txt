I teach a third-year seminar on moral cultures and moral change — 22 students, mixed philosophy and sociology majors — and one 80-minute session is devoted to the three-way scheme that distinguishes cultures organized around honor from those organized around dignity from those organized around victimhood: roughly, the view that a person's worth is socially conferred and must therefore be actively claimed and defended, the view that it is inherent and inalienable and so cannot be diminished by insult or conferred by praise, and the view that standing accrues to those who have been harmed and carries with it a claim to be believed and attended to. My students arrive able to recite the taxonomy fluently and then immediately convert it into a ranking: dignity as the mature position, honor as an atavism surviving in the peripheries, victimhood as either a pathology or a shrewd exploitation of dignity's blind spots. Two years running I have tried to break this with structured discussion and it fails in the same way — they concede politeness points and then reassert the ranking in different words. Last term I ran a crude prototype of what I actually want: I handed out three rule sheets, told each group of seven which one was theirs, and asked them to negotiate over a shared pot of tokens. It collapsed instructively. The first group bullied the others, the second announced that the game was beneath them and drifted into side conversations, the third compiled grievances that nobody had any machinery to hear, and the debrief degenerated into me explaining why each of those failures was precisely the point. I want the failures to do that work without me.

Design the activity properly. I need three rulebooks, distributed under invented neutral names rather than under the labels, that differ mechanically in four respects — how standing is acquired, how it can be lost or forfeited, what counts as a correct response to a slight, and whether third parties have any role in settling matters — with none of those four dimensions ever announced as such to the players. Write out the incident material they adjudicate, a dozen or so concrete cards, since the content of the incidents is carrying much of the load: I need ones that are trivially resolvable under one rulebook and structurally insoluble under another, and ones where the same event is a serious injury under one and literally not an event under a different one. Build in a cross-table phase in which a player operating under one rulebook must deal with a counterpart whose rulebook does not recognize the move just made, with that non-recognition enforced by the rules themselves rather than left to a student's role fidelity. Specify a token economy that requires no real money, group sizes, the physical setup (one room, movable tables, index cards, a whiteboard, and a projector I do not trust), and a minute-by-minute timeline across the 80. Most importantly, arrange the payoffs so that no rulebook simply wins: whoever maximizes their own table's currency should pay for it elsewhere in the game, so that the question of whether one of these is morally better than the others emerges from the scores on the whiteboard instead of from a prompt I read aloud.

Then give me the debrief, mapping particular moments in play onto the three conceptions of standing, and hand me a plan for the two deflections I can predict: the student who insists the second table obviously won because it is the only one that is not exhausting, and the student who insists the whole scheme is a sham trichotomy, just three settings on a single dial measuring how much of a person's worth is socially conferred rather than intrinsic. Say what will go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the room without restarting. Be frank about what the mechanics cannot reproduce — above all that nobody in this game was raised inside their rulebook, chose it, or has any way to leave it, which I suspect is much of what makes the real versions non-optional rather than merely strategic — and tell me what I would have to supply verbally to cover that gap, or concede that it is an unfixable hole in the exercise. If the activity can be made to work with nine students, which happens about once a term, say how it changes; if it cannot, say so. The end state I am after is not consensus but the students noticing that each rulebook renders some injuries vivid and others unintelligible, and finding that they cannot say which set of blind spots they would rather live inside.
