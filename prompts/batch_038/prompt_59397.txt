I serve as senior adviser to the unit of a national government that receives petitions for official acknowledgment of historical wrongs — forced removals of children, detention without trial, dispossession of land, state-sanctioned discrimination in employment and housing. Roughly twenty petitions arrive each year; three or four reach the drafting stage, and each resulting text is read aloud at a public ceremony, entered into the official record, and thereafter cited by claimants' lawyers, by negotiating teams, and by other states weighing comparable questions. The drafting officers who produce these texts are lawyers and historians, not philosophers, and they have no framework for deciding what they are actually producing. The predictable result is that we regularly issue documents described publicly as apologies that do not, on inspection, apologize for anything.

What I need is a decision procedure the unit can run on any draft text of official acknowledgment in about an hour and that yields a written classification we can defend if it is challenged in public or in a tribunal. Ground the tests in whatever speech-act theory, the philosophy of collective and corporate agency, and the ethics of apology can actually support; where the underlying position is disputed and the procedure must simply choose, I want that flagged as a choice rather than reported as a finding. Please deliver the following:

1. An ordered list of tests, each phrased so that a non-specialist can apply it to a sentence in a draft, and each with an explicit statement of what counts as passing and what as failing.
2. A classification vocabulary: the distinct kinds of act a draft might be performing instead of apologizing — sorrow at an outcome, acknowledgment of historical fact, statement of sympathy toward sufferers, disavowal of a past regime, commitment to reform, request for reconciliation — together with the tests that determine placement.
3. For every test, whether it is conceptually necessary for the text to be an apology at all or merely pragmatically advisable given how such texts are received, and which tests could pass while the document still fails as an apology in the eyes of the people it concerns.
4. For each failed test, the minimal repair that would restore it, and a clear marking of the repairs that cannot be accomplished by rewording because they require an accompanying act — a named agent accepting responsibility, an acknowledged obligation, some transfer or restitution. The purpose of the procedure is to stop us producing the appearance of apology, not to help us produce it more cheaply.
5. The points at which the procedure must stop and hand the question to the minister because it is one of value or political judgment rather than of what an apology is.
6. What the procedure cannot settle under any circumstances — I expect this to include sincerity, whether an apology may properly be sought from a party who has not offered one, and whether the wronged party's uptake is required for the apology to have been given.

The output must fit on two pages and be usable by drafting officers without further explanation, since every draft passes legal review and then goes to the minister, and I have about a week per case.

Then run the procedure on the three drafts currently on my desk, and give me the classification, the tests failed, and the repairs available for each.

Draft A — the text our office considers its strongest work, since it has cleared legal review without amendment. The state expresses profound sorrow for the suffering endured by children and their families under a mid-century program of removals; acknowledges that the suffering was real, widespread, and lasting; observes that the program was conceived in accordance with the beliefs and standards of its time and that no person now living bears responsibility for it; and commits funding to a memorial and to a review of current child-welfare practice.

Draft B — the minister, speaking for the state, says that the state did wrong, names the wrong as detention without trial carried out by state officials under color of law, accepts responsibility for it on behalf of the state, and expresses regret and shame, closing with a clause providing that the statement neither creates nor implies any legal or financial obligation and that all claims arising from the period are closed.

Draft C — a petition directed at the present administration concerning acts committed by a prior regime that this administration repudiated and helped to remove. The draft disavows that regime, acknowledges that the present state holds property and advantages acquired during it, and expresses regret for the harms suffered. The petitioners are descendants; the principal representative body has stated it will not accept an apology unaccompanied by reparation, while a second group argues that an official apology will be used to declare the matter closed. The draft nowhere says to whom it is addressed or whether it awaits acceptance.

Tell me plainly which of the three the procedure classifies as an apology, including where the classification is unflattering to us.
