I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest outlet, and I'm reporting a piece on how a very large logistics and retail firm now allocates resources inside itself. The division I'm covering has done away with annual budget-setting. In its place there is a continuous re-planning system: every hour an optimizer assigns shadow prices to delivery windows, dock doors, forklift hours, shelf footage, and labor; forecasts roll forward; and site managers no longer negotiate for resources — they receive an assignment and work to it. The executives I've spoken to describe this, with almost no irony, as a planned economy that happens to work. My editor would like me to say something more substantial than the obvious observation that central planning has been revived under new management, and I'd rather not write the piece as a history lesson either.

What I want from you is the most important implications of the socialist calculation debate — Mises's argument that rational economic calculation is impossible where there are no market-generated prices for the means of production, Hayek's objection that the economically relevant knowledge is dispersed, local, and largely inarticulable, and Lange's reply that a planning board can arrive at prices by setting them parametrically and correcting surpluses and shortages through trial and error — for that specific situation. Please work through the following, in whatever order makes the argument hang together best:

1. Which of the three arguments actually bites here, and which is inert because the firm sits inside a real market and can import genuine prices from outside it? Is the number the optimizer produces a price in the sense Mises and Lange meant, or a Lagrange multiplier wearing a price's clothes — and does the difference matter for the argument?

2. Lange's board iterates on observable aggregates; this system does something structurally comparable, only faster and in finer grain. Does the frequency of iteration answer Hayek, or does his objection not depend on speed at all? What would have to be true of the firm's data capture for the knowledge problem to lose its force?

3. Almost none of the operative local knowledge gets entered: the dock leveler that jams in cold weather, the driver who knows a certain overpass is shut, the packing method that stops a fragile item breaking. My instinct as a reporter is that work-to-rule action is the cleanest natural experiment available — everyone follows the specification exactly, and throughput collapses. Treat that as evidence rather than as colour. Does the calculation debate predict it, and does it also predict the specific accommodations such firms make: deliberate slack, informal bargaining, tolerated non-compliance, internal markets, discretion left nominally intact? What should I be looking for on a site visit, and how would I recognize one of those accommodations when I see it?

4. The standard Hayekian counter to Mises is the question of the firm: if planning works inside a corporation, why not across an economy? The answer usually given is that using the price mechanism has costs, so firms exist only where substituting direction for exchange is cheaper. If the optimizer lowers precisely those costs of measuring, monitoring, and renegotiating, does that imply such firms should grow without limit, or that internal direction will be displaced by internal pricing? What does the debate imply about where the boundary should sit?

5. I keep hearing from people on the computational side that large-scale linear programming is a solved problem, and that the mid-century dispute was really a quarrel about computing power all along. Give me the strongest version of that claim, and the strongest reply to it that is not simply a restatement of the tacit-knowledge point.

Two constraints on form. Give me the analysis rather than the apparatus: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no quotations — I cannot check a stack of titles, and what I need is reasoning whose joints I can see. And assume a reader who is intelligent and curious but has never encountered the debate; define your terms as you go.

Close by telling me how confident you are in your main claims, calibrated rather than hedged, and single out the point in what you have written that you are least sure of — the place where a specialist in the history of this debate would most likely say you have got it wrong or flattened something important. If your honest view is that one side has the better of this case and the other side's argument simply does not reach it, say so plainly instead of balancing the two for tidiness.
