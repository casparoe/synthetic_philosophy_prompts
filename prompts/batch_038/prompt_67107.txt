I sit on the capital planning committee of a regional water utility. Each cycle we divide a fixed tranche of capital among competing projects, and our charter requires every proposal to carry three numbers: a point estimate of the probability of the adverse event the project is meant to prevent, an expected monetary value, and, where the evidence is thin, a ninety-percent interval around that estimate. The charter adds that any proposal whose interval includes zero "must be treated as unproven."

Two projects compete this cycle. The first is a pump-station refurbishment: forty years of failure records, a defensible probability, a modest but solid return. The second is hardening our control systems against a cascading failure we have never experienced and for which no frequency exists; the consultants produced three scenario narratives that are mutually inconsistent in their causal stories but overlapping in their consequences. Under the current rule the second project fails — its interval is enormous and its lower bound negative — and the committee has split four ways. One faction holds that this is precisely what the interval is for: thin evidence should be penalized by width. A second proposes that we add a separate "weight of evidence" score from one to five and multiply it in as a discount on expected value, so that data-rich proposals rank higher automatically. A third thinks the hardening project should be funded on precautionary grounds regardless of the arithmetic. A fourth thinks the rest of us are doing philosophy and wants a vote.

I have been reading Keynes's Treatise on Probability and his later treatment of long-term expectations in The General Theory, and I suspect the quarrel concerns a distinction our charter has never drawn. Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete disposition of both projects with a time frame; explain, in language the engineers and the treasurer can follow, why the interval requirement does not do the work its defenders think it does, and whether the proposed weight-of-evidence multiplier repairs the problem or destroys the distinction it was meant to preserve — including whether an accession of evidence can raise the standing of a claim while lowering its probability, and what our rule should do about that case; say why the Keynesian line licenses neither paralysis nor the precautionary shortcut; and propose a revised decision procedure the committee can actually operate next cycle — what must be recorded, who decides what, what triggers review — under the constraint that it must yield a decision rather than a deferral. I lean toward the second faction's diagnosis but distrust its remedy; press that. Give the strongest case for the faction you expect to lose, and state where you would overrule me. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or piece of evidence would flip your recommendation, and at which step your reasoning is most likely to fail.
