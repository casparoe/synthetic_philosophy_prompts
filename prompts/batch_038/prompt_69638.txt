so last week my friend Dana — we did our undergrad together, she's a case manager at a housing nonprofit now — had a work thing where a coworker she's genuinely friendly with had been quietly padding his timesheets, maybe an hour a week, for months. she typed the whole situation into one of the AI chat assistants, got back an answer that said report it to the supervisor but talk to him first, did exactly that, and it went about as well as could be expected. when she retold it in our group chat she added "honestly I couldn't have told you *why* reporting was the right call, the bot laid it out and it just seemed right to me."

that kicked off a pretty long argument between two other people in the chat, Marta (philosophy phd student) and Eli (Dana's brother, engineer, zero philosophy training but very stubborn). it went roughly like this, in six moves:

1. Marta: adopting a moral conclusion on the machine's say-so is the textbook case of a defective moral belief — right answer, no understanding. so even though Dana did the right thing she doesn't get much credit for it, and worse she's badly placed for the next case, since she can't defend the verdict or see when it stops applying.
2. Eli: that's just a general complaint about testimony and it proves way too much. almost everything any of us believes morally came from parents and friends without us being able to argue for it. if that makes those beliefs defective then basically no ordinary person is a decent moral agent, which is a reductio of Marta's position, not a discovery.
3. Marta: there's a real difference between beliefs soaked up inside a moral community you're actually part of and a one-off verdict you extract from a machine and adopt on the spot. the first comes bundled with a shared practice of giving and asking for reasons you can eventually join; the second is a shortcut around that whole practice.
4. Eli: but the machine isn't a testifier in the first place. nobody is standing behind the assertion — it can't be insincere, it can't be blamed for misleading her, it doesn't know what it's saying. so the entire trust-someone's-judgment framework doesn't apply. asking it was closer to using a calculator or reading a poll than to taking somebody's word.
5. Marta: that distinction helps me, not you. if it's a calculator for moral questions then it's worse than a person, because with a calculator you can check the output yourself, and the whole difficulty in the moral case was that she can't check it — so she outsourced exactly the part that can't be outsourced.
6. Eli: except she did check it. she said it "seemed right" to her, and that gut-check is the only checking anybody ever does, including you when you accept what your supervisors and professors tell you. you don't independently verify their arguments either, you just find them convincing.
