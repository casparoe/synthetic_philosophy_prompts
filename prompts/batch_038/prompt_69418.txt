I sit on the three-member review panel of a state pilot program operating under an explicitly non-retributive protocol: confinement is permitted only insofar as it prevents harm, must be the least restrictive means of doing so, and must come with genuine rehabilitative provision. The legislature adopted this framework on the advice of the Caruso side of the just-deserts debate, and our training materials deny basic desert outright.

This month's case. Mr. R., now 31, was convicted at 24 of aggravated robbery with a firearm; no one was hurt, the statutory minimum is long satisfied, his risk score sits in the lowest decile, and he has housing and work waiting. On the protocol's own logic he should be released. He refuses. He says he has not answered for what he did and that walking out early would make him a liar to himself and to the people he robbed; he has petitioned to serve the balance of his term. The two victims who spoke at his last hearing back him, and say the program's refusal to punish is a second injury nobody asked them to accept. The protocol is silent, and my colleagues have split: one holds that releasing him over his objection is basic desert returning in the guise of state paternalism, the other that honoring his petition smuggles retributivism back in under the name of autonomy.

Draft the opinion I will circulate before we vote: a definite recommendation — release, continued confinement, or some conditional third course — with reasons the other two members can actually contest, and an honest statement of the strongest case against your own recommendation. It should say what the Dennett line can do here, that attributing desert is inseparable from the practices by which we hold people like Mr. R. competent at all, and what Caruso's denial of basic desert leaves the panel entitled to impose; and above all how much weight a subject's own demand to be punished can carry inside a framework that has to justify every restriction forward-lookingly.
