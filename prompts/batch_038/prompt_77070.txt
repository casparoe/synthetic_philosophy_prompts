I'm the unofficial note-taker for our country's Quaternary boundary panel — we're the national body that votes on proposals before they go up to the international commission, so our minutes end up in the record either way. I've been sitting in for two years, and I'm also in a history-of-science seminar this term on periodization, which has me looking at my own panel sideways. We vote on the Anthropocene proposal next month and I want to work out in advance both how we'll actually vote and how we *should* vote if we care about being consistent with ourselves. (My partner thinks the whole exercise is fake precision, which is not helping.)

Our record so far, roughly:

- we ratified the end-Cretaceous boundary on the strength of the global iridium layer plus shocked quartz, and the report was emphatic that what made it acceptable was that the signal is instantaneous on geological timescales and physically permanent;
- we rejected a proposal to base a boundary on a major faunal turnover that everyone in the room agreed was the single most important biological event of its era, because nobody could tie it to one datable horizon — the chair's line was that importance is not a marker;
- we approved a boundary defined by a magnetic reversal recorded in seafloor stripes, even though it marks no biological or climatic turning point and nobody outside palaeomagnetism has ever cared about it;
- we approved a boundary resting on a global oxygen-isotope excursion, but only after the working group added auxiliary sections and a field-usable proxy; the industry reps had argued that a boundary nobody can identify in outcrop is worthless, and they lost the vote but won the conditions;
- we rejected a proposal to fix a boundary at the first appearance of a tool-making hominin, on the grounds that first appearances are diachronous — the species spreads, so it turns up at different times on different continents — and that you can't distinguish first appearance from first preservation. Notably the report never says anything about humans being out of place in a geological column. A few of us think that's the real reason wearing a disguise;
- we've twice deferred a decision to wait for the international body, and once voted early and got overruled, which people still bring up bitterly.

The new proposal: base a new epoch/series on a horizon marked by globally distributed artificial radionuclides from mid-twentieth-century weapons testing, alongside soot spherules, novel alloys and plastic fragments. Type section is an annually laminated lake-sediment core; the signal is synchronous worldwide to within a couple of years and preserved in lake mud, ice, corals and estuarine deposits; the proposed date is around the middle of the last century. The proposal's own argument for why the boundary matters is that this is where one species becomes a planetary force. Two awkward features: the type-section sediment is still unconsolidated mud, and the new unit would be about 70 years long sitting on top of a stage that runs to a couple million years. The working group explicitly considered and rejected earlier horizons — a carbon-dioxide dip in ice cores a few centuries back, and the spread of farming a few thousand years back — because their markers are regional or smeared over time. Also, two new members joined us this year, a historian and an archaeologist, and both have said theyll vote yes; three of the old guard have said in so many words that people who aren't stratigraphers shouldn't have a vote on a stratigraphic boundary.

So: predict our vote, and tell me the rule we're actually running. I've heard two versions from people in the room. One is that we only ever ratify signals that are globally synchronous and permanent, full stop, and everything else is commentary. The other is that we only ratify signals produced by non-intentional Earth-system processes, so the whole thing fails at the gate because this marker is an artifact of decisions rather than of processes. I suspect there are at least two more rules that fit our record just as well and give different answers here, something about usefulness to working correlators and something about what a boundary is even for. Go through the candidates, say which of our precedents each one fits and which it strains, then say what each predicts for the fallout proposal — approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions — and which precedent is the closest analog and why, including whether the hominin case really is distinguishable or whether we're about to contradict ourselves. If the record genuinely underdetermines it, I want to know what principle we'd be adopting with each choice, and separately whether the historian and the archaeologist get to vote on this one. Finally name the further case — a hypothetical proposal we could plausibly be handed — that would best tell the candidate rules apart.

Format, please: open with a **Bottom line** of about three sentences giving your predicted vote and the rule you think explains it. Then **The rules on offer** (~400 words, a paragraph per candidate rule, with the precedents that support it and the ones it strains). Then **Closest analog** (~150 words). Then **Where they diverge** (~300 words, what each rule says about the fallout proposal and about the two new voters). Then **What we'd be committing to** (~200 words, the principle adopted under each possible vote). Then **The case that would settle it** (~150 words, describe the hypothetical proposal concretely enough that I could actually put it on an agenda). Keep it under about 1300 words and skip references and suggested reading entirely — I just want the reasoning.
