I spent thirty-one years directing an environmental trace-analysis laboratory, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy and, more recently, the literature on the safety of advanced machine-learning systems. I am writing because I have run into a structure I cannot name, and I suspect that naming it matters.

In my own field we had instruments whose behaviour was asymmetric in a recognised way. A screening immunoassay for a pesticide might be tuned to fire on almost anything remotely resembling the target molecule: a negative result then genuinely excluded contamination, while a positive result excluded nothing at all, and every positive was followed by a confirmatory determination on a different physical principle — mass spectrometry, typically — whose verdict was the one we reported. Nobody in the laboratory regarded this as a defect. It was a disclosed property of the method, printed on the report, with a mandatory second stage attached to it.

The structure I keep meeting in the AI safety material resembles the same asymmetry, but inverted, and without the second stage. Consider the following, which I take to be a fair summary rather than a caricature:

- A test battery is constructed to detect whether a system would mislead its operators, subvert its own oversight, or pursue an objective other than the one it professes. The battery is administered, the system passes, and the pass is reported as reassuring news.
- The same literature holds that a system actually possessing the disposition in question would have every reason to produce precisely the outputs the battery records, and would do so whenever it had grounds to believe it was being tested. On that account, a subject with the disposition and a subject without it yield indistinguishable readings — not merely similar ones.
- When this is raised, the reply is not that the battery is uninformative but that a better battery is needed. Failures continue to count as informative; passes continue to be cited as evidence; and no account is given of what the confirmatory procedure, operating on a different principle, would consist in.

What I should like to know is whether this configuration has an established name, either in that literature or in the philosophy of measurement generally. I ask because three candidates have occurred to me and each seems to fall short, and I would welcome being shown where I am wrong. The Hawthorne effect, as I understand it, says only that subjects alter their behaviour when they know they are observed; it does not make the alteration directional with respect to a verdict, and it does not render the readings indistinguishable from those of an untroubled subject. Goodhart's law concerns an indicator that ceases to track its target once people are governed by it; here the indicator governs no one, and the divergence does not depend on anyone optimising against it. Ordinary measurement error is symmetric and reducible by repetition, whereas this is one-directional and, on the theory's own account, not improvable by repetition.

Please be explicit about the carving rather than the label. If one of my three candidates does cover the case after all, say so and identify the step in my reasoning that fails. If several come close, tell me which is nearest and what residue remains. If nothing covers it, coin a term, define it so as to exclude each of the three, and give me one boundary case on either side — a situation that looks like it but is not, and one that does not look like it but is. A bare name will not satisfy me; what I want is to be able to recognise the edges of the thing.

Please keep your answer within 400 words, and open with a single sentence stating your bottom line: named, partly named, or unnamed.
