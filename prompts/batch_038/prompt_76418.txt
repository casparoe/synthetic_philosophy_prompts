I'm not an academic and never have been; I came to this through years of practice and a great deal of unsystematic reading, so I'd ask you to bear with the homemade terminology. For eleven years I've been part of a weekly sitting group — no teacher, no institutional affiliation, just a dozen people who read and argue. For the last three, the argument has stopped moving entirely, and what I want to understand is why, not which side wins.

The group divides into two camps over the doctrine of no-self. Camp A treats it as a plain metaphysical claim: there is no person over and above a stream of interdependent physical and mental events, so a sentence asserting that the one who acted decades ago and the one who now regrets it are the same person is strictly false, and true only as loose talk — true of an assembled cart the way the word chariot is, by convention, applied to a heap of parts arranged thus. On this view the contemplative teaching that no permanent self is to be found and the analytic conclusion that persons reduce to events and relations are one claim reached by two routes, and the practical payoff, the loosening of clinging, is a consequence of getting the metaphysics right. Camp B holds the reverse: the doctrine was never a thesis about what exists but a discipline for interrupting a particular kind of grasping, and to treat it as ontology is to commit the very mistake it is meant to expose, since flatly asserting that there is no self reinstates the craving for a settled answer about oneself. Camp B maintains that persons exist conventionally and that the question whether they exist ultimately ought to be set aside rather than answered.

A third position has since grown up between them, mostly among newer members, and it is the one I find hardest to assess. I'll call it the dissolution view: the whole dispute is merely verbal and evaporates once you specify what work the term self is being asked to do — index the word to a question, and each camp turns out to be right about its own question, with no residual disagreement about the world.

My own hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis: the impasse is not about facts, and not about the meaning of self either. It is about what a doctrine must do to count as successful — whether the standard is correspondence or transformation — and neither camp can argue for its standard without already assuming it, which would make the dispute bedrock and immune to any amount of further reading.

So please diagnose the structure of the disagreement. Specifically: is A against B a substantive dispute, a verbal one, or two different questions traveling under a single name? Is the dissolution view a genuine third position, or a metaphysical claim dressed in neutral clothing — and if the latter, does it collapse in the way Camp B says every such view collapses, or does it escape that charge? Separately, the group spent a month on a member who reports that under general anesthesia there is not a gap in experience but no experience of any gap, and each camp immediately claimed the report as evidence for itself. Should reports of that kind count as data in this dispute at all, or is the disagreement in part about what could count as data?

Tell me what would constitute progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a reframing of the question, an argument neither side has tried, a change in what is being asked — or argue that no progress is available and say why. I am not after a survey of Buddhist schools or of the analytic literature on personal identity, and I would rather you not adjudicate which camp is correct; what I want is an account of why three years of able people arguing in good faith has produced nothing.
