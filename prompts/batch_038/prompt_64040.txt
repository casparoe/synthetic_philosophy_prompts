The episode is the St Petersburg lottery, 1713–1738. Nicolas Bernoulli writes to Montmort in 1713 with a game: flip a fair coin until the first head, and if the first head lands on toss n you get paid 2^(n-1) ducats. The expected payoff is 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 + ... = infinity, and yet, as his cousin Daniel later observes, any reasonably sane man would happily sell his chance for twenty ducats. In 1738 Daniel Bernoulli publishes his own treatment in the St Petersburg academy's proceedings and offers the fix everyone remembers: don't average money, average utility, and let utility be logarithmic in your total wealth, so gains count for less the richer you already are. (Cramer had floated a square-root version in a letter a decade earlier.) Under log utility the game's expected utility is finite and the paradox dissolves. Two centuries later Menger points out this can't be the whole story, since for any unbounded utility function you can cook up a game whose prizes grow fast enough to break it again — so the received moral is that utility has to be bounded.
