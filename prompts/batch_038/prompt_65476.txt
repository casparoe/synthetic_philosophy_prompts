I have run a fortnightly philosophy meetup for about eight years, originally spun off from a rationalist discussion group, and I write up our better arguments for a small site that nobody reads but me. Last Thursday we took up the value problem in the Meno: why knowledge should be worth more than true belief, given that Socrates concedes a guide with mere true opinion about the road to Larissa gets you there just as well. Two regulars — Reuben, who runs a systematic trading desk and does prediction-market work on the side, and Ilse, an audit partner — produced the most careful dispute we have had in years. Neither budged, and they have asked me to referee. I would rather have a proper adjudication of the arguments as actually made than a survey of the literature. Here is the exchange.

1. **Reuben:** The problem is a bookkeeping error. Take any true belief; its value is the value of the outcome it secures. Knowledge adds nothing to that. What it adds is a property of the *source*: it raises the probability that the same agent's future beliefs in that domain are true — the conditional-probability line Goldman and Olsson press against the swamping worry. Add the value of this belief to the expected value of the beliefs it makes likely and there is no residue left to explain. Plato has asked us to find loose change in a drawer we have already counted. His own answer points the same way: opinions are like the statues of Daedalus and run away unless tethered by working out the reason, and what tethering buys is retention and reuse — generality again.

2. **Ilse:** That answers a different question. Socrates asks about *this* journey to *this* Larissa and concedes true opinion guides it as well; you only ever point at a second journey. And the swamping objection bites your reply directly: if the extra value of a belief's source is purely instrumental — value as a means to further true belief — then in the case where the true belief is already in hand and has already paid out, that instrumental value is swamped by the realised value of the product. A cup from a reliable espresso machine is worth exactly what a cup from an unreliable one is worth once you are drinking it. Reliability earns its keep only in aggregate, and the aggregate is not what Plato asked about.

3. **Reuben:** Then I bite the bullet, and the bullet is soft. Token for token, in the single instance, knowledge is worth no more than true belief; the contrary intuition is the value of a practice projected illicitly onto a member of it. So here is my challenge. Any extra value you claim for the token must make a difference somewhere. Name one decision, one action, one payment, one emotion that ought to differ between an agent who knows that p and an agent who merely truly believes that p, holding content and downstream consequences fixed. If you cannot, the value you are positing is what I shall call *epistemically inert* — not a value, just a word.

4. **Ilse:** Some goods are constituted by their history rather than merely caused by it, and there the ancestry is anything but inert. An author's original proof and a perfect facsimile differ in nothing you can see, yet museums insure, display and loan them differently — the difference issues in decisions. Two identical apologies differ utterly in what the recipient should do next, depending on whether either was prompted by recognition of the harm or by a communications adviser. And in my own trade: two clinicians both conclude pulmonary embolism and both begin anticoagulation, but one arrived by working a differential and the other by a hunch after a night on call. Same token, same treatment — yet the second has a residual duty the first lacks, namely to order the scan anyway, because his belief is not one he is entitled to rest on. The answer to your challenge: the decision that differs is the decision whether to check again.

5. **Reuben:** Every one of those is a case where the process is *not* reliably truth-conducive — the facsimile, the managed apology, the hunch. They show we attend to provenance where provenance signals unreliability, which is compatible with all I said. So control for reliability. Vera takes a drug that affords her exactly one episode of flawless perception — she correctly locates a buried cable in a field — and permanently destroys the faculty thereafter. Lena forms the identical true belief from a corrupted survey map in which a transcription error happens to point at the right place. Neither will repeat the performance; neither has future beliefs in this domain. Find no difference in value between them, and your provenance intuition was the generality intuition in disguise and I have won. Find one, and tell me where the value sits when content, truth and action are all identical and the future is closed. Notice also what you must do to answer: swamping was an argument against reliabilism, and if you now appeal to the exercise of a competence rather than the reliability of a process — Sosa's move — you have abandoned the target you set out to defend.

6. **Ilse:** The difference sits in standing. Vera may dig. Lena may not: she has bought no corroboration and cannot afford a locator survey, so although she is in fact right, her belief is not one she can act on without an outlay she lacks. Knowledge, in the same episode and with no future whatsoever, removes a deliberative cost. I concede your point about instrumentality, and cheaply: that cost is real and it is instrumental, cashed in time, in survey fees, in risk carried. But it is instrumental to *this* action, not to future beliefs, and that is a third category your accounting does not contain — which is why your bookkeeping looked complete to you. Then I turn your principle against you. If value must be cashable in a difference to what the agent does or gets, your view makes the value of knowledge contingent on how much future the agent has. On a hospital bed with a week to live, my knowledge of the way to Larissa would be worth precisely as much as my luckiest guess. You have not dissolved the value problem; you have made knowledge worthless for the dying.

7. **Reuben:** Standing is just more beliefs. For Vera to be entitled to dig she must hold some view of the trustworthiness of her own perception — a second-order belief, true, and produced reliably in the same way. So your deliberative cost is the cost of a further true belief, and you have granted my accounting: everything you can point to is another true belief plus its consequences. Your dying case proves too much, in any event. The value of a hammer also depends on there being nails to come, and nobody concludes hammers are worthless to the dying.

8. **Ilse:** Vera needs no second-order belief at all. She may be convinced she was guessing; she may deny that her perception is trustworthy; in the case as you described it she has no views whatsoever about her own reliability. Entitlement does not require knowing that one knows, so there is no further belief to count and your ledger is still missing a column. And the tethering image cuts against you: what Socrates says runs away is the opinion itself, in the agent who holds it — an untethered belief is one that will not survive contact with the next piece of counter-evidence — not some future belief he never forms.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the eight, tell me whether it is clearly right, partly right, question-begging, a change of subject, or clearly wrong, with a sentence or two of justification; I want a scorecard rather than a narrative. Then address the following.

(a) Does the Vera and Lena pair do what Reuben wants, and is it well stipulated — or has he mis-described it in a way Ilse failed to catch? In particular, does Vera really have knowledge, and does Lena really lack it?

(b) Is Ilse's standing-to-act reply a genuine third category of value, or is Reuben right in move 7 that it collapses into further true beliefs? If it is genuine, does her concession that the value is instrumental to the same episode cost her the thing she was defending?

(c) Which of the examples in move 4 do real work and which are red herrings?

(d) Was the harder question ever engaged — why knowledge is worth more than *merely reliably produced* true belief, rather than more than true belief simpliciter — and does anything in the exchange bear on it, including Reuben's aside about competence in move 5?

(e) Is Reuben right that Plato's tethering answer is a generality answer, in which case Ilse is quarrelling with the text, or is Ilse right that stability is a property of the same token within the agent's own cognitive economy, in which case Reuben has misread it?

(f) Is any part of this dispute merely verbal — are they using 'value' in different senses? Say where, if anywhere, and what the disagreement would look like once the ambiguity is removed.

I would much rather be told that one of them is straightforwardly wrong than handed a diplomatic draw. Please also state what both of them are missing, and whether any formulation of the value problem survives this exchange intact.
