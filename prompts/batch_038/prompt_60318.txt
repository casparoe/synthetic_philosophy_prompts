This spring I inherited the philosophy seat on the seed-grant committee of our center for cognition and computation when the colleague who held it retired mid-term. I am the only non-modeler among six panelists, and next Thursday we allocate a single two-year award of about $175,000 out of eleven applications. Most of this year's proposals were unremarkable, but one has split the panel badly enough that I have been asked to draft the memo circulated in advance — partly, I suspect, because I am assumed to be sympathetic to it, and I want the document to argue against my own instincts wherever those instincts are weak. The proposal comes from a postdoc in cognitive science and a doctoral student in machine learning who want to build a fresh instance of the Copycat-style architecture in a new microworld and, more controversially, to evaluate it in a way our center's assessment policy does not currently recognize.

Here is the proposal as I understand it. Instead of alphabetic strings, their microworld consists of short sequences of pitches inside a single octave, and each problem has the form: this sequence maps to that sequence, so what does this third sequence map to? One interval rule is engineered to fail at the top of the octave, so a sequence ending on the highest available pitch has no successor inside the world they have defined; the problems are built so that the model, like the person, must let one concept slip into a neighboring one rather than apply a rule mechanically. The architecture is the familiar one: a long-term associative network of pre-programmed concept nodes whose effective link weights shift with activation; a workspace in which partial descriptions of the sequences are constructed and torn down; and a pool of small agents posted to that workspace stochastically, governed by a global scalar they call temperature, which measures how poorly organized the workspace currently is and feeds back to control how nearly at random the next agent is chosen. There is no fixed control flow. The model's route to an answer is an opportunistic parallel scan, and any answer it commits to is treated as one sample from a distribution: they propose to run each problem a thousand times and compare the resulting distribution of answers against human distributions they have already collected in a pilot with forty undergraduates on thirty items. On the hardest item, the one where the top-of-octave rule fails, their human data has no majority at all — roughly forty-five percent wrap around to the bottom of the octave, thirty percent leave the final pitch unchanged, twenty-five percent apply the transformation to every pitch rather than the last. Their central claim is that the model should reproduce that spread, and reproduce which subpopulation slips in which direction, rather than hit any single designated key. In support of feasibility they observe that the same architecture has been instantiated for table-setting analogies, letter styles, number sequences, geometric exploration, and melodic expectation.

Two colleagues have dug in. Our machine-learning member argues that the proposal is unfalsifiable in practice: the concept inventory and initial link weights are hand-built by the proposers, so any answer the system gives can be narrated afterward as a legitimate slippage and any failure excused by enriching the network; he adds that no baseline is offered, that a prompted or fine-tuned large language model would very likely reproduce the human answer distribution with none of the machinery, and that a distribution-matching metric cannot fail informatively. Our developmental psychologist argues the reverse: scoring only the modal answer erases the phenomenon, because on these items the interesting question is not whether people get it right but which concept each of them lets slip, and a benchmark graded against a single key would record the model's most revealing behavior as error. She also notes that center policy requires every funded project to report one primary metric as a scalar accuracy figure, so approval means a waiver, and she wants the memo to say whether we should grant one. My own unease sits between them. I have taught this material for years and like it, which makes me distrust my judgment; but the hand-built network strikes me as a genuine liability, and I do not know what to say to the objection that if an LLM matches the distribution the architecture has explained nothing — unless the answer is that the architecture is offered as an account of *why* particular wrong answers are good wrong answers, which raises the further question whether that is a claim a funding panel can hold anyone to. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate: roughly two pages, written for six technical colleagues who are not specialists in this literature, recommending fund, reject, or fund with conditions, with a bottom line we can actually vote on Thursday. It should define its terms in a sentence or two each (the concept network, the agent pool, temperature, the parallel scan) rather than assume them; state plainly what the proposal's scientific claim is and whether matching an answer distribution is stronger or weaker evidence than matching a single correct answer; treat both colleagues' objections at their strongest, the baseline point included, rather than as caricatures; and, if the recommendation is conditional, specify conditions concrete enough that a reviewer could check compliance at the twelve-month report — pre-registered predictions about which slippages should dominate under specified perturbations, ablations, held-out items not used to tune the network, a stated result that would count against the architecture, and the like. Finally, tell me how to handle the policy waiver: either a principled reason the scalar-metric rule should not apply to this kind of model, or a way to satisfy it without gutting the evaluation. No bibliography and no reading list for the panel, please; I want the reasoning, not the references.
