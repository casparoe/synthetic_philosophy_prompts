I'm two years into a PhD and I have to put in a proposal for our department's summer research fellowship — ten weeks, a small stipend, deliverable is a conference-length paper plus a draft chapter. I want you to act as an expert in medieval political philosophy who has also sat on a lot of fellowship committees, and turn my half-baked idea into a real proposal. Think carefully, and do some brainstorming first — a few bullets on the alternatives you considered and rejected, before you write the actual document. Context for the flavor: I got into this arguing with a friend who works on early modern resistance theory and keeps telling me Aquinas is "obviously" a prohibitionist, which I don't think is quite right either.

The idea, roughly. Everyone writing on Aquinas on tyrannicide sorts his texts in one of two ways. The first is developmental: the early passage in his commentary on the Sentences sounds like it actually commends the man who kills a tyrant to free his country, whereas On Kingship and the Summa are full of cautions — about the disorder that follows, about the would-be liberator turning into something worse than what he removed, and about having to wait for God when there's no human remedy — so the early text is just immature. The second is the distinction between a ruler who is a tyrant because he seized power he had no title to and a ruler who holds power legitimately but exercises it tyrannically; on that reading the permissive-sounding material is all about the first kind and the restrictive material about the second, and the inconsistency disappears. My hunch is that both readings assume Aquinas is answering "may this act be done, and how bad does the tyranny have to get?" when a lot of what he is actually doing is answering "who could possibly do it?" — questions about standing (the community that appointed the king, a superior power, or nobody at all) glued to questions about what the agent could know (his own motives, whether the aftermath would be better). On that reconstruction the mature view isn't a narrow permission: the conditions are epistemic conditions no one can meet, so it's a prohibition in a permission's clothes, and the wait-for-God line isn't pious filler but the device that closes the gap.

So write me the proposal. A precise statement of the question, one that has a determinate answer rather than "Aquinas's position is subtle and develops"; why the two existing readings leave it open; the method — what I'd actually do, week by week across the ten weeks, including how I'd lay the texts out as numbered argument schemas and which test cells the reconstruction has to get right (usurper with no title, moderate versus extreme tyranny of exercise, a community that appointed its ruler versus one subject to a superior power, the outright invader, the self-defence case); and an honest account of what would count as the project failing. The thing I'm most worried about is the exception in the sedition article, where disturbing a tyrant's rule is said not to have the character of sedition unless the disturbance is so violent that the subjects suffer more from the resulting disorder than from the tyranny itself. That looks like a straight harm comparison, which is exactly what a standing-and-evidence account is supposed to avoid — so build that worry into the proposal as a named objection with a stated plan for dealing with it, don't bury it.
