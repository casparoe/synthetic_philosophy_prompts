Phenomenon-hunting question. Mostly I want to know whether the field already has a word for this, and if it doesn't, whether it should.

I've been reading the arguments about whether DNA really is a code, or whether "information" talk in genetics is just decoration laid over plain chemistry. I'm not trying to settle that — both sides have good points. What I keep tripping over is a pattern in how the arguments travel, and it doesn't look like what either side thinks it is.

"Codes for" seems to get used in two very different ways. In one, the claim is checkable and pretty uncontroversial: read this stretch of bases three at a time and it maps onto this string of amino acids, you can do the mapping yourself with the table, and the correspondence looks arbitrary in the sense that nothing about the chemistry of a given codon forces it to mean that particular amino acid. In the other, the sentence frame is identical but the claim is way bigger: the gene codes for blue eyes, for a temperament, for the shape of the organism.

Now watch what happens in the debates. Somebody writes a debunking argument aimed squarely at the second use — development isn't executed from a script, there's no reader running the program, nothing at the trait level survives a translation test. Fine. But the argument gets read and cited as if it had shown the first use was bogus too, and people who work with the codon table every day end up defending themselves against a charge that was never aimed at what they're doing. And it runs the other direction as well: someone defends genetic information talk by pointing at the table, which really is a table, really is a mapping, and readers come away thinking the trait-level claims got vindicated by association. Same verb, same syntax, and credibility flows both ways across a gap the argument never actually crossed.

I see the same shape elsewhere — entropy in thermodynamics versus entropy as social disorder, where debunking the second gets heard as an attack on the first, and invoking the first is treated as licensing the second — but genetics is where I keep hitting it.

I don't think its plain equivocation, because usually no single argument uses both senses. Each party is talking about one of them, and the slippage happens in the audience and in the citation chain, not inside an inference. And it isn't motte-and-bailey either, at least most of the time, since nobody is strategically ducking into the safe sense and then creeping back out — it's passive, it needs no motive, and it works just as well on people who'd never make the move deliberately. It's more like the two uses are welded together by sharing a verb, and warrant leaks across the weld in both directions.

So: is there an established term for this? A case where a technical use and a loose extended use share a word and a sentence frame, and each borrows credibility from the other, in a way that keeps working even after criticism has landed on one of them? If there is one, I want to know how it's marked off from equivocation, from dead metaphor, and from the ordinary stretching that any technical term undergoes once it goes popular — and which of those is actually the better fit for what I described. If there isn't one, propose a term and define it tightly enough that I could tell whether a given instance is a real case or just me seeing the pattern everywhere. Give me some boundary cases and some near-misses you'd exclude. The label itself is the least important part; I want the carving.
