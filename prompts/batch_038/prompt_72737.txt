In May I deliver the final lecture of a thirty-six-year career in this department, and I have been self-indulgent enough to decide that it will open with a poem rather than with an outline. The subject is the last chapter of my own work on levels of description: Dennett's three stances and the argument of Real Patterns — the claim that what makes a pattern real is not that it answers to some extra furniture over and above the underlying stuff, but that describing the world in its terms lets you predict vastly more than you could by enumerating that stuff bit for bit. My audience will be mixed: a dozen colleagues, a great many undergraduates, and my daughter, who has heard me explain the intentional stance at every holiday dinner since 1994. So the poem must succeed as a poem before it succeeds as a summary. I attempted a draft myself and produced a lurching hexameter that rhymed "intentional" with "conventional," which persuaded me to commission the thing instead.

Here is the form I have in mind, and the form is the argument. Three stanzas of strictly descending length — fourteen lines, then seven, then three — all describing one and the same object: a glider in a grid-world automaton, a five-cell configuration that steps one square diagonally every four generations. The first stanza should be the bitmap and should feel like it: a ledger of states at coordinates, cell by cell, nothing but on and off and where, with no shape, no name, and no future in view — the prediction available in principle from constitution and initial conditions, if one had the patience for it. The second should shrink to the machine: something with a period and a displacement, something to be relied upon. One refinement here, since I anticipate the objection from the back row: the middle stanza must not smuggle in a designer, for nothing designed the glider. What it should capture is the signature assumption of that level rather than any blueprint — that the thing will go on working as it has been working unless it is broken or blocked. The third stanza, in its three lines, should shrink again to a wanting: the creature takes the grid ahead of it to be empty and is making for the far corner. Then a rhyming couplet, set slightly apart, that names the criterion — the shorter description earns its purchase by saving more than it omits.

Two requirements of accuracy. First, the labels must never appear: no "physical stance," no "design stance," no "intentional stance," and no use of the word "pattern" until the couplet, if even there. The three levels should be identifiable from what each stanza is doing, so that a reader who knows this literature can say afterwards which stanza was which and why the third is so short. Second, the shortening must not read as a hierarchy of reality. The physical description is not truer, only longer, and the whole point of the view is that beliefs picked out this way are genuinely real rather than convenient fictions; I want the poem on that side of the line, not on the eliminativist's. If you can also get the couplet to concede the thing I always concede in class — that which compressions we bother to notice is a matter of our interests, whereas whether a given compression works is not a matter of ours at all — then the poem will have done everything I need of it, and rather more than I have any right to ask.
