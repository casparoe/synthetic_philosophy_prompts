Our philosophy of mind reading group is co-hosting a panel with the university sleep lab, and the two of us who were meant to debate whether dreams are experiences have agreed to run an adversarial collaboration instead. Write the joint statement we'd publish. One of us holds the Malcolm-style line: that having a dream just amounts to being disposed to produce a narration on waking, and that nothing could count as evidence of experiences had while someone is genuinely asleep, because any evidence of that sort would show they weren't asleep. The other holds the Dennett-style line: that this is a substantive empirical hypothesis wearing a conceptual disguise, best spelled out as something like the cassette theory, on which the content gets assembled retrospectively by unconscious processes, and that it can be tested.

Give me a numbered section of claims both can sign — including what the timing data linking REM duration to lenght of dream narration does and doesn't establish, and what to make of lucid dreamers emitting pre-agreed eye signals from inside REM — then a table of the residual disagreements, each worded so its own side would accept the description of its view, then the cruxes with a prediction from each side recorded in advance. Assume the Malcolmian has already conceded that the pre-agreed eye signals are hard to explain away but insists the concession doesn't touch the conceptual point; say precisely what was and wasn't conceded, and where the two sides are simply talking past each other if you think they are.

Finally, don't be even-handed about it: tell me which crux you think is actually decisive and why, and which side you'd bet on. British English, please.
