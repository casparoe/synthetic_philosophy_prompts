I am a first-year graduate student in a course on trustworthy machine learning, and for the last three weeks we have been reading two literatures that never speak to one another: certified robustness on one side (interval bound propagation, randomized smoothing, the various Lipschitz-bounding schemes) and the safety-case style arguments that circulate on the other side around the deployment of learned policies. Our final assignment is a position paper, and the more I read, the more convinced I am that the two camps are not disagreeing about answers so much as about what would count as an answer. One side publishes a certificate holding for every input within a tiny ℓ∞ ball around a test point, even though the perturbations that actually break the system in deployment are an order of magnitude larger and not metric-shaped at all; the other side publishes an argument whose premises concern the training process and the objective, with no quantifier over environments anywhere in it. Before I commit to either, I want the specification.

So: write the desiderata that any formal guarantee about the behavior of a deployed learned system must satisfy in order to count as a safety guarantee rather than a suggestive statistic. Fix the purpose as follows — take the system to be one whose failure is catastrophic and unrepeatable, a learned controller in an implanted medical device or in an aircraft rather than a recommender — so that correctness in expectation is not by itself enough. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it; I want to see why it belongs on the list, not merely that it does. The list should address at minimum: what the guarantee quantifies over (inputs, environments, adversaries, distribution shift, and any subsequent gradient updates applied to the system itself), and what is at stake in the choice between a worst-case bound over a set and an average-case statement about a distribution; whether the guarantee is per-instance or population-level, and whether a claim that holds for 99 percent of a distribution says anything at all about the case that matters; whether its assumptions are checkable from inside the development process or must simply be trusted by the party bearing the risk; what happens when an assumption is violated — does the guarantee degrade in a stated way, or does it silently become false; whether guarantees compose when the system is assembled from several learned components and wrapped in an outer loop that optimizes against it; and whether a bound that is technically correct but numerically vacuous at the actual operating point deserves the name.

Then do three things with the list. First, sort it: which requirements are non-negotiable for anything calling itself a guarantee, and which are the articulated preferences of a camp — the minimax inheritance from classical decision theory, say, or the empiricist insistence that a guarantee be measured rather than derived. Second, and this is the part I most want, tell me where the requirements are jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect that universal quantification over environments, non-vacuity at realistic perturbation scales, and tractable computation of the certificate cannot all be had at once, and that some no-free-lunch style constraint on learners forces the choice rather than leaving it to ingenuity; if that is right, say which pair gives way and what the residue looks like as a weaker but honest claim. Third, identify the single requirement that excludes the most candidates, and say why it has that much leverage. Close with a short scorecard, a few sentences per contender at most, for certified robustness methods, PAC-style generalization bounds, conformal prediction, and constraint-satisfaction guarantees in safe reinforcement learning. Our instructor has banned bibliographies from the paper on the grounds that we should be able to state our criteria without borrowing someone else's phrasing, so please do not send me to references and do not survey these methods — assume I have read them. I want the criteria argued out on their own terms.
