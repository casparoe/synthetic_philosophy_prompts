I have no academic training in this field. I am a self-taught programmer who spent the past year reading about inverse reinforcement learning, out of a practical interest in systems that learn what a user wants from what the user does. Below is a short draft essay stating a distinction that occurred to me and an argument built on it. I would like criticism rather than encouragement. I am working offline, so please argue from the concepts themselves rather than pointing me toward sources.

DRAFT ESSAY

Inverse reinforcement learning inverts the ordinary order of explanation. Instead of being handed a reward function and asked what behavior optimizes it, you are handed behavior and asked what reward function would make that behavior look sensible. The setup therefore carries a standing assumption: the demonstrator acts at least approximately optimally with respect to the reward being recovered. Behavior is treated as a readout of desire.

Two consequences are familiar. First, the readout is loose. Rewards can be recovered only up to transformations that leave the ranking of policies untouched, and some rewards — a constant one, say — leave every policy equally good, so the data cannot single anything out. Second, humans are plainly not optimal with respect to anything we would call their own values: they are myopic, distracted, misinformed, and weak-willed. Much of the more recent work relaxes optimality accordingly, modeling the demonstrator as noisy rather than mistaken, or as someone who knows the reward and is teaching an agent who does not.

My claim is that these two problems are usually run together, and that they are not problems of the same kind.

Call the first a transmission failure. The person has a reward; the behavior is a distorted encoding of it, distorted by noise, ignorance, limited computation, or self-command. The distortion is a property of the channel. A richer model of the agent — one that knows she discounts the future unevenly, or observes only part of the situation — recovers more signal from the same data. Nothing more is needed than better inference.

Call the second a constitution failure. Here the difficulty is not that behavior distorts the reward, but that there is no determinate reward to distort until someone rules on which of the agent's own attitudes speaks for her. The person who at nine in the morning arranges to be locked out of the kitchen and at nine at night picks the lock is not transmitting two noisy versions of one preference. She is two claimants. Treating one as signal and the other as noise is not an inference from data; it is a decision about authority.

The distinction matters because the standard repairs are all transmission repairs. They enrich the channel model — noise, bias, partial observation, myopia — while continuing to presuppose a single reward that the channel is failing to convey. A better model of the channel cannot settle a dispute about who owns it. And because the machinery has no way to register that dispute, it decides it by default: whichever state the agent occupies across most of the recorded data supplies the recovered reward. Frequency becomes sovereignty. The person who spends most of her recorded hours in a condition she repudiates in her clearer ones will be handed back the repudiated condition's preferences as her own, and the model will report an excellent fit.

The obvious reply is that preferences simply are dispositions to act, so the self that acts at nine at night has the preference and nothing remains to be constituted. Preferences about one's own preferences are then further behavior, recoverable at a higher level. But this relocates the problem rather than dissolving it. The higher level is recovered from behavior by the same method, so the same question returns: when the levels conflict, which level governs? No quantity of data answers that, because the data underdetermine it and the tie is broken by whatever the loss function happens to weight.

END OF DRAFT

Three things I would like from you. On accuracy: is my description of the standard assumption, and of what relaxing it involves, correct — and is it true in particular that a noise or bias model still presupposes a single reward? On the distinction: does transmission versus constitution hold up, or is constitution merely transmission in which the time index of the reward is unknown? Someone suggested to me that a mixture model over selves with learned weights would dissolve the whole issue; I do not see how those weights could be learned rather than chosen, but I may be wrong. On the argument: does the claim that frequency becomes sovereignty actually follow, and is the closing regress a genuine difficulty or a cheap one? Please also identify the weakest passage, and say whether my two coinages earn their place or whether ordinary vocabulary already covers what they are doing.
