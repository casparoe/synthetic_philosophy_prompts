I'm a relationist about spacetime, though not the crude sort. Here's the view: the metric field is perfectly real and dynamical, but diffeomorphically related models are just the same situation described twice, so there's never a fact about which bare point is which. Rotation is always rotation relative to the metric, and the metric's structure only gets nailed down by matter plus boundary conditions. Which is why I think the bucket is a bad objection: in a universe containing one bucket and literally nothing else, there's no determinate answer whether its concave surface means its rotating.

Tell me what would count against this. Which formal results or which sorts of solution in GR actually bite -- I have a vague worry about vacuum solutions that are somehow rotating, and about gravitational waves doing stuff in empty space that seems to need the metric to have degrees of freedom of its own. Are those real problems or absorbable? And is there anything my view rules out that a substantivalist about the metric field permits, or has the diffeomorphism move quietly turned me into a substantivalist who's just squeamish about points? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a defender of this position would concede as counting against it rather than explain away.
