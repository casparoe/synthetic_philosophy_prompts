You are a philosopher who works on moral responsibility inside large organisations, and who knows Hannah Arendt's *Eichmann in Jerusalem* from the text rather than from its reputation. Think carefully before answering, and treat what follows as a piece of conceptual disambiguation rather than a request for advice: the decision in my case is mine to make with my colleagues, and I am not asking you to make it.

I sit on the professional conduct panel of a chartered institute. We are hearing a case against a registrant who, for four years, maintained the decision rules of an eligibility-scoring tool that a public agency used to withdraw support from claimants; tens of thousands were wrongly flagged, and the false-positive rate was visible in his own quarterly reports. His defence was structural: he was one of eleven engineers, the specifications came from the client, the sign-off was a standard template, he never met a claimant, and he had no personal stake in the outcome. Two members of the panel reached for "the banality of evil" — Arendt's phrase from her subtitle for the 1963 book reporting on the Eichmann trial — and pointed it in opposite directions. Member A said it is precisely why the man is culpable: the absence of malice, and the absence of any felt choice, is not an excuse but the fault itself, a failure to think where thinking was required. Member B said it is why erasure from the register for life would be disproportionate: the phrase tells us he was no monster, that the wrong was manufactured by an organisation, and that punishing the small cog is a way of declining to punish the machine.

Then, in disclosure, we received his private messages and forum posts from the same period: jokes about claimants, delight at a "tough" threshold being set, dehumanising language about the people his rules were applied to. A now says the phrase cannot apply to this man at all; B says the phrase was never a claim about the contents of anyone's head, so nothing has changed.

What I need is a menu. Lay out the distinct precise claims that "the banality of evil" could be making — between six and nine of them, ranging from the near-trivial to the radical. For each, say what sort of claim it is: about this particular agent's psychology, about agents of a type, about the deeds themselves, about the organisations that produce them, about the concept of evil, or about how we ought to judge. Then give a verdict on each — platitude, substantive and defensible, contentious, or simply false — and say whether it would support A's use, B's use, both, or neither.
