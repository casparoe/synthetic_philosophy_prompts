I run the patient, family and community advisory program at a mid-size health system — basically every time someone wants to stand up a new engagement body (a chronic pain council, a youth board, a group of former inpatients advising on a ward redesign) it lands on my desk as a draft charter, and we do about 15-20 a year. My staff are nurses, social workers and admin people, nobody has read any philosophy, and I have roughly an afternoon per proposal before it goes up to the governance committee with a recommendation of proceed / proceed with conditions / send it back for redesign.

Someone at a conference pointed me at Hegel's lordship-and-bondage material and the recognition arguments built on it, and said that was the right lens for what I'm actually doing: working out whether a body like this is a place where two sides genuinely recognize each other as independent agents, or a structure where one side is harvesting validation from people it doesn't treat as independent at all. I want you to build me the procedure that lens implies — not an essay about Hegel, a thing a coordinator can run off a two-page worksheet.

So: the questions to ask of a draft charter, in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; which answers force a send-back; which force conditions attached; and where the procedure has to stop and hand the call to me because it's a question of values rather than of what the document says. Also tell me which parts of the theory you had to drop to make it usable, and which distinctions absolutely cannot go without the whole thing collapsing into a generic participation checklist — I've seen the generic version and I can smell it.

Concretely, I've been told three claims are load-bearing here and I can't tell whether they are: that recognition coming from someone you don't yourself recognize as an independent other can't actually confirm anything for you; that the dependent party is the one who develops a stable sense of themselves through the work of shaping things while the dominant party stagnates; and that the dominant party's independence is really held up by the dependent party's activity, so the dependence runs the opposite way to how it looks. Say which of those can be turned into an observable test on a charter or terms of reference, which can't, and what you do with the ones that can't.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A chronic pain council. Members review service redesign proposals, there's a ring-fenced budget they control, the service has to respond in writing within 30 days — but the system appoints every member, a senior executive chairs, and terms are one year, renewable at the chair's discretion.
2. A tenant board on a housing site we partner with. It sets its own agenda, spends a devolved fund with no approval needed, publishes its own minutes and its own dissents — and nothing obliges anybody to answer it.
3. Our flagship lived-experience co-design group, which I will defend to the death. Paid members, trained, co-authors on the papers that come out, and their testimony genuinely changed a funding policy. But the members are people currently in treatment with us, and the program manager reviews who stays on each cycle.

I want the honest verdict on 3 even if its embarassing, and I want at least one case where the procedure comes back "the asymmetry is real and this is still the best thing available," because a diagnosis is not automatically an instruction to tear the body down and my committee will read it as if it is. Finish with what the procedure can't do: the failure modes I should expect, and the ways a determined sponsor could pass every question on the worksheet while gutting the thing anyway.
