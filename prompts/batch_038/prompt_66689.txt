I have been seconded for the past six months to a review of how a welfare state decides who qualifies for its main non-means-tested disability benefit, and the longer I sit with the assessment instruments the less I understand what they think they are measuring. Here is the machinery, as best I can describe it. The benefit is officially justified as helping with the extra costs of living with a long-term health condition or impairment; it is not means-tested, it is not tied to whether a person can work, and it pays at two rates depending on how severely a claimant's ability is limited. Eligibility is settled by scoring a claimant against a fixed schedule of daily-living and mobility activities — preparing food, eating, managing therapy or medication, washing, dressing, communicating, reading, engaging with other people face to face, making budgeting decisions, planning and following a journey, and moving around a stated distance. Each activity has a ladder of descriptors carrying points, the points are summed against thresholds, and the whole determination rests on an assessment conducted by a health professional employed by a private contractor, who weighs the claimant's own account of a bad day against what was observable in the room. Three features of the rules are what keep me up at night. First, ability is assessed as if the claimant were wearing or using any aid or appliance they normally use — and also any aid or appliance they could reasonably be expected to use, whether or not they actually own one or find it bearable. Second, a descriptor is only satisfied if the activity can be done safely, to an acceptable standard, repeatedly — meaning as often as it is reasonably required — and within a reasonable time, where a reasonable time is defined as no more than twice what a person without such a condition would normally take. Third, the same claimant is separately run through a work-capability instrument using broadly similar descriptors but a different rationale and a very different consequence, and the household's income-replacement benefit is means-tested on earnings and capital in a way the disability payment is not.

So: what theory of advantage does all this presuppose? The stated rationale is about extra costs, which sounds like a resourcist claim, yet the instrument never asks what anything costs the claimant and, through the aid rule, actively discounts the aid — the very thing that generates the extra cost — by counting it as restoring the ability. That looks less like compensating for unequal resources than like registering a shortfall against a normalised bodily standard, since every one of the qualifiers is calibrated to a hypothetical person without the condition. But it does not look like welfarism either: nothing on the form asks how the claimant feels about their life, and a claimant who has thoroughly adapted, given up ambitions, and reports contentment scores exactly as if they were miserable, which suggests the designers deliberately refused a metric they might otherwise have found administratively convenient. Nor is it plainly a capabilities measure, since it asks what a person can do in a standardised list of situations rather than what real options they have, and it holds the built environment, the labour market, and other people's attitudes fixed while locating the entire disadvantage inside the claimant. I want the implicit theory reconstructed rather than guessed at: what does the practice strictly commit its authors to about the metric of justice — welfare, resources, capabilities, functionings, or something administrative that maps onto none of them — and where is it merely compatible with several of these, so that rival theorists could each endorse the same form for incompatible reasons? In particular, tell me whether the aid-and-appliance rule commits the state to a view about where the person stops and the world begins, whether the twice-as-long rule smuggles in a substantive ideal of the normal human performance, and whether scoring the individual while bracketing the environment makes the practice a medical-model instrument or a covert measurement of conversion rates between resources and what a person can actually do.

Then I would like the internal consistency tested, because I suspect the state is running two or three theories of justice at once without noticing. Why is the extra-costs benefit exempt from the means test while the income-replacement benefit is not — what does that split presuppose about which inequalities are matters for collective correction and which are left to private luck and family support? Why do the two instruments, disability and work-capability, ask different questions of the same body in different vocabularies, and can they deliver verdicts that are not merely different but incoherent when combined, as they often seem to be when someone scores well on daily activities yet cannot sustain employment? And what would actually have to change, concretely, at the level of the form and the descriptors, if the scheme were rebuilt on a strict resourcist view of the sort that compensates brute bad luck through something like an insurance baseline, on a strict welfarist view, or on a capability view that took the capability set rather than achieved performance as its object — including whether any of those three would have to abandon the standardised schedule altogether in favour of something person-relative and environment-relative that no assessor could administer in an hour. I am not after a reform proposal, and please do not send me references or a reading list; I want the reasoning about what the existing practice is already committed to, and an honest account of the places where it is philosophically underdetermined and several theories would license the same points.
