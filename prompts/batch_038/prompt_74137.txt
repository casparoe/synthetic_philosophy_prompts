I run a small monthly fiction workshop whose members are mostly philosophy graduate students, and this term our theme is minds at the wrong scale — specifically, the old thought experiment, dramatized decades ago with a talking anthill, about whether a colony of individually trivial insects can add up to a single thinker. Everyone in the group already knows the charming versions: the ants speak, the human is humbled, the reader nods. I want to hand them a prompt that forces the genuinely hard version of the problem, which as I see it is not whether the colony thinks but whether the attribution of thought to it is a discovery about the colony or an artifact of the interpreter's scoring scheme. Please write a short story built on the following apparatus, treating it consistently and inventing no new mechanisms once the story is under way.

The setting is a sealed climate room at a field station, over one night, holding a trail-lattice assay: a colony of foragers is kept on a transparent gel grid roughly a meter square, on which their trails accumulate as visible chemical deposits. Stimuli are delivered as patterns of sucrose and gland-secretion micro-drops at grid nodes; the colony's "reply" is the redistribution of trails over the following thirty hours, photographed from above. Crucially, before any trial the experimenter must fix a lexicon — a grouping of nodes into candidate symbols. Under a coarse lexicon of six symbols, the colony's redistribution across eleven trials reads as a consistent, generalizing reply that transfers to a novel grid. Under a finer lexicon of forty-eight symbols, applied to the identical photographs, the same redistributions are unstructured noise. There is no independent procedure for deciding which lexicon is the colony's own, because the grouping is chosen by the researcher in advance rather than read off the insects. The complication that should drive the plot: the fine-grained, unintelligible scoring predicts the colony's behavior in the novel arena measurably better than the coarse, legible one. Predictive success and interpretability have come apart, and the protagonist — a postdoc whose lab has a paper under review asserting that the colony represents quantities — has one night to decide what the plain-language summary sentence in the resubmission will say. Her only interlocutor is a technician who has run the assay for nine years, does not think of it as a philosophical matter, and is unbothered by both readings.

Write it in a dry, restrained register with concrete laboratory detail, dialogue-heavy, and include one short passage set inside the colony, rendered in a way that leaves it permanently unclear whether a mind is present there at all — the passage should not resolve the question by being vivid. Avoid the ants speaking, any reversal in which humans turn out to be the colony, and any ending that lands on a verdict; close instead on a physical detail from which the reader can infer what the protagonist decided to write without being told. Open with a single-sentence logline, then the story itself, and keep the whole response under 1,400 words.
