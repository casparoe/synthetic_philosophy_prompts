So our philosophy club meets in Mr. Okafor's room on Thursdays, mostly so we can argue about stuff instead of doing homework. Last month we did the lottery paradox and then the preface paradox, and I thought I could weld them into one argument that settles both. Turns out the welded version proves something way worse than either of the originals and I can't find where I cheated. Here it is.

(1) Rational belief runs on a threshold: there's some cutoff t, short of certainty but the same for every question, such that you ought to believe p just in case p's probability on your evidence is above t. (2) Our spring raffle sells exactly 500 tickets and exactly one wins, so for any given ticket the chance it loses is 0.998. (3) So if t is 0.99 or anything lower, I ought to believe of each individual ticket that it will lose. (4) If I ought to believe each of a bunch of claims, and I know what they jointly amount to, then I ought to believe what they jointly amount to. (5) So I ought to believe that no ticket wins. (6) But I know one ticket wins, and you can never rationally believe something you know is false. (7) So (1) is dead: no threshold short of certainty survives, and this works for any t you name, because the school can always print more raffle tickets. (8) And basically nothing contingent has probability 1 on my evidence, not even that I have a chem test Thursday or that my mom exists. (9) So there is no contingent claim I ought to believe. (10) But its flat-out irrational of me not to believe I have a chem test Thursday, I'd tank it. (11) Therefore rational belief about the actual world is impossible, or at least rational people should believe nothing beyond logic and math.

Two of our seniors insist I equivocated somewhere but they can't agree on where, which is infuriating. So which step do you throw out, and why did it look totally innocent when I wrote it down? Then price the alternatives for me: if I give up (1) instead, what replaces it, and does the replacement survive if I just make the raffle enormous? Also here's the bit I actually invented, so be honest if it's garbage. My fix is what I'm calling the Bookkeeping Rule: you get to count p as one of your beliefs only if, once p is on the list, you'd still take a bet on the whole list. That seems to kill (4) and (5) without touching thresholds at all, since any single losing ticket is bettable but the pile of 500 of them isn't. My friend says the Bookkeeping Rule just walks the contradiction in through a different door, and her case is the school paper: I'm an editor, and I'm about to run a 200-claim investigation piece where every claim is solidly sourced, each one maybe 0.995 on my evidence, but 0.995 to the 200th power is like 0.37. So by my own rule I can't believe the article I'm publishing, and I have to publish it. Is she right that the rule relocates the problem instead of solving it? And if I have to pick, which do I keep — (1), (4), (6), or (10)? I'd rather sacrifice (6) than (10), but I don't know what (6) is actually costing me.
