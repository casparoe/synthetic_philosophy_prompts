I retired four years ago after 32 years running field trials for an agricultural experiment station — mostly nitrogen response in small plots, which sounds dull but taught me more than anything about how a well-designed experiment can still tell you nothing. Since then I've been sitting in on a philosophy of science reading group at the state college, and this term we've been grinding through the replication crisis and the frequentist/Bayesian fight. I read philosophy the way I used to read methods sections, slowly and with a pencil, so I hope you'll forgive how I've framed this.

The thing that keeps coming up, in the group and in the popular statistics books I've been working through, is the courtroom analogy for significance testing. The null hypothesis is the presumption of innocence; the researcher is the prosecution and carries the burden of proof; alpha is the "beyond reasonable doubt" standard; rejecting the null is a conviction; failing to reject is an acquittal; a type I error is a wrongful conviction and a type II error is letting a guilty person walk free. In our group it gets used to do at least three separate jobs. (a) One member, a retired physician, says a non-significant result is an acquittal and therefore does tell you there's no effect — the court found for the defendant, after all. (b) Another says the 5% level is respectable precisely because it is our version of beyond reasonable doubt, a principled standard rather than an arbitrary number people happen to like. (c) And the general drift of the discussion is that the frequentist trial is more objective than the Bayesian one, because it keeps the juror's private beliefs out and controls the long-run rate of wrongful convictions, whereas a Bayesian smuggles priors in the back door.

So: audit the analogy for me. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the prosecutor, the defendant, the jury, the body of evidence, the judge's instructions, the standard of proof, the verdict, the appeal, and the long run of similar trials that frequentist error rates are supposed to be about — then tell me which disanalogies actually bear on (a), (b), and (c) and which are surface noise I can ignore. I especially want to know what features of a courtroom get imported that the statistical setting simply doesn't have; my suspicion is that a single defendant with a determinate guilt and a coherent narrative are carrying a lot of weight that the composite alternative hypothesis can't support. Two things of my own I'd like you to engage with rather than wave away. First, I tried to patch the analogy by arguing that the prior doesn't disappear, it just lives outside the courtroom in the charging decision — police only bring a case when they already have reason to suspect somebody — so what a Bayesian calls a prior, a frequentist handles as a selection effect on which hypotheses ever reach trial. The working statistician in our group called this a category error and I'm not sure its fair. Second, I keep coming back to the fact that Scots law used to allow a third verdict, "not proven," which is neither conviction nor acquittal, and that a great deal of the trouble with (a) is that our courts have no verdict meaning "we found nothing, and nothing isn't the same as innocence." Is there a three-verdict version of the analogy that does better, or would you rather swap the source domain entirely — I've seen population screening and customs inspection offered instead.

Format, please: prose for the reasoning, but put the mapping in a table with exactly these columns — Courtroom element | Statistical counterpart | Holds / Partial / Breaks | What the mapping smuggles in | Which of (a)(b)(c) it turns on. Then finish with a short structured verdict block, one line per claim, in the form: claim — verdict (survives / survives with repair / fails) — one sentence saying what would have to change for it to survive. No bibliography or reading list, I can't chase references and I'd rather have your own working-out.
