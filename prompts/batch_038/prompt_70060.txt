I am a third-year undergraduate enrolled in a course on Warring States Chinese philosophy, and this week we are working on the short dialogue attributed to Gongsun Long that defends the thesis "A white horse is not a horse." I am not looking for background on the disputers or the School of Names, and I am not looking for a reading list; I have both. What I need is help getting through one specific stretch of the argument, which I have now read perhaps fifteen times without making it cohere.

Because our edition is a library copy I cannot reproduce here, and because the received text is extremely compressed and, according to our instructor, probably corrupt in places, what follows is my own line-by-line rendering of the exchange that has me stuck. I have deliberately kept the structure visible rather than making it read smoothly.

1. Questioner: Is it admissible to hold that a white horse is not a horse?
Gongsun Long: It is admissible.
Questioner: Why?
Gongsun Long: 'Horse' is that by which we name the shape; 'white' is that by which we name the color. What names the color is not what names the shape. Hence: a white horse is not a horse.

2. Questioner: Where there is a white horse, one cannot say there is no horse. If one cannot say there is no horse, is that not to say there is a horse? Given that there is a white horse, how can a white horse be not a horse?
Gongsun Long: If one seeks a horse, a yellow or a black one may be brought; if one seeks a white horse, a yellow or a black one may not be brought. Were a white horse a horse, then what is sought in the two cases would be the same; and were what is sought the same, the seeker would not differ from one who seeks a white horse. Yet the yellow and the black horse are acceptable in the one case and unacceptable in the other, which shows that what is accepted and what is rejected are not the same. That a white horse is not a horse is therefore clear.

3. Gongsun Long: A horse is inherently colored; that is why there are white horses. If a horse were not colored, there would be horse and nothing more — how would one obtain a white horse? White is not horse. A white horse is horse together with white. Hence a white horse is not a horse.

My questions, in order:

(a) I tried to formalize this in the obvious way. Let the extension of 'horse' be H and that of 'white horse' be the intersection of H with W, the white things. The questioner's move in (2) is then unremarkable: a non-empty intersection entails a non-empty H. But the thesis Gongsun Long asserts appears to be the denial of a universally quantified conditional that, on this rendering, is a logical truth and hence not deniable at all. So either his thesis is not about extensions, or the copula in it is not doing predicative work. Tell me precisely what the thesis must say in order to be both deniable and consistent with what he plainly concedes in (2) — namely that from the presence of a white horse one may infer the presence of a horse. I want the exact content, not a list of candidate contents.

(b) My own hypothesis is that (2) turns on an intensional context: seeking is a request, and requests are individuated by what they ask for. But I have begun to doubt that this is an interesting observation, because 'horse' and 'white horse' do not even have the same extension, so no principle of substitutivity is threatened: of course a request for a broader kind may be satisfied by things that do not satisfy a request for a narrower one. Is the popular claim that this dialogue anticipates referential opacity actually supported by the text, or does it mislocate the puzzle by importing a failure of substitution where substitution was never licensed? Please give a verdict with reasons.

(c) A member of my study group maintains that the entire dialogue is an equivocation on the copula — sometimes class inclusion, sometimes identity of what a name picks out, sometimes satisfaction of a request. Construct the diagnosis explicitly: for each of the three steps, which reading of 'is a' makes that step valid, and is there any single uniform reading under which the argument from (1) through (3) is valid and its conclusion non-trivial? If there is none, identify the first step at which it breaks.

(d) Step (3) is the one I understand least. It says color is not accidental to horses, yet also that white is not horse and that a white horse is horse together with white. Is the conclusion about the compound name — that 'white horse' names a compound of two naming functions and so is not the same name as 'horse' — or about the object? Does the step require a separation thesis of the kind the hard-and-white material associated with the same figure relies on, where a quality is treated as standing apart from the thing that has it? If it does, show me where the dependency bites; if it does not, explain how the step can stand without it.

(e) The questioner's move in (2) has the shape of an inference by parallelizing: from something's being a horse to something about having or seeking that thing. My impression from our secondary material is that the later Mohists treat such inferences as licensed only under restrictions. Would you characterize Gongsun Long's reply as identifying a restriction on that inference pattern, and if so, is the restriction he identifies semantic, concerning names and what they pick out, or pragmatic, concerning what counts as satisfying a request?

Please address (a) through (e) in sequence, stating at each point which premises you are attributing to Gongsun Long and why the text supports that attribution. Close by telling me which single reading of the dialogue you judge best survives this scrutiny. I want your argued preference and the cost of adopting it, not a survey of every interpretation that has been proposed.
