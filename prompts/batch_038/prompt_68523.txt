Take the Sèvres arrangement of October 1956 and read it against Hannah Arendt's account of political lying — both the essay "Truth and Politics" and, more pointedly, her 1971 reflections on the Pentagon Papers.

The facts first, so that nothing turns on your recalling them exactly. Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal Company in July 1956. Between 22 and 24 October, at Sèvres, delegations from Britain (Selwyn Lloyd and Patrick Dean), France (Christian Pineau, Maurice Bourgès-Maunoury, General Challe) and Israel (Ben-Gurion, Dayan, Peres) negotiated a seven-point protocol under which Israel would attack across the Sinai towards the Canal; Britain and France would then issue an ultimatum to both belligerents to withdraw from the Canal zone; Egypt, which everyone expected to refuse, would supply the occasion for an Anglo-French intervention presented to the world as the neutral separation of combatants and the protection of an international waterway. At Israeli insistence each delegation left with a signed French-language copy. Israel attacked on 29 October, the ultimatum followed, Anglo-French bombing began on 31 October, and the whole operation collapsed within a week under American and Soviet pressure and a run on sterling. Both governments denied any prior arrangement, in the Security Council and on the floor of the Commons, and held that denial for years; the British copy of the protocol was destroyed, or so the standard accounts say, and the collusion was eventually established not by contemporaneous journalism but by surviving foreign copies and by the participants' memoirs. Only a handful of ministers were party to the arrangement, one Foreign Office minister resigned over the operation, and the Prime Minister was out of office within months.

What I want is a disciplined mapping before any evaluation. Identify each distinct act of deception — to Nasser, to the Security Council, to the Americans, to Parliament, to the rest of the Cabinet, to the press and public — and assign each to Arendt's categories: lies about factual truth versus lies about intentions and future plans; traditional secrecy aimed at an adversary versus the modern defactualisation and image-making she takes to be the novel and dangerous thing; deception that leaves the record intact versus deception that must alter or destroy the record. Then say what her framework predicts about each: which lies she would expect to succeed, which to be self-defeating, and which to corrode the liar's own orientation in reality rather than merely mislead an audience.

Then the harder part. Four features strike me as awkward or as unexploited, and I would like to know whether you agree or whether I am misreading her.

First, the ultimatum. The Anglo-French statement was not false in its factual content: Israel had invaded, fighting was approaching the Canal, both sides were being asked to stand back. The falsehood lay in the provenance and meaning of the events described — events the describers had arranged. Her dichotomy of factual and rational truth, and her account of the liar's advantage as resting on the contingency of whatever happens, does not obviously contain a slot for a lie executed by truthful description of a manufactured fact. Is that a genuine gap, or does she have resources I have missed?

Second, the failure came from outside the epistemic system. Her Pentagon Papers analysis attributes the collapse of official deception largely to internal dynamics — image-making, self-deception, decision-makers who had stopped testing their premises against reality. At Sèvres the deception was not self-deceived. It was cold, competent and well informed about its own assumptions; it failed because a party holding independent information and financial leverage declined to accept the cover story. Is that failure mode anywhere in her account, or does it require a different theory of why political lies come unstuck?

Third, there is a positive test she is well placed to pass or fail. Arendt ties the liar's capacity to the same freedom to begin something new that makes action possible, and she credits the deliberate liar with a clarity the self-deceived image-maker lacks. Sèvres was a supremely deliberate exercise of that faculty. Does her framework allow us to separate skill at constructing a plausible world from skill at predicting an actual one, or does it treat them as a single capacity — and if the latter, is that a defect?

Fourth, the durability of the denial. She writes as though factual truth is peculiarly resilient, because facts are witnessed and reality keeps intruding; yet a denial of this one survived open suspicion, and partial destruction of the record, for the better part of a decade, and its eventual undoing depended on the accident that copies existed in other jurisdictions. If the real lesson is that factual truth persists in proportion to the redundancy of its custody rather than through any intrinsic stubbornness, how much of her account survives that substitution?

Finally, a verdict: is Suez a confirmation, an anomaly she could absorb with a modest amendment, or a case her categories were never built for — given that she was writing about American self-deception in Vietnam and about totalitarianism, not about a successful, traditional European cabinet conspiracy that went wrong for reasons having nothing to do with lying? Say plainly where she is silent rather than making her speak, and tell me whether the Sèvres structure generalises: what class of political deceptions shares its shape, and what would have to be true of an audience for a truthful account of an engineered event to be taken as an innocent one.

I am not after sources or a reading list; I cannot check anything you cite. Where you are unsure of a detail — the fate of the British copy, exactly what was said at the Security Council — work from my summary, and flag any place where the analysis would change if the detail were otherwise.
