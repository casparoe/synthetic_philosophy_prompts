So I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm working on a long piece about that old experimental philosophy finding — the chairman who gets told a new program will either harm or help the environment, says he doesn't care one way or the other and just wants the profit, and then readers overwhelmingly say the harm was done intentionally while the help wasn't. My editor likes the piece but she wants the sidebar to be something readers can actually do rather than me summarizing a decade of arguments. So: can you build me a little sorting instrument that puts people into whichever explanation of the asymmetry they actually go in for, without ever letting them see which one they're heading toward?

What I'm picturing is 7 to 9 items, each one a concrete case — mostly tweaked versions of the chairman setup — with two or three answer choices, and then a scoring key at the end that maps answer patterns onto the real positions in the literature. The leaves I want covered, plus anything you think I've left out: the skill or competence story, where blame is doing proxy work for how capable the agent looked; the norm-violation story, where what matters is that a rule got broken and not that anyone got hurt; the deep-self model, where the harm case supposedly reveals who the chairman really is; the affect view on which emotion just contaminates the judgment so one side of the asymmetry is flat-out an error; the conversational pragmatics reply, where the asymmetry is an artifact of how the question is put; the blame-first models where the intentional verdict comes downstream of a motivation to find someone at fault; the individual differences reply, where different people are using different routes and there is no single folk concept to explain; and the camp that says the effect is smaller, messier and less stable than advertised, so none of this is licensed.

Hard constraints on the items, because this is the whole point. Every one has to be answerable from the case alone by someone who has never heard any of this — no item may name a theory, no item may use words like semantics, norm, competence, deep self or pragmatic, and nothing may ask a reader what they'd call something or which explanation sounds better. Sort them by what they judge, not by what they've read.

And the items need to do real diagnostic work, so please use variants that actually pull the accounts apart rather than the two everyone quotes. At minimum I want: one where the bad outcome violates a rule or an agreement but is not morally bad and nobody is wronged; one where the chairman is visibly clumsy or out of his depth instead of cool and competent; one where the outcome is identical but the person telling the story signals approval rather than disapproval; one where the reader is the chairman and has to judge their own action; one on whether the asymmetry shows up when people are asked what the chairman knew rather than what he did intentionally; and one variant I made up for the piece, where the chairman is replaced by an autonomous routing algorithm that reports it doesn't care about the deliveries it screws up.

Then after the scoring key, two things I need and that most write-ups skip. First, tell me which pairs of leaves my sidebar genuinely cannot separate, and say why the items fail to discriminate them — I'd rather flag it in the piece than pretend. Second, give me the empty cells: the combinations of answers the items permit that don't correspond to any position anyone defends, each with a sentence on whether that slot is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody has bothered yet.

Keep it readable in about ten minutes with a pen and paper, so a few sentences per case at most. And the scoring key has to be something a human can actually apply — not a description of a key.
