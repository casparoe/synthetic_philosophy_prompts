I'm drafting a book chapter whose spine is a question a reader says I haven't earned the right to ask yet: was Malthus wrong, or right but early? Before I argue either way, I want the standard the argument has to meet, written down seperate from the argument, so the verdict doesn't just fall out of how I framed things.

So: write the desiderata for a long-run limits theory — the kind that says some binding constraint (food, land, water, energy, whatever) will eventually outrun our capacity to relax it. What does such a theory have to deliver to count as adequate *for the purpose of settling wrong-vs-right-but-early*? I want the whole spec, not a gesture at it:

- the phenomena it has to explain and the cases it has to get right — including the cases where the limit did seem to bite and the cases where it visibly didn't, and the famines that happened with plenty of aggregate food sitting around
- the distinctions it's not allowed to collapse: the level at which a limit binds (production vs. distribution vs. effective access), a directional force vs. a dated prediction, a constraint that's physically fixed vs. one that's institutionally or technologically fixed, the stock of a resource vs. the flow of services from it
- the results it can't contradict, from demography, agronomy, economics, thermodynamics — whichever you think are actually load-bearing
- what it has to say about its own possible falsification: which observation would count against it, over what horizon, and with what error bars on the margin
- and behind every one of those, the motivating case or consideration, so I can tell a real requirement from a nice-sounding one

Then the part I actually care about. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of a camp: the system-dynamics people will insist on quantified stocks and flows, the economists will insist on prices and substitution elasticities, and somebody will insist the theory be consistent with two centuries of rising real wages and falling resource prices — I suspect that last one is a cornucopian prejudice doing the work of an argument, but I can't show it. And tell me whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. My hunch is that "specific enough to be falsified on a schedule" and "treats innovation and fertility response as endogenous rather than as exogenous surprises" can't both be fully met, which would mean right-but-early isn't a verdict available to any theory that clears the list at all — it's the refuge of theories that fail one of those two. If that's the answer, say it plainly and show the derivation, because it changes the chapter and I'd rather know now.

Also tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and whether some of what I'd be listing is really a constraint on our credences rather than on the theory — that's were I keep sliding, and I want the two sorted.

Finally, briefly — a short paragraph each, no more — score these against the list: Malthus's original ratio version, his later moral-restraint version, the view that population pressure itself drives agricultural intensification, the world-modeling approach out of the early-70s futures work, and a contemporary integrated-assessment forecast of a hard planetary limit.

No bibliography, I've got the reading covered. I just want the spec and the tension.
