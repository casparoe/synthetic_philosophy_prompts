I am a seventeen-year-old senior, and this spring I have been doing an independent study with my English teacher while also sitting in on the art department's typography elective. For the elective's final project we each had to design a letterform on a grid three squares wide and six squares tall, and mine was an "a" — but I could not settle on one, so my specimen sheet ended up with six: the double-storey one from a book face (small upper bowl, a closed counter below it, a stem down the right side, and a leg that kicks out at the baseline with a little spur on it); the single-storey one everyone writes by hand, which is essentially a circle with a curve attached and shares almost no strokes with the first; a stencil version in which the counter is broken open into an aperture; a geometric one built from a perfect circle and a straight bar; a Braille "a," which is a single raised dot in the upper-left corner of the cell; and a Morse "a," one short mark followed by one long one. Our literary magazine is printing a poem of mine as a broadside beside that sheet, and this year's theme is first things, so the poem is meant to answer what makes all six of them the same letter. My teacher lent me Hofstadter's *Letter Spirit*, and alongside it he showed me the passage where Wittgenstein compares the unity of a category to a thread twisted out of many overlapping fibres, no one of which runs the whole length. That has left three accounts in my head: that an "a" is a list of parts you could check any candidate against; that there is no list at all, only criss-crossing resemblances between particular "a"s; and that what is shared is not in the ink but in what a mind that has absorbed thousands of hands does with it — it slips from one style to a neighbouring one and lands on the same letter, and the very same capacity that lets it recognise a letterform in an unfamiliar hand lets it invent one in a familiar style. I want the poem to argue between those three, and I do not want it to end in a shrug.

Please take on the role of a working poet who has also taught a graduate seminar on concepts and categories and who regards typography as a branch of philosophy. Think carefully before you write anything, and begin with a short planning sketch of no more than about 150 words, in bullet points: list the refrain variants you intend to use in order, name the single word that changes at each step, confirm that no word survives all of them, and state which of the three accounts each stanza's first two lines will be pressing. Only then give me the poem.

The form is fixed, because it has to echo the sheet it will be printed beside, and a hand-press printer will be setting it, so please treat these as hard constraints and get the anatomy vocabulary right (bowl, counter, aperture, stem, spur, leg, x-height) without inventing features:

1. Eighteen lines in six tercets — six rows of three cells, like the grid — except that the final tercet has only two lines. The missing third line should simply be absent; do not replace it with a dash, a blank, or a placeholder, and do not explain the absence inside the poem.
2. The third line of each of the first five tercets is a refrain in four stresses, and the refrain is the line that keeps trying to say what an "a" is made of. Each return differs from the previous one by exactly one word — a chain of one-word substitutions — and cumulatively the first and fifth refrains should have no word in common. No word may appear in all five refrains; every consecutive pair must still read as unmistakably the same line.
3. The first two lines of each tercet are a rhyming couplet in loose iambic pentameter, and no rhyme sound may be shared between two different tercets. The only thing running the length of the poem should be the refrain, and even that should keep changing under the reader's hand.
4. The five refrains should be anchored to five of my six letterforms, in an order where each is a plausible neighbour of the next; leave the sixth unanchored, since its line is the one that goes missing.
5. The poem must never name a philosopher, a theory, or a program, and must not use the words essence, category, family resemblance, necessary, sufficient, meaning, or identity. The argument has to be conducted entirely in ink, holes, dots, and marks.
6. A reader who knows this literature should be able to say which tercet is pressing which account, and where the poem comes down. It should come down somewhere: if the answer turns out to be that a letter is whatever we happen to agree to call one, that is a cop-out, and I would rather you not write it. The point I want defended is that the pattern is genuinely there across all six, whether or not anyone can write it down as a list.

After the poem, add two or three sentences telling me what the sixth refrain would have been had you printed it, which of my letterforms it belonged to, and what the reader has to bring in order to supply it. If you find that some of these constraints cannot all be satisfied at once, tell me which ones collide and why, and give me the nearest version that does work rather than quietly dropping one. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list — this is for a literary magazine, not a paper.
