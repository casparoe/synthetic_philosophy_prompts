I run a two-person project on relational accounts of personhood — the ubuntu-flavored line where being a person is something you achieve or enact inside a community rather than a property you simply have, and where it can therefore come in degrees. We've got 18 months of funded time, no empirical work, no fieldwork: just two philosophers and a lot of arguing. Our funder wants something a hospital ethics committee could actually use at the end of it, not another survey.

The obvious target is the margins: advanced dementia, prolonged disorders of consciousness, profound intellectual disability. Relational accounts get accused of either demoting people who clearly shouldn't be demoted, or of quietly rebuilding the equal-status view they were supposed to be an alternative to. My hunch is that this is the one real crux and that everything else — the vagueness complaints, the worry that it's just care ethics in different vocabulary, the charge that it lets the community overrule the individual — sits downstream of it. I could easily be wrong about that.

So: which open questions should we actually spend the 18 months on, and in what order? Defend the ordering. I want to know what makes each question upstream of the others, what two people with no empirical background can genuinely settle in that window, and what the deliverable would look like. And tell me straight whether my crux claim survives. No reading list, we know the literature.

Format please:
- **Bottom line** — four sentences max: the question you'd bet the project on, and a verdict on my hunch.
- **The shortlist, ranked** — around 700 words, three or four questions, each with its place in the order justified and a concrete output named (a distinction, a worked case, a decision procedure a comittee could apply, whatever fits).
- **What I'd cut** — 150 words: which familiar objections to relational personhood aren't worth our time, and why (untractable for us, or wouldn't change anything downstream).
- **Rank-changers** — 150 words: what would move a question up or down over the next year and a half.
