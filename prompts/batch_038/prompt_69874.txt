My sister is a palliative care nurse and for the past year she's been sitting in on sessions for a psilocybin trial with terminal cancer patients who can't stop being terrified of dying, so I get a running report every time we see eachother. Two things from it have been rattling around in my head. First, the investigators care about the experience itself, not about what the patient believes: they score each session on stuff like losing the sense of having a self, feeling in contact with something enormous and benevolent, time dropping out, that sense people describe of having been shown something they can't put into words — and by her account the high scorers are the ones still doing better half a year later, while the people who mainly just felt strange and a bit sick don't move much. Second, the protocol deliberately gives them no religious framing whatsoever: eyeshades, a playlist, two staff in the room, no ritual, no doctrine, nothing in aftercare about God or the soul. And her two most dramatic cases were both longtime atheists who afterwards insisted, unprompted, that they'd met something. One of them has started going to a church. So the drug seems to reliably produce, in people with no interest in it, the kind of experience that used to be the raw material of religion, and then produces relief that tracks how intense that experience was.

That's where Freud comes in, and I want your reconstruction rather than a survey of what he thought about religion in general. He died in 1939 so none of this existed; the question is what he'd do with it. Is a pharmacologically triggered mystical-type state just the oceanic feeling he opens Civilization and Its Discontents with, the one he takes from Romain Rolland and then explains away as a survival of an earlier stage of the ego — except now available on demand, in graded doses, with outcome measures attached? And if so, does the trial data strike him as a gorgeous confirmation that the whole thing is a regressive wish-fulfillment apparatus, or as beside the point, because his target in The Future of an Illusion was belief and doctrine, the business of a church telling people things that aren't so, and a session with no creed in it isn't an illusion in his sense at all? I keep swinging between those two and can't settle it. I'm also curious about the part that looks genuinely awkward for him: an illusion for Freud is a belief that comes out of a wish, and he's careful that being an illusion doesn't make it false — but here the conviction (there was something there, it was kind to me, I am not simply going to stop) is generated by a molecule rather than by the person's own wish, which seems to cut against the claim that the wish is doing the work. Does he have a move available, or does this kind of data embarrass the theory? And where does his hope that the intellect ends up running the show leave him — is a therapy that dissolves a dying person's dread by inducing a state they can only describe religiously a humane local victory he'd accept, or exactly the thing he spent his last decade arguing against? Finally, tell me whether there's any finding in this area that would have forced him to revise instead of absorb, or whether the account is stretchy enough that he'd say the trial just proves his point.
