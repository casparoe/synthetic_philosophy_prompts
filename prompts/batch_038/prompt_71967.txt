I write a science-and-ideas column, mostly about minds and machines, and my editor has greenlit one big reported series for next year — call it six or seven pieces plus the reporting time to actually go visit labs, field biologists, and the people doing welfare work. The subject is supposed to be which computations matter morally: where the line for sentience/patienthood sits once you've got trained models, simulated organisms, insect-scale nervous systems, and dish-grown tissue all in play at the same time.

Here's my problem. There's maybe forty things you could honestly call "the open question" in this area and I can only build a series around three or four. So I want your actual prioritization, not a survey of the terrain.

The framing I've been carrying around, which you should feel free to attack: I think attribution is doing two completely different jobs that everyone keeps running together. One is epistemic — can we tell, from the outside, whether there's anything it's like to be this system? The other is a credibility-budget problem — every time the circle gets successfully widened, the next claim gets cheaper to make, and I worry we're headed for a stretch where could-be-sentient is so cheap to assert that it stops moving anybody, which would be a disaster for the cases that are actually urgent. Call it patienthood inflation. Is that a real crux, or is it a columnist's anxiety with no content? Tell me straight.

Also, I want you to sort the candidate questions into three buckets, because I think they need completely different reporting: (a) ones where more conceptual work would genuinely move things, (b) ones that are really just blocked on empirical facts nobody has yet, and (c) ones that look like metaphysics but are actually disguised fights about money, liability, or status. If a question is in bucket (c), say who's fighting and what they're fighting over.

For the three or four you'd build the series around, give me: how many other questions hang off its answer; whether a non-philosopher with a notebook can make progress on it (I'm not sitting down to build a formal theory of consciousness); how badly it will date — I don't want to spend a year on something one benchmark result renders quaint; and what specific development over the next 12–18 months would move it up or down your list.

Then flag the one you think is most overrated — the question everyone in this space writes about that I should actively steer around — and say why, ideally with an account of what the standard treatment gets wrong rather than just that it's been done to death.

Skip the reading list and the citations, I'll find my own sources. What I want is the argument for the ordering.
