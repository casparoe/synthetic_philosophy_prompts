A Modell hereof.* — the 1630 text that anthologies usually modernize as "A Model of Christian Charity." Our course site reproduces a transcription of the manuscript with Winthrop's original spelling and with the volume's page markers inserted; I have stripped the page markers and copied the excerpt exactly as it appears there, so please do not normalize the orthography or supply a modernized reading unless the modernization changes the sense. **Excerpt A** (contiguous, from the third "Reason" for the ranking of rich and poor through the opening of the law-of-nature discussion):

> 3 Reas. Thirdly, that every man might have need of others, and from hence they might be all knitt more nearly together in the Bonds of brotherly affection. From hence it appears plainly that noe man is made more honourable than another or more wealthy &c., out of any particular and singular respect to himselfe, but for the glory of his creator and the common good of the creature, man. Therefore God still reserves the propperty of these gifts to himself as Ezek. 16. 17. he there calls wealthe, his gold and his silver, and Prov. 3. 9. he claims theire service as his due, honor the Lord with thy riches &c.--All men being thus (by divine providence) ranked into two sorts, riche and poore; under the first are comprehended all such as are able to live comfortably by their own meanes duely improved; and all others are poore according to the former distribution. There are two rules whereby we are to walk one towards another: Justice and Mercy. These are always distinguished in their act and in their object, yet may they both concurre in the same subject in eache respect; as sometimes there may be an occasion of showing mercy to a rich man in some sudden danger or distresse, and alsoe doeing of meere justice to a poor man in regard of some perticular contract &c. There is likewise a double Lawe by which wee are regulated in our conversation towardes another; in both the former respects, the lawe of nature and the lawe of grace, or the morrall lawe or the lawe of the gospell, to omitt the rule of justice as not propperly belonging to this purpose otherwise than it may fall into consideration in some perticular cases. By the first of these lawes man as he was enabled soe withall is commanded to love his neighbour as himself.

**Excerpt B** (from the later list of differences between the law of nature and the law of grace):

> First the lawe of nature was given to man in the estate of innocency; this of the Gospell in the estate of regeneracy. 2ly, the former propunds one man to another, as the same flesh and image of God; this as a brother in Christ allsoe, and in the communion of the same Spirit, and soe teacheth to put a difference between christians and others. Doe good to all, especially to the household of faith; upon this ground the Israelites were to putt a difference betweene the brethren of such as were strangers though not of the Canaanites. 3ly. The Lawe of nature would give no rules for dealing with enemies, for all are to be considered as friends in the state of innocency, but the Gospell commands loue to an enemy.

My questions, in order of importance to me:

1. **The syntax of the "double Lawe" sentence.** What does "in both the former respects" attach to — the two rules (justice and mercy), or the two sorts (rich and poor)? And is "the lawe of nature and the lawe of grace" simply a second naming of "the morrall lawe or the lawe of the gospell," or are these two distinct axes that Winthrop is running together? Most importantly: what is actually being set aside by "to omitt the rule of justice as not propperly belonging to this purpose otherwise than it may fall into consideration in some perticular cases"? My own parse is that justice is dropped for the remainder of the argument and that everything after this point is a treatise on mercy alone, with the trailing "otherwise than..." clause functioning as a hedge rather than a qualification. Tell me whether that parse survives contact with the sentence, and if it does not, give me the parse that does.

2. **Whether the property reservation is load-bearing.** "Therefore God still reserves the propperty of these gifts to himself" — does this sentence change the *ownership* of wealth (a trust or stewardship view, on which the rich hold as God's stewards and the ranking of rich and poor allocates offices and duties rather than entitlements), or does it merely add a religious motive to obligations the rich already owe, leaving ownership untouched? Would the sentence retain any argumentative force if the Ezekiel and Proverbs proof-texts were deleted? Our seminar is split on this and I cannot tell from the wording alone which side is reading something in.

3. **The grid he is working with.** Winthrop says the two rules are "always distinguished in their act and in their object, yet may they both concurre in the same subject in eache respect," then offers two illustrations: mercy shown to a rich man in sudden danger, and "meere justice" done to a poor man on a contract. I cannot determine whether "subject" here means the person acted upon or the situation, nor whether the two illustrations are parallel cases or contrastive ones. Please reconstruct the table he seems to be operating with, and tell me whether the later "difference of seasons and occasions" (ordinary versus extraordinary times) is the same axis as justice/mercy, an axis crossed with it, or a third thing. This matters because the ordinary/extraordinary switch is what licenses giving "beyond their abillity."

4. **What the Canaanite clause contributes.** In Excerpt B the second difference yields a graded circle of obligation — "put a difference between christians and others," glossed by "Doe good to all, especially to the household of faith," and then glossed again by the Israelite distinction "betweene the brethren of such as were strangers though not of the Canaanites." Three readings are circulating in my seminar: (i) the Canaanite clause is a typological aside with no bearing on anyone the colonists will meet; (ii) it is the structural point, since a law that generates degrees of obligation terminates in a category of outright exclusion; (iii) it is an implicit license. Which does the wording best support, and what would the wording have to look like for each of the other two to be right? Separately: is "the estate of innocency" doing explanatory work as the ground of the first law, and does "regeneracy" name a change in the person, in the standard, or in both?

5. **Whether the emergency is the standing condition.** Given that "community of perills calls for extraordinary liberality, and soe doth community in some speciall service for the churche," and given that the company Winthrop is addressing is defined by precisely such a community of perils and such a special service, does the text have a normal/exception structure in which the extraordinary season is not a deviation from the ordinary one but its permanent form? I want to know whether that inference is available from the excerpt by itself, or whether it requires the rest of the document, and I want you to say which.

6. **Vocabulary and allusion.** Gloss "conversation," "meere justice," "duely improved," "meanes" in "by their own meanes," "the household of faith," "the old man of Gibea," and "they of Macedonia" as Winthrop uses them, and mark for each whether the gloss depends on the scriptural referent or on seventeenth-century English usage. Also: in this transcription, do "--" and "&c." carry anything beyond punctuation? I have been reading "&c." as Winthrop trailing off after a proof-text and "--" as a section division, but I want to know whether either could be doing argumentative work — in particular, whether an "&c." list should be treated as deliberately open-ended rather than as an incomplete enumeration I am meant to finish.

7. **What the excerpt gives a later reader.** The passage contains no nation, no land, no mission, no destiny; it is a treatise on charity among a company of believers. Which of its components, if any, are available for later exceptionalist appropriation — designed interdependence, the property reservation, the graded circle of obligation, the standing emergency — and what has to be imported from outside the excerpt to complete that appropriation?
