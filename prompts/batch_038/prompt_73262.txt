I spent thirty-eight years designing feedback control systems for a national laboratory, and in retirement I have taken up the socialist calculation debate: Mises's 1920 paper, Lange's mid-1930s reply, and Hayek's 1945 essay on the use of knowledge in society. Attempting to state the Hayekian case rigorously, I produced an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, and I suspect a step has slipped without my noticing it.

(1) The market price of a productive resource is not a report on some independently existing quantity; it is the output of a competitive bidding process, and it encodes information — local scarcities, competing uses, unarticulated know-how — that exists nowhere else and is held in full by nobody.
(2) If something is nothing but the output of a process P, then it cannot be had except by running P.
(3) A planning board can acquire data only by requiring agents to report them, and only what has already been articulated can be reported.
(4) By (1) and (3), the information carried in prices is nowhere articulated before the bidding; so by (2) the board cannot acquire it, whatever its computing power.
(5) Lange's board announces prices and revises them against reported shortages and surpluses; but those reports presuppose the prices the board is trying to discover, and his managers, instructed to minimize cost at given prices, face no test analogous to a private owner's, since nothing they report can be checked against a rival's bid. So Lange's solution fails.

Now the part that troubles me.

(6) Nothing in (1) through (4) mentions socialism, common ownership, or politics of any kind.
(7) The argument therefore applies to every institution that sets a price without running a competitive process: transfer prices inside a multinational, administered utility tariffs, the auction of radio spectrum, a central bank's policy rate, a supermarket's shelf label.
(8) It applies equally to every closed-loop controller. A controller sees only reported measurements, must generate its own reference signal, and revise that signal by trial and error against observed deviation — structurally Lange's procedure. If (4) is sound, no engineered feedback system could ever hold a setpoint.
(9) Such systems do hold setpoints, reliably enough that I made a career of it.
(10) Therefore (1)–(4) contain a falsehood, and the calculation argument fails not merely against Lange but everywhere.

I would rather keep (1), which I take to be a genuine insight, than accept (10); but I cannot see whether (1) survives without (2). Please diagnose the argument: identify the step to reject, explain why it looked compelling to a reader in my position, and price each available rejection — including the option of biting the bullet at (9) or at (10). Tell me in particular whether (8) is a legitimate reductio or a false analogy I have imported, and if it is a disanalogy, name the property that competitive markets have and my controllers lack, and say whether that property does the work the argument needs it to do. Finally, separate clearly what is settled among those who work on this debate from what remains genuinely contested; I do not want a contested verdict on who won handed to me as the consensus.
