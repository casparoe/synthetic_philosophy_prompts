I'm a software engineer working on a side project in the digital humanities, and I'd like a sanity check on its premise before I invest more time in it.

The pipeline embeds Latin and vernacular scholarly texts from roughly 1250 to 1750, fits a topic model over the corpus, and then runs changepoint detection on the resulting time series of topic proportions. Concretely: I compute a centroid embedding per decade, flag a changepoint wherever the decade-over-decade displacement exceeds a threshold, and cluster the flagged decades into candidate transitions. The stated goal is to locate the boundaries of the Renaissance and the Scientific Revolution empirically, from textual behavior, rather than inherit them from textbooks.

Here is my worry. My understanding of the historiography is secondhand, but my impression is that these period labels are retrospective inventions: the "Middle Ages" was coined by people who needed a dark interval in order to cast themselves as a rebirth, and one prominent line of argument holds that there was no such thing as the Scientific Revolution at all, merely a set of local changes later bundled into a single story. If the periods were manufactured after the fact by people with a stake in the narrative, then it looks to me as though the debate has already settled the question my tool is supposed to answer: there is no boundary out there to be found, and any changepoint I report is simply a reflection of choices I made upstream — which texts happened to survive, which ones got digitized, which embedding model I picked, and above all an arbitrary displacement threshold that I set by eyeballing the curve.

So: is this a fatal objection to the project? Or, if it isn't, where exactly does the constructivist critique bite and where does it not — that is, what would I actually be measuring if I found a robust transition, given that the label for it is admittedly a later coinage? I'd also appreciate being told if I'm conflating two different things here, since I suspect I am but can't say which two.
