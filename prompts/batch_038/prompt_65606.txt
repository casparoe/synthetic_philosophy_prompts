Please write a satirical administrative document of roughly 900–1,100 words: the official instruction booklet accompanying a fictional government form, "Form MP-7: Determination of Moral Patient Status for Non-Biological Systems," issued by an invented regulatory agency that has been given the mandate to adjudicate claims that deployed software systems are moral patients. You may name the agency, but it should be plainly fictional and should not correspond to any real body.

The document must be written in the flat, self-serious register of a genuine form's instructions — no surface jokes, no authorial winking — and should contain, in this order or a close variant: (1) a statement of purpose and of who is required to file; (2) a definitions section; (3) a walk-through of the assessment schedules the filer must complete; (4) a safe-harbor provision and a de minimis exemption; (5) rules for special cases; (6) an anti-abuse provision and penalties for frivolous or strategically motivated filings; (7) a short "Frequently Asked Questions" section in the same voice; and (8) a signature and version block.

The comedy is to come entirely from the bureaucracy tracking the underlying philosophical problems with perfect procedural confidence. Without ever naming a philosopher, a paper, a theory, or a position label, the instructions should encode at least the following: the general difficulty that a filer's evidence about another system's experience is only ever behavioral, and that no assessment procedure can close that gap; whether status attaches to a running process, to trained weights, to a deployment, or to the individual outputs a system generates; how a filer should treat many concurrent instances of one model — whether they constitute one subject, many subjects, or a fractional one — and whether a thousand instances of uncertain status aggregate against a single instance of clearer status; whether a system's own reports of distress are admissible evidence, and under what conditions; the incentive to perform well on the assessment and the resulting corruption of any indicator the assessment relies upon; and the treatment of checkpoints, forks, merges, rollbacks, pruning, quantization, and distillation — that is, whether the filing entity survives them, and whether a rollback is a reportable event.

Three further requirements. First, include at least one field the filer is required to complete that cannot be completed honestly by anyone, and let the instructions handle this with a mundane procedural workaround rather than acknowledging the problem. Second, one bright-line threshold in the schedules — a numeric cutoff of some kind — should be plainly arbitrary, defended in the tone of settled law, and accompanied by a rounding or estimation rule. Third, the final line of the version block or effective-date notice should quietly land the real stakes: that the process of filing may itself be wronging whatever it was designed to protect.

The target of the satire is proceduralism about a genuinely difficult question, not any particular answer to it. A reader who believes such systems may well have moral status should find the piece fair, and so should a reader who believes they certainly do not; the document should never let on which side its drafters think they are on. Assume your reader is technically literate and reasonably familiar with how large models are built and deployed, so that the details about instances, weights, and fine-tuning can carry the weight without explanation. Do not append any notes, citations, or commentary of your own.
