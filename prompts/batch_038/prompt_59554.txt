I'm editing a small anthology of philosophical fiction and I need a story, roughly 1400 to 1800 words, that dramatizes the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism without ever naming it or explaining it.

Here's the setup I want you to use. Keep the mechanics, rename whatever you like. A river town: an upper district of about nine hundred people and a lower one of about forty. The flood authority can build a barrier that saves the upper town permanently by guaranteeing the lower town goes under in the first serious storm. That on its own is the boring numbers case, so here's the twist — fifteen years ago both districts were wired into a civic implant everyone just calls the common. Residents literally feel one another's pleasure, pain and grief, pooled and redistributed across the whole valley. So the defender of the barrier can say, honestly and correctly, that the aggregation isn't a bookkeeping fiction any more: there is now a single subject of experience spanning the two towns, the forty's suffering is felt by everyone and outweighted by the nine hundred's relief, and the objection about nobody being around to receive the compensation has been engineered away. I want that case made in its strongest form by someone decent and sincere — no moustache-twirling, no euphemism.

The story's whole job is to find out what's left over after that concession is granted. I think the sharing turns out to be of feeling and not of survival, but you are absolutely not allowed to say anything that abstract anywhere in the piece. It has to come out of scenes.

Constraints:

- First person, from somebody whose job puts them in the middle of it — a consent officer, an assessor, a translator for the common, whatever you invent.
- Three scenes minimum, one of which is a public meeting where the decision gets defended.
- At least one moment where the common makes the calculation feel genuinely right to the narrator, and a later moment where it fails them in some small concrete way.
- Don't explain how the implant works. It's fifteen years old, it's mundane, people complain about it.
- No philosophers' names, no words like deontology or utility-maximising in dialogue, no narrator essay paragraphs.
- The forty have to be individuals, not a headcount. Give me at least two of them whose wants have nothing whatsoever to do with the vote.
- End on an image, not a verdict. A reader should be able to say which side the story lands on without anyone in it anouncing it.
- Pick your own title.

If you can work in a recurring sensory detail for what the common feels like from the inside — a taste, a pressure behind the eyes, whatever — do it, but only if it earns its keep.
