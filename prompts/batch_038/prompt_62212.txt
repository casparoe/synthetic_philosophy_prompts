So I work a few shifts a week in a small municipal archive and there's a tension in what we do that I can't get my head around, and I'd rather someone reconstruct it for me than agree with me about it.

Almost everything we hold is arranged by provenance — the old French rule, respect des fonds, which as far as I understand it goes back to the middle of the nineteenth century. Records get grouped under whatever office or family created them, kept more or less in the order that office kept them, and the finding aid is titled after the creator. So the sanitation division's payroll books from the fifties live together as one unit called something like Records of the Department of Public Works, and the four Black men who appear in them only as line items — a name, a wage, an occasional address, nothing in their own hand — are inside that unit, named nowhere in the title, and findable only if you already know to go looking. Then, in the same building, we run what the profession calls reparative description: a project whose entire job is to go back through exactly those fonds and surface the names of the people documented inside them, the sanitation workers, the patients in the county hospital intake logs, the tenants in the housing authority's rent rolls, so they exist as searchable names rather than as subject matter inside someone else's bureaucratic record.

Two ongoing projects in one institution, pulling in what look like opposite directions, and nobody here seems bothered by it. So: what does the provenance rule actually presuppose? What view of a record, and of who counts as the author or agent behind one, does arranging by creator only make sense under — does it assume that a document's meaning is fixed by the purpose it was made for and the body that made it, that such bodies are coherent agents with a continuous identity over time, that context belongs to the creator rather than to the people the record is about? Which rival pictures does it quietly rule out: that a record is evidence about a subject matter first and about an institution second, that the person documented is in some sense a co-author of a record made about them without their say, that a name in a ledger is a fact about that person and not merely a fact about the ledger? Please be careful to separate what the rule strictly needs from what merely happens to fit it nicely. I can imagine a view that is deeply skeptical of institutions, and suspicious of the idea that a bureaucracy is a unified agent at all, that would still insist on keeping the oppressor's filing system intact — because the intactness of the filing is itself the evidence of how power worked. If that's coherent, then provenance is philosophically underdetermined in a way I hadn't appreciated, and I want to know how many other theories can license it.

Then tell me whether the two projects are actually consistent with each other. Do arrangement and description presuppose different theories of what a description is for — locating a thing versus recognizing a person — and can one institution hold both without contradiction, or is the remediation work in practice an argument against the arrangement rule it's applied to? And finally: what would have to change, in the physical arrangement, in the cataloging labour, and in the evidential guarantees we hand researchers, if we swapped provenance for subject-based arrangement? Would something irrecoverable be lost, or is the dread of losing original order a professional habit dressed up as a philosophical necessity?

No need for citations or a reading list, I'm not after sources, just the reasoning. And please write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
