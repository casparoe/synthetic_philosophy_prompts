I run a small reading group on decision theory and we've just spent two weeks stuck on the Pasadena game — you know, flip a fair coin until it comes up heads, and if the first heads lands on flip *n* you recieve $2ⁿ/n when n is odd and *pay* $2ⁿ/n when n is even. Multiply through by the probability 2⁻ⁿ and the terms of the expected-value series come out as exactly 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges only conditionally, so the expectation isn't well-defined: rearrange the terms and you can drive the sum to any real number you like, or make it diverge. Two people in my group dug in on opposite sides of what to do about this and neither would budge, and I want to see the argument written out properly before we meet again.

Here's the twist I want, because I'm bored of hearing the coin-flip case recited: don't set the dialogue at a gambling table. Set it in a funding committee's meeting room. The committee has to choose whether to back one research program whose payoff structure is Pasadena-shaped — call the possible outcomes o₁, o₂, o₃…, with the probability that the program lands on oₙ being 2⁻ⁿ, and the net utility of oₙ being (−1)ⁿ⁺¹·2ⁿ/n in units of "lives-equivalent" that the committee treats as linear and unbounded. One committee member is a revise-it type: she thinks decision theory has to be extended so that agents can have genuine attitudes toward prospects like this — weak or conditional expectations, Abel/Cesàro-style summation, non-Archimedean values, stochastic-dominance principles, whatever — and that a theory which can only shrug is incomplete, not careful. The other is a refuse-it type: he thinks this program isn't a well-posed decision problem in the first place, and that the honest moves are to bound the utility function (once utility is bounded the series converges absolutely and no Pasadena-shaped program can exist), or to accept incomparability and deny that rationality demands a complete ranking, or to point out that no real committee ever faces an unbounded payoff schedule because every implementation truncates. Both should be genuinely good — no strawmen, and neither of them is allowed to just repeat themselves louder. I want actual back-and-forth where each one responds to the specific move the other just made, and at one point one of them should accuse the other of relabeling the problem instead of solving it, and the accused should have a real answer.

Also work in this wrinkle, which I made up and want them to argue over rather than wave at: someone at the table proposes a tweaked version of the program — call it the Burbank proposal — which is identical except that every odd-n branch gets a flat +1 lives-equivalent bonus. The bonus terms sum absolutely to 1, so the Burbank series still fails to converge absolutely and still has no defined expectation, but its natural-ordering sum is one unit higher than the original's. Have the two committee members fight about whether that is a reason to prefer Burbank: what does it take for an ordering-sensitive comparison to be trustworthy, and does either side's own framework license the preference or forbid it? Each of them has to concede at least one substantive point by the end, and the concession should cost them something. No citations, no bibliography, and please don't invent paper titles or put words in real authors' mouths — if you refer to a published position, describe it rather than quote it.

Format: one sentence up front saying which of the two you think actually has the better of it and why, then the dialogue itself with speaker labels (something like REVISE / REFUSE), hard cap of 900 words for the dialogue, and one closing line of 40 words max naming the single concession that changed the balance.
