I am about to spend three years as the sole philosopher embedded in a small quantitative-methods unit at a national infrastructure regulator. The unit must decide, on a recurring basis, which of several competing long-horizon simulation models — sea-level and flood-risk projections over fifty- to hundred-year horizons — its permitting and investment decisions should rest on, and it must be able to defend that choice in audit and in litigation. Every candidate is known to be false, and the horizon is too long for the decision to be settled by outcomes arriving within it.

The unit's current proposal is to construct an explicit verisimilitude score: define closeness to the truth formally, estimate each candidate's expected closeness relative to present evidence, and rank. The estimator in view is a convergence proxy — roughly, expected agreement with whatever model turns out to be best eventually, approximated by agreement with an evidence-weighted ensemble of the present candidates. A reviewer has raised two objections: that the proxy rewards correlated error and scores each model partly by its similarity to the very things being scored, and that the certification requirement pushes toward a single scalar even where the underlying ordering is only partial.

I want help designing an agenda, not a survey of the field. Which open questions in the theory of verisimilitude should the unit treat as load-bearing over the next three years, and in what order? For each question you prioritize, give me the reasoning: how much of the design turns on its answer; whether it is tractable for one person working formally — proof-theoretic and measure-theoretic tools, no new field data; how neglected it is relative to how much existing practice silently assumes it is already settled; and what a resolution would concretely change about the score.

Consider at least the following candidates, and add any you judge more important than these: whether truthlikeness should be a scalar or a partial order, and what a decision procedure looks like when the ordering fails to rank precisely the contested pairs; parameterization- and language-dependence, i.e. whether such a measure can be made invariant under re-expression of the same physical content; how to extend accounts built for deductively closed sets of sentences to candidates that are probability distributions rather than sentence sets, and what that has to do with proper scoring rules; the epistemic problem — whether "estimated" verisimilitude relative to available evidence is stable or simply reinstates the uncertainty it was supposed to resolve; whether Arrow-style impossibility results, showing that familiar desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied in richly structured domains, force the unit to give up one of its desiderata, and which one; and how deliberate idealizations, present in every candidate, should figure in closeness to the truth.

Then tell me which of these to deprioritize — where settling the question would leave the design unchanged — and what kinds of arguments or results over the next two or three years would move an item up or down your ordering. Be explicit about the trade-off you are striking between upstream importance and tractability for a single person on a three-year clock, and say what the criteria for your ranking are rather than just presenting the ranking. I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list; describe positions and results by their content and argue from there.
