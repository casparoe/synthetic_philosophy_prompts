I'm the grad-student member on the acquisitions board for a small university press series about non-human history — germs, droughts, volcanoes, crops, animals, the whole guns-germs-and-steel genre, except written by people who are deeply allergic to that genre. The series editor (I'll call her H.) has final say, but the board votes first and she almost never goes against us, so I've been keeping a log of her verdicts on submissions to try and work out what she thinks the series is actually for. So far, six decisions:

She took a book on the Black Death and western European wages — the argument being that the demographic shock raised real wages only where towns were dense and customary tenure weak, so lords' attempts to freeze wages and re-impose labour services failed, while in regions where peasants had nowhere to run the same mortality produced tighter repression instead; it had a chapter on Mamluk Egypt and one on the Low Countries explaining why they diverged. She called it the model for the series. She rejected a manuscript tracing a chain from the Laki eruption in Iceland to failed harvests in Europe to famine to fiscal crisis to the Revolution of 1789, on the grounds that the book ends up making the revolution look like weather with a timetable; her note in the margins was that a volcano does not summon the Estates-General. She accepted with major revisions a study of yellow fever in the French expedition to Saint-Domingue and the Haitian Revolution, but made the author add a comparison with the British expedition a decade earlier — same disease, different outcome, so what is the disease actually explaining — plus a chapter on what the rebel leadership's own decisions contributed. She rejected a Little Ice Age book on the seventeenth-century crisis where every chapter, on Ming China, on England, on the Ottoman provinces, opened with the same hemispheric proxy curve; her comment was that the humans in it are weather vanes. She accepted an Irish Famine book that is maybe eighty percent about relief policy, the poor law, continued food exports, and the ideology of providentialism and political economy, with the blight essentially as the trigger — she praised it, though she grumbled that the pathogen chapters were one paragraph of biology and six of adjectives. And she rejected a book on the 536 dust veil and the Justinianic plague as the explanation for the unraveling of late antiquity, saying a synchronism is not a mechanism and that the author had given a decade of bad weather a plan.

Next month's submission is a Maya lowlands book. The author's argument is that the run of severe ninth-century droughts in the lake-core record didn't kill anyone directly or destroy the agrarian base outright — it destroyed the credibility of divine kingship, since the ruler's legitimacy rested on his ability to guarantee rain and cosmic order through ritual, and once the rituals visibly failed, the whole apparatus lost its purchase. She's extremely careful: she shows why the northern cities with cenotes and coastal trade weathered the same drought while the southern ones that responded by doubling down on monuments, ritual expenditure, and capture warfare fell apart; she has a whole chapter arguing that climate is not an actor and shouldn't be written as one; she insists she is not a determinist. But she also writes, roughly, that the drought explains nothing on its own, and that if you deleted it from the record the political logic of the collapse would be unchanged in kind, only in its timing — the drought set the date, not the outcome. So: predict H.'s verdict and how confident you are. Then do the harder part — reconstruct the rule she's running, keeping in mind that I think at least three different rules fit all six precedents equally well (something about mechanism and mediation, something about explaining variation between cases rather than being present in all of them, something about refusing to make outcomes inevitable, maybe something about how much explanatory weight the non-human thing is allowed to carry). Say what each of those predicts for the Maya book, which one you'd bet on and why, and which of the six is the closest analog and whether that helps or muddies it. Finally: what should I vote if I want us to be consistent with ourselves, and what principle would we be adopting either way? There's something uncomfortable here I can't quite articulate — the series exists to take non-human causes seriously, and we might be about to reject the single most anti-determinist manuscript we've ever seen for under-crediting a drought. And tell me what further submission would discriminate between your candidate rules; I was thinking of proposing a hypothetical one, a single-case narrative arguing that smallpox in 1520 made the fall of Tenochtitlan possible and that without it the outcome is unthinkable, but with no institutional mechanism and no comparison anywhere — does that come out accepted or rejected on each of your rules, and does your answer survive it?
