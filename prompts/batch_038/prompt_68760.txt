Writing a talk for a small philosophy-and-literature group on Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day*, and I want to do it against the lying vs. misleading debate — but not the obvious version. The obvious version is "Stevens is an unreliable narrator, so he lies to the reader," and I think the interesting claim is nearly the opposite: he very rarely asserts anything false. The deception lives almost entirely in what he implies, what he declines to correct, and what he lets other people fill in for themselves.

The scenes I'd build on: Moscombe, where he lets the Taylors go on assuming he's a gentleman guest rather than the butler of the house they're describing to him; the 1923 conference with his father dying upstairs while he keeps serving; the two Jewish housemaids, which he eventually concedes happened and then files under something like "entirely insignificant"; and the evening Miss Kenton tells him about a marriage proposal while he's got the Ribbentrop business running and he brushes her off. If I've muddled a detail, say so rather than playing along.

So run the standard machinery over it: lying as asserting what you believe to be false, versus misleading by means of literally true speech, plus whatever the old casuists thought they were doing with mental reservation — Stevens reads like a textbook practitioner of broad mental reservation, with "dignity" serving as his professional licence to equivocate. Which side of the line does each of those scenes fall on, and does the book endorse the stronger thesis that misleading isn't the lesser wrong but the greater one, since it offloads the inferential work onto the hearer and then lets you disown the outcome?

The wrinkle I want you to actually wrestle with, because I've never seen it stated: in Stevens's case the hearer who does the inferential work and gets stuck with the result is his own later self. Call it deferred misleading — an utterance whose deceived party is the speaker, decades on. Does the intentionalist analysis have any grip on that? It's indexed to what the speaker believes at the time and what the hearer comes to believe right after, and Stevens arguably has no determinate belief either end of the way, which would mean the whole lying/misleading distinction runs out rather than picking a verdict.

Then tell me where the reading breaks — where Ishiguro is doing something the deception literature just has no vocabulary for, and where you'd be straining to make the novel fit my thesis. End with what the book adds to the argument that an argument alone wouldn't give me, and commit to whether you think Stevens is worse than an outright liar or better. No quotes and no reading list, please — I can't check either and neither can you.
