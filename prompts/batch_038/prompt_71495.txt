I constructed the following argument for a graduate seminar on Bayesian epistemology, and I now believe it must fail somewhere, because its conclusion is one that nobody in the room — myself included — can accept. I would like a diagnosis rather than a rebuttal of the conclusion.

**Setup.** My co-author and I are assessing whether a particular tax change will raise national output by more than two percent within five years. We began from the same initial credence. Each of us has since seen evidence the other has not — she has interviewed industry contacts, I have read the relevant econometric literature — and we each update by conditionalization. We have exchanged our current probability distributions in full, honestly, and on more than one occasion; we have also exchanged summaries of the evidence behind them. We each regard the other as a careful reasoner with no incentive to posture. Our credences nonetheless remain a noticeable distance apart, and further rounds of disclosure have not closed the gap.

**The argument.**

1. Aumann's agreement theorem states that if two agents have a common prior over the relevant space and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, then those posteriors are equal.
2. The situation above satisfies the theorem's hypotheses as nearly as any real disagreement can: a shared starting point, correct updating, complete disclosure of one another's opinions, and mutual awareness that the other is reasoning properly.
3. Therefore, if the two credences still differ, at least one of the hypotheses must fail.
4. But step 2 establishes that they all hold; so the failure can only lie in the shared prior — our initial credences must in fact have differed.
5. Priors can differ rationally only where they encode information, and prior to anyone's having private information there is nothing for two agents' priors to differ about; hence rational agents necessarily begin from the same prior.
6. So if our priors differed, at least one of us is not fully rational.
7. Consequently, a transparent and well-conducted disagreement that persists is impossible: any disagreement surviving an honest exchange of opinions shows that someone party to it is irrational or concealing something.
8. Generalizing, the persistence of good-faith disagreement on any contested question is itself evidence that at least one disputant is defective; and since neither disputant has privileged access to whose defect it is, each ought to move halfway toward the other.
9. Iterated across a community of peers, these halfway moves converge on a single credence held by everyone, so reasonable opinion on a contested question is already fixed by the activity of comparing notes.
10. Therefore no distinctive view on a long-disputed question is reasonable, and further inquiry into such questions cannot alter anyone's rational credence.

I should say where my own sympathies lie. I want to keep step 1: I trust the theorem, and I will not be satisfied by a diagnosis that amounts to "the theorem concerns idealized agents, so nothing follows for us." I also want to keep the intuition that two careful people can genuinely and reasonably disagree. I have a vague sense that the trouble falls somewhere between steps 2 and 5, or perhaps at step 8, but I cannot pin it down.

Please address the following:

1. Identify the earliest step at which the argument goes wrong, and say whether the argument is invalid or valid with a false premise.
2. For each of the two or three steps a respondent might plausibly reject, explain why that step looks innocent in context — what it would take for it to be true, and how close the setup comes to supplying that.
3. State precisely which idealization step 2 smuggles into the description of our exchange. I am not looking for the generic observation that real agents are not logically omniscient; I want to know which specific condition the theorem requires that our several rounds of full disclosure fail to generate, and why repeated honest disclosure does not automatically generate it.
4. Once that condition is removed, say what the theorem still licenses for imperfectly informed agents in cases like ours — whether it retains any normative bite at all, or becomes inert.
5. Assess step 5 independently of the rest: can rational agents differ in their priors, and what turns on the answer for steps 4 through 6?
6. Say whether the move to the midpoint in step 8 derives any support from the theorem or is imported from an entirely separate thesis about peer disagreement, and if the latter, name what work it is being made to do.
7. Price each available repair. If I reject step 4, what am I giving up? If I reject step 5? If I restrict step 2 to a class of exchanges far narrower than the one described? I would like to know which concession is cheapest and which is most damaging, not merely which steps are false.
