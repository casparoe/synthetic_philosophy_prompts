"A money pump works on an agent whose preferences go around in a circle. Suppose I prefer A to B, B to C, and C to A, and suppose I start out holding B. Someone offers me A in exchange for B plus a penny, and I accept, since A is what I'd rather have of the two. Then they offer me C for my A plus a penny, which I also accept, and then B for my C plus a penny, which I accept too. At the end I'm holding exactly what I started with and I'm three pennies poorer. Each of the trades looked fine on its own, and that is the point: the argument doesn't rely on the agent making any choice they'd criticise at the time, it relies on the fact that the choices add up to something they would not have chosen all together. The lesson people draw is that an ordering which permits cycles is defective, which is why rational choice theory requires transitivity, or at least acyclicity.

A money pump and a Dutch book are really the same argument run at different speeds. A Dutch book is a set of bets, priced off an agent's betting quotients, that together guarantee a loss; a money pump is a set of trades that together guarantee a loss too, except that the agent doesn't see it coming until the cycle closes. So both are arguments about incoherent degrees of belief, and both work by adding up things that look acceptable one at a time.

The diachronic version targets someone who learns evidence but doesn't update by conditonalizing. Because you know in advance roughly how they'll respond, you can sell them a bet now that their current credences make attractive, and then, once the evidence is in, sell them further bets that their new credences make attractive, with the package arranged so the total is a loss however the evidence turns out. The same reasoning shows the agent is irrational, and it doesn't matter whether anyone could have predicted their updating in advance — the loss comes from the bets themselves, not from the prediction.

The obvious objection to the pump is foresight. If the agent can see the whole sequence coming they simply refuse the first trade, and the cycle never starts. That objection is quite strong, and I think it shows the pump cannot be an argument about the rationality of preferences at all, only about the rationality of agents who are too myopic to see what's coming at them. A fully informed agent with the very same cyclic ranking is immune, so the defect was never in the ranking.

Finally, the diachronic strategy suggests that a rational agent's preferences have to stay stable over time. If what you want can shift around, there is always a sequence of small trades that will exploit the shifts, so a pump-proof agent has to keep wanting the same things. That seems a real cost of the argument: it rules out ordinary changes of mind, like coming to enjoy a wine you used to dislike, unless you can show that those changes track new information in some way the argument doesn't cover."
