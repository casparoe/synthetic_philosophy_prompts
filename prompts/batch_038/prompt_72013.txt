ok so I'm a senior in a philosophy of medicine seminar and our prof does this thing where everyone in the class writes a mock final question for the rest of us and we sit them under real conditions — 45 minutes, closed book, no laptops. Then we mark each other's answers against whatever rubric the question-writer supplied. It's brutal but it's honestly the only reason I understand any of this material. I drew the death-definition week and I want to write the hardest fair question I can, so I need the question plus a model answer plus the marking scheme.

What we actually covered, so you know what's in scope: the UDDA's Section 1 — roughly, that an individual who has sustained either irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions or irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain including the brain stem is dead, with the determination made in accordance with accepted medical standards; the 1968 Harvard committee report on irreversible coma; the 1981 President's Commission report that rejected the higher-brain account in favour of whole-brain; the UK going the other direction with a brain-stem-only formulation; the Maastricht categories for donation after circulatory death, including category III (controlled — ICU patient, treatment withdrawn, arrest awaited); and the stand-off period, which in our readings was usually somewhere in the 5–10 minute range.

Here's the wrinkle I want the question to turn on. Stage it as a case like this: a category III DCD. Life support is withdrawn in the OR, arrest occured, the team waits out a five-minute stand-off, confirms absence of pulse and of spontaneous respiration, and declares death on circulatory grounds. Then instead of a cold flush they cannulate and start warm regional perfusion of the abdominal organs — an ECMO circuit restoring warm oxygenated blood flow to the liver, kidneys and gut. To keep that perfusate out of the head, the surgeon first clamps or ligates the aortic arch vessels. So for the next hour the body has circulating oxygenated blood below the diaphragm and no blood reaching the brain at all.

The question I want is one that makes people say what would have to be true for that declaration of death to have been correct, and then work out whether clamping the arch vessels is compatible with it. The crux should be the word irreversible. The obvious defence is that what matters isn't whether circulation could be restored by some intervention, it's whether it will be: nobody is going to try to resuscitate this patient, the decision has been made and the family has consented, so cessation is permanent, and permanence is what the statute is actually reaching for. I want them to have to decide whether that defence survives the case above, where the retrieval team itself restores circulation to most of the body minutes later and then takes deliberate steps to ensure the brain gets none. If permanence is doing the work, the clamping looks like it's manufacturing the very condition — no brain function, and none returning — that the declaration was supposed to have already detected. If irreversibility is doing the work in the strict sense, then arguably the patient simply wasn't dead at five minutes.

They should also be forced to pick between two readings of what's going on: is death here a biological fact we discover, or a partly normative status we confer — and if it's the latter, what stops that sliding into death being whatever the transplant protocol says it is. Then a seperate short part worth a few marks: does shortening the stand-off from five minutes to sixty seconds, on the grounds that spontaneous autoresuscitation after a minute essentially never happens, change the analysis at all, or does it just make the same move cheaper?

Deliverables:

1. The question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the mark allocation visible, sized for 45 minutes.
2. A model answer of roughly 800 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves a first-class answer makes and what a merely competent one does. I want entries like states the permanence reading and identifies that it makes the truth of the death declaration depend on the retrieval team's own intentions — 4 marks, not discusses irreversibility — 4 marks. For each point also give the wrong thing people reliably write, since we mark each other and I don't want to spend twenty minutes arguing about whether someone deserved the mark.

Two constraints. Closed book means nobody will have read anything beyond what I listed, so don't build a question whose answer only works if you've read some particular paper, and don't name-drop authors or tack a reading list onto the end — if you think some philosopher's view matters here, describe the view instead of citing it. And second: commit. I don't want a model answer that lays out both readings and concludes the issue is genuinely difficult, because that's useless for marking. Tell me which side you think is right, argue for it, and then add a short note at the end saying where your own line is most exposed — I'll use that to decide how much credit to give anyone who attacks it well.
