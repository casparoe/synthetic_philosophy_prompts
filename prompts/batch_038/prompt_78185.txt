You are a specialist in eighteenth-century political economy and in the modern literature on famine causation, and you are helping me prepare a graduate seminar whose organizing question is whether famines should be classified as natural disasters or as political crimes. The following is a passage from the Preface to the first edition (1798, published anonymously) of Malthus's *An Essay on the Principle of Population*:

> "It is an obvious truth, which has been taken notice of by many writers, that population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence; but no writer, that the Author recollects, has inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected: and it is a view of these means, which forms, to his mind, the strongest obstacle in the way to any very great future improvement of society. He hopes it will appear that, in the discussion of this interesting subject, he is actuated solely by a love of truth; and not by any prejudices against any particular set of men, or of opinions. He professes to have read some of the speculations on the future improvement of society, in a temper very different from a wish to find them visionary; but he has not acquired that command over his understanding which would enable him to believe what he wishes, without evidence, or to refuse his assent to what might be unpleasing, when accompanied with evidence.
>
> The view which he has given of human life has a melancholy hue; but he feels conscious, that he has drawn these dark tints, from a conviction that they are really in the picture; and not from a jaundiced eye or an inherent spleen of disposition."

Please think carefully before answering, and open with a brief brainstorm of the range of things this passage could be doing — do not go straight to your preferred interpretation. Then work through the following.

1. Give a close paraphrase in modern prose, and explain the clauses my students reliably misread: what "in a temper very different from a wish to find them visionary" comes to, and what "prejudices against any particular set of men, or of opinions" adds beyond a bare denial of partisanship.

2. Analyze the grammar of "population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence". The verb is passive and the agent unexpressed: does the sentence attribute the keeping-down to nature, to necessity, or does it deliberately leave the agent open? How much turns on that?

3. A colleague has proposed what I will call the feasibility reading: that the key sentence states a constraint any social arrangement must satisfy rather than a natural mechanism, and that because Malthus says nobody has "inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected", the passage is entirely compatible with those means being political and economic — so it cannot be recruited, as it so often is, to cast starvation as a calamity authored by nobody. My colleague adds that the "dark tints" and "jaundiced eye" sentences are methodological rather than moral: Malthus is claiming to have described a constraint, not to have endorsed it. Assess whether the passage sustains this reading. Say what in it supports the reading, what resists it, and what Malthus would have to establish elsewhere in the book for the natural-disaster interpretation to be his own rather than his readers'.

4. Finally, consider whether the protestation of pure evidential motivation ("actuated solely by a love of truth"; the inability to "refuse his assent to what might be unpleasing") functions as argument or as inoculation — that is, whether it is offered as support for his conclusions or as a pre-emptive disqualification of anyone who suspects the conclusions of being ideologically welcome. Explain what difference it makes to the seminar's central question if it is the latter.

Do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations: I want your reasoning about this text, not references. Please write your answer in British English, and be willing to reach a definite view on the feasibility reading rather than suspending judgment.
