I run a two-person mediation practice handling roughly four partnership break-ups a month — family businesses, a shared workshop, a jointly owned rental building — where the pair has to divide assets and future income inside a single three-hour session, with no lawyers present and nobody on call who knows any mathematics. Whatever we land on, I write up on one page and both sides initial it, so I need a rule I can execute in the room, not a theory to admire.

Years ago I came across the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution and it has stayed with me: fix what each side ends up with if they walk away, fix each side's aspiration — the most they could possibly hope for out of the arrangement — and then settle at the furthest point along the line joining those two that the pair can jointly reach, so both sides have conceded the same fraction of what they were reaching for. My current practice is cruder: I ask each side separately for their bottom line and their target and propose something between, which reliably hands the deal to whoever has the stronger outside option.

Please turn that idea into a procedure I can run with two non-experts sitting across from each other: the questions in the order to ask them, what counts as a settled answer to each, how to get non-monetary items (the firm's name, the bench space, the client list, who keeps the apprentice) onto the same scale as money, and the points where the procedure should stop and hand the call back to me or to the parties because the question is one of values rather than arithmetic.

There is one thing I never resolved on my own: is a side's aspiration the most they could extract from the joint arrangement if it went entirely their way, or the most they could manage without the other person at all? Choose one, tell me what turns on the choice, and build the procedure around it.

Then run it on two cases. First, a brother and sister dissolving a catering business worth about $400,000 as a going concern: alone, the brother could clear roughly $150,000 a year, while the sister, who does all the cooking and has no other trade, could earn perhaps $30,000 elsewhere; if they walk away and litigate, the business fetches maybe $120,000 and fees consume most of that. Second, two former partners splitting a furniture workshop where nothing is for sale — one wants the name they built, the other wants the shop, the tools, and the apprentice. I expect at least one of these to come out in a way I will find hard to accept; state that result plainly instead of softening it.

Finish with what the procedure cannot do: where it misfires, which of its steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of arithmetic, and what I should do instead when one of those steps is reached.
