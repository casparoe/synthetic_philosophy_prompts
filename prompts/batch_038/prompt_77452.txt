I TA a senior seminar on moral motivation and I'm building the worksheet for week 6, the moral worth week. We've already burned through the stock cases in section — Kant's honest shopkeeper, the naturally warm person who helps because they like helping, Huck deciding not to write the letter, the burned-out person who keeps doing the right thing purely from duty — and half my students are pre-law so they keep dragging everything back to mens rea, which is not helping me. What I need is one fresh case that forces them to choose between the two accounts of moral worth that disagree about whether knowledge is required, and I want it engineered rather than stumbled into. Quick recap so we're on the same page. Smith-style account: an act has moral worth only if it's the act that's right for the agent to do, the agent does it for the reason that makes it right, and the agent knows both of those things — knows the act is right and knows that it's right for that reason. Arpaly/Markovits-style account: drop the knowledge clause; what matters is that the agent is actually moved by the features that make the act right and that her being so moved isn't a fluke, so she can have no correct moral beliefs at all, or even think she's doing something rotten, and still act with moral worth. The two are supposed to part company on inverted-belief cases, but every example anyone reaches for is Huck and Jim and my students have seen that one dissected six ways already.

So build me a case where they come apart, to these specs. (1) The act is right, and right on any plausible normative theory — I don't want a fight about rightness contaminating the fight about worth. (2) The agent is moved by exactly the feature or features that make it right. (3) She has no knowledge, and not even a true belief, that what she's doing is right; her explicit moral view of the situation is inverted, so the Smith knowledge clause fails cleanly rather than marginally. (4) She cant get the belief from anyone either — nobody in her vicinity has it — so you can't rescue her knowledge by way of testimony. (5) She neither enjoys it nor thinks well of herself for it, because the second enjoyment shows up my students relitigate Kant. (6) Setting is contemporary, ordinary, and low stakes: no trolleys, no life and death, nothing about slavery or abolition, something that could plausibly happen on a Tuesday afternoon. (7) She's neither saint nor monster, and she has at least one genuinely unlovely motive in the mix, but not so many that it becomes debatable whether she acted from the right-making feature at all — the divergence has to survive contact with the details. Scenario itself: two paragraphs max, and no philosopher names inside the story.

Then show me the case works instead of just asserting that it does. Walk the Smith-style account through condition by condition and say exactly which one fails and why; walk the no-knowledge account through and show how non-accidentality is secured, which means naming a few nearby variations on the situation and telling me what she does in each. Then say which single feature of the case is doing the work — I'd guess it's (3) or (4), but argue it, don't just agree with me. Then two stress tests: if I let her have the true belief without the knowledge, does the split survive or does it get weird? and if I delete the unlovely mixed motive, do the two accounts converge? Finish with the best attack a Smith-style defender can mount, especially the charge that my case smuggles the knowledge back in — that being non-accidentally moved by the right-making feature just is knowledge under another name, or that she's been given a de re grasp of the reason that does the work the de dicto belief was supposed to do. If you think that charge lands and the case can't be built to spec, tell me so and say which spec to relax; I'd much rather hear it now than hand out a worksheet that falls apart in section. The whole point is that the verdicts have to come out of the two accounts as stated and not out of how I've described her feelings, so if you catch yourself stipulating a verdict rather than deriving one, flag it.
