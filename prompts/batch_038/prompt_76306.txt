In my normative ethics seminar we are working through the demandingness objection, and in section a classmate offered a response I want to test on you. His claim is that a duty to aid the global poor is structured like a liability to pay taxes in a cooperative scheme: there is a total that needs to be raised, an assessment fixes your share of it, and your bill does not grow merely because others fail to pay theirs. On that picture consequentialism is not devastatingly demanding after all — you owe what an ideal scheme would have levied on you, and the shortfall created by everyone else's default is not yours to absorb. He said this is how to reconcile the intuition behind the demandingness objection with a wholly impersonal account of the good, without conceding any agent-relative prerogatives.

Could you audit the tax analogy for me? I would like the mapping stated explicitly — what plays the role of the scheme, the assessing authority, the taxpayer, the public good, full versus partial compliance — and then the points where the mapping breaks. Which of those failures are fatal to the intended use, and which are harmless? I am especially unsure about three things: whether the absence of any actual collecting agency matters, whether a fair share is determinable at all without one, and whether the analogy is carrying the argument or merely illustrating a prior principle about partial compliance that would need independent defence. Finally, does the moderate conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the tax picture cannot carry it, is there a better analogy that could?
