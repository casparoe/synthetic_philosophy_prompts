Every other week I take the ethics pager for the county hospital across town — the philosophy department has this arrangement with them and I'm the "ethics resource," which in practice means a resident or a social worker calls me at eleven at night to ask whether they can put a tube in somebody. A lot of these are advance-directive calls, and I've had to admit to myself that I don't actually have a procedure for them. I have intuitions and a reading list, and neither one is any use to a tired ICU fellow on the phone.

So write me a decision procedure I could hand to that fellow — or to a social worker, somebody with no philosophy — and have them run it in about twenty minutes on a directive conflict. Ground it in what's actually load-bearing here: the earlier-self-versus-now-self problem; Dworkin's distinction between interests in how a particular moment feels and interests in how a whole life goes, and the argument that the earlier self gets to bind the later one; and the counterargument, which I think is stronger than I used to, that we simply don't know what its like to be the patient in the bed now, so we shouldn't be so sure what she'd want. If you think the honest answer is graded rather than binary — that how much a directive counts for depends on how much the person in the bed is still psychologically the person who wrote it — then build that in as a real step with a real test, not a footnote, and tell me what evidence obtainable at 11 p.m. on a night shift could possibly bear on it.

Here's the wrinkle I keep hitting that I want handled explicitly. I think there are two different things a directive can be and the whole debate talks as if there were one: an instruction ("do not intubate me") and a piece of testimony about why the person thought that ("because I watched my father spend two years unable to swallow and I don't want my kids watching that with me"). When the testimony is there and the stated reason no longer applies — the patient isn't suffering, isn't watching anything, seems genuinely okay — does the instruction still bind? Give me a step for this and tell me straight which way you came out, because I lean hard toward the instruction binding regardless and I want to know if your procedure has to overrule me.

Then the mechanics. Questions in order; for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as "we don't know, so escalate"; where the procedure stops at a flat no; where it hands the decision to a human because the question is about values rather than facts — and *which* human, since at that hour it's a covering attending who hasn't read the chart; what gets written in the note; and what to do with a directive that's thirty years old, or five words long, or contradicted by something the patient said last month while arguably still competent. Also one line on where you send the calls where there's no directive at all, which is honestly half of them. Don't give me a bibliography, I know the literature, I want the machinery.

Then run it on three cases. One: a 79-year-old with moderate dementia whose directive refuses antibiotics for pneumonia; he's cheerful, sings along to the radio, asks for ice cream three times a day; his daughter wants him treated and says her father is "not the man who wrote this." Two: a woman in her forties with Huntington's whose directive says no feeding tube once she stops recognizing her children; she still recognizes them, but she's stopped eating enough and losing weight fast, and when asked she says "I'm not hungry" and ten minutes later says "when can I go home." Three: a man who wrote what the psychiatrists call a Ulysses contract — during a stable stretch he directed that he be given a particular medication if he became manic and refused; he is now manic, refusing, arguing articulately that he's fine, and asking to revoke the directive. For that last one the procedure has to answer the revocation question head-on: can the self at the mast cut the ropes?

Finish with where this thing will misfire. Not the hard cases it struggles with — the systematic failures, and the spots where it will generate a defensible-sounding answer that's really just the caller's gut wearing a flowchart.
