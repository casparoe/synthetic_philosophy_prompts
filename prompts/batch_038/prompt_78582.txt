I am drafting the specification for a priority-setting procedure that ranks public health interventions by expected welfare gain per person affected, and before anyone defends it I want to know what it is entitled to assume about comparing one person's utility with another's. Write the desiderata: what any adequate account of interpersonal utility comparison must deliver in order to underwrite that use, with the case or result behind each requirement.

The list should confront at least the following. Why cardinal utilities fixed only up to a positive affine transformation for each person separately leave cross-person comparisons underdetermined, and what a non-arbitrary common origin and common unit would have to consist in rather than merely be stipulated. How the account is to treat intensity reports from people whose baselines differ through adaptation or long deprivation, so that equal numbers do not silently mean equal welfare. And the gap between saying what makes a comparison true and saying how a committee that can introspect neither subject could come to know it — an account that collapses the second into the first has not answered the question I am asking.

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable given the intended use and which are really the metaphysical preferences of a particular camp, and identify any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied; if the specification turns out to be incoherent, that is the result I most want. Close with the single requirement that rules out the largest number of candidate accounts. Please write in American English, and include no citations or reading list.
