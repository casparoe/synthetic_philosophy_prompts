I'm a fourth-year PhD student in history, and this term I hold one of the two graduate seats on my university library's Special Collections Acquisitions and Access Committee. In three weeks we rule on a gift that has already been physically deposited in our building, and members have been asked to circulate short written opinions in advance. I would like you to draft mine.

The case. An estate has offered us the papers of a man who served from 1938 to 1969 as an agricultural extension officer in a colonial plantation district: roughly twenty-two linear feet, mostly his diaries, correspondence with the colonial department of agriculture, crop-yield tables, and photographs. What has drawn attention is a subset — eleven bound volumes of field notebooks in which the officer, or a clerk he employed, wrote down in summary form and in his own orthography what local laborers said to him: grievances about wages and rations, complaints about foremen, accounts of illness, requests for days off for funerals, a handful of songs. So far as anyone can establish, these notebooks are the only written trace of any of those individuals by name. There is no mission register, no labor bureau file, no family papers; I have asked colleagues who work on the region.

Two complications, one procedural and one physical. Procedurally, the deed of gift requires that the entire collection be digitized and posted to an unrestricted open reading room within twenty-four months of acceptance, with no series withheld, and provides that if we fail to do so the estate may reclaim the materials and place them elsewhere. Our counsel reads that clause as enforceable, and the development office is enthusiastic about the family. Physically, the notebooks are interleaved in the same bound volumes as the officer's diary entries and marginal commentary. The laborers' reported speech sits inside his handwriting, in his volumes, in the same ink, often inside the same sentence — something like "N. came again today asking two days for a burial; the whole district is sullen" (my illustration, not a transcription). We cannot separate one voice from the other without destroying the volumes, and even a careful transcription into a separate digital object would be a new artifact made by us out of his hand.

The committee is split three ways. One faction says accept and comply: the materials survive at all only because a man of the officer's class kept them, and if we refuse they go to a dealer and into a private collection; a descendant who wants her great-grandfather's name should not have to ask a librarian's permission first. A second says accept but refuse the open-access condition and go back to the estate even at the risk of losing the gift: publishing these names in the officer's idiom — malingerer, sullen, troublemaker — attaches those people permanently to the only description of them history produced, and a descendant searching an ancestor will find, first and forever, a foreman's characterization of him. A third, smaller faction says decline outright: the evidentiary value is inseparable from the harm of the form.

A fourth question cuts across all three. Our library has a standing Descriptive Practice Statement, adopted some years ago, committing us to describing "the material as it presents itself" and to keeping interpretive apparatus out of finding aids; the intent was to stop cataloguers moralizing over collections. One colleague argues that a prominent contextual note — stating that every word attributed to a laborer in these volumes reaches us through the officer's hand, spelling, summarization, and purposes — violates that statement and tells researchers how to read before they have read anything. Another argues that omitting the note launders the officer's framing by making his summary look like a record.

What I want is the memo itself: roughly 900 to 1,200 words, addressed to a body that includes the university's general counsel, the head of development, two faculty members from unrelated departments, and the other graduate representative, none of whom are archivists. It should (a) recommend one of the three options — accept and comply, accept and renegotiate, or decline — with conditions attached and a concrete sequence of steps and deadlines keyed to the twenty-four-month clause; (b) give reasons a dissenting member could actually answer, rather than a recitation of professional norms; (c) take up the strongest case on the losing side and say precisely what I concede to it and what I do not; and (d) settle the Descriptive Practice Statement question, because whatever we decide about access we will also have to decide how the collection is described.

Two further constraints. I don't want a literature review, and please give me no citations, footnotes, or bibliography — I cannot verify them and my colleagues will not read them; I want the argument, not the apparatus. But I do want the memo to be honest about the cost of its own recommendation: on any of these courses some of these people's names will be fixed in someone else's words, and I would rather the document state what we are choosing to live with than pretend the dilemma dissolves. Finally, if you think my three options are the wrong menu — that there is a fourth course genuinely available to us given the binding, the deed, and the clock — say so and make that your recommendation instead.
