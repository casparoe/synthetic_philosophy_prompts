**The question as set (50 points of a 100-point take-home).** *The paradigm below was designed for this examination. Evaluate it strictly as described.*

In a blocked sequence, a red disc is flashed for 40 ms to the left of fixation and a green disc for 40 ms to the right, with an onset-to-onset interval of 200 ms, at a spacing that reliably yields apparent motion. Subjects report what is reported in the standard color-change version of the phi phenomenon: a single disc travelling from left to right and changing colour somewhere near the middle of its path. On probe trials a brief tone sounds at a time *t* chosen at random within and around the interval, and the subject must indicate, within 500 ms of tone onset, the colour the travelling disc appeared to have at the instant of the tone. The colour order and the interval are constant across the block. Results: for tones earlier than 150 ms before the onset of the green disc, "red" responses exceed 95 per cent. For tones falling in the final 120 ms before the green disc appears — that is, at a moment when the green disc has not yet been presented — "green" responses rise to 71 per cent, the remainder "red". On control trials in which no tone sounds, end-of-trial unprompted reports describe a mid-path colour change, as usual.

(i) [15] State the view Dennett calls Cartesian materialism and explain what the multiple drafts model proposes in its place.
(ii) [20] Do the probe data distinguish the Stalinesque from the Orwellian account of the colour-phi result? Argue your answer.
(iii) [15] What do the data, if anything, establish about whether there is a determinate fact as to when a given content becomes conscious?

**The student's answer.**

(i) Cartesian materialism, as Dennett uses the term, is the view that somewhere in the brain there is a finish line: a place and a time at which neural processing crosses over into experience, such that the order in which things arrive at that point is the order in which they are presented to the subject. The controlling image is a theatre, a screen on which the show is mounted for an inner audience. The multiple drafts model denies the finish line. Across many parallel channels the brain produces partial and provisional interpretations of the same input; these are revised, superseded and elaborated, and none of them is ever finalized for an audience. What matters is not presentation to a viewer but the downstream effects of a discrimination: as soon as a discrimination is accomplished it becomes available to guide behaviour and to be written into memory, and Dennett makes such writing criterial for consciousness. This is why the regress of inner observers is not vicious — the work is distributed among processes each simpler than the whole, none of which needs to be conscious in its own right.

It is worth being precise about what replaces the theatre, since the model is often caricatured as pure flux. What makes a content conscious rather than merely processed is its entry into the single running narrative the system produces of itself, the stream maintained by the speech and memory systems and available for report. A content becomes conscious when the system takes it as the object of a higher-order representation; because that higher-order representation is itself just another draft, editable like any other, no homunculus is required. There is thus still a determinate order in which contents enter the narrative. What is denied is only that this order coincides with the arrival order at any single anatomical station.

(ii) The two accounts of the colour-phi result are the Stalinesque and the Orwellian. On the Stalinesque account the brain waits, reconciles the two flashes, and delivers the finished episode — motion with a mid-path colour change — all at once, so that experience is delayed relative to the stimuli. On the Orwellian account the subject first experiences the red disc and the record is afterwards rewritten to include the change en route. Dennett and Kinsbourne argue that where the two accounts agree about every possible behavioural output there is no fact of the matter which is correct; both, on their telling, presuppose a finish line and differ only about which side of it a given neural event falls on.

The probe data break that tie. The tones interrogate the system during the interval rather than only at the end of the trial, and the responses vary systematically with tone time. In the 120 ms before the green disc appears, subjects say "green" on 71 per cent of trials: the content is already composed and available to be interrogated before its last physical ingredient has arrived. That is what the Stalinesque account predicts, and what the Orwellian account cannot allow, since on the Orwellian account there is not yet anything for the rewriting to operate on. The data therefore favour the Stalinesque account.

(iii) More importantly, they show that content fixation has a measurable time. The transition occurs somewhere within the 120 ms window, and the width of that window bounds the delay between the second stimulus and the completion of the episode; with a finer sampling of tone times one could locate fixation to within tens of milliseconds. The claim that there is no determinate fact as to when a content becomes conscious was therefore an artefact of relying on unprompted end-of-trial reports, which sample the system only once. Where an interrogating probe is available, the timing of the narrative is readable from the probes, and the underdetermination argument fails as an empirical matter.
