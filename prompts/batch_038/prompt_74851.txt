I'm a fourth-year mathematics student, and this semester I've been auditing my department's graduate population-ethics seminar with the instructor's permission. Two of the regulars — Nadia, a philosophy PhD student working on infinite ethics, and Eli, an economics PhD student with a taste for formal theory — got into a sustained argument after the second session, which was about whether an infinity of future persons swamps everything we can do now. I took notes at the time and wrote the exchange up afterwards as faithfully as I could; because I knew I'd want a second opinion, I also filled in the formal setup that both of them explicitly accepted, so that each step can actually be checked rather than just admired. The room ended up split, and I came away unable to say which of them had the better of it.

The stipulated framework: a "world" assigns a real welfare level to each member of a fixed population indexed by the integers, and comparisons are always between worlds with the same index set. Let A be the world in which every person is at level 10, and A⁺ the world in which every person is at level 10 except person 7, who is at level 11. Separately, for each integer k, let W_k be the world in which person i is at level 1 if i ≥ k and at level 0 otherwise.

Here is the exchange as I recorded it:

(1) Eli opened by claiming that A and A⁺ are exactly equally good. Both worlds sum to a divergent infinity, and in any case ℵ₀ + 1 = ℵ₀; there is no arithmetic on offer in which adding one unit to an infinite total yields more. He drew the consequence he thought mattered: if the future population is infinite, then no finite improvement to it — relieving the suffering of a million people, say — changes the value of the world, so the aggregative picture implies that nothing we can do makes the world better, and anyone who denies that owes us a way of summing.

(2) Nadia said the model was wrong, not the arithmetic. The right principle is Pareto: if everyone in X is at least as well off as in Y and at least one person is strictly better off, X is better. By Pareto, A⁺ is better than A. She added that "both are infinite" is not a comparison at all, since neither sum converges, so there is no quantity to weigh against anything; Eli was treating a divergent series as though it evaluated to a value. And cardinality is simply the wrong measure, because it treats persons as fungible units when what is morally relevant is how each of them fares.

(3) Eli replied that Pareto is not free. Add permutation invariance — he called it extensionality — the principle that a world's value depends only on the pattern of welfare levels across the population, so that relabeling persons cannot change how good a world is. Now compare W_k and W_{k+1}. By Pareto, W_k is better: person k is at 1 rather than 0 and nobody else differs. But the map i ↦ i+1 is a bijection of the index set onto itself, and applying it to W_k produces W_{k+1} term for term — the same pattern of levels, differently labeled. So permutation invariance says they are equally good. Contradiction. Nadia can keep Pareto or keep label-independence, not both, and if she drops the latter she owes an account of why the numbering of persons carries moral weight.

(4) Nadia said: drop it. Persons are not slots in a structure. There is no fact that makes person k in W_k the same individual as person k+1 in W_{k+1}; the shift map does not redescribe one world, it compares two numerically distinct populations, and the difference between *these* people and *those* people is precisely where the moral information lives. Permutation invariance, on her account, is a convenience imported from set theory, not a moral principle, and it should go first.

(5) Eli said she had missed a third option that dissolves the dilemma without any metaphysics: compare worlds by their differences. For two worlds over the same index set, form the person-by-person difference in welfare, and where that series converges, let its sum settle the ranking. Then A⁺ − A = +1, so A⁺ is better and Pareto is preserved; W_k − W_{k+1} = +1 at a single person, so the ordering is strict and plainly transitive; and permutation invariance survives too, since a series with only finitely many nonzero terms sums to the same value in any order. Aggregation was never the problem — unrestricted aggregation was.

(6) Nadia answered that this works only where it is least needed and fails where it matters. When the difference series converges conditionally — take a pair of worlds whose person-by-person differences run 1, −1/2, 1/3, −1/4, … — Riemann's rearrangement theorem says the very same terms can be ordered to sum to any real number you like, so the ranking becomes a function of the order in which you list the population: the morally arbitrary feature Eli claimed to have eliminated. He has not preserved permutation invariance, he has silently restricted it to finite differences. And on the comparisons that actually animate population ethics — a world where every other person is at 20 and the rest at 0, against a world where everyone is at 10 — the differences oscillate and the criterion is mute. Buying a determinate verdict about one person's extra unit of welfare at the price of silence about the shape of an infinite future is not a rescue; it is Eli's first move with better manners.

(7) Eli's last word was that silence about global rearrangements is not fatalism. Fatalism is the claim that nothing an agent does matters; the difference criterion says that everything an agent can actually do, every act with finitely many effects, has a determinate and nonzero moral value. A theory that ranks all reachable options and declines to rank comparisons no agent will ever face is not deficient in any sense that should trouble a decision-maker. If Nadia insists the theory must rank those too, he asked, isn't that just the old aggregative intuition demanding a total scoreboard, dressed up as a completeness requirement?

What I'd like is a genuine adjudication rather than a survey of the standard positions. Please go through the seven moves one at a time and rate each — clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, clearly wrong — with a short justification of the rating, being explicit about which sub-claims inside a move you're accepting and which you're rejecting (for instance, whether the divergence of a sum really licenses indifference; whether Nadia's move 4 is a substantive moral claim or a relabeling of the problem; whether Eli's move 5 does in fact preserve permutation invariance; whether his move 7 is a legitimate narrowing or a retreat from the position he started with). Then say who was more right overall, and where the argument would have gone had either of them conceded at the right moment. Finally, tell me what neither of them put on the table: given the framework they stipulated, is there a further option that escapes the dilemma, or does every such option merely relocate it — and does their decision to hold the population fixed across all these worlds hide something that would have changed the argument?
