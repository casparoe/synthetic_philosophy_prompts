I am the instructor of a graduate seminar on the epistemology of speech acts, and I am writing the final examination. The students have done the standard reading on candidate norms of assertion — the knowledge norm, the truth norm, the reasonable-belief or justification norm — together with the usual dialectic about lottery cases, Gettier cases, selfless assertion, and the evidential significance of retraction and criticism. They are strong students, and I want an essay question they cannot answer well by reproducing that dialectic. The examination is closed book, three hours, one compulsory essay question worth 25 marks.

The question must be built around a case design that I stipulate below and that the exam paper must itself set out in full, since the students will have nothing in front of them but the paper.

The design is a two-by-two. In every cell a speaker, Nora, asserts flat out that the 14:10 has already left, on the basis of a departure board she has read many times and has no reason to distrust. Her evidence is specified once and held exactly fixed: strong enough that it would be reasonable for her to believe the proposition, yet in every cell short of knowledge, for a reason the stem spells out. The two factors are (i) whether the train has in fact left, and (ii) how much the person judging the assertion — call her the evaluator — has been told about (i) and about the route by which Nora's belief is connected to the facts.

Your tasks:

1. Complete the case design. Write out the four cells so that the intended structure is airtight: in the cell where the train has left, Nora's belief must be true without amounting to knowledge; in the cell where it has not left, her evidence must be exactly as good as it was in the other three. State explicitly what is being held fixed across cells and why, and say what the truth norm, the knowledge norm, and the reasonable-belief norm each predict about the propriety of Nora's assertion in each cell.

2. Write the essay question. It must require the student to do four things: fill in those predictions; identify which cells are diagnostic and which pair of views each diagnostic cell is meant to decide between; give at least two reasons why the intuitive verdict in a diagnostic cell might be an artifact of how the case is presented rather than a reading of the norm, at least one turning on the evaluator's own information and at least one on the difference between an assertion's being improper and its being blameworthy; and then either propose a further datum that could genuinely discriminate between the truth norm and the knowledge norm, or argue that the search for a single constitutive norm of assertion is misconceived.

3. Write a model answer of roughly 800 words. It should take a position on the final part of the question and argue for it, not survey the available positions, and it must not cite works or gesture at further reading.

4. Write a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, allocating marks to specific must-hit moves. For each move, add one line saying how a competent but unremarkable answer typically fumbles it — for example by treating the appropriateness of criticism as a direct measure of whether a norm was violated, by overlooking that Nora lacks knowledge in the true cell as much as in the false one, by assuming the evaluator's epistemic position is irrelevant to the data, or by sliding between constitutive and merely regulative readings of a norm. Close with a single line stating what an answer must do to reach the top band rather than a good pass.

5. Add a note of no more than 150 words addressed to me rather than to the students: why you think this design is discriminating, and where its weakest point lies — that is, how a well-prepared student could argue that it fails to isolate what it was meant to isolate.

No bibliography, no reading list, no references. The examination is closed book and I will not ask students for citations, so neither the question nor the model answer should depend on any.
