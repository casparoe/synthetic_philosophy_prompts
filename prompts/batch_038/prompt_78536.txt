Here's the question the students were given:

**Question 3 (25 points, ~600 words).** Consider the following view. Call it the Dual-Scale View (DSV):

1. Hedonic states have an intensity dimension, and there is a threshold τ on that dimension.
2. For episodes strictly below τ, pleasure and pain are cardinally measurable on a single common scale and are fully fungible: sub-τ pleasures can offset sub-τ pains, and sub-τ sufferings aggregate additively across persons.
3. For episodes at or above τ, the badness is not expressible on that scale at all. Any finite collection of sub-τ suffering — however many people, however long — is outweighed by a single episode of supra-τ suffering. Note that τ is a threshold on the intensity of individual episodes, not on aggregated amounts.

Is the *arbitrariness objection* fatal to DSV? The objection: hedonic intensity looks continuous, so wherever τ is placed there will be a neighbouring intensity on the wrong side of the line, and no principled reason to draw the line there rather than somewhere else. Then apply DSV to this case and say whether the result is a reductio, a cost, or a bullet worth biting: a city can install an alarm system that prevents one person from undergoing five minutes of supra-τ agony, but installing it requires exposing each of four billion people to ten minutes of sub-τ pain (a bad headache, comfortably below τ).

And here's the student's answer:

---

DSV says install the alarm. Clause 3 makes five minutes of supra-τ agony lexically worse than any finite pile of sub-τ pain, so the four billion headaches cannot add up to an outweighing quantity no matter how we tally them. On the view's own terms the verdict isn't close. Whether that's a reductio is a different question, and I think the honest answer is that it's a very heavy cost that DSV can only partly soften.

The softening goes like this. DSV isn't committed to ignoring the headaches altogether. Because clause 2 puts sub-τ states on a common cardinal scale, the four billion headaches have a determinate total disvalue, and DSV registers that disvalue in every choice that doesn't happen to involve a supra-τ episode — which is nearly all of ordinary policy. And even in the alarm case the headaches carry weight of a sort: summed together they amount to far more suffering than the one agony, so once that sum crosses τ the two quantities are on the same scale after all and the comparison becomes an ordinary quantitative one. That's the resource DSV's defenders should press, since it converts the case from a lexical trump into a trade-off.

The arbitrariness objection is the more serious of the two problems, and I don't think DSV escapes it. Since intensity is continuous, for any candidate τ there's a neighbouring intensity that intuitively belongs on the other side, and the placement of the line does all the work in the theory. Because DSV treats pleasure and pain as incommensurable, it can't say how much mild pleasure compensates how much mild pain, which is exactly why the location of the line matters so much and why its arbitrariness is so damaging.

A defender will reply that the objection proves too much. Plenty of respectable moral and legal standards are thresholds on continua: the age of majority, the line between recklessness and negligence, the point at which a risk becomes unacceptable to impose. We don't scrap those just because the line is sharp and the underlying continuum is smooth. That reply has force, but it disanalogises in the way that matters. With the age of majority the sharp line is a convention we're free to set however we like; with τ, DSV claims to be tracking something that makes the difference between two states being commensurable and not being so at all. If the line is set by fiat, DSV's claim to have located a real discontinuity in value collapses. If it's supposed to be discovered, the objection stands untouched. So the charge lands — as a serious cost rather than a knockout.

Two closing notes. First, if the objection is right that we simply can't know where τ falls, then DSV isn't so much false as indeterminate: there's no fact of the matter about which episodes are supra-τ, so the view delivers no verdicts rather than wrong ones, and the alarm case is simply not decidable. Second, DSV is best read as negative utilitarianism with a threshold, so it inherits the standard NU problem: since on that family of views only suffering counts, the addition of happy sub-τ lives is neutral at best, which is an independently hard thing to sell.
