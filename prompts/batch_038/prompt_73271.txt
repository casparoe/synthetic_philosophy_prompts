I am a novelist, and I am four years into a book set at a lazaretto — a quarantine station on a low island off a northern port — in the late 1840s. Part Three is narrated, in effect, by the paperwork: the chapters are headed by facsimiles of documents, and the human story reaches the reader only as those documents permit it to. I invented the station's surviving record series myself for the book, and you should treat them as given rather than hunting for real-world analogues. There is a passenger manifest on which one woman appears only inside a numeral — the line reads, in my draft, "and two women, steerage, unnamed"; a keeper's day book that logs weather, wind, rations, and the number of bodies landed; a storekeeper's inventory of deceased passengers' effects, in which her entire estate is one pair of silver ear-rings, noted as bearing a mark that cannot be read; a burial register entry giving her as an unknown female of about thirty; the port physician's case notes, which describe a fever precisely and a person not at all; and an index to all of the above, compiled by a historical society some sixty years after the fact, which lists her under U. She is in every one of these documents and is the subject of none of them.

What I want from you is a sestina — six stanzas of six lines and a three-line envoi — to stand as the epigraph to Part Three, and I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. Choose your six end-words from the vocabulary of the records themselves (candidates might include *manifest*, *inventory*, *register*, *index*, *day book*, *effects*, *unknown*, *ditto*, *illegible*, *blank*, *tally*, *witness*, *hand*, *erased*), and let at least one of them be a word that describes the condition of the record rather than its contents — something like *illegible* or *ditto* — since the archive's own procedures are among the few things it truly preserves. The point I am asking the permutation to make is this: the end-words rotate in a closed order, each returning exactly six times, and no seventh word can ever enter at a line's end; the poem's lexicon of endings is closed the way the sources are closed, and the woman who is their object has no end-word of her own. The envoi, where all six words are gathered into three lines, is the only place the poem can turn toward her — and it must turn toward her without speaking for her. She is not to be given a voice, a name, an interiority, or a plausible life; to supply those would be to commit in three lines the exact offense the poem is about.

Approach this as a poet who also knows the historiography of the archive, and think carefully before you write. Begin with a working note of no more than about 250 words in which you (a) name your six end-words and justify each in a sentence, (b) write out the order in which they will fall at the ends of the lines in all six stanzas and say how the envoi will dispose them, and (c) state in two or three sentences what you take to be the defensible posture toward a source that was never designed to preserve the person one is looking for — whether its omissions are themselves evidence, bearing the shape of the power that produced them, or whether inference beyond the document's own horizon is simply fiction in period costume. The poem should stage that tension rather than settle it: no villainous clerks, no pious recovery, no consolation. Keep the theoretical vocabulary entirely out of the verse — no words about hegemony, discourse, or the subaltern — and make the poem legible to a reader who has never heard this debate while remaining recognizable as exact to one who has. Hold the pattern strictly; if you find at some point that you cannot, say so in the note rather than quietly loosening it. Please do not append a bibliography or a reading list, and do not cite sources — the note and the poem should stand on their own.
