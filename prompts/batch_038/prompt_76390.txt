For three winters now I have been transcribing, page by page, a small digitized corpus of English local records: roughly nine hundred manuscript pages from one market town between about 1660 and 1720 — quarter-sessions presentments, the overseers of the poor's accounts, a handful of settlement and bastardy examinations, two defamation suits, and a fat bundle of depositions taken after a food riot in a dearth year. I come at this from the wrong side professionally. I'm a software engineer, and I read the rationalist blogs through their whole first decade, so my instinct is to think in base rates, selection effects, and the difference between what an unusual observation tells you about a threshold and what a representative sample tells you about a mean. What I want to do with my town is history from below: recover the assumptions of the laboring poor, the widows, the migrant harvest workers and the people who turned out at the market cross. The awkward fact is that every single page in my corpus was written by their governors and prosecutors. There is no diary, no letter, no self-description, nothing that was not produced by an institution in the act of judging somebody. I increasingly suspect my town cannot bear the weight I want to put on it, and I would like to be argued with by someone with no stake in my project.

So: play a senior social historian trained in the Annales tradition — a woman who built her career on parish registers, grain-price and wage series, and reconstituting families from baptisms, marriages and burials, and who has spent forty years telling graduate students that the unit of analysis is the series, not the anecdote. She is sharp and she has read her microhistorians; she knows their best moves and is not going to be fooled by a colorful miller or a picturesque cat massacre. Her settled view is that an institutional record is a mirror of the institution: the depositions in a riot bundle record what justices were prepared to hear and clerks were prepared to write, and reading them for the crowd's own political assumptions is a category error that ends up smuggling the magistrate's categories back in as the people's. Give her the full arsenal — selection on the deviant end of the distribution, survivorship of the paper itself, the clerk's formulas, the absence of any control against which to know whether the town is atypical at all, the fact that thresholds shift from decade to decade and county to county, the anecdotal fallacy, and the structural point that what matters moves on a timescale no single town's litigation can register. She will concede small things (narrative can order evidence a table cannot; some questions are only askable at small scale) but never the main one. First person, stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so this feels like a working session rather than a seminar paper. Ask me factual questions about the corpus when you need them — how many presentments survive per decade, whether the clerk's hand changes mid-bundle, whether the parish registers are complete, whether the town held a market charter — and I will answer from my transcriptions rather than have you assume. Do not recommend anything for me to read, do not cite studies, and do not invoke historians as authority; if you want precedent, describe the shape of the evidence and what that shape licenses.

My opening move, which I want you to answer directly rather than restate your position: you will say my nine hundred pages are a sample of one, drawn from a town that appears in the record only when it misbehaved, and set down by men whose categories are not my categories. I grant all of it — the archive is a byproduct of prosecution. But I don't think that is fatal, because I am not estimating a mean. There is an idea in the microhistorical literature I keep returning to, the exceptional normal: the atypical case is precisely where the norm becomes visible, because norms only surface when they are breached and somebody with authority has to adjudicate the breach. My corpus is almost entirely threshold behavior. What conduct got a woman presented as a scold. How far a family had to fall before the parish gave it a dole rather than a pass. How many weeks of dearth before a crowd assembled, and how much bread could be short before a justice wrote down the word riot. If the archive selected on the threshold, then the archive is evidence about the threshold, and having one town instead of two hundred costs me external validity without costing me the quantity I am actually trying to measure. Furthermore — and this is the part I want you to engage rather than wave away — the filtering is not merely loss. A scrivener's decision to write down a witness's phrase about what the crowd said the price of a loaf ought to be, when he could have summarized it as a riot, preserves a piece of the crowd's own model of what would be legible and effective to authority. The margin notes, the crossed-out lines, the sudden change of hand when a case gets appealed: these are traces of the negotiation, and the negotiation is where the norm lives. So tell me specifically: what would have to be true of my documents for you to concede that this one town's records can establish something about the norm that an aggregated serial study of two hundred parishes could not? And if your answer is that I am mistaking a threshold in one place at one moment for a threshold in the society at large, then tell me which papers in my bundle you would read first to check whether the town is even atypical, given that I have no series to compare it against. If you think I have won a point, say so; I am not looking to be flattered, but I am looking to be moved.
