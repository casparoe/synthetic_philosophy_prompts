I am three chapters into a master's dissertation on perceptual justification, and the chapter I am supposed to be writing now is the one on cognitive penetration — the question of when a belief, an expectation or a desire of the subject's actually changes what they see, as opposed to changing what they say they see, or changing what they would have seen anyway given where they happened to be looking. My supervisor's standing advice is that I cannot settle this from the armchair, and a friend of mine who works in experimental psychology assures me that every case I bring her will dissolve into response bias. Neither remark has helped, because I do have settled intuitions about a good many of these cases, and I would like to know what they add up to before I decide whether to give them up.

My working principle, arrived at by accretion rather than argument, is this: perception is cognitively penetrated only when some cognitive state of the subject's — a belief, a standing expectation, a concept they deploy — brings about a change in the experience itself, and only when that change is the sort of thing that could be assessed as apt or inappropriate in the light of the content of that state. Everything else is, on my principle, either post-perceptual judgement, or attention, or slow perceptual learning, and none of those is penetration. Now for the cases. The Müller-Lyer arrows and the checkerboard illusion with the two identical squares of grey: I know perfectly well what the facts are, I see the difference regardless, and I say not penetrated. An experienced radiologist looking at a chest film reports seeing a nodule where I see only mottled grey, and I say her experience genuinely differs from mine — penetrated, though I concede that someone will call this perceptual learning and I am not sure I can resist the charge. In that colour-matching study in which people set the background behind a heart-shaped orange card distinctly redder than the background behind a square card of exactly the same hue, I say penetrated, and this is the case I feel most confident about, since shape ought to have nothing to say about hue. When I am hungry, food photographs look larger and more vivid to me than they do after a meal, and here I say not penetrated: a drive state, and I suspect the change is in the reporting rather than in the seeing. Those two are a pair I cannot reconcile, since structurally they look to me like the same thing. When somebody says 'it's a seal' about an ambiguous drawing and I duly see a seal, I say not penetrated, because both readings were available all along and the remark merely selected between them; but when a colleague of mine who was firmly in the white-and-gold camp over that photograph of the dress was told the right answer and thereafter saw blue and black, involuntarily and for good, I say penetrated — and I am aware that this second pair is no easier than the first, since the only differences I can point to are that one flip is voluntary and reversible and the other is neither. Finally: after being told what a badly degraded recording says, I hear the words plainly and can never hear it as noise again, which I call penetration; and after days in inverting goggles the world looks the right way up again without any belief of the wearer's being implicated, which I call not penetration. The case I keep reversing on is the eighty-pound bottle of wine: the price has been mentioned, the wine tastes better, and the drinker insists that the flavour itself has changed rather than their opinion of it.

Here is what I want. Test my principle against these cases and either repair it or replace it — I am not attached to the principle, only to the verdicts. State your best candidate precisely enough that I could apply it to a case neither of us has thought of, or, if you think the honest answer is that no single principle will do all this work, state your two best rivals and the difference between them. Lead with the principle itself in a single sentence before you do anything else, and identify which clause is doing the work in each of my two problem pairs. Then go through the cases in order, saying which verdicts the principle reproduces and which it overturns, and be blunt about the overturning: I would rather have a simple principle that forces me to abandon either the hunger verdict or the ambiguous-seal verdict than an elaborate one that saves both, and I want you to say which course you recommend and why. Close with one case of your own devising on which your principle and the simplest rival come apart, together with the verdict each of them delivers. Please write in British English, and keep the whole thing under 650 words.
