I am leaving my post as Assistant Director for Housing at a Midlands borough council at the end of the month, and on my last morning we are formally switching off HALO — the Housing Allocation and Liaison Optimiser, which nobody ever called anything but its acronym, and whose name I approved without a moment's thought in 2015. It ran for nine years and ranked applicants for our discretionary allocation list. One of its inputs was a residential-continuity measure built from address history and council tax records, on the grounds — correct grounds, the inquiry confirmed them — that continuity predicted whether a tenancy would be sustained. It therefore ranked people who had moved often, or who had no fixed address, below applicants with unremarkable histories: women leaving violent partners, care leavers, people who had slept rough. The other thing it did was show the housing officers a number and no reasons; they could override, but an override required a written justification that went to a line manager, and the override rate settled at two per cent. Three people were killed in the sense that matters here. One of the families is coming to the ceremony, and so are two of their advocates, and so are six of the data team who built the thing, four of whom I recruited, and two local journalists, and the current leadership. About a hundred and twenty people in the chamber. I want you to write the remarks I will give — seven minutes, spoken aloud, roughly the length of a page and a half.

The difficulty is conceptual before it is emotional. Half that room has spent nine years being told that the model was neutral and that any unfairness came from how people used it, and they are proud of their work and not stupid; the other half has been told, since the inquiry, that the model itself did the harm, as though it were a weather event. Both formulations are available to me and both are false, and I have three drafts that collapse into one or the other by the second minute. I read Langdon Winner's essay on whether artifacts have politics in a policy module twenty years ago and thought it was clever; I have spent the last eighteen months discovering that the distinction it draws — between the politics of a tool's use and the politics that are already arranged into it, before anyone decides anything — is the only description that fits what we actually built. So the speech has to make that distinction visible in the room without ever naming it, without any theory, without a single quotation or citation from anyone: half the chamber would hear a quotation as a man hiding behind a book, and the other half would hear it as a lesson. I also want you to know that the Long Island parkway bridges, the famous example, are unusable here — the engineers quote them at each other in Slack threads to feel sophisticated, and it has changed nothing about how they work. Whatever carries the idea has to be drawn from our own case.

Some specifics I need honoured. There must be one passage in which I say what I myself argued at the model review in 2016 — that continuity was a sound predictor and that any unfairness could be managed at the point of decision, both halves of which turned out to be true at once — in my own voice, unhedged, functioning as evidence about how these things get built rather than as a request for absolution or as a performance of my discomfort. There must be real thanks to the data team that is true, is not condescending, and is not a set-up: nothing that turns on an 'and yet'. The word bias must not appear at all, in any of its forms; it has become an anaesthetic in that building and I will not use it once more. At one point the speech must turn to the family who are present, without telling them what they ought to feel and without thanking them for their courage. I must not claim the replacement system is neutral or fair: say specifically what it does differently — published reasons, overrides that need no justification, continuity removed entirely — and say honestly what that will cost us, in speed, in staff, in arguments we will now have to have out loud in front of applicants. And the ending must not be a moral, a promise or a quotation; it has to be the thing a person says at a switch-off, when the lights go on in the chamber and everyone has to go back to work.
