I am putting together a problem set for an advanced graduate seminar in distributive ethics, and my students have absorbed the standard Parfitian line almost too comfortably. As they now recite it: the leveling-down objection is supposed to be decisive against telic egalitarianism, because equality is comparative and so can call for a change that makes nobody better off, whereas prioritarianism escapes the objection entirely, since weighting benefits by how well off the recipient is yields a strictly increasing, non-comparative ranking that could never prefer such a change. I want a designed case that unsettles that complacency from inside the priority view — not by resurrecting the leveling-down objection, which I agree fails against prioritarianism, but by exposing a parameter that the standard statement of the view leaves unspecified: the temporal unit of the currency being weighted.

Please construct a decision problem to the following specifications. Exactly one agent — a discretionary fund, a clinic, a small foundation, your choice, provided the setting does not itself tilt the reader — faces two mutually exclusive options, only one of which can be realized, and exactly two people are affected. The two are strangers, have the same expected remaining lifespan, and differ in no relevant respect of desert, responsibility, relationship, or prior claim. Each option benefits exactly one of them and harms no one, so that relative to a stated status quo nobody is worse off under either option, and neither option is Pareto-superior to the other. The scenario must be realistic and contemporary: no experience machines, no population change, no neurotechnology, no exotic physics. The decisive specification is this: prioritarianism applied to lifetime well-being, and prioritarianism applied to well-being during the period the option touches, must rank the two options in opposite orders while using the very same strictly increasing, strictly concave transformation; and the reversal must survive at least two different choices of that transformation. Report the well-being figures on an explicit cardinal scale with the zero point identified, and choose numbers that make the arithmetic easy to check by hand. Keep the narrative to a single paragraph, followed by a compact table giving, for each person and for each of the three states — status quo, option A, option B — both lifetime well-being and well-being during the affected period. Hold all other periods of both lives identical across the three states and say so explicitly, since only the changed periods enter the comparison. One further constraint, which is what makes the exercise non-trivial rather than a curiosity: the wretched stretch you deploy must not substantially reduce the lifetime total of the person enduring it, because otherwise the two applications converge and there is nothing left to show.

The case is meant to demonstrate that the priority view is a family of views whose verdicts turn on a question its standard formulation never answers, and that this difficulty is a different animal from the leveling-down objection; that fixes what counts as cheating. The verdicts must fall out of the weighting function and the numbers you selected, not be declared: if you find yourself writing that a view "sees" or "treats" something without the arithmetic producing that result, the design has failed. After the scenario and the table, deliver the following, in numbered sections. (1) The two computations, line by line, for each of the two weighting functions, with each application's verdict stated outright. (2) A constraint check, item by item, against every specification above, including the two-function robustness requirement. (3) An identification of which feature of the case does the work, plus a perturbation that removes it — letting the bad stretch persist long enough to dent the lifetime total is the obvious candidate — shown numerically to make the two applications agree again. (4) A short discussion of whether the case depends on where zero sits on the well-being scale, and what a prioritarian should say about fixing that scale. (5) A closing section explaining why this is not the leveling-down objection, what it nonetheless shares with it, why neither application of prioritarianism can prefer a change that leaves someone worse off while leaving everyone else no better off, and whether the temporal question looks any different for egalitarians who argue about time slices versus whole lives. (6) The strongest available attack on the case from each direction: one paragraph from a defender of the lifetime application and one from a defender of the momentary application, each competent enough that the other would recognize it as a serious objection rather than a strawman. Roughly 900 to 1,200 words in total, entirely self-contained — define any technical term as you use it, and do not include quotations, citations, a bibliography, or a reading list.
