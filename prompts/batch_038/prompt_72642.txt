I am drafting a chapter on the claim that reading lives is a mode of moral education, and I need a designed case rather than another survey of the debate. Plutarch announces at the outset of the Life of Alexander that he is writing lives and not histories, on the ground that a trifling incident — a remark, a joke, a small domestic scene — discloses character more reliably than the great public deeds that history attends to. Hagiography, in most of its forms, goes further and strips the life of moral residue altogether: no ambivalence, no regret, no luck, no competing claims the subject got wrong. The standard critical response, shared by literary particularists and by anyone sympathetic to them, is that this flattening is precisely what disqualifies hagiography as moral instruction: a reader trained on univocal lives cannot cope with the mixed cases that make up actual moral experience. I want a case built to run the other way.

Construct a scenario in which a hagiographically flattened biography produces better moral judgment than a psychologically realistic one — where judgment means the capacity to appraise novel, unfamiliar cases correctly, and not merely motivation, admiration, or willingness to comply. The scenario should be no more than two paragraphs.

Constraints:

1. The setting must be an ordinary contemporary institution — a formation program, a residency, a professional ethics seminar, a novitiate — with no miracles, no speculative technology, and no appeal to an ancient world.
2. Invent the subject of the biography. Do not use a real historical figure, so that the case cannot turn on contested facts about anyone.
3. The two lives in the case must agree on the facts and differ only in narrative treatment: one presents the virtue as unified and the costs as chosen without remainder, the other gives the reader interior access to mixed motives, second thoughts, and the role of contingency in how things turned out.
4. The readers must be neither credulous nor especially sophisticated, and the gain must not be explained by the flattened life happening to be right about the subject or by the readers being duped into something useful.

After the scenario, run the check. State what each of three accounts predicts about the two groups of readers and why: an admiration-based exemplarist account on which exemplars fix the reference of virtue terms and imitation is the route to virtue; a literary-particularist account on which attention to concrete, ambiguous particulars is what cultivates moral perception and idealized models are a liability; and an account on which the value of a life-narrative lies in the exercise of appraising a life as a whole, including its failures. Say which of these can accommodate the reversal, and identify the mechanism by which the flattening improves judgment rather than merely feeling like it does.

Then tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, what happens to each verdict if I remove it, and how a defender of the particularist account would attack the case — in particular the charge that the flattened biography produces trained conformity or a taste for the edifying rather than judgment, and what in the scenario would discriminate between those two outcomes. If you think no such case can be constructed without stipulating the result instead of generating it from the accounts, say so plainly, give me the nearest near-miss, and name what blocks the full version.

Please do not supply citations or a bibliography; I will add those myself.
