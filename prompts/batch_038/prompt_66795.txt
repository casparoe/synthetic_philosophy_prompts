I work as a research assistant in a lab that sits uncomfortably between vision science and philosophy of mind, and at last week's group meeting our PI invoked the slogan "all observation is theory-laden" twice in twenty minutes, each time to close down a line of discussion. Both times it did real argumentative work. I want to be able to say precisely what work, so I can raise it at the next meeting without sounding like I'm just repeating a slogan back at him in a different tone.

**First use.** I reported that our preregistered replication had come out flat: the published effect we were chasing was that speeded same/different judgements about the hue of two colour patches are reliably faster or more accurate when the patches straddle a lexically marked category boundary, and we found nothing. His response was that this is exactly what one should expect, because an experiment's outcome is never a theory-free deliverance — our team's operational choices (the stimulus spacing, the response mapping, the way we defined "same") already encode a modular account of colour vision, so the null result is a measurement of our commitments rather than of colour discrimination. When I said that this makes the hypothesis immune to any result we could obtain, he said that immunity from "raw" data is a mark of sophistication, not a defect.

**Second use.** I asked how the classic persistence-of-illusion data are supposed to be handled: people who have measured the two fins of a Müller-Lyer figure with calipers, or who have physically cut the two critical squares out of Adelson's checkerboard figure and held them side by side, still see the lines as unequal and the squares as different in shade. He said this proves nothing in either direction, because there is no such thing as a report of how things look that is not already an interpretation; and, separately, that the documented variation in illusion magnitude across populations and across levels of relevant expertise shows that early vision is not sealed off from what an observer knows anyway.

What I want from you is a disambiguation, not a defence, a refutation, or a history of the phrase — and please no bibliography or reading list, since I cannot check it. Specifically:

1. Give me a numbered list (call the entries R1, R2, …) of the distinct precise claims the slogan could be making. I expect at least six, and they must differ along the axes that actually matter here: *what* is supposed to be laden (the concepts available for describing an experience; the phenomenal content of the experience itself; the observation report; the evidential weight a datum can carry against a hypothesis) and *how* the dependence runs (constitutive, causal-and-online, developmental, or merely inferential at the level of interpretation). For each entry: state it as a single claim precise enough to be assessed; tag it **TRIVIAL**, **SUBSTANTIVE-DEFENSIBLE**, **CONTESTED**, or **FALSE**; say in one or two sentences what would have to be true for it to hold and what observation would refute it; and say whether it is compatible with illusions persisting under full, verified knowledge of the distal layout.

2. Then take up the two uses separately. For each, say which of your readings — if any — would actually license the move, and be explicit when the move requires a reading that is stronger than the one the slogan is usually taken to express. Where the inference fails, name the missing premise rather than just declaring it invalid.

3. Tell me what my PI would have to surrender if he retreated from the strong reading to a weaker one in each case, and be blunt about which of the two uses is hopeless and which has something going for it. I have a suspicion that his point about expertise and cross-population variation in illusion magnitude is doing something quite different from the slogan — possibly something about how visual systems are calibrated rather than about how beliefs colour what is seen — and I would like that suspicion either sharpened or corrected.
