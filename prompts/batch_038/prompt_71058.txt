For the past two years I've co-organized a monthly discussion group that mostly argues about applied ethics, and this term I've found myself unable to let go of a topic that I can only describe as trading on moral questions: the general practice of striking deals in which each side helps, tolerates, or refrains from something the other cares about, in exchange for the same. It comes up everywhere once you start looking — legislative horse-trading, matched donations between people with opposed causes, payments to get someone to stop doing something you have no standing to stop, paying children to do things you'd like them to want to do. I've been writing down my reactions to particular cases, and they don't line up the way I expected them to. Here they are, numbered so I can refer back to them.

1. *Logrolling.* I'm on a city council. I vote to fund a parade I'm confident is a waste of public money because a colleague has agreed to vote for the overnight shelter beds I think are essential. Fine — better, I'd say, than both of us abstaining on principle.

2. *Vote-buying.* An ordinary voter accepts fifty dollars from a candidate to vote for him. Not fine, and I feel strongly about it. The thing being traded is the same as in case 1, and the arithmetic could be made identical. "One of them holds office" feels like a description of the setup rather than an explanation of my verdict.

3. A friend and I disagree about where giving does the most good. She cares about the local animal shelter; I care about global health. We agree that for every hundred dollars she gives the shelter, I'll give a hundred to a malaria charity. Fine — I'd call it admirable. Both causes end up richer than they were.

4. Same structure, different counterpart. A man I know funds lobbying against vaccination and offers me the identical deal: for every hundred he gives his group, I give a hundred to a measles program. Not fine. I feel I'd be an accomplice, or at least that I'd be paying someone to keep doing harm, even though the numbers are the same as in case 3.

5. My neighbor intends to cut down a mature tree that shades my vegetable garden. He's within his rights. I offer him five hundred dollars to leave it standing and he accepts. Fine; I'd feel like a fool for not offering.

6. A contractor tells me he has been rinsing solvents into the creek behind my land — legally permitted, morally not. I pay him to stop. Here I hold two things at once: the creek is better off and I'm glad the money went there, but the payment was degrading for both of us, and a world where compliance with duties has to be purchased is worse than one where it doesn't.

7. I pay my nephew twenty dollars for every book he finishes over the summer. Fine, and it worked.

8. My sister pays her daughter twenty dollars for every visit to her grandmother. Not fine. The grandmother is lonelier than my nephew was behind in reading, the visits went up, everyone reports being pleased — and I still think my sister made something worse.

9. *Offsets.* I fly a fair amount and buy carbon offsets against the flights. Better than flying and doing nothing, but the offset doesn't render the flight permissible, and I resent it when people treat my ledger as balanced.

My own tentative principle, which I don't trust: a trade is acceptable when what is exchanged is something whose value does not depend on the reason it was given, and when refusing would leave both parties worse off by their own lights; a trade is not acceptable when the value of the act depends on the motive behind it, or when one side's cause is bad enough that assisting it is not a mere difference of opinion. I can already feel that this is two principles stapled together, that the second clause is doing all the work in cases 4 and 8 and none of it in case 2, and that "bad enough" is where I've hidden the thing I was supposed to be explaining.

Could you do the reflective equilibrium for me? I'm not after a survey of the field and I specifically don't want a reading list — I have no way to chase one down. What I want is the best single principle, or the smallest set of principles, that my verdicts point to, stated precisely enough to be applied to a case I haven't thought of without consulting me, plus a candid account of where it breaks. Please write in American English and use the following sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — three to five sentences: the principle you land on, and the one verdict of mine you think I should abandon.
2. **The principle** — about 150 words, stated precisely, defining rather than assuming any term it leans on (reasonable disagreement, reason-dependence, the counterfactual baseline against which "worse off" is judged).
3. **Clause by clause** — roughly 400 words going through the principle one clause at a time, naming which of my nine cases each clause exists to handle and what it delivers in that case.
4. **The pairs that hurt** — about 300 words on 1/2, 3/4, and 7/8. For each pair, say whether your principle saves both verdicts, saves one, or saves neither. If a distinction you need amounts to restating my reaction rather than explaining it, say so plainly instead of dressing it up.
5. **New cases** — four cases I haven't listed, chosen so that one turns on a trade with a future person who cannot reciprocate, one on paying someone to refrain from something merely supererogatory, and one on a trade in which both parties are certain the other is wrong about a matter of plain fact rather than of values. Give your principle's verdict on each with a confidence level, and then add a fifth case on which you think the principle genuinely runs out.
6. **What I have to give up** — about 200 words on which of my nine verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, ranked by how much that should worry me, and on what a simpler principle would look like if I agreed to sacrifice one or two of them.
