I'm writing a novel — literary thriller-ish, about a restorer who quietly starts producing better-than-original versions of damaged pictures — and every chapter opens with an in-universe document. For chapter nine I need you to actually write the document, not describe it.

It's a strategy paper from a fictional consortium of museums and their advisers, the Parity Trust, proposing that its members sell off the originals, hang perfect facsimiles in their place, and use the proceeds to endow the whole sector. The Trust definately believes its own argument: if a replica is visually indistinguishable from the original then the original has no aesthetic value the replica lacks, so whatever people feel they've lost is sentiment, and sentiment can be priced.

The target is that argument, and the consultancy register it's delivered in — not the art market in general, not any real institution, not any real person.

Form: a board strategy paper, first person plural, numbered sections and subheadings, roughly 750 words. Please include a short section on how you value provenance once attribution stops mattering, a risk register with three or four entries, and one invented case study (a misattributed work that everyone had been admiring for decades). Invent every case and every name — no real ones, and no references, no bibliography, I'm not going to be able to fact-check you and I don't want to have to.

Two things I care about most. First, the jokes should come from the philosophy being right rather than from cheap digs: the indiscernibility premise, achievement as opposed to mere result, the difference between copying a surface and an object's history of production — someone who works on this stuff should laugh and think it fair, not wince. Second, somewhere in the middle the paper has to run into the obvious problem for its own view, namely that the same reasoning seems to strip the facsimiles of value too, and the Trust's proposed fix should make things worse rather than better.

Never state the objection outright and don't wink at the reader. Deadpan all the way through, and land it on a closing line that turns the stomach.

Begin straight in with the paper itself — no preface, no explanation of your choices, and nothing after the final line either.
