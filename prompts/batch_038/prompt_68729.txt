Jeffrey conditionalization and the observation-proposition account are rival descriptions of perceptual learning. On the first, an experience directly sets new probabilities for the members of a sensory partition while your conditional probabilities on that partition remain rigid; on the second, the experience renders certain a proposition describing the experience itself, and ordinary conditionalization does the rest.

Consider a sommelier who smells a wine and moves her credences over corked, oxidized, and sound from .1, .1, and .8 to .5, .2, and .3, with nothing reaching certainty. She then recalls that her detection of cork taint is unreliable in wines of this age, and lowers her credence in corked to .35.

Compare the two accounts across this sequence. In particular: what each requires to be in place beforehand, and whether the observation-proposition account pays for its extra machinery with a proposition that the experience was supposed to be evidentially upstream of; where each can house the self-distrust, given that rigidity bars the Jeffreyan from revising conditional credences on the partition; and whether the order of the two revisions matters on either account. Close with a judgment about which of the two better explains the numbers rather than merely accommodating them, and defend that judgment.
