Retired paleoceanographer, 68. Thirty-five years of splitting sediment cores and counting foraminfera, then about two years of philosophy, which is what I've taken up since I handed back my bench. The bridge between the two for me is periodization: I spent my whole career working to a timescale that is frankly a committee artifact, so I'm fascinated by how people argue over whether a chunk of time is a real thing.

I'm in a Tuesday reading group at the library. It's run by a retired medieval historian, call her Helen, and I've been keeping a list of her verdicts because they seem to follow a rule I cant reconstruct:

- The Middle Ages: real, though she's quick to add that 476 and 1453 are bookends nobody living through them would have noticed, and that the phrase was coined as an insult.
- The Dark Ages: not real, an insult that never had anything under it.
- The Renaissance: not real as a period. Her complaint is that it's continuous with the late medieval world on one side and the early modern on the other, and that it was invented by people who wanted a break to be proud of.
- The Scientific Revolution: same verdict, plus she objects that it makes modern science sound like one event that happened once, in Europe, with everyone else catching up.
- Early modern: real, and better than Renaissance, even though nobody in 1600 said it and it's obviously a convenience.
- Late antiquity: real, also a twentieth-century invention.
- The long nineteenth century, roughly 1789 to 1914: real, and she's firm about this one, because there's an actual package of causes in there (coal, states, empires, the demographic curve) that you can't do any work with unless you name it.
- The Enlightenment: here she hedges. She'll say it's a real movement but you shouldn't use it as the name of an age, because movements and periods are different kinds of thing and mixing them up is how you get nonsense.

Next month we're on the Anthropocene and I want to predict her before I hear her, partly because its a case where I have skin in the game. Background, as you probably know: the word was pushed by atmospheric chemists around 2000, there was a formal proposal with a start date of about 1950 and the bomb-test plutonium spike as the marker, and a couple of years back the stratigraphers voted it down as an official epoch, which hasn't stopped anyone using it. Meanwhile some people want the start pushed back to the origins of agriculture, and others prefer a rival coinage that blames a particular economic system rather than the species.

So: give me the two rules that best account for all eight verdicts at once, what each of those rules makes of the Anthropocene, which one you'd bet on and why, which of her earlier calls is the closest analog to this one, and the single question I could put to her in the meeting that would tell the two rules apart. Answer as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, in that order, no preamble and nothing wrapping it up at the end.
