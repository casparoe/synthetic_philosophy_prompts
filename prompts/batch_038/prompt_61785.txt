Here is his paragraph, in his words:

'Back in 1970 Amartya Sen proved something that free-market economists have never really recovered from. Take a society of two people and a single copy of *Lady Chatterley's Lover*. The book can be read by Lewd, who enjoys that sort of thing; read by Prude, who thinks it obscene; or disposed of unread. Lewd would most like Prude to be forced to read it, next would like to read it himself, and least likes the idea of it being destroyed. Prude would most like it destroyed unread, next would rather read it himself than let Lewd enjoy it, and least likes Lewd reading it. Now impose three conditions on any rule that turns such preferences into a social ranking. Unrestricted domain: the rule must deliver an answer for every logically possible profile of preferences. The Pareto principle: if everyone prefers one outcome to another, society must prefer it too. And minimal liberalism — the weakest plausible reading of what a right amounts to — each person is decisive over at least one pair of social states concerning his own private affairs, so that Lewd's own preference settles whether he reads the book and Prude's settles whether Prude does. Sen showed these cannot all hold. Give Lewd his way and "Lewd reads" outranks "no one reads"; give Prude his way and "no one reads" outranks "Prude reads"; transitivity then makes "Lewd reads" socially better than "Prude reads," even though both men prefer the reverse. So liberty and efficiency are logically incompatible: no society that genuinely respects individual rights can also be Pareto-efficient, and the outcome the liberal rule picks in Sen's own example is one that everybody concerned agrees is worse. Note also that "decisive over one pair of states" is just dictatorship over that pair, which means Sen's theorem and Arrow's are really the same theorem seen from two angles — rights are dictatorial power in a private corner, and Arrow showed that dictatorship is the only way to escape cycles. The sole reason anyone still argues about the result is that it depends on Lewd having meddlesome preferences about Prude's conduct; once you exclude preferences over other people's private behaviour — a move Sen himself came round to — the contradiction dissolves, which is why the paradox is now treated more as a curiosity than as a live result about liberty.'
