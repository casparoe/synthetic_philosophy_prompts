I TA a first-year archaeological theory seminar, and for next week's session I've cooked up a synthetic case study — a fake assemblage stitched together out of bits of real ones — so the students can argue about inference without me having to brief them on five different regions first. Here's the setup I'm handing them:

A small single-period settlement mound, occupied maybe 150 years, three seasons of excavation. Under the floor of one house: a deposit of 61 fired-clay objects, all 2–3 cm across, all flattened cones, each with a single incised mark. No two marks are quite alike, but roughly a dozen of them appear twice. Packed in among them, a broken but refittable coarseware jar, a sheep/goat scapula with cut marks, and a thin lens of ash. Nothing comparable at any contemporary site within about 200 km. A new floor was laid directly on top of the deposit and the house stayed in use for another two generations. Under two other house floors there are much smaller deposits of a different sort — four perforated stone beads in one, seven fragmentary bone points in the other, no ash. And separately, crude versions of some of the same incised marks turn up on the rims and handles of ordinary storage jars all over the site, in plain domestic rubbish.

That's the whole brief. Now I want you to write the dialogue I'll give them as a model, between two of the seminar's students — call them Marta and Dev. Marta is convinced this is an early token or counting system, and therefore evidence of household-level record-keeping with possible implications for how much we should credit this place with redistribution or ranking. Dev thinks that's exactly the kind of reading that drags our own categories onto a deposit we have no way into, and that the defensible conclusion is a foundation or house-consecration deposit whose actual content is unrecoverable — and probably shouldn't be chased.

Please keep both of them genuinely sharp, because the whole pedagogical point is that neither is a strawman. I want real back-and-forth: each one answering the thing the other actually just said, not delivering parallel lectures. Have Marta pressed hard on what her inference consists of — which analogy is doing the work, what makes it a controlled comparison rather than a story she liked, what the mark repetition does and doesn't license, and what observation would falsify her. Have Dev pressed just as hard on why his category is better supported than hers, whether "it was meaningful to them in ways we can't recover" is a claim or an abdication, and whether the ritual reading has any content left once he's admitted the meaning is inaccessible. Both should actually use the specific features rather than gestures: the jar-rim marks in the domestic trash, the fact the house kept being lived in, the two small heterogeneous deposits under the other floors, the 200 km absence of parallels, the refittable jar. And at least once each, let them take a mundane alternative seriously — gaming pieces, a craft tally, some kind of loom or textile kit, a cache with no symbolic content at all — rather than just trading the two big readings.

One student should raise the idea, maybe mid-argument, that reliable archaeological inference works by braiding together several weak but independent lines of evidence instead of hanging everything on one strong analogy, and I'd like both of them to react to that — does it help Marta, does it trap Dev, or does it just relocate the dispute? They can nod at the classic processual/interpretivist positions where it comes up naturally, but don't build the thing around names and definitely no bibliography or reading list.

End it without a winner, but not with a shrug either: have them part company over what to do next in the field. What gets recorded, sampled, measured, or re-excavated, what they'd ask the conservator or the zooarchaeologist to look at again, and which single finding each of them would admit settles the question against them. Somewhere around 1,100–1,500 words, minimal scene-setting — a line or two of stage direction is plenty, I don't want a seminar room rendered in loving detail. Oh, and if it comes out a bit lopsided toward one side on your first pass, rebalance it; my students smell a rigged dialogue instantly.
