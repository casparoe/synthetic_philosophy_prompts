For the past twelve years I have worked the overnight console at a county dispatch center in northern Minnesota, where a single dispatcher handles road crew traffic and ambulance traffic on the same frequency until six in the morning. Most of what I say over the air is not something I have seen: a plow operator calls from a township road to report drifting snow or a washed-out culvert, and I have twenty seconds to decide what to tell the unit behind him. Over the years I have developed a rule of thumb of my own, which I have never managed to justify to anyone's satisfaction, least of all my own. When I pass something along, I keep the source attached to it and say whose mouth it came out of. When I vouch for something, I drop the source and say it flat, in my own name, and everyone on the frequency treats it as settled. My difficulty is that I cannot tell whether that is a real difference in what I am entitled to say, or only a difference in how much cover I have left myself in case it goes wrong later. Late at night I have read enough to learn that there is a standing argument about the general version of this question, though I could not name the people on either side of it and would rather not. As I understand it, one side holds that a flat assertion is in order whenever what you say is in fact the case, so that being in no position to know it makes no difference to whether you may say it. The other side holds that truth is not the operative thing at all: you may say it flat only if you actually know it, so that a true claim arrived at badly is out of line even when nothing comes of it and nobody is hurt. There is also, apparently, a more permissive view on which honest belief or reasonable belief is enough. I keep a notebook at the console where I write down the flat things I said that I should not have, and I would like a poem that uses three entries from it.

The entries are these. The first is easy: at two in the morning I drove the county road north of the reservation myself, coming on shift, and saw it bare and clear to the junction, and said so flat, and it was so, and I had seen it. The second is the ordinary sin: at four in the morning a plow operator reported the same stretch clear, and I relayed it flat because radio discipline at that hour is short and adding a name takes a second I did not have; it was true, and the only thing wrong with it was that it was his and not mine. The third is the one I have never been able to think my way around. I said the stretch was clear on the strength of a temperature reading from a station two townships over, reasoning that it could not have set ice at those numbers; the road was in fact clear; but it was clear because a grader had gone through at midnight and the county had it flagged and would not open it to through traffic until six. Nothing followed from my having said it. Nobody was sent onto a bad road. It was true when I said it and it stayed true. I wrote it in the notebook anyway, and I still cannot say what I think I did wrong, or whether I did anything wrong, or whether I simply do not like having been right by accident. If it helps to have a fourth: with forty units in the fleet and one of them going into the ditch every winter, I have told the mechanic flatly that a given truck would make it through the season, and I have never once known such a thing about any particular truck.

What I would like is a ballade, as near to the form as you can get it in English: three eight-line stanzas and a four-line envoy, two rhyme sounds throughout, and one refrain line closing every stanza and the envoy as well. The refrain must come back word for word, with no variation in its wording at all, and it should be the sort of flat thing I actually say on the air at four in the morning, something like a road being clear to a named junction. That sameness is the whole point of using this form: the sentence never changes, and yet my standing to have said it is different in each stanza, so the difference cannot be located anywhere in the words. I would like the first stanza to be the case where I drove it myself, the second the relayed report, the third the temperature reading and the grader, and the envoy to carry the refrain a fourth time. The envoy in this form traditionally goes out to some personage; send mine to the dispatcher who relieves me at six, which is where the fourth refrain can be made to mean something the first three did not. Please keep every philosopher's name out of the poem and keep words like norm, warrant, and justification out of the verse altogether. It has to work as a poem for someone who has never heard of this argument, and it has to be exact enough that someone who has heard of it can see which rule each stanza is testing. Do not let the poem settle the argument. I want the fourth refrain to leave open whether what changed across those stanzas was what I was permitted to say or only what I would have had to answer for afterward. After the poem, add a few sentences of ordinary prose telling me which stanza puts pressure on which of the rules as I described them, what you make of my distinction between passing along and vouching, and which side you think the fourth refrain belongs to. Please give me the poem first and the prose after it, and keep the whole reply free of formatting apart from the poem's own line breaks: no headings, no numbered or bulleted lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. Write the notes the way you would write a letter.
