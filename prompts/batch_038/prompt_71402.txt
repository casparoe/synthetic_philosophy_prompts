I am moderating a forty-five-minute author-meets-critics session at a workshop on the early modern philosophy of mind, and the respondent is a historian of philosophy whose recent monograph advances a distinctive reading of Descartes on non-human animals. I would like you to draft the questions I will put to her.

Her reading has three interlocking components, which I will state as I understand them so that the questions can engage their content rather than its surface. First, Descartes' denial of thought to animals is not, on this account, a denial of sentience: beasts genuinely undergo sensory states — pain, appetite, the appearance of colour and sound — but these states are non-cognitive in the strict sense, involving no judging, no conceptual content, and no awareness of being aware. Second, the language criterion is to be read as a modal rather than a performance test: what separates human beings from animals is not the production of articulated sounds but the capacity to deploy signs as a universal instrument, applicable to circumstances never previously encountered, whereas an animal's repertoire is fixed by the disposition of its organs. Third, and consequentially, she holds that no finite body of observed behaviour could ever refute the criterion, since the criterion concerns an unbounded generality of possible use rather than any particular display.

Her standard deflection, which I want the sequence to make progressively more difficult to deploy, is that all the comparative-cognition material pressed against her — vocal learning in psittacines and corvids, cetacean signature whistles, sign-trained apes — is a matter of "cue-following, not sign-using," and therefore irrelevant to a claim about what an organism could do in circumstances it has never met.

Please write nine questions, each accompanied by a single follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. I need at least one question directed to each of the following, and the follow-ups should be specific rather than generic prompts to elaborate:

- The metaphysics of union. If in human beings sensory experience depends on the union of an immaterial thinking thing with the body, what resources remain for attributing genuine sensory states to a creature she treats as wholly extended — and does she thereby reintroduce something functionally equivalent to the sensitive soul she is trying to avoid?
- Internal exegetical pressure. Whether Descartes' own physiological account of animal spirits, of habituation in the brain, and of the training of animals is compatible with the non-cognitive sentience she attributes to them, or whether that physiology points the other way.
- The motive question. Whether the reading is driven chiefly by the need to keep the human soul immortal and its author orthodox, and if so whether that makes her interpretation charitable or anachronistic.
- The collapse question. Once animals are granted real sensory states while humans are granted real thought, whether her position is distinguishable at all either from the scholastic view Descartes displaced or from a contemporary higher-order account of consciousness.
- The falsifiability question. What conceivable result from comparative psychology or from the neuroscience of nociception she would accept as counting against the modal reading, and what she says about the worry that a criterion immune to every possible observation is not a criterion at all.

Please exclude any question that invites a restatement of the view, any question about her career or how she came to this topic, and any question whose force depends on her conceding something she has explicitly refused to concede. Each question should be phrased so that it can be answered in two or three minutes and so that a non-specialist audience can follow why the answer matters. End the sequence with one question asking her, directly, what piece of textual evidence in the corpus would defeat her reading — and a follow-up prepared for the case in which she says that no single passage could do so.
