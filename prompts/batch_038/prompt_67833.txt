I write items for a benchmark that scores how well language models continue short pattern prompts, and roughly weekly one ships that turns out to be ill-posed: a model produces an answer we mark wrong, and its defenders exhibit a rule that fits every term we displayed and yields that answer instead. Our adjudication is currently an argument about naturalness that ends when the most senior person in the room tires of it.

Write the pre-publication review procedure I can hand to reviewers with no philosophy background and about twenty minutes per item: the tests to run in order, what passing each looks like, the families of rival rule we are obliged to construct before shipping and the point at which we are entitled to stop constructing them, how to repair an item that fails, and — the part I care about most — where the procedure has to concede that the verdict rests on what a trained population of respondents does rather than on anything present in the item. Ground it in what the rule-following debate around Kripke's Wittgenstein actually delivers about why a finite list of examples underdetermines the rule, and flag any step that only looks mechanical. Note explicitly what the procedure cannot do.

Then run it on three disputed items and give me a disposition for each: 3, 5, 7, __ (we intend 11 as the next prime; 9 on the odds rule keeps coming back); 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, __ (we intend 32; a respondent answered 31 and insists on a geometric rule); and J, F, M, A, M, J, __ (we intend J). Tell me which we should kill outright, even if that costs us an item the team likes.

If you think the request is itself confused — that no procedure can render such items determinate, or that I have misdescribed what the skeptical problem is about — say so plainly and tell me what the honest fallback would be, rather than handing me a checklist that papers over the difficulty.
