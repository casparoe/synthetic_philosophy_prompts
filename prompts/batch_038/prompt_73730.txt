I'm two years into a novel set in a constitutional monarchy closely modeled on Britain's, and one of my chapters has come to turn on a real procedure I stumbled across. In the UK Parliament, a bill that touches the Crown's prerogative, or the monarch's hereditary revenues and personal estates, cannot be debated until consent has been signified in advance by a minister — King's Consent — and although the sovereign grants or withholds that consent purely on government advice, critics have documented drafts altered before introduction in order to meet the palace's concerns. Yet the same sovereign must assent to whatever Parliament finally sends up (no British monarch has refused a bill since 1708), never votes, never speaks on policy, can do no legal wrong, and inherits the office precisely so that it may be held by someone with no political will worth consulting. My editor insists those two halves cannot describe one institution, and that a king who works the consent procedure to protect his own property is no constitutional monarch at all. I want to know what I would be committing to by writing him anyway.

What theory of political authority does the practice presuppose — the Crown as a genuine party whose interests the legislature must negotiate with, or the Crown as nothing more than the state's own legal personality, so that ministers are consulting themselves behind a borrowed name? Which of those does the procedure strictly require, and which would merely suit it? Is there any reading on which advance consent over the royal estates and automatic assent to everything else belong to a single coherent conception of sovereignty rather than to a contradiction papered over by convention? And if the rival view were adopted, what in the actual machinery of government would have to change?

Please take a definite position and argue for it rather than laying the options out for me to choose among; where the practice genuinely leaves the theory underdetermined, say so and then tell me which reading you would still bet on.
