QUESTION: Bernard Williams argues that an agent's reasons for action are all internal, in the sense that a true statement of the form "A has a reason to phi" must be one that A could arrive at by a sound deliberative route from the elements of his actual subjective motivational set. (a) Explain how Williams argues for this claim and what he thinks it shows about the external reasons theorist. (b) Does the internal reasons thesis make our reasons too subjective, in the sense that an agent with a corrupt or badly informed motivational set simply lacks reasons that we want to insist he has? Explain what resources Williams does or does not have for answering that charge.

ANSWER: Williams wants to show that all reasons are internal. His starting point is that if we say "A has a reason to phi," that statement has to be capable of figuring in an explanation of A's phi-ing, or of A's failure to phi. So the truth of a reason-attribution depends on something in A himself. Williams calls this A's subjective motivational set, or S, and he stresses that it is wider than a list of desires: it also includes dispositions of evaluation, patterns of emotional reaction, personal loyalties, and various projects, and elements of S can themselves be criticized when they rest on false beliefs. An internal reason statement, on his view, says that A could reach the conclusion that he should phi by a sound deliberative route from the elements of his actual S, and Williams is clear that deliberation here is broad, covering imagination, the consideration of means, the weighing of consequences, and the correction of misinformation.

Given this, an external reason statement, meaning one that says A has a reason to phi even when no route from A's actual S could get him there, has no true reading. Williams holds that such statements are either false or, if the externalist cannot say what would make them true, not really truth-apt statements at all. His discussion of the blameless amoralist shows the bite of the view: a person whose S contains nothing that moral deliberation could engage can act in ways we condemn, and yet on Williams's account it is not the case that he had a reason not to so act. We can still blame him, or find him contemptible; we simply cannot cite a reason that he had.

Because Williams is a Humean about motivation, the argument is essentially the claim that beliefs on their own never move us: an agent is moved only when a desire is present, so reasons have to be indexed to desires, and any reason not already traceable to something in S is inert. This is also why his position is sometimes confused with the thesis that sincerely judging that you ought to phi necessarily motivates you to phi. On Williams's account the internal reason statement guarantees the availability of motivation, and the ought-judgment merely reports that availability.

The "too subjective" objection says that Williams cannot handle the agent with a depraved or badly formed S, since on his view that agent has all the reasons his S supplies and no others. I think the objection partly fails. Williams permits rational criticism of elements of S that rest on false belief, and he permits that deliberation may lead an agent to relinquish a motivation he would not have had if he had thought the matter through. So what Williams is really offering is a counterfactual test: A has a reason to phi if A would be moved to phi were he fully informed and fully rational. That keeps the view from collapsing into the claim that whatever you happen to want is a reason for you, because most corrupt motivations would not survive being held up to full information and rational scrutiny.
