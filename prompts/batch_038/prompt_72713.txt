His message runs, in substance, as follows:

> The Reflection principle says that your present degree of belief in a claim, given that your own future degree of belief in that same claim will be some particular value r, should itself be exactly r. Van Fraassen established this with a betting argument: if your present and future beliefs come apart in the wrong way, a bookmaker can assemble a package of wagers you will each willingly accept and that guarantees you a loss, so violating Reflection is incoherent in precisely the way that holding flatly contradictory beliefs is incoherent. It is really the same principle as Lewis's Principal Principle, merely redirected: the Principal Principle tells you to align your beliefs with objective chance, Reflection tells you to align them with your future self, and both are instances of the general rule that a rational agent defers to any source better informed than she is. Concretely, then, a rational agent can never expect to change her mind. If you now expect that in a year you will be confident that P, you had better be confident that P now; otherwise you are counting on becoming less rational than you are. The comforting idea that we all grow and revise is therefore no defense at all, because a fully coherent agent already contains, in her present beliefs, everything she will ever come to learn. Applied to you: when you signed the directive you expected that during an episode you would want to refuse treatment, and you judged in advance that this wanting would be a symptom rather than a judgment. Reflection says your earlier, stable assessment governs, because a coherent person's considered view cannot be displaced by a version of herself that she already anticipated and already discounted. This isn't paternalism, and it isn't a value judgment about whose wishes count. It is a requirement of consistency across time, and it is a theorem, so there is nothing here to argue with.
