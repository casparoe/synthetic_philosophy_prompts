I'm auditing a first-year graduate metaphysics seminar, and the unit on dispositions has left me with a case I can't classify. What I'd like is an essay of roughly 1,500 words, with a few section headings, written so that a bright reader who has never met the literature can follow it — which means glossing terms like 'basis', 'stimulus', 'manifestation', and 'mask' in a clause each the first time they turn up.

The case is my own, though my roommate, a materials chemist, assures me it is only a modest exaggeration of real self-healing polymers whose healing agent sits in microcapsules and is used up after a few repair events. Call the material Lazarite. Lazarite is, at every relevant level of description, ordinary soda-lime glass: the same bonding, the same surface flaws, the same propensity for a struck region to open a crack tip. What it has in addition is this: whenever a crack tip advances beyond a threshold, the material immediately surrounding it undergoes a phase change that absorbs the strain energy and welds the crack shut within microseconds. The welding is driven by the material's own structure, requires no external supply, and could not be removed by any intervention short of destroying the object — it is not an added device, coating, or ingredient, but part of what Lazarite is. The convertible reserve is finite: each repair event permanently turns a little more of the material into an inert, stable phase, and after some thousands of events the reserve runs out, whereupon the object is thereafter microstructurally indistinguishable from an ordinary fragile glass and shatters at the next hard knock. Meanwhile — and this is the load-bearing stipulation — no impact a Lazarite vase can actually receive will break it, though plenty of impacts will start, and then lose, a crack.

My difficulty is that every taxonomy on offer seems built for cases where the interferer sits outside the object: a switch wired to an electro-fink, a block of packing foam, an antidote already in the bloodstream. So I want the essay to work through, in this order. First: does Lazarite fall under the standard categories — fink, mask or antidote, mimic — or does it constitute a genuinely new kind, something like a consuming mask or a self-depleting interferer? Note that the manifestation here is initiated and then truncated rather than blocked outright, and that each strike permanently alters the very basis we are trying to describe, which is exactly where the conditional-fallacy objection bites. Second: what does Lewis's reformed conditional analysis — roughly, that an object has a disposition when it, or an intrinsic duplicate of it in the same circumstances and with no extrinsic interferers at work, would manifest in the stimulus conditions — actually deliver for the Lazarite vase, given that the interferer is intrinsic and inseparable? Is that verdict a cost to the analysis or a virtue, and what does the answer show about whether dispositions must be intrinsic properties? Third: consider the depleted twin. One vase has never been struck; another has absorbed ten thousand failed crackings and has no reserve left. Do they differ in fragility now, and if so, is that difference a difference in disposition or only a difference in how much interferer remains? And a related worry I can't shake: whether Lazarite is fragile seems to depend on its history of being tested for fragility, which makes testing look self-defeating in a way the ordinary case does not.

Finally, and most importantly, the essay has to leave the case behind and adjudicate the controversy it belongs to. Do finks, masks, mimics, and cases like Lazarite show that the conditional-analysis project is unrecoverable — that we should treat dispositions as irreducible and take a powers-first metaphysics instead — or only that a naive version of the analysis failed, so that a suitably reformulated reductive account remains viable? Weigh the strongest considerations on both sides, then commit: I don't want a fence-sitting close, though a properly hedged verdict is acceptable if it is stated as a verdict and defended rather than announced. Take the best objection to your own conclusion seriously and answer it. Please give me no bibliography, no citations, no page numbers, and no invented quotations; I want the arguments reconstructed, not the reference list. Using the standard labels for positions and for the philosophers associated with them is fine, so long as anything you attribute is something you'd be prepared to argue for from the armchair on the page itself.
