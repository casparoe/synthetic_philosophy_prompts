I assess claims for a disability income scheme — about twenty cases a week, forty-five minutes each, mostly from paper medical records, and my decision determines whether someone's support continues. The rulebook's threshold word is substantial: the impairment must have a substantial effect on the person's ability to manage ordinary daily activities. There is no list, no point score, and no further definition of that word. Two problems keep biting. First, colleagues and I disagree at the edges, and refused claimants appeal, so I need reasons that hold up. Second — and this is the one I cannot handle — many files contain a long list of difficulties each of which I would individually call minor: forgets appointments, cannot stand more than ten minutes, avoids crowded shops. I have no principled way to say whether twenty small things add up to something substantial, and I suspect I am currently settling those cases by mood.

Write me a procedure I can actually run in forty-five minutes with no philosophy training: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to handle a borderline file without pretending the line is sharper than it is, how to keep my verdict consistent with a colleague's on a near-identical file, and where the procedure must stop and hand the call to a supervisor because the question is one of values rather than facts. Ground it in whatever the work on vague thresholds and borderline cases offers that survives contact with a caseload, and be honest about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume. Then run it on three files: a claimant with a single, well-documented severe limitation; the twenty-small-things file described above; and a claimant whose condition worsened in imperceptible increments over four years, with each annual reassessment finding them barely changed from the last. At least one of the three should come out with a verdict I will not like, and finish with what the procedure cannot do.
