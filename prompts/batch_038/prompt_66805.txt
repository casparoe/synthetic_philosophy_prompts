I am in the fourth year of a PhD in non-classical logic, working on the dialetheist treatment of the semantic paradoxes, and I have been asked to contribute a short piece to our department's annual anthology, which by tradition is entirely comic. Every year I attempt the same thing and it fails in the same way: the moment a reader hears that a view countenances true contradictions, they conclude that the view must be trivialist nonsense, and nothing I write dislodges that. My supervisor's diagnosis is that this position cannot be conveyed by an argument, only by an artifact — by a document in which somebody is actually living with a contradiction and being extremely careful about it. I would like to try that, and I would like it to be good enough to hand to a first-year logic class as a warm-up before we get to the formal material.

So: please write an internal administrative circular, roughly 750–850 words, in numbered clauses, complete with a reference number, a date, a distribution list and a subject line, from the legal directorate of an invented mid-sized public agency, announcing to staff that two provisions of the agency's own enabling statute have been found to be flatly incompatible, that the directorate has nonetheless determined both to remain in force, and setting out how the agency is to proceed in the meantime. The voice should be deadpan bureaucratic English — the register of a memo about parking permits and expense claims — and the drafter should be an earnest, precise, somewhat stiff lawyer who has evidently absorbed the dialetheist literature and is implementing it as policy without ever being permitted to say so in those words. No winking at the reader, no asides, no real people; the target is the position's self-presentation as disciplined and conservative rather than any individual. Please invent the two provisions yourself and make them a genuine logical contradiction rather than a mere conflict of duties, since that distinction is part of what I want the piece to smuggle in: two rules pulling in different directions can be ranked or prioritised, but a proposition and its negation cannot. Do resist Monty Python naming conventions — the plausibility is the joke.

The humour has to come from getting the logic right rather than from bureaucrats behaving badly, so a few constraints. First, the circular must explain, in administrative language with the technical vocabulary suppressed, why the coexistence of the two provisions does not licence arbitrary conclusions, and that explanation must be a real rule about what may be derived from a contradiction rather than a hand-wave; I would also like the strain of maintaining such a rule to show somewhere in the document, since the strain is the honest part of the story. Second, include a clause disposing of a junior colleague who has already exploited the situation, and let the exploit have the shape of Curry's paradox rather than the liar — a self-referential conditional of the form "if this circular binds, then …", which the usual treatment of gluts does not reach, so the drafter is forced to reach for something considerably less comfortable. Third, include a clause concerning a provision that purports to describe its own status, which obliges the drafter to deny of that clause the very thing the rest of the document is doing: the revenge problem in a blazer. Fourth, include a clause on who determines which contradictions are admissible, and let it be transparently question-begging in a way the drafter does not notice. Finally, append a short annex — a single paragraph — containing a reply from a classically minded deputy director whose objection sounds like the naive explosion objection but in fact lands somewhere the drafter has no clean answer to. The result should be carried along by its register for a reader who has never heard of paraconsistency, and should make a specialist laugh rather than wince.
