I've been sitting in on an epistemic game theory reading group and I jotted down my gut verdicts on the stock cases, and they dont add up. Muddy children: I accept it completely, including that the father's announcement does something real even though every kid already knew at least one forehead was muddy. Two generals: I accept that no chain of acknowledgments ever amounts to common knowledge, but I also think a general who has swapped fifty confirmations and then refuses to attack isn't making a mistake, and whoever says he is has confused a formal ideal with a practical one. Finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma with a known last round: the unraveling convinces me, I think defecting from round one is right. Centipede game, the one where players alternate taking a growing pot or passing it along: same logic, and I reject it outright, I don't believe taking on the first move is what the two of them should do. Surprise exam: I'm certain the teacher can pull it off, since it happens every semester, so the students' proof has a bad step, but I can't find it. Aumann: I accept that rational people with a common prior can't agree to disagree, though I think that's the least interesting part of it, and I'd still say two honest friends who've seen all the same evidence shouldn't disagree even if they don't share a prior. And the one that bothers me most: I don't believe any actual person can hold more than three or four nested "I know that you know that I know" beliefs, yet I accept the muddy children argument with twenty muddy kids.

My tentative principle is that common knowledge comes from a common source, so a public event supplies it and a private message chain never does no matter how long, and that the iterated stuff in muddy children is something everyone is *in a position* to run rather than something anyone actually runs. Test that against the list: does it survive contact, what does it have to be bent into to survive, and which of my verdicts does it end up contradicting rather than explaining? I'd rather hear that one of them has to go than watch the principle sprout epicycles. And tell me what it predicts about a case I haven't given you, like a referee's whistle heard by everyone in a stadium where nobody checks that anyone else heard it.
