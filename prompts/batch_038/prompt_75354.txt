I serve on the arts commission of a town of about twelve thousand, and this spring I have to write the recommendation that decides which of two programs survives the next funding cycle. I want to get the call right, and I have come to suspect that the ordinary way of making it — which one gets more bodies through the door — is answering a question I shouldn't be asking.

The first program is a semi-professional theater company. For nine years it has staged new plays about local subjects: the mill closure, the pain clinic out on the state route, the lawsuit over the reservoir. The plays are commissioned from a writer two states over who comes and does a week of interviews; the leads are paid, which the artistic director defends on the grounds that nobody should be expected to feel things for free. The productions are good. People cry. There is a long standing ovation. Our post-show survey shows roughly eight in ten attendees reporting that they left with a sharper sense of the problem. When we looked at who actually buys the tickets, though, it was overwhelmingly the same two hundred people who already volunteer at the food bank, sit on the school committee, and give blood. The people a play is *about* almost never come. In nine years I cannot name one local organization that gained a volunteer after a play about it. The measurable effects have been money and attention from people who were already sympathetic — one donor funded a clinic annex after seeing the pain-clinic play — rather than any new disposition in anyone.

The second program is a monthly gathering in the town square. There is no stage, no seating, no program, no ticket. A brass band that anyone may sit in with, one long table, speeches from whoever wants to make one, a lot of bad dancing. Attendance is erratic, it is cliquey, several residents have told me they find the idea of performing in front of their neighbors mortifying and won't go near it, and the regional arts council calls it amateurish and says it denies working actors an audience. Yet the same people keep coming back, and a surprising number of them turn up later on the fire committee, the library board, the youth coaching rota.

My colleague on the board says the two are just entertainment with different production values, and that the only difference is who is standing on the platform. He also has a case that cuts against me: a girl who was apathetic about everything acted in the school production of the mill play and is now organizing a youth council. He says that shows watching and performing can convert people; I say she may well have been that way already and the play merely gave her somewhere to put it. Neither of us can settle the matter.

The grant we are renewing scores applications on audience reach, defined as tickets distributed. That measure favors the company structurally: you can hand out six hundred tickets for a run, and the square has no tickets at all, so if we report the second program honestly, as best we can count it, we look smaller than we are.

Three questions. First: is there a difference in kind between what happens to a person in the audience of a well-made play about a real local problem and what happens to a person at the table in the square, or is my colleague right that these are one activity in two formats? Second: if there is a difference in kind, does it give me a principled reason to cut the company — given the girl who now runs the youth council, and given the clinic annex, which was real money that did real good? Third: what should I do about the reach metric — report the square in a way that flatters it, refuse to compete on that measure, or let the measure decide? I would like a definite recommendation on the funding, and I would rather have the argument than a list of considerations.

Lead with your bottom line — the funding recommendation and your answer to the first question — in your opening sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the situation.
