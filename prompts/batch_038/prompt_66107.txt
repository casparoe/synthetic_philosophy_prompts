I teach a graduate seminar on diachronic rationality, and I have lately spent more time than I expected in the methods sections of experimental decision theory. One design choice recurs so constantly that it is treated as mere housekeeping, and I want it treated instead as a piece of philosophy.

In the standard laboratory procedure, a subject answers thirty or forty decision problems — lotteries, multiple price lists, allocation choices — and at the end a single one of them is selected by a die roll, and only that problem's payoff is paid. The stated rationale is technical: paying for all of them would let an early outcome change the subject's circumstances for later ones, would introduce wealth and portfolio effects, and would permit hedging across problems, whereas paying for one at random makes every problem equally worth answering seriously.

What conception of the choosing agent does this procedure presuppose? Does it require that preferences be borne by isolated decisions rather than by sequences, bundles, or plans? Consider a subject whose preferences cycle and who would be pumpable if paid for all forty problems: the payment rule removes the pump without changing the subject at all. Is that a reason to think the design measures preference more cleanly, or a reason to think it cannot measure the thing my seminar is actually about — coherence across time?

The tension I cannot resolve is this. The same literature elsewhere treats narrow bracketing as a bias, and several of its best-known arguments about small-stakes risk aversion depend on the subject evaluating a gamble against total wealth rather than in isolation; meanwhile, direct comparisons find that subjects sometimes choose differently under random-lottery payment than when a single task is played for real on its own. Does the design presuppose that agents bracket narrowly, or that bracketing is irrelevant to what they choose? It cannot surely be both.

Please reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. Distinguish what the procedure strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with — I suspect a purely practical story about stake size, fatigue, and budget would license much of the same practice — and say what an experimental design would have to look like if it treated diachronic coherence as the object of measurement rather than as an enabling assumption.

Finally, tell me which part of your reconstruction you hold most confidently, and what would change your mind: which argument or piece of evidence would push you to a different verdict about what this practice commits us to, and where your reading is most likely to have gone wrong — in particular by locating a substantive philosophical commitment where there is only convenience. Please write in American English.
