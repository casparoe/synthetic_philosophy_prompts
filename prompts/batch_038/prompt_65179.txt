THE DRAFT

Title: Precision, not prediction

§1. The standard pitch for predictive processing is that the brain is a prediction engine. Perception isn't a readout of the senses; it's the top of a hierarchy of guesses, and what the senses contribute is error — the mismatch between guess and input. The system's job is to shrink that mismatch, either by revising the guess (perception) or by arranging the world so the guess comes true (action). The free energy principle generalizes this past brains: anything that persists long enough to count as a thing at all, anything whose states can be statistically separated from the rest of the world by a Markov blanket, can be described as minimizing a quantity — variational free energy — that bounds how surprised it is by its own sensory states. Defenders often present this less as a hypothesis you could falsify than as a formal constraint on there being a system at all, which is part of why critics say it explains everything and therefore nothing.

§2. The classic objection is the dark room: if the aim is to minimize surprise, the optimum is a padded cell. No input, no error, nothing to be wrong about. The reply is that what an agent minimizes when it acts isn't free energy but expected free energy, which splits into a pragmatic term (how well a candidate action lines up with the outcomes the system is disposed to expect) and an epistemic term (how much uncertainty a candidate action is expected to resolve). Curiosity isn't bolted onto the picture, it's the second term. So the dark room objection is a red herring: it assumes the agent wants to be unsurprised now, when what it wants is to be unsurprised across the futures it can bring about, and the cheapest way to secure that is to go and look. I think that reply basically works. But notice what it costs. Once you grant the epistemic term, the substantive question stops being "what does the system predict?" and becomes "what does the system expect to learn, from where, and how much is that worth?" That's a question about how a limited resource gets allocated.

§3. The resource is precision. Prediction error isn't treated uniformly; each error signal is weighted by an estimate of its reliability — its precision, roughly inverse variance. High-precision errors get to revise the model, low-precision errors are written off as noise. Attention, on the usual reading, just is the optimization of these weightings, and the neuromodulatory systems are the standard candidates for implementing them. Here's the move I want to make. If precision determines which mismatches are allowed to force a revision, then controlling what a system has evidence of doesn't require changing any of its predictions. You only have to change what it counts as worth listening to. Two consequences. First, on this picture an agent's values — the states it acts to keep true — aren't a separate desiderata module hooked onto inference; they're the entrenched, high-precision priors about the conditions it expects to find itself in. Second, and this is the interesting one, precision-setting is asymmetric in a way belief-setting isn't. A change in the model can be undone by better evidence. A change in what counts as evidence can't be undone by evidence, because evidence only carries the force the weighting gives it. That asymmetry is what I mean by authority, and I think it's what lets predictive processing say something about forms of epistemic harm that belief-centered frameworks struggle with. Undermining someone's grip on her own experience isn't mainly a matter of getting her to adopt false beliefs; it's a matter of getting her own error signals down-weighted, so that nothing she registers can dislodge what she's been told. The victim of sustained gaslighting often still has accurate predictions about what happened. What she's lost is the standing to treat her own registration of it as evidence.

§4. That's also where I want to say the empirical content of the whole framework lives. The sweeping claims about self-organization are the part nobody can test; the precision-allocation claims are local, and they're the ones doing the actual explanatory work in the clinical literature — the thought that psychosis involves aberrant precision, so that ordinary noise acquires the weight of a revelation, or that autism involves a different balance between the strength of priors and the pull of input. If that's right, the deflationary reading of the free energy principle and the ambitious reading are aimed at different parts of the theory, and both can be true at once.

§5. The objection I expect. Precision is set by higher-level expectations about reliability — but what sets the precision of those? Either the hierarchy bottoms out in weightings fixed by the body and by evolutionary history, in which case my talk of authority collapses back into physiology and there's nothing social about it, or it never bottoms out. And separately: I may be sliding between precision-as-parameter and precision-as-trust. A weighting in a statistical model is not a normative endorsement, and nothing in the mathematics licenses the jump from "this error signal is down-weighted" to "this subject has been deprived of standing."

§6. Where I want to take it. If precision can be set from outside the skin — by other people, by the design of an interface, by the tempo and format in which a workplace returns feedback — then the extended-cognition question stops being about where the model lives and becomes about where the weightings get set. Interface design, on this reading, is precision engineering, which is a considerably less innocent description of it than "user experience."
