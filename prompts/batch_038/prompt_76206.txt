I am writing the framework chapter of a dissertation on accuracy-first epistemology, and before engaging with any particular proposal I want to fix the target: a list of requirements that any accuracy-first framework — one that treats closeness to the truth as the fundamental epistemic value and tries to derive the norms of belief from it — must satisfy to count as adequate. The purpose is not a survey but a standard of assessment I can apply consistently. Specifically, I want a framework good enough to (i) replace coherence-based and Dutch-book considerations as the primary justification of the probabilistic norms, and (ii) be extended beyond single credences — to full belief, to imprecise or set-valued doxastic states, and to self-locating belief — without the extension changing the verdicts the framework already delivers in the simple cases.

Deliverable: a numbered list of requirements. For each, state precisely what it demands ("must vindicate probabilism" is too crude; say what a vindication would have to look like, and what would show that it had failed), give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable given the purpose above or is a preference of a particular camp. Where it is negotiable, say what a framework gives up by dropping it. Then — the part I most want — identify any subsets of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. For each such subset, spell out the conflict concretely rather than announcing it, and say which member is most plausibly given up. Finally, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding candidates, and why.

I expect that some of what I have jotted down is wrong-headed: a smuggled commitment of one camp, two requirements wearing a trench coat, or a desired conclusion disguised as a constraint. Prune the list, correct it, and tell me what is missing from it. Argue with it rather than ratifying it, and flag explicitly which of my entries are of the last two kinds.

My draft:

1. Veritism. Accuracy is the only fundamental epistemic value, and every other epistemic norm must be derivable from accuracy considerations alone.
2. Non-pragmatism. No step in the framework may appeal to the agent's practical interests, stakes, or goals; the justification of a belief norm must be purely epistemic.
3. Dominance. For any incoherent credal state there is a coherent alternative that is strictly more accurate at every world, so that incoherence is a demonstrable defect rather than a stipulation.
4. Unique measure. There is a single privileged way of measuring inaccuracy, and the framework's results do not depend on an arbitrary choice among candidate measures.
5. Immodesty. An agent's own credal state maximizes expected accuracy as judged by her own lights.
6. Extensionality. The accuracy of a credal state depends only on the credences it assigns and on the world at which it is evaluated, not on how the state is described or on what else the agent believes.
7. Additivity. The inaccuracy of a credence function over a set of propositions is the sum of the inaccuracies of its component credences.
8. Feasibility. The framework does not require of a finite agent what she cannot do — in particular it does not demand credence one in every logical truth.
9. No brute accuracy. The framework explains why accuracy matters rather than assuming that it does.

Three further requirements I am more confident about, and one I invented and distrust:

10. The bridge. If the framework's central argument is dominance-flavored, it must say why being dominated is a defect for an agent who does not care about accuracy, or who knowingly takes the dominated option because she likes it. Concretely: an agent chooses between two ways of distributing her confidence over the outcomes of an event, one of which is strictly less accurate than the other at every world, but she prefers the worse one for reasons of her own. The framework owes an account of whether she is irrational, merely unwise, or neither, and of what the difference between those amounts to.
11. Stability under extension. Extending the framework to full belief by a threshold rule, to imprecise doxastic states, or to centered propositions must not overturn or undercut the norms it delivered for plain credences. If the extension forces precision on an agent whose evidence underdetermines her credences, I want to know whether that is a cost or a virtue, and on what grounds one would decide.
12. Representational neutrality (mine, and I suspect it is either too strong or unsatisfiable). Two agents with identical total evidence and identical underlying epistemic situations, one of whom represents her opinions on a fine-grained algebra and the other on the coarse algebra obtained by merging cells, should not receive different normative verdicts merely because of that difference in carving. My worry is that this collides with 4: any candidate measure scores a credence function relative to a fixed representation, so merging or splitting cells changes the numbers and hence the verdicts. Either tell me the collision is real and say which of the two gives way, or show me a reading of neutrality that a measure-based framework can actually meet.

I already suspect that 1 and 9 are in tension, and that 2 is doing more work than its proponents admit, since dominance reasoning is decision-theoretic machinery. I may be wrong about both.

Keep the scorecard short. At the end, in a few sentences per contender, say how the three main approaches fare against your final list: deriving the norms from dominance considerations, deriving them from expected-accuracy-maximizing considerations, and the program of fixing a unique measure by imposing structural constraints on it. If a contender fails only because of a requirement you classified as negotiable, say so explicitly — I would rather have that than a false negative.
