The central chapter of my dissertation has stalled on a proposal from my supervisor, and I would like a check on it before my prospectus defense.

The proposal concerns the familiar objection to agent-relative constraints: that it is incoherent to forbear from violating a rule when one violation would prevent five. My supervisor says the objection is not really about value at all; it only bites if we grant that violations form a single quantity an agent can be asked to minimize. But acts are not delivered to us at one canonical grain, and how finely an act-type is individuated is something a deontologist may settle on independent grounds. At the right grain, my killing one and the five killings I could have prevented are not two available amounts of the same thing, and the pressure to minimize evaporates. She anticipates that this resembles the long-running objection to double effect on which a sufficiently fine description of an act swallows its consequences and makes everything intended — and she thinks that resemblance is a feature rather than a bug.

Give me your credence that the reply is both correct and non-question-begging: that a deontologist who adopts it has an answer to the objection rather than a restatement of her own position. Decompose it — the probability that act-individuation can be made fine-grained in the way she needs without every act collapsing into its consequences; the probability that some principled, independently motivated criterion fixes the grain rather than the grain being chosen to block the argument; the probability that the objection loses its force once the grain is fixed; and any further term you think belongs. One line of justification per term, then the combination, then which term the final number is most sensitive to and what argument would move it. End with \answer{0.XX}.
