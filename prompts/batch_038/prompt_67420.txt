I work as a regulatory affairs analyst at a mid-sized medical-device manufacturer, and four times a year I am the person who signs the accuracy attestation on our post-market clinical follow-up dossier. The one going out this Friday runs to roughly 4,900 discrete factual assertions: device serial ranges, dates of procedure, investigator names, adverse-event narratives transcribed from site reports, follow-up intervals, and so on. My signature covers a sentence to the effect that each factual assertion in this submission is accurate to the best of my knowledge and belief. I do check them — not by hand in every case, but each one passes through a verification step that I designed or approved, and if you pointed me at any single line and asked me whether it is right, I would say yes without hedging. Separately, our quality function keeps a log of what the notified-body auditors and our own internal review actually find after submission, and over the last six years the number of defects traced to a submitted dossier has ranged from one to four. It has never been zero. So I also firmly believe, of this Friday's dossier, that at least one of its 4,900 assertions is wrong. I just cannot tell you which.

I spent about three hours last night arguing with a friend who did a philosophy degree and now works in data governance, and she thinks my position is not merely uncomfortable but flatly irrational. Her first claim was arithmetic: if each assertion is, say, 99.9% likely to be correct and the assertions are anywhere near independent, then the probability that all 4,900 are correct is well under one percent, so I am committed to believing there is an error while also believing, of each individual assertion, that there is not — and those cannot both be believed, since together they contradict. Her second claim was practical and, I admit, more worrying: because I believe at least one assertion is false, the attestation I am about to sign is something I believe to be untrue, and signing it is therefore a lie I have dressed up as diligence. Her proposed fix was that I should stop holding outright beliefs about the individual lines altogether and keep only probabilities in my head, and, for the document itself, flag the ten or so assertions I am least confident about and hedge them explicitly. I objected that this is arbitrary — why those ten, when the defect could sit anywhere among the other 4,890, and my confidence ranking is really a ranking of which verification steps were automated rather than which lines are likelier to be wrong. She said that an arbitrary hedge is still better than an inconsistent belief set, and that inconsistency is the one thing I am not permitted. I said that if I may not believe each line and may not believe there is an error, she has left me nothing to believe at all, and that a regulator who told me to work that way would get no dossiers from anybody. We never resolved it, and I have to sign on Friday.

Please take a side rather than laying the options out for me. I want to know, first, whether my friend is right that my belief set is irrational — and if you think the argument dissolves once some distinction is drawn (between believing and being disposed to assert, between what I owe each line and what I owe the whole, between the epistemic question and the signing question), say which distinction and commit to it rather than listing candidates. Second, I want a definite recommendation about Friday: what I should sign, whether the attestation wording should change and to what, and what I should do with the six-year defect log, which is currently an informal spreadsheet nobody outside quality has seen. If you think her probability calculation is doing illegitimate work, show where it breaks, not merely that it feels harsh.
