I drafted the following argument for a workshop session on collective forecasting, where my assignment was to explain why crowds of individually informed forecasters can nonetheless go badly wrong. As a critique of the naive "wisdom of crowds" view it played well, and several colleagues found it persuasive. Then one of them observed that the same reasoning, if sound, shows that nobody should ever treat anybody else's conduct as evidence about anything at all — which would take down market prices, replication, and ordinary testimony along with the forecasters. I cannot see how to block that without surrendering the critique I set out to make. The argument as it stands:

(1) A rational agent chooses the action that maximizes expected utility conditional on her total evidence, which here consists of her private signal together with her observation of her predecessors' actions.

(2) In the standard sequential herding model, the public evidence carried by the action history eventually becomes strong enough to outweigh any private signal an individual agent might receive; from that point on she takes the action the crowd has taken whatever her own signal says.

(3) An agent whose action does not vary with her private signal transmits nothing about that signal to those who observe her.

(4) So once a cascade begins, the action history stops accumulating information: every later agent ignores her signal, and all the private information held by later agents is discarded.

(5) Therefore the eventual belief of an arbitrarily large crowd rests on the signals of the first two or three people and nothing more; group size buys no additional accuracy once the cascade has begun.

(6) But nothing in (3) or (4) depends on the special structure of the model. Whenever an action is influenced by what others have already done, it fails to be a clean read-out of the actor's own evidence, and to that extent it is degraded as evidence for anyone else.

(7) And no human action is uninfluenced by others: we are all downstream of testimony, of prices, of citation counts, of applause, of the length of the queue outside a restaurant.

(8) So no one's conduct is ever clean evidence about anything, and treating the behavior of others as evidence is never rational.

(9) (8) is intolerable, since it makes reading a market price, repeating an experiment, and believing a colleague's report of what she observed all irrational.

(10) Therefore (1) is false: agents facing common uncertainty under sequential observation are not in fact rational, and "rational herding" is a contradiction in terms.

For what it is worth, I would rather keep (1), because if I stop assuming that agents condition on everything they know, I lose any way to distinguish herding from panic or fashion; and (9) is not negotiable. I am also attached to (5), since that is the substantive claim the session was supposed to deliver.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a defense.

- Identify the step at which the argument actually goes wrong, and state precisely what is being confused there — an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, the substitution of a special case for the general claim, a slide from degree to kind, a descriptive/normative slide, or something else. If there are two independent breaks, say so and rank them by how much work each does.
- Explain why that step looked innocent to me when I wrote it. I want an account of the error that explains its appeal, not merely a label for it.
- Price the alternatives. For each of (2), (3), (4), (5), (6), and (7), say what it would cost to reject it and what would be left of my workshop critique afterward.
- Give the minimal repair that preserves (5) and (9) while stopping short of (8). If no repair preserves (5), say so plainly and tell me what weaker claim about crowds I should be advancing instead.
- Note what the argument assumes about the underlying model — what agents observe, how informative their private signals can be, whether they know each other's payoffs and each other's models — that a critic could reasonably contest, and say whether contesting it changes your diagnosis.

Please stay concrete about the mechanism of the error. Do not retreat to the observation that the cascade model is a stylization: every model is a stylization, and that alone does not explain how a valid-looking inference yields (8).

Close with your diagnosis in machine-readable form on a separate final line, exactly in the pattern \answer{(n): phrase}, where n is the number of the single step whose rejection does the most work and the phrase is at most ten words naming the confusion.
