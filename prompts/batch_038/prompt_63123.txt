My aunt retired in June after thirty-nine years as the senior proofreader at a university press, and the department is giving her a dinner next month; I have been asked to deliver the toast. What almost nobody in that room knows is that since she was twenty-two she has kept a small ruled book in the manner Franklin describes in his autobiography: a fixed list of thirteen virtues she set for herself, one taken up per week, each day's lapses marked with a small black tick, four cycles a year for nearly half a century. Last spring she handed me the final volume. It contains a single page. She abandoned the project years ago in precisely the sense of Franklin's story about the man who takes his new axe to the smith to have the whole of it ground as bright as the edge, wearies of turning the wheel, and decides he prefers it speckled — a judgment she has repeated to our family in some form for thirty years.

Three constraints follow, and they are where I am stuck. She forbids me to say that she attained, or came near to attaining, what the books were for; she considers that both false and an insult to the standard. She equally forbids the consolation she expects from an American room — that she was a perfectionist who at last learned to be kind to herself. On her account the project was never about how she felt, and relinquishing the attempt at flawlessness revised her estimate of what a person can carry, not the requirement itself. The third difficulty is one she flagged in the margin: Franklin's observation that a man who had thoroughly conquered pride would probably then be proud of his humility applies, she wrote, to any compliment I pay her for her modesty.

Half the room will be editors, printers, and authors who knew her only as exacting and faintly alarming; the rest is family, including two teenage grandchildren who have never read a word of Franklin. Write the toast: five minutes, roughly seven hundred words, one allusion to the axe that lands for people who have never heard the story, no lecture on Franklin, and an ending in affection rather than in a lesson.
