I've talked myself into a pretty firm view about updating on soft evidence and I want it stress-tested, not validated.

The view: Jeffrey conditioning — probability kinematics — is the correct and basically unique rule for revising when your evidence doesn't come as a certainty. You're told something that fixes new probabilities over a partition; you adopt those, you hold all your conditional probabilities *within* the cells fixed (the rigidity assumption), and everything else falls out. I also think the Judy Benjamin problem is not the refutation it's usually treated as. Van Fraassen's informant reports a conditional, and my line is that the testimony is simply incomplete: it constrains P(picture | red) or whatever, but it says nothing about what your new probability for the antecedent itself should be, so no rule whatsoever is going to hand you a unique posterior. The puzzle is generated by demanding one. Once you're explicit that the antecedent probability was left untouched, rigid Jeffrey gives the right answer, and the reflection-principle answer plus the minimum-relative-entropy answer start to look like unmotivated patches bolted on to make the numbers come out interesting. I also have a vague sense that there's a Diaconis–Zabell style symmetry/exchangeability result that pins Jeffrey's rule down uniquely among candidates, which would mean its not an arbitrary choice — but I'm fuzzy on what that result actually assumes.

So: what would count against this? Not "reasonable people disagree." I want the defeaters.

1. Where does rigidity fail on its own terms? Give me cases where freezing the within-cell conditionals produces something I'd have to call flatly wrong, and say for each whether it's a genuine counterexample or whether I'm entitled to redescribe what the testimony constrained.

2. Order dependence. If I receive two pieces of soft evidence in sequence, does my rule commute? My guess is no in general. How damaging is that — is non-commutativity a bug that some rival rule actually avoids, or does every rule that takes the content of soft evidence seriously inherit it? If the latter, I want that said, because it changes how much weight this objection carries.

3. The symmetry result. What exactly does exchangeability buy, and what would my evidence or the situation have to look like for it to stop applying? I suspect I'm leaning on it much harder than it can bear, and I'd rather find that out here.

4. A frequency check on a variant I made up. Urn with 100 balls: 25 red-wood, 25 red-plastic, 10 blue-wood, 40 blue-plastic. So P(red) = 0.5, P(wood | red) = 0.5, P(wood | blue) = 0.2, and P(wood) = 0.35. An informant tells me P(wood | red) = 0.8. Rigid Jeffrey says: keep P(red) = 0.5, keep P(wood | blue) = 0.2, so P(wood) goes to 0.5. Now suppose I've separately calibrated this informant over 200 past reports and the record shows that when she makes statements of that form, the situation typically turns out to be one where P(red) is more like 0.65 — she's telling me something about red too, indirectly. Is that calibration data a defeater for my rule, or is it just evidence that I mis-specified what her testimony constrained? Be honest about whether my "the testimony was underspecified" move is doing unfalsifiable work here, because it feels like it might be.

5. Sort these into what a committed probability-kinematics person would concede as genuinely counting against them versus what they'd wave off, and rank the full set from fatal to merely awkward.

6. And if your honest conclusion is that the view is heavily insulated — that any counterexample gets absorbed by re-reading what the evidence "really" said — then say that plainly, and tell me whether that insulation is fatal for a rule that's supposed to be normative or whether that's just what a normative rule looks like when the input is genuinely ambiguous.
