I'm seventy-one years old, a retired insurance adjuster, and for the last four years I've been putting together a book about the spring of 1953 in the county where I grew up — specifically a fire at the grain elevator that March that killed two men, and the argument that went on for a year afterward about who owned the wheat that was still sitting in the bins. The county historical society has agreed to print it for the sesquicentennial next spring. I never went to college and I've never taken a history class, though I suspect thirty years of settling claims left me with habits that are either an advantage here or a trap, and I can't tell which. I've hit a wall, and I want to describe it as precisely as I can, because my family's position is that I'm ruining a good story out of stubbornness.

What I have is four things. First, my cousin Dale, who is ninety-one and was nineteen at the time and had started work at the elevator about two weeks before the fire; he told me the whole thing at length in 2019 and then told it somewhat differently in 2023, and in his version the two men who died were elevator hands. Second, a woman named Ruth Albright, who was six years old that March but who recalls standing on the running board of her father's truck watching the elevator burn, and whose account is full of particulars — the name of the sheriff's dog, the color of a coat, the fact that the man who came out of the office with a book under his arm walked with a bad leg. Third, an unsigned half-page write-up in the Centerville Reporter from the following month, which I read on microfilm at the library. Fourth, a payroll book from the elevator company covering that month, which lists eleven names, and neither of the two dead men is among them. In 2021 I learned two things that undid the shape I'd built out of the first three. Ruth Albright's mother and Dale's wife were sisters, Ruth was raised within earshot of that story at a table where Dale was regularly sitting, and the Reporter piece uses a turn of phrase — the elevator "lit up like a lantern" — that I have heard Ruth use and that I now believe Ruth got from her mother. So what I had been counting as three accounts pointing the same way may be one account, repeated for seventy years, in three voices. Meanwhile the payroll book points the other way, and when I put it to Dale he said the new men were paid in cash for the first month and wouldn't be on the books. That sounds reasonable. It also happens to be exactly what he would need to say for his version to survive, and I have noticed I'm inclined to accept it.

There is also a standing fight between Dale and me about what you do with a man you've caught out. His rule, which he says he picked up in the army, is that a man who gets a date wrong has probably got the rest of it wrong too, and you drop the whole account. Mine is the opposite: dates are hard, dates don't matter, what matters is whether the shape of the thing holds. But I've caught myself applying my rule unevenly — I excuse Ruth the dog and the bad leg because I like her account and it costs me nothing, and I refuse Dale his cash explanation because his account costs me something. And then Dale makes an argument I don't know how to answer: nobody invents a limp, so somebody saw it, and whoever saw it was standing there, and standing there is the only reason to believe any of it in the first place. Separately, I've been keeping a spreadsheet in which every individual claim gets a number from one to five for how well it's supported, and I average each person's numbers, and I've been using those averages to decide what to believe on the claims I have no way of checking. My daughter, who teaches fourth grade, looked at it over Thanksgiving and told me I was wearing my own preferences as a costume. That stung and I haven't been able to argue with it.

Please answer in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths. "The short answer": about a hundred words telling me plainly what to do with the book. "What I've actually got myself into": about two hundred words — I want you to say what this situation is a case of, in whatever terms you consider correct, because I don't have the words for it and I'm confident the words exist. "The four things": about four hundred words, taking Dale, Ruth, the Reporter piece, and the payroll book one at a time and telling me how much weight each of them can still carry after what I found out in 2021. "Dale's rule and my rule": about two hundred words on which of us is closer to right, or whether both of us are wrong, and what I could say to him that he'd actually hear. "The limp, and the spreadsheet": about two hundred words on whether his argument about details nobody would invent works, and whether my averaging method can be rescued in some other form or should be deleted. "What goes in the book": about a hundred and fifty words of concrete instruction, including whether I should print the parts I cannot settle and, if so, in what words.
