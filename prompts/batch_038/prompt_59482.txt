I am a staff engineer on the platform team at a mid-size infrastructure company. My training is in distributed systems rather than philosophy, though I have been reading in the philosophy of science on my own time, and one argument has stuck with me: the observation that the history of science contains a long list of theories that were once impressive and empirically successful and are now regarded as false, together with the inference that our current best theories are likely headed for the same list, and the various replies to that inference about which parts of a theory survive its replacement. I want to publish a satirical piece about this on our internal engineering blog, written for an audience of software engineers who mostly have not encountered the debate but who live with deprecation churn every day. Please write it for me.

Premise: scientific theories are maintained as software packages by an invented standards body — something along the lines of an Ontology Maintenance Consortium, though a better name is welcome — which publishes a versioning and deprecation policy for them, complete with semantic versioning of ontological commitments, support tiers, long-term support windows, end-of-life dates, deprecation notices, and migration guides.

Form: a single mock policy document of roughly 900–1,100 words, in deadpan corporate documentation prose, with numbered sections, one table, and an FAQ. It should read like something genuinely posted on an internal wiki.

Sections I would like:

1. Purpose and scope, including an explicitly weaselly statement of what the consortium does and does not warrant to users.
2. A versioning policy defining patch, minor, and major releases of a theory. This section should carry the philosophical weight. It must contain, without labeling them, two rival provisions: one that warrants only externally observable behavior and disclaims everything about internals as an implementation detail replaceable without notice; and a competing one under which a major release may replace the implementation wholesale provided the public interface is preserved, with some stated criterion for what counts as preserved. The tension between the two provisions should be visible and left unresolved.
3. A deprecation table covering at least five real historical theories — phlogiston, caloric, and the luminiferous ether at minimum, plus two of your choosing — each with a status such as deprecated, removed, or unmaintained-but-still-linked, and a note on whether a migration guide exists. Include dates only where you are confident they are right; otherwise leave them out.
4. One entry where no migration guide can be written at all, because the superseded release's identifiers no longer map onto anything in its replacement, so the consortium cannot coherently file a defect report against it and can only mark it unsupported. The joke should come from the paperwork failing, not from anyone remarking on why.
5. One reverted deprecation — a release whose support was withdrawn and later reinstated — and the awkwardness this creates for anyone reasoning inductively from the consortium's withdrawal record.
6. An FAQ in the voice of a support team that has answered the same questions too many times, including: why users should not infer from the graveyard of withdrawn releases that the current flagship will also be withdrawn; the counter-answer that a withdrawal only counts as evidence of a defect if the withdrawn release was actually successful in the respects the policy warrants; and whether the consortium warrants that current internals describe anything at all.
7. A closing section in which the policy, applied to itself and to its own flagship release, produces an end-of-life date, stated flatly and without commentary, followed by a single line on whether this document falls within its own versioning scope.

Constraints:

- Do not name any philosopher, do not use the terms scientific realism, anti-realism, pessimistic induction, or incommensurability, and do not put any words in the mouth of a real person. Real theory names from the history of science are fine and wanted; the people, ticket numbers, review boards, and internal metrics should be invented, and the invented jargon should be original rather than borrowed from the philosophical literature.
- The target is a practice and an inference, not an individual. The humor must come from encoding the actual arguments correctly, so that a philosopher of science recognizes the positions built into the versioning rules and finds the satire fair rather than a straw man. Where accuracy and a laugh conflict, choose accuracy and find a funnier accurate version.
- Stay deadpan throughout: no winking, no asides to the reader, nothing flagged as a punchline.
- Keep the internal rules consistent. Where the consortium grants itself an exception, show it being granted in a footnote rather than silently breaking the rules.
