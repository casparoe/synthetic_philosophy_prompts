I have been asked to circulate my position on character in advance of a graduate seminar in moral psychology, and I would like a consistency audit of the package before I send it. My commitments:

(a) Virtues are global traits: a courageous person is disposed to act courageously across a wide and heterogeneous range of situations, and it is precisely this robustness that makes character an object of prediction, of education, and of praise.

(b) The classic situationist studies — Milgram's obedience experiments, Darley and Batson's seminary study, Isen and Levin's study of people who had unexpectedly found a dime — are methodologically sound, and what they show generalizes: in ordinary agents, situational factors do most of the explanatory work we had been assigning to dispositions.

(c) The replication crisis gives me no reason to soften (b). My methodological skepticism is aimed at priming effects and findings of that sort, not at these paradigms.

(d) Situationism accordingly completes rather than threatens Aristotelian virtue ethics: on Aristotle's own account character is built by habituation under the right laws and institutions, so the virtue ethicist should be the person most interested in the design of situations.

(e) Situation-indexed "local traits" of the kind Doris proposes are no refuge. They license no interesting predictions, and they cannot support the claim that the virtues are unified in a single character.

(f) The people who went all the way in Milgram's experiments are blameworthy. That they were ordinary rather than monstrous is the finding, not an excuse; the lesson of the study is that we cannot trust our own characters, and that lesson presupposes that we have them.

(g) Praise and blame attach to an action only insofar as the action expresses the agent's character. What a room did to a person is not something that can be laid at that person's door.

Audit the set as I have stated it. I want to know which pairs genuinely pull against one another and how badly; which apparent conflicts dissolve once the claims are made precise; and, for each genuine conflict, which commitment is cheapest to surrender and what that surrender costs me elsewhere in the list. Rank the conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as a flat inventory. Close with the single revision that would do the most work, and say whether the resulting package is still recognizably Aristotelian. Please do not answer with general cautions about the empirical literature being contested: take each claim at face value and trace what it commits me to.
