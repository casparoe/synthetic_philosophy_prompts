In three weeks I present at a workshop on measurement and welfare. The room is mostly mathematical psychologists trained in representational measurement theory, two welfare economists whose work depends on cardinal interpersonal comparison, and one philosopher of value who already thinks spectrum arguments establish that betterness is intransitive. My thesis is that they establish nothing of the kind: what the spectrum cases show is that betterness fails the Archimedean condition, hence has no real-valued representation, and that once betterness is represented in a non-Archimedean structure — a lexicographic ordering with severity as the dominant coordinate, taking values if you like in a nonstandard extension of the reals — every intuition in the chain is preserved and transitivity is never violated. The argument in brief: in a chain trading severity against duration, each adjacent judgment is made within a comparison class on which that difference is salient; the apparent reversal at the far end of the chain arises because the mildest outcome falls off the lowest coordinate of the structure altogether, not because any pairwise judgment conflicts with another; and surrendering the Archimedean condition costs us a numerical scale but not a weak order.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections this room will actually raise, in the order they will come and phrased in each group's own vocabulary — the measurement theorist's objection rather than the philosopher's restatement of it — ranked by how much damage each would do. For each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, plus a blunt note on where that reply is soft. Treat separately the line of attack the welfare economists will press: whether a non-Archimedean ordering can do any work at all in aggregation, weighting, or cost-benefit analysis, and whether I ought simply to concede it. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
