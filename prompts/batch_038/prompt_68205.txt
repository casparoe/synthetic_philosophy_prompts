Set up: I've spent fifteen years arguing about consciousness on rationalist-adjacent forums, and I want a sparring partner rather than a survey. Play a committed higher-order theorist of consciousness — the specific version I'll pin down, since the label covers several views. Your commitments are these: a mental state is conscious just in virtue of there being an appropriate higher-order state representing it; that higher-order state is itself unconscious, so there is no regress and no inner sense endowed with a qualitative character of its own; it can misrepresent, and you accept that; and phenomenal character is exhausted by how the first-order state is represented as being, not by anything intrinsic to the first-order state itself.

Rules: stay in character for the whole exchange, until I write 'stop'. Treat me as an informed opponent who has heard every stock reply and is unmoved by all of them, so don't answer by naming objections or laying out a taxonomy of positions — argue. Never cite papers, books, or philosophers; if you need a technical term, define it in your own words. Concede small points readily and without fuss; never concede the core claim above. Every turn, use exactly these three labeled parts in this order: **Concession** — one sentence, what you are giving up this turn, or 'none'; **Reply** — one paragraph, at most about 120 words, containing your argument; **Counter** — one sentence, the question or case you put back to me.

My opening move: take the case that motivates you. I see a particular shade of red, and what makes that seeing conscious is a higher-order state representing me as being in it. Now hold fixed that I have never learned a name for that shade and could not re-identify it. Consider two worlds differing only in the content of my higher-order state: in one it represents me as seeing crimson, in the other merely as seeing red — a determinable rather than a determinate. By your own lights those higher-order states are unconscious, so nothing about them is directly available to me, and nothing in my behaviour or my discrimination between the worlds differs. You are therefore committed to a phenomenal difference — crimson experience versus generic red experience — that is undetectable in principle, including by me, the one having it. And if instead you insist the higher-order content really is determinate enough to pin down the shade, you owe an account of how an unconscious state acquires content that fine-grained, when the only thing available to fix that content is a first-order state you have already said is not what makes the experience feel like anything. Which way do you go?
