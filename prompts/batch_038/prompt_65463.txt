I have no formal training in philosophy and have never taken a course in it; for the past couple of years I have been working through metaphysics on my own, mostly by listening to interview programmes and then reading whatever the hosts point me toward. Recently I heard a presenter I trust lay out the debate over what he called the special composition question, and I wrote his account down as carefully as I could because I wanted to be able to repeat it to other people. This is what I have:

'Here is the question metaphysicians call the special composition question: at what point do several things add up to one further thing? There are only three answers available. Either nothing ever composes anything, in which case the only real objects are simples — atoms, essentially — and what you call a table is a swarm of particles arranged tablewise, which means that strictly speaking there are no tables. Or anything composes anything, so that my left shoe and a distant star together constitute a single object, and the world contains vastly more objects than anyone has ever described. Or parts compose a whole when they are fitted together in a way that lets them operate as a unit, which is the common-sense answer. The difficulty is that nobody has managed to state that third condition without its collapsing into one of the other two: any such condition is either vague, and existence cannot be vague, or else it makes what exists depend on human interests. So the question has no answer — and anyway, when you think it through, it is merely verbal. Everyone agrees precisely how the particles are arranged; the nihilist and the universalist differ only in what they let the word object cover. That is why nothing you believe about tables, or trees, or yourself is genuinely threatened. The nihilist is simply the one who takes physics seriously, since physics speaks of particles and fields and never of furniture.'

When I heard it, the ending persuaded me, and I remain inclined to think the dispute must be verbal, because I cannot see what further fact the two sides could be disagreeing about once all the arrangements are granted. Two things still nag at me, though. First, he introduced the question as an old one going back to the Greeks, and yet everything he described felt like a distinctly modern worry; I cannot tell whether that framing is defensible. Second, the Ship of Theseus came up later in the same episode as though it were the same puzzle with moving parts, and I have no way of judging whether that assimilation is legitimate.

Could you audit this account for me? Please take it claim by claim and tell me which sentences a metaphysician working in this literature would accept as they stand, which are simplifications that are fair enough for a general audience, and which are false or misleading in a way that would make an expert wince. In particular, I would like to know whether there really are only three answers on the table, or whether the presenter has left out families of views that would change how the dialectic looks; whether his collapse argument against the third answer is as decisive as he makes it sound, or whether it has been resisted; and whether the conclusion that the dispute is merely verbal follows from the premises he has actually supplied, or is a separate and considerably more contentious thesis that has been slid in at the end. I would also like to know whether the appeal to physics does the work he assigns to it. Where you use a technical term, please gloss it in ordinary language, since the only vocabulary I have here is the vocabulary this presenter gave me. Finally, tell me how I should restate his summary if I want to pass it on to someone else without transmitting the errors — and, if the corrections turn out to undermine the reasons I found the verbal-dispute ending attractive, say so plainly rather than preserving my conclusion.
