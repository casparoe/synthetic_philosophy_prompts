I am three years into a novel whose centre of gravity is a regional human-rights court: a treaty body to which some forty-odd states have submitted, which hears complaints brought by individuals directly against their own governments, and whose judgments those governments describe, in the operative text of the treaty, as binding and as ones they undertake to execute. My protagonist is a lawyer in the court's registry, and because her crisis of faith is the whole of the second half of the book, my editor has asked that the procedural texture be exact; so I have spent this year reading the machinery rather than the case law, and I keep coming up against an arrangement I cannot make philosophical sense of. There is no sheriff. Execution of a judgment is supervised by a committee composed of the member states' own representatives — including, when their turn comes round, the representatives of the state that has just lost — and that committee's instruments are publication, pressure, deferral, and patience. The heaviest thing in the cupboard is expulsion from the organisation, which has the peculiar property that the ultimate sanction for breaking a treaty obligation is that the obligation stops applying to you; a state may also denounce the treaty outright, lawfully and prospectively, on notice. The federation disclaims any power to compel by force while its members keep standing armies. And yet compliance is high: governments pay the awards, reopen closed proceedings, amend offending statutes, and brief the committee at length on how thoroughly they have complied, rather than shrugging.

Alongside this sits a second and stranger practice. Where a person is about to be removed from a country and claims he will be tortured or killed on arrival, the court can indicate an urgent interim measure — not a judgment, not a ruling on the merits, but an administrative indication issued under its own rules of procedure and reserved for cases of imminent and irreparable harm — asking the government to keep the man on the ground until the case has been examined. The treaty itself says nothing about such indications; there is no penalty attached to them anywhere. The court has nonetheless held that a state's failure to honour one can itself violate the provision guaranteeing the right of individual petition, and governments overwhelmingly obey, sometimes at real domestic cost, sometimes while protesting loudly that they were not obliged to.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the theory of law and obligation this machinery presupposes: what has to be true about the nature of a legal duty for a duty of exactly this shape — unenforced, self-supervised by the parties to it, individually invokable against one's own sovereign, terminable by exit, and nevertheless generally kept — to be a legal duty at all, rather than a species of promise or of etiquette. Which rival accounts of law does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it merely happen to sit comfortably with? I am especially interested in how it looks from Kant's side of the fence. In the peace essay he wants a federation of free states rather than a world republic, because a state of nations would be a contradiction in terms and a world state a soulless despotism; elsewhere he binds right inseparably to the authorization to use coercion, and holds that outside a civil condition obligations remain provisional rather than peremptory. Is this court the federation he was reaching for, or is it the thing he thought impossible — peremptory law among sovereigns resting on nothing but reciprocal expectation and reputational exposure? If the latter, does the practice presuppose that states are entities capable of being bound by their own recognition alone, and that individuals rather than states are the ultimate units of right? Or could precisely the same procedures be run on quite different premises? Please keep strict presupposition separate from good fit, mark the places where the machinery is philosophically underdetermined — where two incompatible theories would license the identical procedure — and say which of its parts are in tension with each other, the interim measures and the judgment-enforcement apparatus being my own chief suspicion. Finally, tell me what would have to be added to this court, or deleted from it, for a Hobbesian replacement or a genuinely Kantian world republic to become visible in its daily working; I want to know which of those two institutions my character could coherently believe she serves. No references or reading list, please — the argument, not the scholarship.
