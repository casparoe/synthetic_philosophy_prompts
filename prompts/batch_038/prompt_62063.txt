I'm a doctoral candidate in ethics, and our graduate-student magazine is running an issue on moderation; I've been asked to contribute something livelier than another position paper. I'd like a Swiftian modest proposal of roughly 700 words, formatted as the executive summary of a think-tank white paper with short numbered sections and headings, written entirely in character and entirely straight — no winking, no authorial nudge.

The premise: a policy analyst announces that he has finally solved the overdemandingness problem in ethics. His solution is the Moral Demand Index, a continuously updated public dashboard that sets the level at which morality may permissibly require anything by reference to what the relevant peer group is actually doing. He is sincere, he thinks of himself as rescuing ethics from a high-demand regulatory regime, and he cannot see that anything has gone wrong.

The comedy has to come from getting the debate right rather than from the analyst being dim. Please build in:

1. His pride in the fact that under his scheme moral failure becomes statistically impossible — the threshold rises and falls to meet compliance — and his treatment of that as a design success rather than a cost.
2. A fine-print section on the partial-compliance adjustment: since what any one person owes depends on how much everyone else contributed in the last measurement period, he must explain how an ordinary agent is supposed to determine her own duty without access to the underlying data, and what the index says during the interval before the data arrive.
3. A lag-and-downturn clause covering the case in which aggregate giving falls all at once, so that everyone's obligations shrink simultaneously and the shortfall never appears in anyone's ledger.
4. A paragraph absorbing the obvious objection — that his baseline is nothing but the settled reluctance of the very people who find the demanding theory demanding — by reclassifying that objection as stakeholder feedback to be weighted into the next update.
5. A closing section in which a reporter asks what the index requires of a man walking past a shallow pond with a child in it, and he discovers that the relevant peer group has not been surveyed.

The target is the structure of the overdemandingness argument and the moderate views built on top of it, not any named person; invent nothing about real philosophers and cite nothing. Someone who has worked on the debate should be able to identify every move being parodied, and someone who hasn't should still enjoy it as bureaucratic self-parody.
