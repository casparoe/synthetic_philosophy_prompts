Last night I argued for the better part of four hours with a friend of mine — a development economist who works on labor markets, and not at all a hostile interlocutor — about whether Marx's critique of capitalism survives the abandonment of the labor theory of value. He kept circling back to a single objection: strip out the value theory and what remains is a complaint about unequal bargaining power that plenty of non-Marxists, including several he counts as colleagues, would happily sign, so the specifically Marxist content has done no work. I don't think he landed the blow, but I was unable to say why at the time, and afterwards I sat down and tried to write out precisely what I am committed to, so that the shape of my own position would be visible and I could see whether it holds. What follows is that list, stated as neutrally as I can manage. I would like you to treat it as material for an audit.

My commitments are these. (1) I do not accept the labor theory of value: I do not think the exchange-value of a commodity is fixed by the socially necessary labor time embodied in it, and I do not believe I need that thesis for anything else either. (2) Nonetheless I think Marx identified something real and specific in calling wage labor exploitative — a wrong, and not merely an unfortunate arrangement. (3) I locate that wrong in the background conditions of the exchange rather than in its terms: a labor contract can be non-fraudulent, competitively priced, mutually beneficial, and entered without anyone holding a gun, and still be exploitative, because of what the worker brings to the table, namely no access to productive assets of her own and therefore no realistic option but to sell her capacity to work to someone who has them. (4) At the same time I think exploitation is a comparative notion, in that it requires the exploited party to come out worse than she would under some relevantly fair alternative arrangement; being on the weak side of a voluntary deal that still improves her situation is not, on my view, automatically exploitation — there has to be a baseline she falls short of. (5) I am not an immiseration theorist: I think capitalism did raise the material standard of living of the wage-dependent classes above what any feasible alternative actually available at the time would have delivered, and I think Marx's expectations of absolute pauperization and of inevitable collapse were simply wrong. (6) I think alienation is a harm distinct from exploitation and not merely a description of how exploitation feels — it can persist where wages are good and bargaining power is comparatively symmetrical, so a secure, well-paid worker doing fragmented and externally directed work can be alienated in the full sense. (7) I think alienation is objective rather than experiential: a person can be alienated and feel entirely satisfied, and her satisfaction does not defeat the diagnosis. (8) I think Marx's critique is a moral one, in that he takes capitalism to be condemnable and not simply doomed, and I think at least part of the standard he invokes is internal to capitalism's own self-understanding — it advertises freedom and equality in the sphere of exchange and then delivers something that makes a mockery of both. (9) Finally, I think the political upshot is the socialization of productive assets rather than redistribution within capitalism: transfers, wage floors, and stronger unions can mitigate exploitation but cannot abolish it, since they leave the underlying property relations untouched.

Please go through this set and tell me which of these commitments genuinely cannot all be held together, ranked by severity rather than presented as a flat list of mild uneasiness. For each real tension I want the specific pair or triple in play, the argument that they cannot coexist, the cheapest available revision, and what that revision would cost me both philosophically and politically — and I want you to commit to a view about which single revision would clean up the most. I also want the converse: tell me which of the tensions only look like tensions, where the appearance comes from how I have phrased things rather than from any substantive incompatibility, since I expect at least a couple of these and I do not know which. One further question, and it is the one that would actually let me answer my friend. If some subset of this list is doing all the work in supporting (2), identify the subset, and tell me honestly whether it is distinctively Marxist or whether he is right that I have reinvented a generic complaint about bargaining power with a period costume on it. I would rather hear that the costume is doing the work than be reassured.
