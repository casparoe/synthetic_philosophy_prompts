Last night I argued for three hours with a friend from my philosophy-of-mind reading group about the color phi case, and we reached an impasse I would like help locating. We had been working through the timing material in Dennett and Kinsbourne alongside the original Kolers and von Grünau reports — which, my friend was pleased to note, were prompted by a question from Goodman about what a change of color would do to apparent motion. The display we were arguing over: a red dot at one location and, some tens of milliseconds later, a green dot at another, at a spacing and asynchrony that yields apparent motion; the subject reports a single dot that moved and changed color somewhere around the middle of the path, although the information needed to place the change mid-path did not exist until after the second dot was presented. One family of explanations says the subject genuinely had an experience as of a red dot at the first location and that what was altered afterward was the record of it, so the report is a retroactive edit — the Orwellian option. The other says the brain postponed the point at which a determinate experience was available until after the second dot had been processed, and then delivered a percept with the mid-path change already built into it, so nothing was ever edited — the Stalinesque option. My friend holds, following Dennett, that these are not rival empirical hypotheses at all: any observation compatible with one is compatible with the other, because what gets measured is always the content of a report, and reports can be generated at different times out of different drafts.

I pressed an experiment against this. Take a feature of the first dot that plays no part in the motion percept — a small notch in its outline, say — and require a discrimination about it during the inter-stimulus interval. Since no button press can be made inside a window of a few tens of milliseconds, I stipulated that the discrimination is read out neurally rather than behaviorally: with intracranial electrodes in an epilepsy monitoring patient, ask whether, before the second dot, there exists a state carrying the notch and color information *in the form that counts as an experience*, and whether the later report is produced by altering that state or by bypassing it. On the Orwellian story such a state exists and is subsequently rewritten; on the Stalinesque story there is at that moment no determinate perceptual fact about the mid-path change to be had. My friend's reply was what she calls the marker problem, and I suspect it is the actual crux: any such recording measures when information becomes available and when a report is prepared, and both hypotheses agree about those timings. To turn a neural timestamp into a claim about when an experience occurred you need a bridge principle identifying which states are experiential at which times, and supplying that principle is exactly what the dispute is about; so the experiment either presupposes one hypothesis or measures something the two do not disagree about. She added that if I doubted the in-principle claim I should consider an artificial system whose entire processing architecture I stipulate and which produces color-phi-style reports: there the answer looks plain, which suggests the human case is merely unmeasured rather than undecidable. My response was that knowing the architecture gives me the temporal profile of a computation, and the step from that to the temporal profile of an experience is the same contested bridge principle. A third member of the group thought we were both missing the point, on the grounds that an experience can have as its content that a change occurred mid-path without itself being spread over that path, so the question confuses the timing of the content with the timing of the vehicle.

Here is the proposition I want a number on. P: for a subject undergoing a standard color phi display, no possible third-person observation — behavioral, verbal, or neural, assuming no technological limit short of physical or logical impossibility — could ever provide more support for the Orwellian description than for the Stalinesque one, or vice versa. Give me your credence in P, and separately what your number would be on the stronger version in which the third-person restriction is dropped and the subject's own ideally attentive introspection counts as evidence. Then decompose it. I would suggest terms along these lines: (i) the probability that the reported data survive the conditions the argument needs — trained subjects, unpredictable second-dot colors, catch trials with the second dot omitted, instructions varied across blocks — rather than being an artifact of how the question is put; (ii) the probability that there is a determinate fact about when such an experience occurs, as opposed to the question being ill-posed; (iii) given such a fact, the probability that it is fixed by neural goings-on at all rather than by relations among dispositions to report; (iv) the probability that those goings-on could in principle be measured with the required resolution and coverage; (v) the probability that such a measurement could be read as settling the question without a bridge principle that already favors one side; and (vi) the probability that neither hypothesis survives anyway, because a postdictive or predictive-processing account of the mid-path change, or the content/vehicle distinction, dissolves the pair instead of adjudicating it. Add or drop terms as you see fit; give each a number and one line of justification; show me how you combine them; and say explicitly whether your terms are independent or whether — as I suspect of (ii) and (vi) — they double-count, in which case restate the decomposition so that they do not. Finally, tell me which single term your number is most sensitive to, what one finding or argument would move you most, and where the disagreement between me (I am at roughly 0.2 on P) and my friend (roughly 0.85) most likely lives: which term we assign differently, as opposed to which term we merely talk past. If you think P is not truth-apt as stated, do not refuse it — give me your credence in the nearest well-posed proposition and tell me precisely what you had to change.
