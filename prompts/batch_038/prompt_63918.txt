I work as a staff software engineer at an infrastructure company, with no philosophy coursework at any point in my life; my exposure to this material is one Wikipedia entry and a book chapter a colleague sent me. For two years I have organized a monthly evening session for engineers here — call it a recreational-reasoning group — usually 10 to 14 people, 90 minutes, everyone has a laptop and Python, everyone is comfortable with undergraduate probability, nobody has read anything in philosophy, and at least two people in the room hold that a question without a test harness is not a real question.

Next month I want to do Bertrand's paradox on the random chord of a circle. What I want participants to come away seeing is not the standard shrug — "the problem is under-specified, of course you get different answers" — but something sharper: that "choose uniformly at random" silently picks a parameterization, that the principle of indifference can be applied conscientiously to one and the same question in ways that contradict each other, and that settling which application is right is not a coding matter but a claim about how the chords are physically produced or about which symmetries the problem is meant to respect.

Please design the session as a runnable activity. Specifically:

1. Withhold the name. Nobody should hear "Bertrand" or "principle of indifference" until the collision has already happened. Structure the first act so that the collision is loud: groups work in parallel on what they believe is the identical question, all of them are confident their simulation is correct, and the numbers go up on one slide at once. Tell me what framing sentence to open with that makes everyone believe they are solving one determinate problem.

2. Give me four or five sampling procedures, one per group, each implementable in roughly fifteen lines and each able to run a million trials in under a minute. For each one I need: the plain-English description exactly as the group will read it on their card, worded so that it sounds like the neutral, obvious way to pick a random chord; the implementation recipe; and the exact analytic probability that the chord exceeds the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle, with a derivation short enough that I can check it by hand on the train. Include the three textbook procedures, and include one motivated by physics rather than geometry — straws thrown at random from far away onto a sheet of paper with a circle drawn on it, keeping those that cross.

3. I also want a procedure in which the randomizing happens at the rim: an entry point uniform on the circumference, and a direction uniform over the inward half-plane of directions. My own algebra says the perpendicular distance from the center comes out as R times the absolute value of the sine of a uniform angle, which is the same distribution as you get from the two-random-endpoints procedure, so the two should agree numerically at 1/3 despite sounding nothing alike. Please check whether I have this right. If I do, I want the session built so that these two groups compare results, find they match to four decimal places, and treat that as mutual confirmation — and then the debrief has to take it away from them: two procedures agreeing is not evidence that either is correct when they turn out to be the same procedure in disguise. That reversal is the moment I care most about, and I have no idea how to stage it without standing up and lecturing.

4. A short third act that lifts the structure out of geometry and into something the room does for a living, so it stops looking like a Victorian party trick. I was thinking of a latency-versus-throughput choice in a load-testing tool: draw a per-request latency uniformly from a range and invert it, versus draw a request rate uniformly from the corresponding range — both described by their authors as "uniform," and they disagree. Propose the version of this you think lands hardest in fifteen minutes, and say what the participants should be asked to do rather than be told.

5. Assume the deflationary response arrives early. Within five minutes someone will say that this is not a paradox at all, just an unspecified problem, and that once you state the distribution there is no contradiction — and they will be right about the mechanism. Build into the activity something that makes them feel what survives that remark: that where there is a fact about the generating process, the choice among procedures is not a matter of taste, and that there are realistic descriptions of the generating process which underdetermine it. A bet, a forced commitment, or a case where their own stated principle gives them two answers at once would all work; tell me which you chose and why.

6. Materials and timing: a minute-by-minute rundown, what is on the projector at each point, what is printed, what I must have run and debugged beforehand, how I get each group's number onto a single slide quickly, and what I do if a group's code is still broken at minute forty.

7. The debrief: the questions I ask, in the order I ask them, mapping what happened onto the principle of indifference, onto the fact that uniformity is always uniformity in some parameter, and onto the invariance-based reply — that a well-posed answer should be unchanged if you translate or scale the whole experiment, which is the argument I understand Jaynes to have made in the seventies when he concluded that the answer is one half and that the straw-throwing procedure is the one that respects it. If that is a fair summary, work it in; if I have it wrong, correct me. Also cover why that reply is itself contested — I gather the objection is that symmetry requirements can be applied so as to license more than one of the answers — and give me a way to present that objection honestly to a room that will otherwise leave believing the puzzle has been solved by a physicist.

8. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. Which procedure people will code incorrectly and in which direction the bug biases their number; the group that compares a floating-point chord length against the square root of three times the radius and gets a slightly wrong count; the group that finishes twelve minutes early and starts checking their phones; the person who insists the whole thing is a stale frequency-versus-Bayes dispute; how the numbers change if I have to use a hundred thousand trials instead of a million; and what I cut if only six people show up.

9. An honest limits section. My suspicion is that simulation can demonstrate the disagreement between procedures but cannot demonstrate that any of them is wrong, and that nothing in the activity will generate in the participants the intuition that indifference is a reasonable principle to begin with — so people who never felt its pull will experience the evening as a cautionary tale about parameterizing your sampler, which is useful but not the point. Tell me what I have to supply by hand, and whether any staged moment could actually produce that pull rather than merely assert it.

Please do not include a bibliography or a reading list; I will track down whatever sources I need myself. What I do need is for every number you give me to be exact and every derivation to be short enough to verify by hand, because I intend to check all of it against my own implementation before the session.
