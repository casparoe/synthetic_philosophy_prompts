I am writing a short paper for my school's philosophy society on why pain is bad, and I would like a consistency check before I present it. Here is roughly where I have landed:

1. Unpleasantness is not something added on top of pain but part of what makes an experience a pain at all; a sensation that lacked it would belong to a different family altogether (pressure, warmth, tickle).
2. Patients with pain asymbolia — who, typically after damage to the insula, report that they do feel the pain and can localize it and rate its intensity, but say they are not bothered by it, and who characteristically neither withdraw from nor avoid the stimulus — really feel pain rather than something merely pain-like.
3. What makes an unpleasant experience bad is that it is disliked: its badness consists in the subject's wanting it to stop.
4. I am entirely untroubled by the thought that pain can be worth having all things considered, as with the distance runner who welcomes the burn.
5. A person cannot be mistaken about whether her own present experience is unpleasant, though she can be mistaken about its cause, its location, and its intensity.
6. An asymbolic patient who leaves her hand on the hot stove is making a rational mistake, not merely an unusual choice.

Please tell me which of these actually pull against one another and how badly, ranking the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious. For each tension, say what would have to be given up and what giving it up would cost, and then identify the single revision that would clean up the most. Finally, I would like you to say where your own reasoning is most likely to fail: what argument or consideration, if I pressed you on it, would most plausibly change your verdict about which commitment has to go.
