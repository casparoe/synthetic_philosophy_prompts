I've been an OR nurse for fourteen years and this past July I got made nurse manager, which means I now sit on the side of the table that has to write things down. And writing things down has made me notice a pattern in how one of our norms died, and I don't have a word for it, and I've realized the word matters because I have to decide what to put in a policy. When I started, everyone knew which three surgeons shouted. It was onboarding lore — don't take a call from the vascular guy on a Monday, count to ten, he's a perfectionist, you'd want him if it was your mother on the table. There were arguments against it the whole time, and I mean real ones: staff turnover numbers, an exit interview summary, a patient-safety committee slide about psychological safety that somebody presented around 2011 and got nowhere with, a girl who cried in the stairwell and was told to toughen up. None of that moved anything. Then in about a eighteen month stretch two of our best scrub techs quit and went to the system across town and told people there, a fellow posted something public about his attending, and a recruit we badly wanted turned us down and somebody connected the dots. After that being known as a yeller started to cost you things — your techs put in requests not to be on your cases, the hospitalists got thinner about sending you referrals, med students ranked you low, you didn't get the committee seat. Within three years the same men were saying "tell me if I'm coming across as curt." Nobody's arguments won. The costs moved.

Here's the part I keep coming back to, because it doesn't fit how people usually tell these stories. I don't think a single one of them changed his mind. One of them told me straight out, in a hallway, that he thinks a tight room runs better on a bit of fear and he just can't afford it now — and I believed him, he wasn't performing. Meanwhile a bunch of us who always hated it had said nothing for a decade, and when it finally opened up we discovered that each of us had assumed all the others were fine with it. So the private beliefs on both sides stayed put, and the only thing that changed was what standing attached to doing it in public. Then the record quietly rewrote itself: people who used to say the perfectionist line now say we all saw it coming, and I know that's false because I have the emails, including ones where I'm the one saying it. The ones enforcing the new rule hardest are the hires who arrived after the flip and have no memory of how it was, plus two guys who used to shout themselves. And the behavior didn't vanish, it relocated — cold silence for days after a case, brutally worded chart comments, 11pm emails to the CNO, vaguely stated "concerns" about a nurse's competence that are each individually defensible and impossible to answer, surgeons going out into the hallway to blow off. Overt abuse is way down. People describe the room as more tense, not less. Our new rule is about visible conduct in the room, so none of this touches it.

So: does this shape have a name, or names, and where do the pieces stop being one thing? I've half-heard bits of vocabulary and I don't trust that any of them is right — people say norm cascade, or moral panic, or cancel culture, or something about preferences changing, and somebody in my book club once described a philosopher's argument that dueling ended for reasons having to do with honor rather than with anyone being convinced, which sounds adjacent. I want the carving, not a label: what exactly is the mechanism that did the work here, how is it different from being persuaded and different from being paid off, does the part where conduct changed and belief didn't have its own concept, is the retroactive re-telling of your own past defenses distinct from plain hypocrisy or from just misremembering, is "the converts enforce hardest" a known regularity or am I pattern-matching on two guys, and is the migration into unpoliced channels its own thing — plus whether that migration is evidence the change was shallow or just evidence that any rule has edges. And underneath all of it: if not one person's moral belief changed, is what happened here moral progress at all, or is it only a re-pricing that happened to land somewhere decent — and what would have to be true to tell those two apart. Please tell me which of my sub-questions are really the same question wearing different hats, and if nothing established covers the whole thing, coin me a term with a real definition and tell me the boundary cases — where it shades into something else, and whether the same mechanism running the other way would produce a norm change everyone would call bad. Not looking for a reading list or citations, I just want the distinctions sorted out.
