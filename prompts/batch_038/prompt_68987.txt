I'm TA'ing a grad epistemology seminar and I've got a take-home to mark, and I'd rather not just eyeball it. The question I set was this:

QUESTION. Fitch's paradox is normally presented as a dilemma for the verificationist. Consider instead the following restricted principle — call it KP*: for every true proposition p whose statement contains no epistemic vocabulary (no "knows", "is known", "is verified", and so on), it is possible that someone knows p.
(a) Does Fitch's argument go through against KP*? If it doesn't, show exactly where it stops.
(b) Is KP* a principled restriction or an ad hoc patch? Say what would settle the question either way.
(c) A verificationist might instead go for the ideal-limit reading, on which truth is what would be known at the end of inquiry. Does that dissolve the paradox or just relocate it?
No citations needed; I want the reasoning.

Here's the answer that came back:

ANSWER. Fitch's argument starts from the knowability principle, KP: for all p, if p is true then it's possible to know p. Suppose for reductio that there's some truth nobody knows, p ∧ ¬Kp. By KP this conjunction is possibly known. Now suppose it is known. Since knowledge distributes over conjunction, we get Kp and K¬Kp. But here's the step people always miss: getting from K¬Kp to a contradiction requires the KK principle, that whoever knows something knows that they know it. Knowing that you don't know p means knowing that you know that you don't know p, and that is what clashes with Kp. So the contradiction is really a product of KK, and an anti-realist with independent reasons to reject KK — lots of epistemologists do, since KK makes knowledge very demanding — can simply refuse the argument. That's the first way out.

As for KP*, the answer to (a) is that the argument does stop. The move that generates all the trouble is applying the knowability principle to the conjunction p ∧ ¬Kp, and that conjunction is built out of epistemic vocabulary, so KP* doesn't license the possibility claim at all. Nothing else in the derivation needs KP*, and for an ordinary non-epistemic truth — say that there are exactly a trillion grains of sand on this beach — KP* only gives you ◇Kp, which is harmless, since knowing that is perfectly possible in principle. So KP* survives. Whether that's principled or a patch, part (b), is the harder question, and I think it's a patch, because the line is drawn exactly where it needs to be drawn to make the argument fail, which is what ad hoc means. A restriction would be principled if there were some independent account of why epistemic propositions differ in kind from the rest, rather than just differing in what happens when you apply KP to them.

(c) is where I think the verificationist actually wins. If truth just means what would be known at the ideal limit of inquiry, then the conclusion that every truth is known isn't an absurdity at all, it's the thesis restated. On that reading Fitch doesn't refute verificationism, he proves it. The usual objection — that we're obviously not omniscient — misses the point, since on this reading "known" was never meant to be cashed out as known by us, now.

There's also the intuitionist line, which rejects bivalence. On that view the troublesome conjunction p ∧ ¬Kp need be neither true nor false when p is undecided, so the knowability principle never gets a grip on it and the argument never starts. I don't find this convincing, mostly because it seems to give up too much, but it does show the paradox isn't a straightforward refutation of anything.
