Acting as someone who knows both the philosophy-of-psychiatry literature and how clinical measurement actually gets done — treat this as a real consulting job, not a survey essay.

I'm a postdoc in a reading group that has been arguing about looping effects for the better part of two years and we have not moved an inch. I don't want you to tell me who's right. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, because I suspect we're not actually having one argument.

The setup. Everybody in the group accepts Hacking's basic observation: classifying people can change the people classified, so the kind and our description of it are coupled — psychiatric categories look like interactive kinds rather than the sort of thing you can validate the way you validate a mineral or an enzyme. Where we split is what follows.

Camp A (I'll call them the loop-skeptics-about-validity, they'd hate that) says the coupling is fatal to the whole checklist tradition in diagnostic validity — the one where you accumulate clinical description, biological markers, delimitation from neighbouring disorders, follow-up data, family aggregation, and call the category validated. Their argument: every one those evidential channels runs through populations whose self-understanding is partly constituted by the category, so a validation study doesn't measure a stable target, it measures a target that has moved since the criteria were written, and it moves again once the study is published and the label spreads. Worse, the movement isn't random: the criteria shape who gets identified, who gets identified shapes the symptom profile in the clinic, and that profile shapes the next revision of the criteria. So there's no independent thing for convergent evidence to converge on.

Camp B says A is inflating a measurement problem into a metaphysical catastrophe. Reactivity is everywhere in the human sciences — survey effects, Hawthorne-type stuff, market participants reading the models. You handle a moving target the way you handle any moving target: date-stamp the operational criteria, report validity relative to criteria-as-instantiated-at-time-t, model drift explicitly. And they add a further claim I find shakier: that the transdiagnostic, circuit-and-behaviour-measure style of construct definition (the RDoC-ish approach) is loop-immune by construction, because a construct defined by reward-learning rate or startle potentiation doesn't depend on the subject's self-description the way "major depressive disorder" does.

Camp B's immunity claim gets attacked from our side by a colleague I'll call the third person. Her point: constructs don't stay at the circuit level. They get translated back into eligibility criteria for trials, into intake forms, into what insurance codes, into what a 19-year-old reads about herself online at 2am. Uptake happens downstream of the construct definition, not inside it, so defining the construct in loop-immune terms just relocates where the loop closes. She has a term for the key variable — the uptake lag: the delay between a category or construct being introduced and the classified population's self-understanding actually changing in response. Her hypothesis about our stalemate, which neither camp accepts, is that A and B agree on the existence of looping and disagree, without noticing, about whether the object of classification is supposed to be a historically indexed kind or a kind as currently instantiated — and that this plus the size of the uptake lag does all the work. If the lag is long relative to the validation cycle, you can in principle validate before the loop closes, and B is closer to right, though for the wrong reasons; if it's short, A's objection bites but not as a metaphysical point, as a speed point. She also thinks the loop-immunity claim and her uptake-lag claim are answers to different questions entirely.

So: diagnose this. Work through what each camp is actually committed to — not their slogans, the commitments — and tell me whether this is (i) a genuine substantive disagreement about what makes a kind projectible, (ii) a disguised disagreement about what "validity" means here, with one side scoring a convergent-evidence test and the other a predictive or causal-explanatory test, (iii) a verbal dispute over what "the category" refers to across revisions, (iv) a hidden empirical question about the magnitude and timescale of uptake that both sides are posture around because nobody has the data, (v) two questions sharing a name — an epistemic one about projectibility and a normative one about whether we should be classifying at all — or (vi) something my list doesn't include. If you think my taxonomy is itself the problem, say so and give a better one.

Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis. I mean concretely: is there a distinction that would dissolve it, a piece of data that would settle it (and if so, what would the study look like — what's the measurement, what's the comparison, why would loop-people and no-loop-people both accept the result), or is this the kind of impasse where the right move is to stop asking? If your answer is "no progress should be expected," argue for that rather than just asserting it.

Please start with some actual brainstorming before you commit — sketch the candidate diagnoses, give reasons for and against each, then pick and defend. Think carefully; I'd rather have a well-reasoned single diagnosis than a hedge that says all six are partly true.

One thing I do want you to hold onto: be explicit about what is settled among people who know this material versus what is genuinely contested, and don't dress up a contested position as the consensus view. Also flag anywhere you're extrapolating from my sketch rather than from the literature, since I've compressed two years of arguing into a few paragraphs and I'm sure I've made my friends sound more coherent than they are.
