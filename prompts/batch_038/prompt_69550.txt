— The claim is correct, and the strongest defence of it is analytical rather than moral. Sen's entitlement approach, developed in Poverty and Famines (1981), replaced the older food-availability account of famine with an account of how particular groups lose their command over food. Starvation, on Sen's formulation, is the characteristic of some people not having enough to eat, not the characteristic of there not being enough to eat. A person's entitlement set is what they can command by trade, by production, by their own labour, or by transfer; a famine is the collapse of the entitlement sets of a large group, and that collapse is produced by the way a society allocates claims on food rather than by the weather.

Bengal in 1943 illustrates the point. There were natural triggers — a cyclone, tidal flooding, and a rice blight — but mortality bore no proportion to the fall in the harvest. What killed was the wartime boom economy of Calcutta: inflation, the prioritisation of war workers and civil servants in official distribution schemes, hoarding once price controls were abandoned, and the denial policies that stripped rural Bengal of its boats and its rice stocks. Rural transport workers, fishermen, and landless labourers found their money incomes worth steadily less against a rice price that rose far faster than wages, while the province as a whole was not short of grain. The catastrophe was a transfer of purchasing power from the countryside to the war economy. That is a political event, and it was effected by decisions taken in Delhi and London.

Ireland offers a starker case still. Throughout the famine years Ireland continued to export grain and livestock to Britain, so the food required to feed the starving existed inside the country; it was simply commanded by people with better entitlements. Here again the victims were not those who lacked food in the aggregate but those whose purchasing power had collapsed, and the continuing exports are the clearest possible evidence that what occurred was a distribution problem rather than a supply problem — and hence a crime rather than a misfortune.

China 1958–62 completes the argument. The Great Leap Forward famine killed tens of millions and is usually described as the largest in recorded history. Sen's explanation of how it was possible is institutional: there was no free press and no competitive election, so officials who inflated harvest reports met no correction, procurement quotas were set on the strength of those reports, and the household registration system prevented the starving from moving toward food. The comparison runs the other way too — India, with its elections and its newspapers, has had no famine since independence despite being considerably poorer. Famine, on this account, requires that nobody in power be answerable for it. Sen argues in Development as Freedom (1999) that this is also why prevention is cheap: a government that will lose an election if people starve organises relief, and the relief required is small relative to the loss it averts, a point developed further with Drèze in Hunger and Public Action (1989).

If all of this is right, then describing these events as natural disasters is not merely incomplete but affirmatively misleading, and a stronger description is available: they were political crimes. The officials who set procurement quotas against figures they had reason to distrust, who shipped food out of a starving country, and who suppressed the reporting that would have triggered relief did not merely fail to prevent deaths they could have prevented; they brought those deaths about. Crimes against humanity, on the ordinary understanding of the phrase, are exactly this — acts carried out as part of a policy that destroys a civilian population. Nothing in the entitlement analysis requires proof of a wish to kill, and that is precisely why the entitlement analysis does the normative work: once we know that the deaths followed from assignable decisions about who may command food, we know whom to hold responsible, and we know that the word disaster was doing the work of concealment. —
