I am a fourth-year doctoral student in behavioral ecology, and I am co-writing an invited review chapter with my adviser on honest signaling in animals. She is the senior author and has final say over which examples we write up as paradigms; the publisher wants roughly a dozen worked cases, and the chapter will also carry a boxed section on signals that lie. Her practice when I propose an example is to reply in a single line — "yes, this is one of ours," or "no, this goes in the box" — almost never with a reason. I am trying to reconstruct the rule she is applying before I spend my last open slot on a pitch that gets bounced, so I would like your help reading her track record.

Her verdicts so far, with each case as I framed it for her:

1. The peacock's train, argued to be a condition-dependent ornament whose full expression tracks ectoparasite load, with the authors backing this up by experimental manipulation of parasite load and by showing that train elaboration predicts mating success and survival: include.
2. The acoustic structure of a red deer stag's roar — specifically the component fixed by the dimensions of the vocal tract — which carries accurate information about body size and weight. The authors I proposed made no appeal to cost whatsoever; they appealed only to anatomical constraint, plus playback trials in which stags responded more cautiously to roars that sounded larger: include.
3. Stotting in Thomson's gazelles: conspicuous, energetically expensive jumping performed at approaching coursing predators, with evidence that stotters are less likely to be chased and more likely to escape if they are: include.
4. A manuscript claiming that a brilliantly conspicuous plumage is an honest signal simply because conspicuousness exposes its bearer to predators — a real cost, but the authors' own data showed no association between plumage intensity and any measure of condition, health, or age: exclude.
5. A frog-calling study arguing that prolonged calling bouts are honest because calling is expensive, in which the authors' own measurements showed small and large males paying roughly the same energetic price per call, with small males still gaining matings by calling anyway: exclude.
6. Regenerated claws in male fiddler crabs. After a claw is lost, the regrown claw reaches a similar length but is lighter and weaker, so claw size remains a fair guide to body size while ceasing to be a guide to closing force; rivals are usually deterred without fighting and seldom test the claim. The authors argued that regrowing the claw is genuinely costly and called the display honest: exclude, filed under deception.

The only general principle she has ever volunteered, in a margin note on item 4, was that a signal belongs in the chapter only if a low-quality animal "couldn't get away with" displaying it.

The example I want to propose for my remaining slot is the black facial patterning of female paper wasps (Polistes dominulus). The pigment is cheap to lay down, the pattern varies widely among individuals who are otherwise difficult to tell apart, and the authors experimentally altered subordinate wasps' faces so that their badges looked exaggerated; these manufactured "cheaters" then received markedly more aggression from rivals than sham-handled controls did, and the authors conclude that the badge is honest precisely because lying gets policed. First, predict her verdict. Then do the part I actually need: state as precisely as you can — precisely enough that I could apply it to an example neither of us has seen — the rule her six verdicts are consistent with, and enumerate every other rule that fits those six verdicts just as well, saying for each what it predicts about the wasp and where the candidates stop agreeing. My own hunch is that she will include it, because nothing she has ever said sounds like a requirement that the trait itself be expensive; but item 6 is obviously the nearest precedent in her record, and I cannot tell whether she will see a cheap badge that reliably gets punished as the same kind of thing as a cheap-looking claw that usually does not. Please address that comparison directly: if she excludes the wasp, what principle is she committed to, and does that principle cost her item 2? If she includes it, what must she say about item 6, and is there any distinction she can hold onto other than how often the bluff gets tested? Finally, name the one further example — described concretely enough, with a species and a mechanism, that I could pitch it to her next — that would most sharply separate the rules that survive her record, and say what verdict each of those rules would force her to give on it. Please write your answer in American English.
