You are an expert in the philosophy and methodology of well-being measurement: someone who has taught a graduate seminar on the construction of subjective well-being indices, who can explain without notes why a life-evaluation instrument, an affect-balance instrument, and a eudaimonic or flourishing instrument are not measuring the same thing, and who also writes comedy for a living. You know how self-report scales behave under changes of wording, translation, reference group, anchoring, and national income, and you know the adaptation literature. Take a moment to think carefully before you draft.

**Step one — planning block (no more than 150 words).** List the measurement phenomena you judge most exploitable for satire, decide which single one will carry the closing beat of the piece, and note in one sentence what the satire is actually aimed at. Do not begin writing the memo until this is done.

**Step two — the piece.** Write a satirical internal government memorandum, 750–950 words, from a mid-ranking country that has just slipped several places in the world's most publicised league table of national happiness and has been instructed to recover the lost ground before the next edition is published. Invent the country, its ministry, its task force, its special adviser, and its current position in the table. Format it as a genuine strategy memo: numbered sections with dry headings, bullet points, a short risk register, an acroynm introduced once and then used throughout, and a final one-line recommendation to the Minister. The register is civil-service deadpan — passive voice, euphemism, managerial seriousness about trivialities and trivial tone about serious things. No winking, no asides to the reader, no exclamation marks.

The humour must come from getting the measurement right rather than from caricaturing bureaucrats as merely stupid. Every methodological point the memo makes should be a real one, simply followed further than anyone would publicly admit. Develop each of the following as an actual policy initiative, not as a passing reference:

1. An intervention targeting the ladder question itself — the instrument asks respondents to place their life as a whole on a scale whose top rung they are told to define as the best life personally imaginable for them and whose bottom rung is the worst. The task force proposes to take an interest in what citizens are imagining at the top of the ladder, and explains why this is a legitimate line of policy.
2. A reference-group management programme: life evaluation is partly a comparison, so the comparison set is a policy variable. Propose how to administer it.
3. The aspiration problem, worked to its uncomfortable end. If a score reflects a life judged against a standard, the standard can be lowered as readily as the life can be raised. At least one junior official must point out, in writing, that a given initiative improves the metric by making the underlying thing worse, and must be overruled on grounds of scope, sequencing, or communications risk — never on the merits.
4. A definitional turf war between three ministries, each of which collects a different kind of well-being data and each of which is privately certain that the league table is measuring its own quantity.
5. The residualisation gambit: explaining the national score away with the usual handful of covariates and then claiming credit for whatever is left over.
6. An aggregation issue — what a national mean does and does not show about the distribution beneath it — together with an initiative that exploits it.
7. An adaptation-based cost-efficiency argument: helping citizens to habituate to adverse conditions is cheaper per point of score than remedying the conditions, and the memo says so in the language of value for money.
8. One further proposal whose implication the author declines to write down, referring it instead to a committee and asking that it not be minuted. Leave the reader to complete the thought.

**Constraints.** Do not name any real country, city, index, report, polling organisation, university, researcher, politician, or year, and do not include any real statistic. Invent all figures. No citations, references, footnotes, or bibliography of any kind, and no verbatim quotation from any text — paraphrase instead. The target of the satire is a practice and a discourse, not a person: no identifiable real individual may appear, and the invented officials should be recognisably reasonable people doing something unreasonable. No character may state the obvious objection that the ranking does not measure happiness or that topping it is not a proper aim of government; those objections must be structurally felt in what the initiatives amount to.

**Step three — appendix.** After the memorandum, and clearly separated from it under a heading marking it as not part of the document, add a 120–180 word note identifying, in a sentence or two each, the real phenomenon that initiatives 1, 3, 5, and 8 exaggerate. The memorandum must stand on its own without this appendix; the appendix exists so a specialist reader can confirm that the exaggeration is faithful.
