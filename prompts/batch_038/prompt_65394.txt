The episode is the American response to the 2008 crash and what came out of it politically. Facts I'm supplying so you don't have to guess: the TARP bank rescues in autumn 2008, carried on into the new administration; the stress tests of spring 2009 designed so that no large bank had to be taken over; the AIG bonus fury in March 2009, when the House passed a 90% punitive tax on bonuses at firms that had taken bailout money and the thing then quietly died in the Senate; no senior banker criminally prosecuted; a CNBC rant about mortgage relief in February 2009 that is usually credited with kicking off the Tea Party; and then Occupy, from September 2011, with its "we are the 99%" slogan.

The lens is the standard political-economy account of why the US redistributes so much less than Europe. First, the fairness-equilibrium line from Alesina and Angeletos: Americans believe market outcomes largely track effort, Europeans believe they track luck, birth and connections, and each belief is self-confirming because it shapes the tax policy that shapes what income actually consists of. Second, the POUM line from Bénabou and Ok: the median voter puts up with inequality now because she expects to be above the mean later, or expects her children to be. Keep the institutional rivals — electoral rules, racial heterogeneity — in the background as things to check your story against, not as the main lens.

Here's the part I want you to build rather than retrieve. Before you get to the politics, define me an effort-attribution share: for any given top income, the fraction of it a voter assigns to effort as against luck, inheritance, insider access and outright fraud. Make the model your own — I'm not asking for anyone's equations — but state its assumptions out loud, and then use the episode to trace what 2008 did to that share in different parts of the electorate. The crux is the anomaly. One shock, two movements, opposite distributive programmes. The fairness account predicts a collapse in effort-attribution and hence a lurch toward redistribution. What happened on the American right was a re-assertion: the complaint was that the state was taking from the deserving and handing it to bankers, subprime borrowers and public-sector workers. Tell me precisely whether that is a counterexample, a prediction the theory makes once you are careful about *which* belief got updated — markets fair, government not — or a sign the theory was never about beliefs in the way its critics suppose.
