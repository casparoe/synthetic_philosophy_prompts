I am assembling a seminar on the epistemology of historical knowledge, and before we read any proposed accounts of the boundary between legitimate historical revisionism and denialism, I want the specification: what such an account has to be able to do to count as adequate.

Fix the purpose first, since I take adequacy to be relative to it. The account is to serve an institution — a history department, a prize committee, a public broadcaster — that must decide whether to platform, publish, or formally describe as denial a given body of historical claims. It is not primarily a guide for the individual inquirer deciding what to believe, and the institution is not in a position to adjudicate the specialist historiography itself.

Write the desiderata: which distinctions the account must not collapse, which cases it must get right, which features of actual historical practice it must be consistent with. For each, give the case or consideration behind it and mark it as non-negotiable or as a demand peculiar to one camp. The list must at minimum handle:

1. Disputes in which the substantive facts are agreed and only the legal-moral category is contested — scholars who accept a state-engineered famine that killed millions while rejecting the label genocide for it, or who accept mass deportation and killing in 1915 while contesting genocidal intent. Is this revisionism, denialism, or a third category, and what does the account owe us here?
2. Legitimate revision that strikes a central interpretive claim rather than a peripheral detail — for instance the demotion of the Wannsee conference minutes from the decision that initiated extermination to the coordination of a policy already under way.
3. A contested quantity inside an atrocity no serious party denies, such as the Nanjing death toll: what may be argued about, and what does arguing about it presuppose?
4. The claimant who is methodologically fastidious — archival citation, footnotes, professed openness to correction — and who demands that the account not simply assume the established consensus is true. Any line drawn purely by reference to the truth of the conclusion begs the question and is useless to my institution; yet I suspect that the familiar diagnostic of an asymmetric standard of evidence, of demanding documentary certainty for one side's case while resting the other's on circumstantial inference, cannot be dropped without letting this claimant through.

Then tell me which single desideratum does the most work in excluding candidate accounts, and whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable — I suspect (4) collides with something, but I want your argument, not my hunch.

Be explicit about confidence. Where is this list most likely to be wrong, which requirement would you demote or abandon if persuaded, and by what consideration — in particular, how much of it survives if the purpose shifts from institutional gatekeeping to the guidance of an individual inquirer?

Format: numbered desiderata, each with its motivating case and its negotiability marking; no scorecard of existing views, which I will handle in class. Close with one line, and nothing after it, of exactly this form:
Answer: {the requirement that does the most excluding work, in a short phrase}
