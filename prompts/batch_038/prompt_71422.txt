The situation: our reading group is slowly making its way through Hofstadter's *Le Ton beau de Marot*, and we're at the Marot poem the book keeps orbiting. Eleven of us, two hours on a Saturday afternoon in the back room of a bookshop. No projector, no budget, just pens, paper, a whiteboard and tea. What I want is an activity that makes them feel the force of the book's central problem: that a poem's form isn't a container you can pour the meaning out of and into a differently-shaped container, and that the readers who *can't* check the original are precisely the ones who end up deciding what counted as a good translation. And here's a rough literal crib I made myself (not lifted from any published translation), which everyone gets, so the seven non-readers can actually play:

1 my dear [a feminine diminutive — "my little sweet one"] / 2 I give you [formal *vous*] / 3 good day; / 4 staying in bed / 5 is a prison. / 6 Recovery / 7 regain [formal], / 8 then open / 9 your [formal] door / 10 and let people out / 11 quickly [archaic word for *vite*], / 12 for Clément [the poet's own first name] / 13 sends you word of it. / 14 Go on, greedy one [feminine; note the switch to informal *tu* from here on] / 15 of your [informal] mouth, / 16 which lies / 17 in danger / 18 in order to eat / 19 preserves; / 20 if you keep on / 21 too long ill, / 22 a washed-out colour / 23 you will take on / 24 and will lose / 25 your plumpness. / 26 God grant you [archaic verb form] / 27 good health, / 28 my dear.

Structural facts I've checked against the text myself, so build on these rather than guessing: 28 lines, every one of them three syllables in French; rhyming couplets all the way through; the closing couplet rhymes with the opening one, and the last line is word-for-word the first line again. Also: the poem switches from formal *vous* to intimate *tu* partway through, which English simply cannot do; the poet's own name sits in line 12 where it has to rhyme; and there are two archaisms (*Vitement*, *doint*) that force a decision about register. Three or four nasty traps in a very small box.
