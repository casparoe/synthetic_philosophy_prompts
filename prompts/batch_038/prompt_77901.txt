Several interlocking features of modern securities regulation strike me as resting on a philosophy of knowledge that nobody states explicitly, and I would like it reconstructed. Let me describe the practice as precisely as I can.

First, in most major jurisdictions it is unlawful to deal in an issuer's securities while in possession of material non-public information. The prohibition is broadly objective: the authorities need not show that the trader intended to profit from what she knew, and the burden falls in practice on the trader to establish that the information played no part — that the order was placed under a pre-committed plan, that the trade would have been made anyway, and so forth. The prohibition also reaches disclosure in the absence of dealing: passing the information on can be unlawful even if nobody trades on it, as can recommending that another person deal.

Second, the same regulators impose continuous disclosure duties on issuers, and justify them by appeal to the informational function of prices: prices ought to reflect what is publicly known, and investors, boards and officials are encouraged to rely on them as the best available collective judgement about a firm's prospects.

Third, when that machinery fails, private litigation typically presumes reliance rather than requiring it to be proved — on the footing that someone who dealt in an efficient market dealt at a price already embodying the misstatement — and computes recoverable loss from the fall in price following the corrective disclosure.

Fourth, insiders must notify their own dealings within a few business days, and the notifications are published. So what the trader was forbidden to exploit becomes public shortly afterwards in any event.

Please treat this package as a single practice and tell me what it presupposes. In particular:

1. What conception of information does it take for granted — is inside information being treated as something that can be owned, as a good whose unequal distribution is itself the harm, or as a mere input whose flow must be kept even? What conception of the market does it assume, given that the entity whose beliefs are supposedly protected from distortion is not any person but a distributed process? Does the practice commit regulators to thinking of prices as beliefs, or only as useful summaries?

2. Is the package internally consistent? The prohibition aims at equality of information; the presumption of reliance and the damages calculation assume prices track information closely enough to serve as counterfactual benchmarks; the notification rule publicises the asymmetry a few days later regardless. If the prohibition's ideal were achieved — everyone in possession of the same information, and everyone knowing that this is so — then Aumann's agreement theorem and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade result seem to imply that there would be no room for speculative trade at all, since a bid would simply reveal what the bidder knows. Yet enormous volume is treated as a mark of market health. Does that show the practice is incoherent, or do the theorems' assumptions block the inference? Say which assumption is doing the work in whichever direction you take.

3. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. I suspect several rival theories license much of it: a fairness or level-playing-field account, an account on which information is the issuer's property, an adverse-selection account under which the rule exists to protect market makers and hence liquidity, and a Hayekian–Manne-style account on which the informativeness of prices is precisely what informed trading produces. For each, say what further detail of the practice it explains that its rivals cannot, and what it would predict the rules should look like if it were the true rationale. Which of these, if any, is ruled out by the fact that tipping is unlawful absent any trade?

4. What would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with a rival one? Take two replacements: a Hayekian view on which rewarding informed trading is how dispersed knowledge gets into prices, and a sceptical view on which prices mostly reflect noise, fashion and self-referential expectation — Keynes's beauty contest rather than an epistemic instrument. For each, say what happens to the disclosure regime, to the presumption of reliance, and to measuring harm by the fall in price after a corrective disclosure.

5. Finally, mark the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where the same rules could be read as committing us to nothing much — against the places where it makes a substantive commitment it cannot avoid, such as the assumption that there is a determinate price the market would have reached absent the concealment. Tell me whether that last assumption is a claim about a counterfactual fact or a legal fiction the practice cannot do without, and what difference that makes.

Please write in British English, under numbered headings corresponding to the questions above.
