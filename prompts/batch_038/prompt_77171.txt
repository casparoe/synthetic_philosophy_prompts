Our department's philosophy-of-science group spent last spring brokering an adversarial collaboration between two colleagues who have been talking past each other for years about idealization in scientific models. One is a mathematical economist of thoroughly Friedmanite temperament whose working credo is the move Samuelson nicknamed the F-twist: he quotes Friedman's line that "the more significant the theory, the more unrealistic the assumptions" at every opportunity, and he maintains that whether an assumption is false is simply the wrong question to ask of a model — what matters is whether the model's predictions hold up across the range of uses its builders actually put it to. The other is a philosopher with a background in statistical physics who thinks this gets things backwards: on her account, predictive reliability is a symptom rather than a ground of a model's warrant, and a model earns trust by getting the right things approximately right in the right respects and by being robust in the sense that its deliverances survive systematic variation of the idealizations that are admittedly false. Neither is a strawman, and I would ask you not to turn either into one. The economist has genuinely formidable resources, chief among them the universality cases from critical phenomena, where microscopic details that are demonstrably wrong wash out entirely and yet the same critical exponents come out right — so that a model can be maximally unrealistic in its assumptions and still maximally reliable in its predictions. The philosopher has an equally formidable reply in extrapolation and intervention, where models with spotless in-domain predictive records fail in ways their users had no way to anticipate. I was roped in as the third member, nominally to referee, and I have been handed the job of drafting the joint statement the two of them should have produced a year ago.

Please draft that statement. It should read as an actual document both would put their names to — a brief preamble, then sections — not as a report about such a document, and not as an adjudication of who is right. It needs, first, a numbered list of claims both can sign: what models are; how ubiquitous and how indispensable idealization is; what "unrealistic" can and cannot coherently mean when predicated of an assumption rather than of a sentence; what a de-idealization program amounts to and when one is owed; and what separates an idealization that has been deliberately chosen and controlled from one that has merely gone unexamined. Second, a disagreements table with these columns: the disputed thesis stated neutrally; the economist's formulation in his own preferred vocabulary; the philosopher's formulation in hers; and a verdict on whether that particular disagreement is substantive or verbal — whether, that is, it survives restatement in a vocabulary both already accept. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them, each paired with a concrete case or line of evidence both agree bears on it and with each side's prediction recorded in advance of the result. Consider at least the following as candidate cruxes, and then tell me which survive scrutiny: whether any non-question-begging criterion separates harmless idealizations from harmful ones without quietly reinstating the philosopher's demand for structural fidelity; whether an intact predictive record inside a model's construction domain licenses any confidence at all about what the model says outside it; whether the universality cases refute the philosopher or merely show that "the right structure" is a scale-relative notion she can absorb; whether ceteris paribus hedges are eliminable in principle or permanently load-bearing; and whether a model can be predictively excellent and explanatorily bankrupt, and if so whether that combination is epistemically tolerable. Fourth, a section in which each party states what they accept would count as evidence against their own position — for the economist, a case where a model's predictive record is fully intact yet the philosopher can specify in advance exactly where it will break; for the philosopher, a model whose assumptions are false in a respect she counts as load-bearing and which nonetheless keeps predicting well and keeps supporting correct interventions well outside the domain it was built for. Fifth, if you conclude that some identifiable part of this dispute is about words, say precisely which part, which of them was talking past the other, and what substantive disagreement remains once the vocabulary is pinned down. I expect at least one crux to prove genuinely substantive and at least one to dissolve; the value of the document lies in being able to say which is which.

Two further constraints. Keep the argument self-contained: no bibliography, no citation apparatus, no suggested readings. The two parties may gesture at familiar scientific examples — ideal gases, frictionless planes, infinite populations in population genetics, homogeneous mixing in epidemic models, perfectly competitive markets, point masses, universality classes at critical points — but every example invoked should be one a reader can follow without looking anything up, and none of the argumentative weight may rest on knowing the literature. Relatedly, I want the statement built around one constructed toy case rather than a real historical episode, because we found that arguing about actual episodes lets each side reinterpret the episode in its own image. So invent the case and describe it precisely: a model of some simple system with a stated false assumption, a stated domain in which its predictions are measurably accurate, and a stated extrapolation or intervention on which the two disagree about what will happen. Make it simple enough that a reader can check both parties' reasoning by hand, but not so rigged that the outcome is settled by the construction. Have the economist and the philosopher each record a prediction about it, and have them agree in advance what result would oblige each of them to revise.
