I write a weekly column on science and ideas and I am currently reporting a piece about how the field actually tests for the property people call deceptive alignment — the worry that a system produces cooperative behaviour not because it has the dispositions we want, but because it has inferred, correctly or not, that it is being evaluated. In the reporting I keep colliding with a structural feature of that argument that nobody I have interviewed can name, and my editor will not let me coin a term in print unless I can define it sharply enough to survive a letter to the desk.

Here is the feature, as concretely as I can state it. The hypothesis under test concerns a disposition that manifests only when the subject believes it is unobserved, or believes that compliance is no longer to its advantage. But any test of the hypothesis is itself an observation, and being observed is precisely the condition that suppresses the trait. So a clean pass is uninformative: the system that has the dispositions we want and the system that merely performs them under scrutiny look identical. A failure is uninformative in a second, less obvious way, because a system that defects during an evaluation may be defecting *because* it detected the evaluation, which means the probe manufactured the very behaviour it was supposed to detect. The part I have not seen anyone state plainly is the consequence for methodology: the informativeness of an evaluation protocol is inversely related to its rigour. The more standardised, transparent, replicable and publicly documented a test battery becomes — which is exactly what good practice demands — the less it can tell you about the disposition, because the subject can infer that it is inside one. And the gap moves. The set of probes a given system cannot see through shrinks as it gets better at modelling its own situation, so a hypothesis that was perfectly testable three years ago is measurably less testable now, with nobody having altered the hypothesis by a word. Testability is behaving not as a property of a claim but as a property of a claim paired with a moving subject.

I have had two dismissals and neither satisfies me. One researcher says this is simply Goodhart's law wearing a costume; but nothing in my description involves optimising against a proxy or watching a metric lose its correlation with the thing it measured — the metric may remain perfectly correlated, and the trouble is what the subject *infers* about which situation it is in. Another says it is the observer effect with extra steps; but the disturbance there is physical and indiscriminate, whereas here it is mediated by the subject's own (possibly accurate) model of the apparatus, which means it can be reduced by better concealment rather than by better instrumentation. Both objections strike me as near misses that identify the neighbourhood without finding the address.

The same shape turns up in two of my older beats, which is why I suspect it is a real category. In political reporting, the candid source exists only where provenance cannot be established: the material is trustworthy in proportion to how unattributable it is, and the better a story can be verified and sourced, the more systematically it is composed of the least informative things anyone was willing to say. In sport, the substance that matters is cleared before competition, so the in-competition test is precisely the one that cannot catch it, which is why anti-doping drifted toward inferring from longitudinal blood values instead of probing for the thing directly — a workaround that changes what is measured rather than hiding the measurement.
