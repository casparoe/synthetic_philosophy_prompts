My aunt does the ratings for a nonpartisan outfit that scores electoral-reform bills in state legislatures — endorse or reject, plus a two-sentence rationale. The rationales are mush, but they publish their model's numbers, and every bill gets scored against the same baseline: our current 60 single-member plurality districts, where they say the vote-to-seat mismatch is 13.8 points and 51% of ballots don't help elect anybody.

Two house conventions so the numbers mean something. "Mismatch" is half the sum of the absolute gaps between each party's vote share and seat share, in points. "Wasted" is the share of votes that don't contribute to electing anyone — for candidate systems, your vote is wasted if the person you marked loses; for list systems, votes for parties taking no seats plus the surplus above the last seat each winner took; for two-vote systems they average the two tiers.

The record so far:

1. Closed party list, six regions of ten seats, 5% threshhold. Mismatch 3.1, wasted 27%. Endorsed — "the chamber would finally look like the electorate."
2. One statewide list, 60 seats, 1% threshold. Mismatch 1.2, wasted 19%. Rejected — "a near-perfect mirror that only works if eleven parties can agree, and nobody in it answers to a neighborhood."
3. Parallel system: keep the 60 districts, add 40 statewide list seats on top, non-compensatory, 5% threshold. Mismatch 9.4, wasted 38%. Rejected — "the old system in a nice jacket."
4. STV, twelve five-seat districts, rank up to five, Hare quota. Mismatch 4.0, wasted 21%. Rejected — "eleven transfer rounds, double the spoiled ballots, a count no observer can follow."
5. Keep the districts, add a top-two runoff whenever nobody clears 40%. Mismatch 13.1, wasted 46% (decisive round only). Rejected — "same math, twice the cost."
6. Limited vote: twenty three-seat districts, two marks per ballot, top three win. Mismatch 5.5, wasted 22%. Endorsed — "for once almost nobody's ballot ends up in the bin."
7. Closed party list, two regions of thirty seats, 5% threshold. Mismatch 2.6, wasted 24%. Endorsed — "the best votes-to-seats match of anything with a real threshold, and no splinters."

Heres the one coming up, and it's the first bill where their two numbers point in opposite directions, which is why I cant just read the answer off the track record. Single non-transferable vote: fifteen four-seat districts, one mark per ballot, top four win, no ranking, no legal threshold, one counting pass. Their model says mismatch 9.8 (the biggest party takes 34% of the seats on 40% of the vote, and two regional parties overperform their statewide share), wasted 26%. Projected chamber has four parties, smallest at 8%, and since roughly a fifth of a district's vote buys a seat, nothing smaller gets in. Four members per neighborhood, so the "nobody to call" line doesn't apply. Ballot is one X.

What do you bet they say?

I want the machinery more than the guess. Which rules is this record actually consistent with? I can feel at least one that keys on the mismatch number and at least one that keys on the wasted number, and I suspect both need extra conditions about thresholds and ballot complexity to survive bills 2 and 4 — so lay out the candidates properly, including the attractive ones that a specific precedent kills (I thought "keep the districts small and local" was live until I looked again at 7). Then tell me which of the seven is the closest analog to the new bill, and whether that comparison actually does any work or just feels like it does. Every endorsed bill so far is good on both numbers and every rejected one is bad on both, so maybe the record just doesn't decide this — in which case say what the group would be committing itself to with each possible verdict, what a fair observer should say the honest rule is, and what they ought to vote if they want to be consistent with themselves. Last thing: invent one more bill for them to score, the mirror image of this one — good mismatch, bad wasted share — describe the system, predict its two numbers, and say what each candidate rule predicts about it, so that between the two cases every rule gets a different scorecard.
