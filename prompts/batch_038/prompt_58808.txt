For about four years I've been the volunteer who tries to get two groups in my town to stop fighting and actually agree to something, running out of a community program at the library. I should say up front I have no philosophy or economics training, I've just sat through a lot of these and started thinking about why some of them work.

Here's the idea I keep turning over. Most of these fights get run like there's one pie and we're arguing about slices, but usually the two sides care about different things, so they could trade: you take the thing you care way more about, I take the thing you don't care that much about. Somebody on a podcast called that gains from trade, and then another guest said it can't work with values because you can't measure caring on the same scale across two groups. I don't know who's right. I just know that when it works it really works, and when it fails I can't predict it in advance.

What goes wrong, from my side of the table:

- Both sides insist everything is a dealbreaker, so I can't tell who actually cares more, and the louder one wins by default. Ive tried getting each side to privately write down what they'd trade and what they'd never trade, and it just produces two lists of nevers.
- Some offers look like a trade but feel wrong in a way I can't name, especially when money is offered in exchange for the other side publicly saying it supports something.
- Sometimes there is genuinely nothing to trade, because both sides want the same physical thing used two different ways, and splitting it gives each of them a worse version of what they wanted. Half a building and half a grove of oaks is a bad building and dead trees.
- One side usually has money and the other doesn't, so I worry that a deal which only works because of the money is just bribery with extra steps.

What I want is something I can actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no-deal, and where it hands the decision back to the two groups because it's about thier values and not a fact I could check. Conditions: two hours, one room, six or so people from each side, nobody trained in any of this, and whatever comes out has to be written down in plain language and then ratified by both groups' committees a week later, so it can't depend on subtlety. I get one of these maybe every couple of months, so it has to be something I can remember rather than study for.

Run it on two real-ish cases from here. Both are about a parcel with old oaks and a crumbling cemetery fence that the preservation side wants kept as is and the housing side wants to put 40 units on the edge of. Case one: preservation drops its appeal against the project if the developer pays to restore the fence and stonework properly, and everybody keeps saying in public what they actually believe. Case two: same money, but preservation also agrees to publicly endorse the project and say in the paper that they support it, which would get it built about a year sooner. I'll admit I want case two to come out fine, because the money is real and the year matters to people who need housing. Tell me if it isn't.

Give me the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, a sentence or two each, with nothing before the list and nothing after it: items 1 through 3 are the steps of the procedure in order, item 4 says what each of the two cases comes out as, and item 5 says which of the three steps you're least sure of, what would change your mind about it, and where you expect me to watch this fall apart in the room.
