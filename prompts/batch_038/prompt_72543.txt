I'm assembling a closing reading for a seminar on explanation in history, and I'd like a Petrarchan sonnet — ABBAABBA CDECDE, with a hard volta at line 9 — on the question of why Britain industrialized and China did not. Let the octave speak in the question's own grammar: two finished civilizations lined up in 1750, each with commercialized farming, dense markets, and lives of comparable length, one of them pulling away. At the turn, the speaker should notice that the two terms of the comparison are not the same sort of thing — that the evidence concerns a river delta and an island, while the question concerns an empire and a nation — and that once the foil is redrawn, the familiar answer (coal seams luckily placed, overseas imports standing in for acreage Britain did not have) reads less like a destiny than like luck attached to a particular patch of ground. The closing tercet should leave the reader with the sense that a contrastive question is never simply asked: the foil, and what would count as a relevant difference, are settled before the evidence is consulted. Concrete and unsentimental; a historian of the period should recognize the argument, not merely the topic.
