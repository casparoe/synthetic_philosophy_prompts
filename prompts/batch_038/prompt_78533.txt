I cover science and ideas for a mid-sized general-interest magazine — mostly physics and cognition, philosophy when my editor lets me get away with it. I've got a long feature coming up on why meaning might be more like a weather forecast than a label stuck on a jar, and the spine of it is Quine's radical translation thought experiment: the field linguist, the scuttling rabbit, the shout of gavagai, and the fact that the pointing gesture can't decide whether the word picks out the animal, one of its undetached parts, the brief stage of it that's happening right now, or something else entirely. My editor likes to run a piece of verse at the top of these features as an on-ramp for readers who'd otherwise bounce off the argument, and this time she's told me to bring something original rather than license a poem, which we couldn't afford anyway. Last one I did was a sonnet about an entirely different topic and it worked, but a sonnet turns once and then resolves, and this argument doesn't resolve — it goes round. So I want a sestina.

The reason I want a sestina specifically, and this is the whole commission: its trick is that the same six end-words keep coming back in a permuted order, which is pretty much exactly the shape of the problem. The words stay put, the assignment underneath them slides, and nothing in the behavioral evidence tells you which rotation is the right one. I want the six end-words to *be* the candidate referents the linguist is cycling through. Rabbit, part, and stage are the three I'd insist on; for the other three you choose from the same family — a universal, a fusion, an event, whatever you think genuinely preserves the pattern of assent and dissent when the natives say the word. If any of those three I'm guessing at is something Quine wouldn't actually countenance, tell me before the poem, not after, because this piece is going to a fact-checker. Full sestina, six sestets and the three-line envoi; if you think a shorter variant carries the rotation better for a magazine page, make the case in a sentence and then do it, but keep the rotation honest — a reader who counts should find the permutation actually there.

Some constraints I'd really like you to hold to:

- Speaker is the linguist, first person, notebook in hand, and the poem stays inside the encounter: the clearing, the rabbit, the finger, the assent, the growing suspicion that the notebook is the unreliable instrument. Don't let the linguist step outside the scene and lecture the reader about philosophy of language.
- Banned words, hard rule: meaning, reference, referent, translation, semantics, signification, denote. The argument has to be carried by rabbits and parts and stages and pointing and nodding, not by the jargon. Gavagai itself is obviously allowed, it's the hook.
- The envoi has to land what I take to be the actual bite, which is not that we'll never work out what gavagai means. It's that there's no further fact to work out — all the behavioral evidence is already on the table and the question "which is it" isn't one the world is holding an answer to. And I think the twist Quine adds is that the same goes for my own English, that the question can only be asked against some background language you're already standing in. If you think I'm overstating or mangling that, say so plainly and write the envoi to the correct version.
- Dry humor welcome, Quine is funny, but no winking at the reader and no punchline ending. The last three lines should be straight.
- It has to read aloud cleanly, because we'll cut an audio version — I don't want a form so knotted that a listener loses the thread on the third stanza.

Two last things. After the poem, give me five or six lines of plain prose — not for publication, just for me — saying which end-word is standing in for which ontological option, so I can check the poem against what I'm claiming in the piece. And please don't reach for a Quine epigraph: I can't clear permissions, and I don't trust either of us to reproduce him exactly from memory, so paraphrase if you need to gesture at anything he said. I'll be reading this on a plane with no wifi, so anything you'd normally look up, just flag as something you're unsure about rather than dressing it up.
