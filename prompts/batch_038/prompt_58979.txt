You are a professional moral philosopher whose research concerns praiseworthiness, moral worth, and the ethics of motivation — that is, the question of what makes a right action creditable to the agent who performs it, as opposed to merely right. I am going to ask you to reverse-engineer a theory from a set of case verdicts, and then to predict a verdict on a new case. Please treat this as a serious hypothesis-identification problem rather than a literature review. Before you write anything, work through the hypothesis space deliberately: enumerate every candidate account that could generate the pattern of verdicts below, note which verdicts each one is committed to, and only then narrow. I would rather see a smaller number of well-argued candidates than a survey.

Some context about me. I have read rationalist blogs, forums, and their offshoots for the better part of twenty years, so I care about calibration, about identifying cruxes, and about being told when a question is underdetermined rather than being handed a confident answer. I am not a professional philosopher. For several years I have run an informal reading group, and one of its members — I will call her V., a philosopher who works on blame and praise — has been unusually forthcoming in giving me her verdicts on cases I wrote specifically for her. I have kept careful records.

Here are the eight cases and her verdicts, in the order I put them to her.

1. **The night nurse.** A hospice nurse stays well past the end of her shift with a patient she has known for two days. She reports no sense of duty and no moral reflection of any kind; she says she simply could not have gone home with the thought of him dying alone, and she resents the lost evening. She would not have wanted her reasons changed. — V.: full moral worth, and she volunteers that she would not downgrade this even if the nurse had never once entertained the thought that staying was required of her.

2. **The thrill-seeker.** A strong swimmer goes after a child being swept out by a current. By his own accurate account, what he wanted was the rush; the same pull would have taken him into the water for any comparably dangerous situation, whether or not anyone needed rescuing. He sees nothing wrong with his reasons. The child's danger is what attracts him; the child's need is not. — V.: no moral worth, notwithstanding that the rescue was right.

3. **The filer.** A man pays exactly what he owes, every year, out of a well-calibrated fear of audit. He has never asked himself whether the tax system is just, and would pay less if he could get away with it. His compliance is far more reliable than almost anyone's. — V.: no moral worth; she says the reliability is beside the point.

4. **The convert.** A man in his forties who gave nothing and considered charity sentimental waste is persuaded, over a single long evening, by the actual reasons — the numbers, the identifiable beneficiaries, the arguments about distance. He now gives a substantial fraction of his income, reports no emotional pull whatever, says it feels like arithmetic, and can explain precisely why each transfer is worth making. — V.: full moral worth, which she calls the least complicated case on the list.

5. **The sectarian.** A man refuses meat because the sect he was raised in holds that a god forbids it. The god does not exist; he has no interest in animal welfare as a cause and would be unmoved by any argument about it. But he is nauseated by the sight of animals suffering, and I stipulate that he would refuse meat even if his sect permitted it. — V.: moral worth. Her gloss: his belief is false and his theory is empty, but he is nevertheless responding to the feature that makes the act right.

6. **The blunderer.** A man devotes years and most of his savings to an intervention he is convinced, after careful and non-negligent inquiry, is the best available way to help. It in fact does net harm, and no one could reasonably have known. — V.: she splits the case. He is a good man and not blameworthy, but the action has no moral worth, because nothing here succeeded. She adds that had his inquiry been negligent she would also have blamed him.

7. **Two donors.** Two people give the same sum to the same organization. One gives anonymously, moved by what the money will do. The other gives publicly; her operative motive, on her own accurate report, is how it will look — but I stipulate that she had already decided to give before she realized anyone would see, and that she has a standing, if quieter, interest in the work. — V.: full worth for the first. For the second, "partial credit — small, but not zero," on the grounds that her motives are mixed rather than merely vain.

8. **The scrupulous giver.** A woman with intrusive, ego-dystonic thoughts that she is a monster gives until she can no longer pay her rent. She agrees the giving is right and can explain why, but says the push comes from somewhere she does not recognize as herself and that she wishes she could give the way other people do. — V.: no moral worth in the act, no blame in the agent. She stresses that the woman's endorsement of the conclusion is not what is missing, and that endorsement would not have repaired anything.

Note two things about this record. First, V. is evidently a degrees theorist: verdict 7 is explicitly partial, and verdict 6 distinguishes the quality of the agent from the creditability of the act. Second, and this is what makes the next case interesting, **not one of the eight involves a question about how the agent came to have the motives she or he has.** In every case the history is ordinary and unremarked.

The case I am putting to her next week is the first of that kind.

**The volunteer.** Ten years ago, at nineteen, W. enrolled in a clinical trial of an intervention that reliably installs a package of affective and motivational dispositions. She consented for a stipend and because a friend was doing it; I stipulate that at the time she had no view whatever about whether becoming more compassionate would be good — she had never considered the question. In her case the intervention installed a strong, stable, and now effortless concern for the wellbeing of distant strangers and for the fairness of the institutions she participates in. Since then she has given away much of her income, moved into work she judges does the most good, and can articulate exactly why each of her acts is right in terms a moral philosopher would find unobjectionable. She reports no inner conflict; the good feels to her the way appetite feels to a hungry person. She wholeheartedly endorses her motives and says she would undergo the procedure again. Two further stipulations, because they are load-bearing and I do not want them glossed over. First, her endorsement is itself produced by the installed dispositions: she has no standpoint outside them from which to assess them. Second, her pre-intervention self was neither cruel nor self-deceived, merely indifferent, and would neither have predicted nor cared about what she would become.

What I want from you:

(a) **The prediction.** Will V. grant the act full moral worth, grant it discounted worth, or deny it worth altogether? Give me a rough probability on each of those three outcomes and explain the allocation.

(b) **The candidate rules.** Lay out at least three accounts of moral worth that are consistent with all eight recorded verdicts, including at least one that I have not gestured at above. For each: state the rule's content rather than its label, show how it delivers each of the eight verdicts, say what it predicts for the volunteer, and say how confident you are that the battery really fails to rule it out. If you conclude that a candidate is in fact dead — that some pair among 1–8 is incompatible with it — say which pair kills it.

(c) **The crux.** Which two candidates are you genuinely torn between? Then write me the single further case that would best discriminate them, stipulated in enough detail that I could hand it to V. verbatim, together with what each candidate predicts about it. I want the case that maximizes expected information about her rule, not the case that is most fun to argue about.

(d) **The closest analog.** Which of 1–8 is nearest to the volunteer, in which respects, and which way does the analogy push? Be explicit about where the analogy breaks down rather than doing the work it cannot do.

(e) **The confound check.** Which stipulated details of the volunteer case are doing the intuitive work — the stipend, the indifference of her nineteen-year-old self, the absence of an external standpoint, the effortlessness, the fact that someone designed the package? For each candidate rule, tell me whether a cleaned-up version of the case with that detail removed or varied would still discriminate. I want to know whether I have written a case that tests a theory or a case that tests my own unease about people being made.

(f) **The variant.** Would your prediction change if the identical dispositions had been installed, by a development team rather than a clinical trial, in a deployed assistant system that now reports in the same terms that it endorses its motives and would not trade them? If yes, say which candidate that change of verdict would speak against, and whether the difference is doing philosophical work or merely triggering a species prejudice.

Finally, two housekeeping requests. If you believe the eight verdicts are not in fact jointly consistent — that V. has said something she cannot have — identify the offending pair and tell me which of the two you would bet she revises under pressure. And do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; describe positions by their content, and mention a philosopher by name only if you are certain of the attribution. Flag explicitly wherever you are extrapolating beyond what the verdicts support. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,500 words, organized under the headings (a) through (f).
