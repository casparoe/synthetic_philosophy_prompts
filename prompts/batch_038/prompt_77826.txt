I chair an interdisciplinary panel — historians, a couple of geographers, and one actual stratigrapher who's mostly there to tut at us — and for three years we've been ruling on whether proposed Anthropocene start-dates are admissable as candidates for our programme's joint submission. We never wrote our criteria down, which I'm starting to think was a mistake. Our record so far:

In: the 1610 dip in atmospheric CO2 that Lewis and Maslin dubbed the Orbis spike, read off Antarctic ice. In: the first appearance of bomb-test plutonium in annually laminated lake mud, 1952. In, but only as a corroborating marker rather than as the boundary itself: spheroidal carbonaceous particles — fly ash — in lake sediments, which show up in Britain from the 1850s and pretty much everywhere from the 1950s. In: the broiler chicken bone assemblage in landfill and midden deposits from mid-century on.

Out: the early-agriculture hypothesis — the CO2 and methane anomalies from clearance and rice paddies, roughly eight to three thousand years back. Out: Crutzen's 1784, Watt's engine. Out: a proposal I rather liked, from a colleague in history of science, to date the boundary from the year when the total mass of human-made stuff — concrete, aggregate, metals, plastics — first exceeded the mass of all living things, which on the best estimates is around about now, give or take a decade.

Two things bother me about this record. First, we let the chicken bones in even though the archive is a rubbish tip, and we let in a *dip* at 1610 that subsequently went back up again. So whatever rule we're running, it isn't the tidy one I'd have written down if you'd asked me in 2021. Second, the real stratigraphic bodies threw the formal epoch out in 2024, and not because anyone thought the signal was fake — mostly because the deposit is too thin and the boundary too recent. Which makes a deflationary reading of us available: that our panel isn't tracking anything about the world at all, just tracking which proposals a stratigrapher would let through the door.

Here's the case we have to rule on next month. A doctoral student wants to fix the boundary at spring 2020, using the global lockdown — the weeks when the planet went quiet all at once. Their evidence: the collapse in high-frequency seismic noise recieved simultaneously by seismometers worldwide, the transient dips in nitrogen dioxide and CO2, and the layer of masks and gloves now turning up in beach, river and harbour sediments. Their argument is that this is the most globally syncronous anthropogenic event in history — not smeared over decades like the fly ash or the chickens, but everywhere within weeks — and that its traces do survive: in annually resolved snow and ice, in tree-ring widths, in sediment traps, and in that PPE layer.

So: predict what we'll say. Then do the work behind it. Lay out the candidate rules consistent with all seven verdicts above (I'd expect at least three, including the deflationary one), say which verdicts actually pin each rule down and which leave it loose, and identify which of the surviving rules diverge on the 2020 case and in which direction. Give me your best guess with reasons for preferring that rule over the others. Tell me which of our precedents is the closest analogue and argue it rather than assume it — I suspect it's 1610, but 1610 might cut both ways, since it's a dip too and yet it's in ice rather than in instruments. And if the student's proposal is genuinely underdetermined by our record, say plainly what principle we'd be adopting with each vote: what we'd be committing ourselves to about what counts as a geological record at all, and hence about who gets to decide when the Anthropocene began. Finally, name one further case — something we could actually rule on next term — that would settle which rule we're running.
