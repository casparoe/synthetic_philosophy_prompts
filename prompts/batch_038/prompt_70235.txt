I'm in a formal epistemology seminar this term and we've now spent three weeks on accuracy-first epistemology, and the discussion has gone in circles in a way that's genuinely driving me nuts. The weird thing is that nobody in the room disputes a single proof. Everyone accepts the representation results, everyone accepts that an incoherent credence function gets accuracy-dominated while a coherent one never does. What they can't agree on is whether any of it establishes anything.

One camp says the dominance arguments do real work: a Dutch book only shows that incoherent agents lose money on bets they'd accept, which is a fact about their betting behavior, whereas dominance shows a purely epistemic defect — there's another credence function that comes out more accurate no matter which world is actual — and that's exactly the kind of reason an epistemology ought to trade in. The other camp says it's repackaging: you have to choose an inaccuracy measure before you can run anything, and the constraints you impose on it (addative across propositions, symmetric, proper so each credence function expects itself to do best) already encode the norms you claim to derive, so the theorem is unpacking assumptions, not discovering anything. And the choosing isn't innocent — Brier and log disagree wildly about how hard to punish near-certainties, and nothing inside the framework tells you which to use, so "accuracy-first" isn't actually first.

My working theory, which I want you to tell me is wrong if it is, is that this isn't a substantive disagreement at all. Both sides agree on every formal fact; they're just using "vindicate" to mean two different things, one a mathematical relation and one a normative one, and the whole thing is a verbal dispute dressed up as metaepistemology. Or maybe they're answering different questions under one heading: "is your credence function dominated?" has a settled answer, "does being dominated give you a reason to change it?" doesn't follow from any theorem.

Diagnose that for me using what each side actually claims — I don't want a tour of the framework, I can get that from the lecture notes. Two specific things I want folded in:

First, a toy case I worked out. Three worlds, atoms A, B, C, Brier score. An agent with 0.6 on each atom is dominated by the coherent (1/3, 1/3, 1/3), which beats it at every world, 0.88 versus about 0.667. Fine. But now a second agent who gives A 0.5 and not-A 0.5 — perfectly coherent on that coarse pair, so never dominated if you only score the coarse algebra — while also giving B 0.1 and C 0.6. Score the fine algebra and she's dominated; score only the coarse one and she's untouchable. Same agent, opposite verdicts, purely because of a choice about which propositions get scored. Is that a real objection, in that it makes the framework's verdicts hostage to a stipulation, or a red herring because obviously you score the whole algebra and that's built into the setup? And does your answer depend on which diagnosis of the debate you land on? That connection is what I most want to see.

Second, the immodesty point: a proper measure recommends itself, so the accuracy argument can't give a reason to anyone who doesn't already care about accuracy as measured that way. Is that fatal, or just a sign the project is explication rather than persuasion?

Then tell me what would count as progress. Be concrete — a distinction someone could draw, an argument that pins down a measure without presupposing coherence, a reframing of what the project is even for, or a demonstration that no progress should be expected. If it's that last one, explain why we should keep teaching this as though the debate were live.
