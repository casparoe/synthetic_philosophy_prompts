I tagged along to a reading group last week (I'm not in the philosophy department, just going with a friend) and two of the grad students, Marta and Elias, got into a pretty heated argument about Lewis's triviality result for indicative conditionals. Neither would give an inch. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it — I'm paraphrasing from memory, so if I've garbled something substantive, tell me straight rather than quietly working around it.

Marta started: Lewis assumes the conditional expresses a proposition with a probability, and that this probability equals the corresponding conditional probability, for every probability function you like; he then shows that can only happen if the probabilities are trivial. So if you want to keep that equation — and you do, since it's most of what makes conditionals worth having — you have to give up the idea that an indicative conditional says something straightforwardly true or false. Adams made exactly this move: indicatives aren't truth-apt, their assertability just tracks conditional probability.

Elias: you're sliding two different claims into one. The equation is about the probability of a content; Adams's thesis is about assertability. Lewis's result is a formal theorem about the first and says nothing whatever about the second. Someone could accept Adams on assertability while denying truth-aptness for reasons that have nothing to do with Lewis, and someone could reject Adams while accepting the theorem.

Marta: call it a slide, but it isn't accidental. If a conditional isn't truth-apt it has no probability at all, so the equation can't even be written down. That's precisely where the theorem pushes you: truth-aptness plus the equation is impossible, so one of them has to go, and the theorem tells you which is cheaper.

Elias: except Lewis didn't reach that conclusion. He kept truth conditions for indicatives — basically the material conditional — and shoved the probabilistic story over to assertability, Grice-style. On his own account the equation is simply false and the conditional is still perfectly truth-apt. Which makes the result just a refuted conjecture, like any other equation that turned out not to hold. Nothing deep about it.

Marta: that's too glib. The refutation isn't local. Lewis doesn't test one particular connective; he shows that no connective at all can satisfy the equation without trivializing the probabilities. And it generalizes past Adams to Stalnaker-style accounts too, unless you let the proposition expressed shift around from one probability function to the next — which nobody independently motivated. It's just a hatch for dodging the theorem.

Elias: the shifting-content move is independently motivated, because indicatives are context-sensitive anyway, and Lewis's argument only gets going if the very same content gets plugged into every probability function in the family. If contents are context-dependent, the hypothesis the theorem kills isn't the thesis anyone actually held. And this is the part Marta keeps skiping: the proof runs by conditionalizing, so it needs conditional probabilities to be defined for antecedents your own credence gives zero probability, and that assumption is doing real work.
