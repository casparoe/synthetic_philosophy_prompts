I'm an editor on the science desk of a mid-size news site, and I keep ending up as the person who has to referee fights about the word "function." A paper or a press release says some stretch of DNA, some protein, some organ has a function, a reader or a rival lab emails in saying that's not a function at all, it's just something the thing happens to do, and suddenly I'm mediating a philosophy of biology dispute with no training in it and a headline that locks in an hour. It happens a couple times a month, sometimes several times a week during the big consortium announcements.

I know the rough shape of the disagreement — that one camp says a trait's function is whatever it was selected for, so function is a historical claim about why the trait is there, and the other camp says function is just the contribution the trait makes to some capacity of the system right now, no history required. What I need is that turned into something my reporters can actually run.

So: write me a decision procedure, step by step, that a reporter with a biology degree but zero philosophy can apply to an incoming function claim. For each step I want the question to ask, what counts as a pass, what the possible outputs are at that point (kill it, run as written, run with a specific fix, hand it to me, hand it to an outside biologist), and which steps are real judgment calls wearing a procedure costume. It has to handle the messy middle cases rather than only the clean ones: a trait that does several things at once, a trait that got co-opted for something new recently so there's no selection history for the new use yet, a trait that clearly does something and is clearly useless or even harmful, and a claim where the two camps would both say the sentence is true but mean different things by it — because for me that last one is a wording problem, not a truth problem, and the procedure should treat it that way.

I also need it to distinguish where the claim appears. A scientist saying it in a quote, my reporter paraphrasing it, the headline, and the caption are four different levels of commitment and the procedure should say what's allowed at each.

Give me the reader-facing bits too: a one-sentence plain-language gloss for each of the two senses that doesn't talk down to anyone, and a rule for when we have to tell readers there's a dispute versus when the distinction doesn't matter to the story and dragging it in would just be noise. Then a crib card version, short enough to read in ninety seconds before deadline.

Run the whole thing on three cases: the ENCODE fight over how much of the human genome counts as functional, a press release announcing a newly discovered second job for a protein that was already well characterized, and a story about some structure we've been calling vestigial that turns out to do something measurable. For at least one of them the procedure should land somewhere my desk will hate — a kill, or a rewrite that guts the hook — and I want you to say so plainly instead of softening it.

Finally, be straight with me about the costs. Which distinctions from the technical debate did you drop to make this usable, and what will this procedure systematically get wrong as a result? If I want, name a couple of the refinements people have proposed to patch the historical account and tell me whether any of them is worth adding a step for or whether they're all downstream of stuff a newsroom can safely ignore.
