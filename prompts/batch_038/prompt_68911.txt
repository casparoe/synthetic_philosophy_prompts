our ancient historiography seminar wraps next week and the last session is on how we know anything at all about the distant past. we've done the Herodotus part where he passes along what the various cities claim while insisting that setting a story down isn't the same thing as believing it, and the Thucydides part where he grumbles that people accept stories untested because they prefer whatever's pleasant to hear, and then we moved to the manuscript side, where literally everything survives as copies of copies and the tradition branches like a family tree. i want to close the session with a poem built like a stemma codicum, because I think the shape can carry the argument better than any five-minute summary.

here's roughly what I'm picturing, call it 22-28 lines:

- at the top, the archetype. it's lost, so don't write it — put a lacuna there, a bracketed gap, and let the reader see that the source is missing rather than being told about it.
- underneath, two branches, alpha and beta, each a stanza or two, so we end up with four witnesses in all. every one of them is handing down the same single reported sentence — something like the last words of a general, or what a messenger said at a gate before it opened. keep it invented; I don't want anyone trying to fact-check a real quotation.
- the constraint that does the work: corruption always runs downhill toward the smoother reading. each copy tidies the grammar, mends the meter, makes the speaker nobler or the story more edifying, so the further down the tree you go the prettier and more coherent it gets. the awkward, unmetrical, faintly embarassing bits should be the ones that survive closest to the top — because the harder reading is the likelier original, and I want the poem to obey that rule rather than name-check it.
- the witnesses should disagree in specific, collatable ways: a name mangled into a more flattering one, a word swapped to smooth out a rough join, a whole clause dropped because it was uncomfortable. a reader should be able to lay them side by side and see which readings group together.
- the final stanza steps out of the tree: it's the editor's conjecture, back at the left margin, alone. here's the turn — the reconstruction is not an average of the branches and not the most pleasing version, it's harder and stranger than any surviving copy. that's what the principle actually implies, and I want it to land as a surprise.

meter can be loose, but the copies within a branch should be recognizably the same line underneath the damage, so you feel the descent. someone who's done textual criticism should see exactly what I'm doing and enjoy it; someone who hasn't should just come away with a good poem about how thin the thread is between us and anything that ever happened.
