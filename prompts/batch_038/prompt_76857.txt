Act as a professional philosopher of religion who has argued both sides of the problem of evil, and help me prepare for a question period rather than write the talk itself. I am a high school senior, and next month I present at my school's "Faith and Reason" evening series, which the chaplaincy and the philosophy club run jointly.

My thesis: the free will defense cannot reach the suffering that the pre-human fossil record documents — hundreds of millions of years of predation, parasitism, disease, and mass extinction — because no free creature existed to misuse anything; and soul-making theodicies are hostage to an empirical claim they cannot meet for those creatures, since an animal dying slowly of an infection is not thereby being formed into anything. So the live question is not whether God might have reasons in principle, but why the distribution and timing of suffering looks the way paleontology says it does, and an appeal to mystery is not an answer to a question about distribution.

The room will be: our chaplain, a Reformed Presbyterian with seminary training who knows the free will defense and skeptical theism well and will be courteous but exacting; two AP Biology teachers who will not tolerate deep time being treated as a side issue; roughly fifteen students, most of them religious, almost none of whom have read philosophy; and one alumna, now a second-year philosophy major, who is a classical theist and thinks in terms of divine simplicity and timelessness.

Please think carefully, and begin with a diagnosis of three or four sentences about what each subgroup in that room is actually worried about, since that determines which questions will be asked. Then produce my prep sheet: the objections I should expect, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, each with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds and a candid note on where that reply is softest. Finish by telling me what I should concede in my first two minutes so it cannot be turned against me later, which single reply has to land for the talk to survive, and the one question I am privately hoping nobody asks.
