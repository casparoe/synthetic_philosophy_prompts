I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of religion seminar of sixteen students; we meet once a week for eighty minutes. This week's topic is divine hiddenness. We've read Schellenberg's argument in the version that turns on non-resistant non-belief, along with the two replies that dominated our section: the suggestion that God must remain at some epistemic distance if our moral and religious choices are to stay genuinely our own, and the broader skeptical-theist worry that we are badly placed to say what a perfectly loving God would or would not make evident to a given person. I've been given the first forty minutes of the session to run something, with the rest left for discussion.

I want an activity in which the students collide with the argument's central difficulty themselves rather than being told what it is. Please design it in full. Requirements:

**Setting and resources.** Sixteen students, forty minutes of activity plus debrief, a seminar room with movable chairs, a whiteboard, no technology, and no budget beyond small consumable stakes (chocolate, or participation marks I can award).

**Core mechanic.** A role-play in which every student occupies both positions in turn: someone sincerely and persistently trying to make contact with one particular other person, and someone deliberately withholding contact while holding a private card explaining why the withholding is, they are assured, in the seeker's own interest. The thing I want them to feel is not that hiddenness is either cruel or kind, but the gap between the judgements each side makes of the same conduct. From inside the seeker's position, the absence of any recognizable contact reads as indifference or as absence full stop; from inside the hider's position, that identical silence reads as care. I want them to notice that they have just made both judgements about the very same behaviour within ten minutes, and then to have to explain how both could be true — or to say which one the hiddenness argument needs to be mistaken.

**Round structure.** Please build it as a short ladder of rounds in which one condition changes at a time, so that the group generates its own comparison cases instead of being handed a list. At minimum the rounds should vary (i) whether the hider may send ambiguous or partial signals as opposed to maintaining strict silence, and (ii) whether the seeker is sincere, informed, and persistent or merely lukewarm and easily distracted. The debrief should then be able to point to exactly one cell as the analogue of non-resistant non-belief, and to the others as cases the argument was never about — a distinction several of my classmates currently run together.

**Stakes.** Something the seeker actually wants must be on the line, and it must be settled publicly in the room at the end of each round, so that the choices have consequences rather than remaining hypothetical.

**Neutrality.** Nobody should be told in advance which philosopher's argument they are enacting. It must work equally well for the devout students and the atheists in the room: no one should be asked to disclose or adopt a view about God, and it must not drift into a confessional.

**Deliverables.** A minute-by-minute timeline; the exact wording of the role cards and any private instructions; the scoring or reward scheme; a materials list; and a debrief that maps particular moments in particular rounds onto particular premises and replies — which round stages the greater-good or epistemic-distance reply, which one exposes the objection that this reply only works if a loving God would see to it that the seeker could recognize the signals being sent, and which one shows what the argument gains by restricting itself to seekers who are trying.

**Two further things**, which I expect to be pressed on. First: tell me where this is most likely to break down when actually run — where students will guess the point early, break character, find the human analogy trivializing or offensive, or where a hider will simply refuse to withhold — and what I should do in each case. Second, and more important: be frank about which features of the divine case the role-play cannot reproduce at all, and which I will have to supply verbally myself. I have in mind the hider's ignorance of what is genuinely good for the seeker, the fact that the seeker's whole existence and highest good are supposedly at issue rather than a chocolate bar, and the permanence and universality of the silence; but I assume there are others, and I would rather hear them from you now than from my professor on Thursday.
