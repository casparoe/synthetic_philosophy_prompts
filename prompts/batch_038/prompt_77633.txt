I have no academic training in philosophy. I left school at sixteen and have spent eleven years in supported housing, the last four of them on the management committee of a small provider. Over the past year two funders and one local authority have begun asking us to certify something about each arrangement we house people in: that the arrangement leaves the people in it free. Nobody has said what that means, and there is no agreed way of establishing it. We are expected to produce a one-page standard, apply it, and defend the results, and I have been given the job of writing the page.

I have read a little around the subject — enough to discover that people who write 'freedom' are often not writing the same thing: some mean the absence of anyone getting in your way, some mean actually being able to direct your own life, and some, following Berlin and then Pettit, mean not being at the mercy of another person's discretion. Reading further without knowing what I am looking for has not helped. So I want the specification first: the list of requirements that any account of freedom must satisfy before I would build a standard on it. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it, and say whether it is strict for my purpose or is really a preference belonging to one camp.

My purpose fixes some of the answers, so let me state it precisely. The standard will be applied by people like me, not by philosophers, to arrangements we can observe largely from outside: a portfolio of assured shorthold tenancies held by one landlord; a supported-living scheme run by a religious charity; a depot where drivers work under algorithmic dispatch; an adult daughter caring for a parent with dementia in the family home. We usually cannot interview the people involved at length, and where we can, what they tell us is tangled up with gratitude, shame and adaptation. We must produce a grade, justify it to a funder, and say what would have to change to raise it.

These are the cases I keep coming back to. Please build the requirement list around them rather than merely checking that it does not contradict them.

1. A landlord who has never raised a rent above the going rate, never entered without knocking, and tells his tenants plainly that he never will — but who holds their tenancies periodically, with no statutory protection, so that he could turn any of them out next month for any reason or for none, and there is nothing they could do about it. My intuition is that those tenants are not fully free, although nothing has been done to them.
2. Its mirror image: a tenant with a lease enforceable in court against anyone, including the landlord and his heirs, who is severely agoraphobic and has not left the building in three years. Something has plainly gone wrong for her, and I do not know whether an account of freedom should be able to say what, or whether it should stay quiet.
3. A resident of a faith-based scheme who may leave whenever he wishes and would be helped to leave, whose every material need is met, and who is affronted by the suggestion that he is not free to go. I cannot tell whether his indignation counts as evidence or as part of the problem.
4. Two depot drivers on identical schedules. One has a contractual right to swap a shift, exercisable without giving a reason; the other has to ask the supervisor, who almost always says yes. In any given week they do the same things. I think they are differently situated, and I want to know whether an account can say so without appealing to how either of them feels about it.
5. A resident choosing between forty varieties of the same packaged meal, and the same resident with no say at all in whether she is moved to a different home next year. If freedom is a matter of how many options a person has, the first looks like a great deal of it. I suspect that counting is the wrong shape of question altogether, and I would like to be told whether that suspicion can be made precise.
6. A young man who asks us to hold his benefit payments and release a small weekly sum, because he does not trust himself with the whole amount. He has arranged to be constrained. I cannot tell whether that is a loss of freedom, a gain, or neither, and a standard that cannot say so is no use to me.
7. A woman who has cared for her son for thirty years and could stop: the door is not locked, there is a respite service she has never used, and nobody is forcing her. She would call herself free, and I would be uneasy calling her that. I want to know whether an account can register my unease without turning freedom into a measure of how well a person is living — because if it does, our grade becomes a judgement about her character rather than about her circumstances, and we should not be issuing it.

Beyond the cases, the list should tell me at least the following. Whether there are requirements about whose freedom is being counted, and about what to do when a scheme's residents are freer precisely because their neighbours are less so, since a grading standard cannot dodge that. Whether an account can be required to work without access to people's desires and feelings, given that mine must, and what that constraint costs — my hunch is that this one requirement excludes more candidate accounts than any other, and I would rather have that hunch tested than confirmed. How the list accommodates the obvious fact that some rules increase what people can do, so that the older view, on which every law takes so much from liberty, cannot be right; I want to know what has to be true for that to be so. And whether some of what is demanded of these accounts — that freedom hold up against everything a powerful person might possibly do, for instance — is really an egalitarian political programme built into the definition of a word. If it is, say so, and say what should be put in its place.

Above all: tell me whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. If it is, identify the subset, say which member of it has to be weakened or dropped, and say what we lose by dropping it. If the list amounts to a demonstration that no account can serve my purpose, I would much rather learn that now than after we have printed the page.

Please take a definite position throughout. I am not after a survey of what each camp says about each requirement, and I do not want the options laid out for me to choose between; I want your judgement on what the requirements are, which are strict, which one or two must be relaxed, and why you think so. Where you believe a requirement implied by my cases is spurious, say so plainly. And keep the literature out of it — I need the specification, not a set of references.

Write in British English. End with your conclusion in machine-readable form on the very last line, as \answer{...} containing the single requirement you judge cannot be retained alongside the rest, in no more than eight words.
