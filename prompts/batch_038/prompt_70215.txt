You are to write as a historian of twentieth-century analytic philosophy who has worked closely on Gilbert Ryle and who is equally at home in the contemporary debate over the extended mind thesis. Please think carefully rather than answering on the first pass.

Some background, so that we are working from the same case. Clark and Chalmers argue in "The Extended Mind" that when an external resource is reliably available, routinely invoked, and its contents automatically endorsed, it can be a constituent of the cognitive process it serves; their Otto and Inga example is meant to show that a notebook can play for Otto the role biological memory plays for Inga, so that the skin-and-skull boundary is not the boundary of the mind. Critics have replied that coupling a process to a resource is not the same as constituting it by that resource. A further distinction, pressed by later critics, is between scaffolds an agent has genuinely integrated — she can correct the tool, notices when it fails, and can carry on reasoning when it is switched off — and scaffolds that merely substitute for capacities she would otherwise exercise, and which atrophy in the substitution.

The case on which I want Ryle's verdict is this. A national medical regulator is considering a rule under which clinicians may practise, and be examined for licensure, in the continuous company of a diagnostic assistant: a current large-language-model system that surfaces findings, proposes differentials, and flags possible oversights in real time. The regulator's draft rationale states that what is being licensed is the clinician-plus-assistant system, on the ground that the boundary of the cognizing agent is not the boundary of the skull. Two clinicians appear in the accompanying impact assessment. Dr A has used such a system for a decade, argues with it, has learned where it goes wrong, and diagnoses competently when it is unavailable. Dr B adopted one last year, accepts its prompts without further thought, and has stopped performing several procedures she once did unaided.

What would Ryle have made of this — of the regulator's rationale, and of the two clinicians? Specifically: would he accept that there is a substantive question about whether the cognition in these cases lies inside Dr A or extends into the software, or would he hold that the regulator, and for that matter Clark and Chalmers, are in the grip of a picture of the mind as a container with contents and are merely disputing how large the container should be? Does his contrast between mere habits and intelligent capacities, or his treatment of knowing-how and knowing-that, let him say something definite about the difference between Dr A and Dr B without taking either side on the location question? And would he think either clinician knows the diagnosis when she gets it right?
