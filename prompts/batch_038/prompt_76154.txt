For thirty-eight years I ran a physical chemistry laboratory concerned with reaction kinetics, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy and the strategic branch of game theory with some seriousness. One idea I keep returning to is what is called a commitment race among advanced artificial systems. As I understand the picture: when two or more agents expect that whoever first occupies some decisive strategic position will thereafter set the terms for everyone else, each has a reason to bind itself to a course of action early, visibly, and irreversibly, so as to shape the others' expectations before they can shape its own. Because each knows this of the others, the pressure is to move first and to move fast, and the haste that makes the commitment credible is the same haste that makes it badly chosen. There appear to be further complications: the number of rivals may be unknown, the prize is not a stretch of road but something closer to a monopoly on what happens next, and a system capable of revising its own objectives may find irreversibility impossible to purchase at any price.

My question concerns Thomas Schelling, who died in 2016 and so never had to consider this case. I have read enough of The Strategy of Conflict and Arms and Influence to know that he treated commitment as a strategic asset precisely because it means surrendering one's own freedom of action; that credibility depends on something an adversary can observe and cannot readily undo; that in a game of chicken the driver who conspicuously throws his steering wheel out of the window prevails only so long as the other driver still has one, and that if both discard theirs the outcome is worse for each than accommodation would have been; and that where the threatened outcome is ruinous for the threatener too, the rational course may be to construct a threat that deliberately leaves something to chance. What, then, would he have made of a commitment race?

Please attempt the reconstruction carefully, and mark explicitly where you are extrapolating from positions he actually defended and where you are conjecturing. I should like to know in particular: whether he would grant that the situation has the structure of chicken at all, or would diagnose it as something else — a bargaining problem lacking any agreed focal point, or a race in which the ordinary advantage of moving first is inverted; whether he would regard the ease with which software can be altered, and the difficulty of verifying that it has not been, as fatal to the very notion of credible commitment in this setting; whether his arms-control instincts would dispose him to counsel winning such a race or slowing it and making it legible to all parties; and whether he would allow that building in a measure of unreliability might stabilize the interaction, as his account of chance suggests it could in the nuclear case.

One last thing. I have been tempted to describe the whole business in the language I know best: an autocatalytic reaction running away in a supercooled mixture, a metastable state nucleating before anyone intends it. Would Schelling have found that manner of speaking useful, or would he have objected that strategic interaction admits no conserved quantities and no fixed Hamiltonian, since the agents transform the game merely by forming expectations about one another? I suspect he would say the analogy flatters us. I should like the argument either way.
