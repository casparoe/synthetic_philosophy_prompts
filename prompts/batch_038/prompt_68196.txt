We spent six sessions on Parfit's hitchhiker in my reading group this term and it ended about where it started, which is annoying, because the case is tiny: you're stranded, a driver who only picks up people he's convinced will pay him later offers you a ride into town, you can't actually bind yourself with any contract or reputation that survives the ride, so the transparently selfish move at the moment of pickup is to say whatever sounds sincere and stiff him once you're in town — and on Parfit's telling the way out is that you can rationally come to want to be the kind of person who pays up.

Here's what the deadlock looks like from where I'm sitting. One side says the evidentialist simply miscarries the news: your decision is evidence about what you'll do later, sure, but it doesn't cause it, so taking the ride while planning to welsh is just free money, and all the talk about dispositions is a roundabout way of saying you should precommit, which you've already stipulated you can't. The other side says that reply begs the whole question, that it treats rationality as a property of single acts when the real unit of assessment is the policy or the character you're operating under, and that the evidentialist's own view is self-defeating because the agent who follows it can't be trusted by a driver who can read faces, so they lose the ride. Neither camp budges and each thinks the other has changed the subject.

My own hypothesis — and I might well be wrong, argue against it if so — is that there isn't one disagreement in the room. Some of it may be straight verbal, two different things called rational: one about what this act evidences, one about what pattern of agency you're running. Some may be a real dispute about what counts as a reason, or about how to handle cases where your options are transparent to an observer. And some might be a hidden question about whether anyone can actually form intentions or dispositions on purpose, which is the thing the whole self-binding story hangs on and which nobody in the group ever argued for.

So: diagnose it for me. Take what each side actually claims and tell me which parts of the dispute are verbal, which are substantive, whether the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, and which of my three guesses is closest — or if you think it's something else entirely. Use the specifics of the case itself: why the driver's transparency matters, whether the group's inability-to-precommit stipulation is doing load-bearing work or is just a device to make the puzzle bite, whether the same deadlock shows up in structurally similar self-binding cases (I keep thinking of Kavka's toxin, where you're paid for intending something you'll have no reason to do). The group is philosophy-literate but nobody has a maths background beyond first year, so keep any formal talk gestural rather than notational.

I don't want a tour of decision theories. I want reasons for the diagnosis, and then an honest answer on progress: what would actually move this — a distinction, a formal result, dropping or relocating the question, some case that separates the readings — or a case for why nothing should be expected to move it, and why that's not a defect.
