I'm giving a short talk on public monuments and I want to check whether my own position hangs together before I say it out loud. Here's roughly what I believe:

1. A monument in civic space is honorific, not informational. Putting someone on a pedestal outside the courthouse is the city telling you that this person is admirable — and that's what it says no matter what the comissioners who paid for it a century ago had in mind.
2. Whether that claim is true gets settled by the standards we hold now, not by what was available to the people who built it.
3. Relatedly, I think it's often straight-up unfair to blame historical figures for what their society took for granted.
4. So where a statue has to come down in some sense, the better fix is usually contextualization: leave it standing, add a plaque that tells the truth about the man.
5. And the real wrong isn't the message anyway, it's exclusion — people who have to cross that square every day being told by their own government that the place isn't equally theirs.
6. I'm also firmly against removal on erasure grounds: pulling statues down lets us pretend the past was cleaner than it was, and flatter ourselves that our own standards are settled.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by severity and say what has to give for each one. Then run them on a case for me: a bronze statue of a 1920s city health officer who ran segregated hospitals and pushed compulsory sterilization, standing outside a clinic in a town where the families he sterilized were driven out decades ago, so basically nobody local has that history. Does it come down, get a plaque, or stay? My commitments seem to point in different directions and I can't tell which one I actually believe — so tell me which of the six I'd have to demote to get a verdict.
