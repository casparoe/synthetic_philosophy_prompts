You are a professional philosopher of mind writing for an audience of graduate students. Think carefully before you begin, and first sketch, in no more than five lines, the single strongest consideration available to each of the two positions below.

Then write a dialogue between an epiphenomenalist about phenomenal consciousness — someone who holds that qualia are real, non-physical, and causally inert — and a critic who presses the paradox of phenomenal judgment against her: if experience makes no causal difference, then our beliefs and reports about it are produced entirely by physical processes, so the systematic match between what we say and what we feel looks like an enormous coincidence, one that apparently deprives us of knowledge of our own minds and hence of the very evidence on which epiphenomenalism was built.

Build the exchange around a specific development rather than the standard run of the argument. Let the critic, after the usual trading of blows over common-cause explanations (the brain state that produces both the experience and the judgment) and over whether knowledge requires causal contact, press a generalization: on her diagnosis the paradox is structurally the same problem that besets any realist about a causally inert subject matter — Benacerraf's challenge to the platonist about numbers, and the parallel challenge to non-naturalist moral realism. Either that family of problems is survivable, in which case epiphenomenalism survives it too, or it is fatal, in which case the critic owes an account of why the physicalist's own mathematical and modal beliefs escape it. Let the epiphenomenalist both welcome and resist this reframing, and let her deploy a constitution-style account of phenomenal concepts — the idea that a concept can be partly constituted by the experience it picks out without that experience causing anything — as a way of breaking the symmetry.

Requirements: neither character may be a strawman; both must be as dialectically skilled as the other, and every turn must engage the previous turn's actual content rather than restate a position. Do not resolve the disagreement by having one side concede; end instead with each stating precisely what she would have to give up in order to accept the other's conclusion. Keep the dialogue to roughly 900–1200 words.

Afterward, add a brief note: say how confident you are in the dialectical claims your characters make, single out the point in the exchange you are least sure of, and flag anywhere you have taken liberties with a real philosophical position or leaned on a contested reading of one.
