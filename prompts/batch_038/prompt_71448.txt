Act as a philosopher of history who has also had to write annotation guidelines for a real project — that combination matters, because I need something operational, not a survey.

Some context so you know what the standard is *for*. I run a volunteer annotation scheme for a crowdsourced reference platform. Readers flag statements in our history articles as disputed, and volunteers apply a guideline to decide whether a claim counts as settled, contested, or unfounded. The volunteers have no archive access, no languages, no training. Right now the guideline is one sentence and it's Rankean by default — basically, is it supported by documents — and it is failing badly on exactly the cases we care about.

Three of them, which I want you to treat as the test battery for any standard you write:

1. A demographic reconstruction. A death toll given as a range, back-projected from censuses and ration registers produced by the administration whose policies the deaths are being attributed to. The numbers are the best anyone will ever have, and the source is also a party to the dispute.
2. An explanatory claim, not a factual one: that grain requisition policy was the principal cause of the famine, as against weather, or wartime dislocation, or the deliberate choice to export. Every individual document behind it is uncontested. The claim is still fought over.
3. A statement that is true and checkable and functions as apologetics — literacy rates doubled, industrial output tripled — because of what sits around it and what the article never mentions.

So: think carefully, and before writing the list, spend a few lines brainstorming candidate requirements and then cut the ones you don't think are real. Then give me the spec — what a standard of historical objectivity has to deliver to be adequate to that job. For each requirement: what it demands, the case or consideration behind it, and whether it's non-negotiable or just the taste of a particular camp. Flag anything you think is a prejudice masquerading as a criterion (my suspicion: the demand that the standard be statable without evaluative vocabulary).

Two things I especially want:

- Any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied. If the list is impossible, that's a result, and I'd rather hear it than get a harmonious spec. In particular, whether "answerable to the evidence" can survive both the fact that the archive is interested and the fact that my volunteers can never check it themselves — I suspect an adequate standard has to be about procedures and division of epistemic labour rather than about evidence, and I want to know if you agree.
- Which single requirement does the most work eliminating candidates.

Also say whether the standard you write is itself objective by its own lights. If it isn't, say what that costs.

Finish with a short paragraph each on how a Rankean documentary standard, Carr's interaction-between-historian-and-facts picture, and White's emplottment view do against the list — including whether White can keep the distinction he clearly wants between a history and a novel. Keep that part brief, the requirements are the deliverable. A table is fine for the list. No bibliography, I'll find my own reading.
