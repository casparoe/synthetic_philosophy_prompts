I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of mind each autumn to about a dozen students drawn from both the philosophy department and the cognitive science program, and the term opens on one question: is the distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness a distinction between two things, or between two descriptions of a single thing? By the second week I reliably have students announcing that they are realists about qualia, or illusionists, or global-workspace theorists, in most cases on the strength of a paragraph they read somewhere and without having tested that self-ascription against a single case. I want to replace this vocabulary-first self-identification with an instrument that sorts people by their verdicts. Please write me a decision tree of nine to eleven nodes and eight to ten leaves, beginning from a shared minimal starting point that any of my students would accept — say, that it makes sense to ask what it is like to be a subject at a time, and that some of what a subject discriminates and acts on is not something she reports — and terminating in a named position on the access/phenomenal question. Every node must be a concrete scenario on which the candidate positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing where the answer leads, and each node should carry an explicit statement of the claim that separates the branches it generates.

Use at least the following scenarios, in whatever order serves the tree, and add two of your own design that probe joints the list misses: a briefly displayed array of twelve letters which the subject can report almost any row of when cued after offset but only about four items of when asked for all of them, and who insists that a moment later the whole array was still there, fading; a subject who fails to notice a large alteration in a scene and afterwards denies having seen the pre-change layout in any detail, though she would have discriminated it; an image held invisible for seconds by suppression, where forced-choice guesses about its orientation run above chance while the subject says there was nothing it was like — set against a variant subject who reports a contentless sense that something was there; a subject who localizes and discriminates within a scotoma while sincerely denying that she sees anything there; a word rendered unreportable by a following mask which nonetheless speeds a related judgment; a patient who shows implicit memory for words presented under light anaesthesia and no report at all; and a subject whose first-order discrimination is flawless but who has lost all confidence in it, saying that she can tell you the patch is blue though it does not seem to her that she sees blue. Also include one wholly hypothetical architectural case: a system engineered as a literal global workspace — competing specialist modules, a shared bus, broadcast to consumers — which was never trained to talk about experience and which, once given a language module, denies that there is anything it is like to be it. The leaves must include at minimum the view that phenomenal consciousness simply is access consciousness, with no overflow; the view that it overflows what is accessible; the intermediate view on which access is partial and graded rather than all-or-none; the broadcast view; the local or recurrent-processing view; the higher-order view on which consciousness requires a suitable state about a state; the first-order representational view that requires no such second state; illusionism or deflationism about phenomenal properties; Russellian monism; panpsychism; and substance dualism. Merge two of these only where the tree's own questions genuinely fail to distinguish them, and never for tidiness. For each leaf give the name of the position, or "unnamed" if you think nobody actually holds it, one sentence on what the position is obliged to say about the scenario that delivered the student there, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment.

Then step back from the tree and give me the map of the space it carves: list the combinations of answers that are available but unoccupied, and for each say whether the region is empty because the view would be incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because nobody has yet thought to stand there. Flag every pair of leaves your instrument cannot separate, and say what further case would separate them if one exists; if two positions turn out to be inseparable by any first-person or behavioural evidence, say so plainly rather than inventing a tie-breaking question. Two constraints I care about. First, no node may smuggle in an answer — a question of the form "does it seem to you that your experience has a qualitative character over and above its functional role?" is worthless to me, since one reply merely reports the conclusion of a view the student has not yet been shown she holds. Second, I want no bibliography, no further reading, and no citations; where a leaf has a canonical defender a bare name will do, and where you are unsure of an attribution, describe the view instead of naming it. Finally, and this matters to me more than the polish of the instrument: tell me how confident you are that each leaf is correctly placed and correctly characterized, and single out the one point in the whole tree where you are least sure you have the philosophy right. My own suspicion is that the shakiest joint is the boundary between the illusionist leaf and the identity leaf, which may differ only in tone and in which side of the ledger they write the same fact on; if you agree, tell me what, if anything, could drive a wedge between them, and if you think the weak point lies elsewhere, tell me where it lies and why I have mislocated it.
