I lead a measurement/analytics team at a logistics company, so I live in a vocabulary of estimands, sampling frames, selection bias, aggregation bias, panel data, units of analysis, measurement error, and "did you check for Simpson's paradox before you shipped that dashboard." I read history on the side, no training in it — I've read The Cheese and the Worms and one essay collection on microhistory, and I've been chewing on the fight between the Italians (Ginzburg, Levi, Grendi) and the older serial/aggregate tradition — price series, parish registers, states and elites as the default object of study.

Here's what I want: take the microhistorians' side of that fight and restate it, claim by claim, in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. Specifically these four:

1. Levi's point that reducing scale is not just zooming in with the same instrument on a smaller patch. The object changes: a group that looked homogeneous at aggregate level breaks apart into competing strategies and factions once you can see individuals, so what you would have handed the model as "context" turns out to be an output of the analysis rather than an input.
2. Grendi's "normal exception" — the idea that a genuinely atypical single case can be the thing that unlocks a general system, that the outlier is where the structure shows itself most clearly.
3. Ginzburg's sense that the historian's method is conjectural and diagnostic — closer to a tracker reading traces or a clinician reading symptoms than to estimating a parameter — where the individuating, non-repeatable detail is what carries the knowledge.
4. The related move, which I think is in both Ginzburg and Levi, that the distortions in the record (inquisition transcripts filtered through the interrogators, silences, gaps) get built into the account as evidence rather than treated as noise to be cleaned, and that the historian's own position becomes part of what's reported.

Plus the underlying motivation from history-from-below: that the point is to recover the agency of people who left no self-generated records at all, and that treating them only as an aggregate is a kind of condescension, not just a loss of resolution.

Then give me the residue report, and be honest about it — don't smuggle my framework's assumptions in and call it translation. Three buckets: what maps cleanly, what you had to distort or stretch to make fit (say what you bent), and what has no expression in sampling-and-inference language at all. I especially want you to engage with these:

- Is a microhistorical case a sample of size one, or is it not a sample at all? My language seems to require every case to be evidence about some population — so is there any way to say "this case is the object of interest, not an estimate of anything," or does that thought just fall out of my vocabulary? If it falls out, is that a real cost?
- Does "normal exception" translate into heavy tails, high-leverage points, and outlier-sensitive inference, or is that translation quietly reversing the claim? My outlier language treats the weird case as a threat to the estimate; Grendi seems to treat it as the estimate's best friend.
- Is the conjectural paradigm just Bayesian updating with a very informative prior, in which case Ginzburg is not saying anything my people don't already know? Or is there a live difference between updating on a trace and estimating a parameter?
- Is treating the archive's distortions as signal just measurement-error modeling and label noise? If yes, say so plainly — I'd rather hear that the historians are describing something we already do than have you flatter them.
- Where does the ethical/political commitment go? Rescuing obscure people from condescension seems to have no slot at all in a vocabulary built on estimands and populations. Does that show my framework is normatively empty, or does it show that commitment is doing no epistemic work in the argument?

End with a verdict, and pick a side if you can: is the microhistorical claim about scale a substantive methodological claim that sampling theory genuinely cannot express (and if so, what's the missing piece — a theory of what a unit of analysis is, something about non-exchangeability, something else), or is it largely a stylistic preference wearing theory as a costume — read closely, tell a good story, distrust aggregates? And separately: does the elite/state, serial-history side translate into my vocabulary noticeably better than microhistory does, and if it does, is that because it's more rigorous or just because my vocabulary was built by people who wanted the same kind of aggregation they did?
