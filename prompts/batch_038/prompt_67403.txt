I attend a reading group on the ethics of the long-run future. Last week two members — I will call them Iris and Jonah — got into a sustained argument about how a total-utilitarian is supposed to compare outcomes when infinitely many people are involved. It ran for seven rounds. I was taking notes, and I have reconstructed the exchange as neutrally as I can; I am reporting it as a bystander, I have no stake in either position, and I genuinely cannot tell which of them is ahead at any given point.

Round 1. Iris: total utilitarianism breaks down here. If two outcomes each contain infinitely many people with positive welfare, both totals are infinite, the comparison is undefined, and there is no fact about which is better. The least dishonest policy is what she called the finite-difference rule: if two outcomes differ with respect to only finitely many people, compare those differences; otherwise return no verdict rather than a verdict you cannot support.

Round 2. Jonah: no verdict is not a neutral shrug — it is a practical recommendation of indifference, and one case refutes it. Outcome P: from today onward, exactly one person exists on each day, at welfare +10. Outcome Q: exactly the same, except that on each day one additional person exists, at welfare +9. For every finite n, the first n days of Q contain more welfare than the first n days of P, and Q is P with people added rather than anyone made worse off. A rule that declines to rank Q above P is not merely incomplete; it is disqualified.

Round 3. Iris: she grants Q > P, but says Jonah now owes a general rule, and the natural candidate — the overtaking criterion, on which B is better than A if from some point onward the cumulative welfare of B's first n units always exceeds that of A's first n — is not one. Two problems. First, it presupposes an ordering of the population, and when infinitely many people exist simultaneously there is no canonical order; relabel the very same people and the ranking can change. Second, its verdicts are fixed entirely by the distant tail. It ranks Outcome R (one person on day n at welfare n) above Outcome S (one person on day n at welfare +100), and more generally, once one outcome has overtaken another, no finite amount of additional early suffering can ever change that verdict. A rule with that property is not a theory of value.

Round 4. Jonah: the first problem is real but narrow — time supplies the ordering in every case that matters for the long-run future, and admitting that the criterion cannot rank simultaneous infinite populations is incompleteness, not incoherence. On the second, he bites the bullet and reverses the charge: in R versus S, infinitely many people in R are much better off than anyone in S, and Iris is objecting on the strength of the first hundred lives only — which is exactly the finite-horizon intuition she accused total utilitarians of smuggling in during Round 1.

Round 5. Iris: then consider the alternative she takes him to be reaching for — ranking outcomes by a shift-invariant generalized average, a Banach-limit-style value assigned to the stream of welfare levels, which exists for every bounded stream and is unaffected by how you slice time into blocks. This fails worse. Shift-invariance is what destroys Pareto: Outcome T has one person at welfare −1000 on day 1 and +10 on every day thereafter; Outcome U has +10 on every day. U is better for the person on day 1 and no worse for anyone, yet shifting T forward one day yields U, so any shift-invariant average assigns them the same value — the theory cannot see a torture case. And such averages are not unique: different generalized limits assign different values to the same bounded stream. Jonah has therefore traded an honest refusal for a verdict fixed by an arbitrary mathematical choice.

Round 6. Jonah: the arbitrariness charge proves too much, since Iris cannot rank anything either without relying on mathematical convention, and the difference is that his choices are constrained — linearity, positivity, shift-invariance — while hers is a blanket refusal. The Pareto failure, he concedes, is real but local, and it can be patched: apply Pareto directly wherever two outcomes differ at only finitely many times, and use the generalized average as a tiebreaker everywhere else. That recovers T > U. Iris replies that this two-tier rule is her finite-difference rule with a tiebreaker bolted onto it, that the tiebreaker inherits every objection from Round 3, and that Jonah has thus conceded Round 1 while refusing to concede Round 3.

Round 7. Iris: the lesson is that we should be finitists — no infinite population will ever exist, so the dispute is idle. Jonah: that changes the subject, because the same pathologies appear in the limit of finite cases; if a rule's verdicts swing wildly as our estimate of the horizon grows, the finite version is infected by the infinite one, and the only way to stabilize it is to discount the far future, which arbitrarily devalues precisely the people the long-run view was meant to take seriously.

Three things I would like from you. First, adjudicate the exchange round by round: for each of the seven, say who is right, whether wholly or only partly, and what turns on the difference — and flag any round where a party reaches a correct conclusion for a bad reason. Second, identify what both of them missed or mischaracterized; I suspect there is at least one place where an argument they both accepted does not mean what they think it means, and at least one distinction the whole exchange slides past. Third, among the proposals actually on the table — abstention on infinite differences, the overtaking criterion, a shift-invariant generalized average, or the two-tier combination — say which you would endorse and exactly what it costs you, including which familiar principle you would have to surrender to get it.
