I volunteer as a coordinator for a community restorative justice program in a mid-sized city — the sort that diverts first-time juvenile offenses away from court and into a facilitated conference with the person harmed, both families, and a couple of trained volunteers. I have no philosophy background. I came to this topic sideways, after fifteen years of sitting in circles listening to fourteen-year-olds describe what they felt, and noticing that the adults in the room never agree about what the child is actually expressing. This spring I have been asked to take over one ninety-minute evening session in our volunteer training on the difference between guilt and shame, for twelve people whose only prior exposure is a workplace wellness seminar that told them shame is always damaging, plus two older volunteers who are convinced that some offenders simply have none of it and that this is the real problem. I do not want the session to be a lecture about two feelings. I want it to change how they behave in a conference, and I am not sure that is possible in ninety minutes.

My own working view, arrived at badly and probably wrong in places, is this: guilt says I did something bad, shame says I am bad; guilt only needs a conscience while shame needs a witness, real or imagined; and guilt pushes a person toward confession and repair while shame pushes a person toward hiding, covering, and disappearance. I would also like to raise the question the trainees always raise on their own — whether a group, a family, or a culture can decide which of the two it runs on, and whether our program is in the business of producing one or the other. But I know my formula breaks down against cases I have actually sat through. There was a boy who took money from a relative's house while everyone was out, was never suspected, and told me two years later that he could not stand to look at himself in the months afterward; there was no witness anywhere and yet everything he said was shame-language and none of it was guilt-language. And there are people in our conferences — mothers mostly, sometimes the harmed party's brother — who are consumed by guilt over things they plainly did not do. Meanwhile the case that has our facilitators split right now involves a mother who, at the end of a conference, said loudly that her son had no shame at all, and half my volunteers think she was making a fair observation about him and half think she was doing damage to him in front of a room full of strangers. I cannot tell them what to think about that until I understand the distinction better than they do, and I do not think I can get there in the order I have sketched.

So I am asking you to plan the session rather than write it for me. Give me the sequence: which piece has to be in place before which other piece can mean anything, and why the order matters — I want to know specifically whether the action-tendency asymmetry can be taught before the audience question, or whether teaching it first guarantees that the room walks out with the slogan never shame anyone, which would be actively harmful in conferences where the wrongdoer's own relatives are visibly appalled and cannot be edited out of the evening. Tell me the one case to build the whole ninety minutes around, and what that case must contain and must conspicuously lack — I suspect it cannot be a thought experiment and cannot be one of our own conferences, but I may be wrong about both. Then map the misconceptions: the ones the trainees will arrive with, including the wellness version and the he-has-no-shame version, and the ones they will construct in the room out of what I say, with the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than argued down at the end. Include one thing I can put to them in the last ten minutes that would tell me the distinction has actually landed rather than just been repeated back to me politely. And tell me plainly what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out — including whether the can-a-culture-choose question belongs in the session at all or should be deferred, and if it belongs, where it can sit without collapsing everything built before it.
