ok so I've been sitting on this one for a while and I want to see whether a story can actually carry it, because I think the standard telling of the sphex thing is way too clean and the interesting version is much messier.

Background, mostly to get us on the same page. You know the wasp parable that goes around under Fabre's name: digger wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of the burrow, goes inside to check the place over first, comes back out to haul the cricket in. Somebody slides the cricket a few inches while she's in there. She finds it, drags it back to the mouth, goes inside to check again. Loop. And there's the other bit where she drags the prey by the antennae, so if you snip the antennae off she just stands there rather than thinking to grab a leg instead. Pure mechanism, no way out of the groove — sphexishness, I think it's Hofstadter's word, and Dennett made it famous. The whole point of the parable is that we're supposedly not like that, we break the loop.

Here's the wrinkle I actually want the story to be about. Two things are wrong with using that as your test for rigidity, and I want both of them live in the plot:

1. What counts as "breaking the loop" depends entirely on when the observer stops watching and which kinds of surprise they thought to try. Any finite battery of interruptions can be aced by a system whose entire personality is: detect unexpected state change, abandon current action, re-acquire goal, restart. That's not flexibility, that's a loop one level up, and from the outside the two are indistinguishable. Its basically a grue problem for flexibilty testing — the evidence underdetermines whether you're looking at a genuinely open-ended agent or a very well-parameterized sphex, and the only way to tell them apart is to keep going longer than anyone has the patience to.

2. The people who went back and looked at actual wasps found they do adapt if you keep at it long enough. Nobody interrupts forty times in a row; they interrupt three times, get the loop, and write it up. And re-checking a nest that somebody has clearly been messing with is arguably the right move, not a broken record. So a decent chunk of the parable is about how the parable gets told.

The story I want: put it somewhere that a flexibility audit is a real institution with real stakes — a certification board, a licensing exam, an insurance thing, whatever you like, and it can be people or machines or both, I don't care which, but failing an audit has to cost somebody something they'd rather not lose. Your protagonist is on one side of the table or the other. Over the course of the story they run into both wrinkles, and specifically: a case where the thing that passes every single trial is the more sphexish of the two candidates, and/or a case where the one marked down as hopeless only failed because the test ran out of patience before the adaption kicked in.

Constraints, because otherwise this turns into a talk with a plot bolted on:

- About 1500 words, give or take a few hundred.
- No character explains the concept to another character out loud. If a line could be lifted straight into a blog post about rigidity and agency, cut it.
- The wasp stays oblique. Nobody tells the Fabre story as an analogy, nobody says the word sphexishness. If you can make it present without naming it, that's the win.
- Give me a second character whose rigid-or-not status is genuinely ambiguous, and don't resolve it in the last paragraph with a wink.
- Free will shows up as a lived worry, not as a topic. The protagonist has some small personal habit — a way they always handle being stuck — that they half-suspect is their own reset-and-retry subroutine. The story never tells us whether they're right.
- End on the ambiguity. Nobody learns a lesson, and no one gets to say what the moral was.

Obviously don't bother with citations or a reading list, it's fiction. Just make it good.
