Our family foundation disburses roughly $18 million a year, and the docket now mixes two very different kinds of proposal: near-term interventions with measurable effects on identifiable people, and speculative ones whose case rests on astronomically large stakes multiplied by very small probabilities — a seed grant to reduce risks from advanced AI, a program to preserve and later revive cryonically stored patients, a campaign to accelerate space settlement. The board has adopted a standing rule I must operationalize: no proposal may be rejected merely because its stated probability of success falls below some fixed floor, since that is the one move that guarantees we never fund anything transformative. At the same time, our last review cycle produced an internal report arguing that the organization has drifted into what it called "practical fanaticism," accepting unbounded expected-value reasoning in every case where it happened to favor a charismatic applicant.

Write the decision procedure my team should run on each such proposal before it goes to the board. It must be executable in about an hour by an officer without formal training in philosophy; it must not resolve the underlying debate about whether expected value maximization is the right decision rule, but instead specify what the office should do while that debate is unresolved; and it must state in order what to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure returns a refusal, and where it hands the choice to the board because the question is one of values. Include explicit handling of the two structural problems these proposals raise — that the probability estimates are produced by the applicants themselves, and that the claimed payoffs are so large that small differences in probability swamp every other consideration — and say what the procedure cannot settle. Then run it on two cases: a request to fund research on hibernation-like torpor for long-duration spaceflight, and a request to fund a routine, well-evaluated mosquito-net distribution program whose officers argue the nets' value is understated.

Mark clearly which parts of your procedure reflect positions that are settled among people who work on these questions and which are genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Where a step is a judgment call dressed up as a rule, say so.
