This fall the historical society where I work opens a large exhibition marking the bicentennial of our state constitution, and I have been seated on the committee that writes the wall texts. I became a citizen four years ago, and the naturalization ceremony I remember most vividly is the one the exhibition will open with — which is presumably part of why I was asked to join the committee, and also why I do not want to cast a vote on Thursday without understanding the argument being put to us.

At last month's meeting, one of the trustees — a thoughtful man with a graduate degree in an adjacent field — circulated a paragraph from a popular history book he has been reading, in support of a proposal to reframe the entire show. The paragraph, more or less as he rendered it:

> Benedict Anderson and Eric Hobsbawm established, in two books that both appeared in 1983, that nations are not ancient but recent, and recent in a particular way: they were manufactured. Anderson's phrase "imagined community" means a community people have been persuaded to imagine — imagined in the sense of made up, like an imaginary friend. His account of the mechanism is genuinely brilliant: the printing press, channeled by capitalist markets into a handful of vernacular languages, created mass readerships, and the daily newspaper and the novel trained millions of strangers to think of themselves as acting together in one shared present. Hobsbawm supplies the other half. What we call tradition — the anthem, the flag, the kilt, the folk song rescued by a patriotic antiquarian — is largely a nineteenth-century invention, ritually repeated so as to imply continuity with a past that never existed. His stronger claim is that nations do not make states; states make nations, and then go back and invent a folk to justify themselves. Both authors treat the older, premodern ethnic materials as beside the point, since there is nothing for the invention to work with; that is also why nationalism is the first great political movement with no philosophers worth reading. The practical consequence is that a commemoration is not a memory but a continuation of the fabrication, and anyone who feels otherwise is simply feeling the machinery work.

His proposal, which has real support on the committee, is that the opening gallery should present the nation as a deliberate fabrication by elites and should treat visitors' own sense of belonging as an effect to be dispelled rather than an experience to be taken seriously. My own instinct is that he is right about the printing press and about flags and anthems, and wrong about almost everything else — but I suspect that instinct is itself sloppy, and I would rather not discover that in front of the board.

Please audit the paragraph rather than give me a general introduction to either author. I have no way to check sources, so please do not include citations, page numbers, or a reading list; where you are relying on a claim you are not confident the authors actually make, say so plainly instead of asserting it. Structure your answer with these six sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom Line** — about 80 words. Is the paragraph fair, unfair, or fair in its scholarship and unfair in its conclusion, and where does the weight of the distortion sit?

2. **Clause-by-Clause Audit** — a table, roughly 600 words in total. One row per substantive claim in the paragraph, with a verdict (Accurate / Acceptable simplification / Distortion / Wrong) and one or two sentences of correction. Add a final column marking which of the distortions the trustee's reframing proposal actually depends on, so I can see which errors are load-bearing and which are decorative.

3. **The Word Doing the Damage** — about 300 words. Take the three terms that carry the paragraph's argument — "imagined," "invention," and the slide from a claim about origins to a claim about legitimacy — and explain what each does in the original argument that it is not doing here.

4. **Where Anderson and Hobsbawm Part Company** — about 250 words. The paragraph treats them as two halves of a single doctrine. Explain how their accounts differ in what they take as the unit of analysis, in how much agency they assign to elites and states, and in what they think nationalism is a rival to, and say which of those differences would matter for how a museum should tell the story.

5. **Does the Reframing Follow?** — about 250 words. Once the paragraph is corrected, what survives of the trustee's conclusion? Is there a defensible version of his proposal that the corrected argument would still support, and what would it cost us to adopt it?

6. **What I Can Say Out Loud on Thursday** — under 120 words. A short, self-contained passage I could read to the committee without misrepresenting either author and without conceding more than the evidence warrants.
