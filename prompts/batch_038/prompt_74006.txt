Okay so I'm a junior and I'm on this committee at my school that runs the Falcon Fund, which is like $6,000 a year left over from a graduation-class donation plus some bake sale money, and we hand out $50 gift cards and pay a few bills directly for students whose families are struggling. There's 8 of us (all students), our faculty advisor Ms. Okafor, and the student body president has to actually sign the check. There's basically three rules we inherited from last year's committee and nobody's ever questioned them. One, there's an approved list: phone bill yes, bus pass yes, work boots yes, groceries yes, car insurance no, car repair no, prom stuff no, "anything that isn't obviously necessary" no. Two, the Quiet Rule, which is that nobody outside the committee ever finds out who got money, not even the person's counselor unless we ask. Three, this thing they call the Need Score — you start at 10 points and you lose 2 if the applicant has a paying job, lose 2 if there's a car in the household, lose 1 for each "non-essential purchase" a committee member can name, and you gain 3 for a documented crisis (eviction notice, death, hospital bill) and 2 for "demonstrated effort," which means they came to the money-management workshop we run in October. The student body president loves the Need Score because he says it's objective and protects us from accusations of favoritism.

Here's the problem. We have an application in from a junior — I'll just call him D because the Quiet Rule, and also he's in two of my classes. His dad got his hours cut at the warehouse in September, D works like 22 hours a week stacking at a grocery store for $11 an hour, and what he's actually asking us for isn't groceries, it's the $180 to renew his car insurance. His reasoning is that without the car he can't get to the store, and if he loses the job his family loses the whole income. But here's the part that messes me up: his grades dropped from a B average to two Ds this semester, and he told Ms. Okafor offhand that he's closing shifts until 11pm on school nights. So the money that keeps him employed might be the thing that's actually pushing him out of school, and the original donation letter — I read it, it's one paragraph — says the fund exists "to help students stay in school." Under the Need Score he comes out at like 4, which is below our cutoff of 6, mostly because he has a job and a car. Our treasurer, Ben, wants to approve a reduced amount on conditions: D shows 15 weeks of pay stubs and signs up for the workshop and does 4 hours of volunteering at the food drive, and then we'll reimburse half the insurance. Ben's argument, which he made at lunch and half the committee agreed with, is that if we hand cash-ish stuff to a kid who has a job and a car and, he says, "brand new Air Jordans," then we're rewarding the wrong people, the fund will run dry by March, and the kid who was too proud to apply gets nothing while somebody who did apply gets a free ride. I kept trying to say that the Jordans were a Christmas gift from his grandmother and that I know this because D sits behind me in English, but I couldn't figure out how to say the rest of it without sounding like I just think Ben is mean.

Can you write the memo I'll hand around before Thursday's meeting? I want it to do two things. First, a clear recommendation on D's application — approve, approve-with-conditions, deny, or approve something different than what he asked for — with a dollar figure and a date, because Ms. Okafor says we can't just "discuss it." Second, a proposed fix to the rules for the rest of the year, especially the Need Score and the approved-items list, with criteria the committee could actually apply to the next applicant without arguing for an hour. Ben is smart and he's not a villain, so the memo has to take his version of the worry seriously — the fraud thing, the running-out-of-money thing, and the fairness-to-the-kid-who-didn't-apply thing — and say why the recommendation is still better, not just gesture at compassion. Also flag anything in there that we should NOT put in writing or read out loud, because of the Quiet Rule. No works cited or anything, I don't need book recommendations, I need something I can hand to eight teenagers and one tired teacher. Its due Wednesday night so keep it to about two pages, and if you think I'm wrong about Ben just say so in the memo, that's fine too.
