I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I am assembling a long feature on the reliability of political forecasting — specifically on the tension between the inside view (mechanism-by-mechanism reasoning about the case in front of you) and the outside view that Kahneman and Flyvbjerg pressed on planners: find the right reference class, start from its base rate, and adjust only sparingly. My central exhibit is the collapse of the Soviet Union between 1985 and December 1991, an event almost no Western specialist saw coming in either its timing or its shape.

So that the analysis does not turn on debatable detail, here is the timeline I shall take as given. Gorbachev became general secretary in March 1985 and pursued perestroika and glasnost; by 1989 Moscow had effectively renounced armed intervention in its own bloc. That year Poland held semi-free elections after the Round Table talks, the Berlin Wall opened in November, Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution followed, Bulgaria's long-serving leader was eased out from within, and Romania was the exception — force was used, and the regime fell anyway. The earlier record inside the bloc is instructive: force was used in Hungary in 1956, in Czechoslovakia in 1968 and in Poland in 1981, and in each case the regime survived. On 17 March 1991 the USSR held its only ever union-wide referendum, asking voters whether they considered it necessary to preserve the Union as a renewed federation of equal sovereign republics; the authorities of six of the fifteen republics boycotted it, turnout elsewhere was about 80 per cent and roughly 78 per cent of those voting said yes. A new union treaty was scheduled for signature on 20 August; the hardline coup of 19–21 August intervened, the Belavezha accords followed on 8 December, and the Union was formally dissolved on 26 December 1991.

Please analyse that episode through reference-class forecasting, and keep the mapping disciplined rather than impressionistic. I want four things.

First, identify the reference class the forecasters of the mid-1980s were in fact consulting. My working hypothesis is that it was the Soviet regime's own crisis-survival record — 1953, 1956, 1962, 1968, and the leadership transitions that produced no systemic change — which made the inside view feel like an outside view. Say whether that diagnosis holds, and what it implies about how easily a forecaster can mistake a well-populated class for the right one.

Second, construct the outside view properly and show that the construction is where the difficulty lies. Is the USSR's class communist regimes, great powers, or multi-ethnic empires? If empires, does the base rate change between contiguous land empires of the Habsburg, Ottoman and Russian type and overseas maritime empires of the British, French, Dutch and Spanish type — and is there any non-question-begging way to choose between those classes, given that the choice is itself a causal judgement of the kind the method is meant to displace? Deal explicitly with the small-sample problem: when n is a handful and the members are heterogeneous and non-repeatable, does reference-class forecasting degrade into dressed-up analogy? And handle Romania rather than explaining it away.

Third, separate what the outside view gets right from what it cannot reach. I suspect it handles the direction — a stagnant multi-ethnic empire that renounces coercion loses its periphery — far better than it handles the sequence, the speed, and above all the near absence of bloodshed, since the base rate for imperial dissolution is violent. Tell me where the theory is silent instead of forcing it to speak, including on the March 1991 referendum: is that vote inside-view evidence a proper base rate would have discounted, or is it something the method has no category for at all? And say what the theory's lack of any slot for individual agency does here, given how much turns on one man's refusal to shoot.

Fourth, give me your verdict in both directions: is 1985–91 a confirmation of the outside view, an anomaly, or a case it was never built for — and what should a well-calibrated analyst have said out loud in 1985, in what probability range, and what would have counted as evidence to revise it?

Lead with your bottom line in the opening sentence or two — your judgement on whether reference-class forecasting survives contact with this case — and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question. Write throughout in British English.
