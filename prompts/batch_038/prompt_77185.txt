Write me a short story, roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words, set in the appeals section of a bureaucracy that administers a benefit defined by a vague predicate — long-term resident, serious injury, substantial hardship, your choice. The plot should turn on two adjudicators who disagree about what is actually going on when a genuinely borderline claimant comes before them.

One holds that every claimant determinately qualifies or fails to, right down to the individual, but that the line could never be located by anyone. I want that unknowability to come out as principled rather than merely practical: it has to follow from what knowledge itself requires, so that better instruments, more time, and a complete record of the claimant's life are all shown not to help. Give this adjudicator one scene with a stack of near-identical files that gets split down the middle, with no discoverable difference between the ones allowed and the ones refused.

The other holds that for a true borderline case there is no fact of the matter at all, while the disjunction — she either qualifies or she doesn't — comes out true regardless. Make them pay for that view. The ruling has to be signed, the ruling asserts one disjunct, and the story should catch them in the moment they realize that the disjunction they are certain of does not license the sentence they are about to write.

Constraints: no theory gets named, cited, or explained; nobody has read the literature; the office's own vocabulary stays flat and procedural. I want the philosophy load-bearing rather than decorative — it should show up only in what each character is and isn't willing to put on paper. End at the signing, and leave clear what each of them takes themselves to have done. Then, in one paragraph outside the story, tell me which lines were carrying which view, and what you would have to break in the setup to make the story come down the other way.
