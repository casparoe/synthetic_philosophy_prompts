Ok so pretend you're a working literary writer who also happens to be a proper specialist in 19th-century British moral philosophy — a novelist with a real Sidgwick habit. I want a short story out of you, but I want the thinking to show up in the shape of the thing rather than in speeches.

Here's the seed. Henry Sidgwick — the man who wrote The Methods of Ethics — was also the founding president of the Society for Psychical Research and spent years of his life sitting in darkened rooms watching people try to talk to the dead. That usually gets treated as charming Victorian eccentricity, a footnote under the ethics. I don't think it was a footnote. My read (and you're welcome to push back from inside the story) is that it was downstream of the ethics: he thought self-interest and impartial benevolence couldn't be reconciled on purely this-worldly grounds, so unless the universe is somehow arranged to make duty and happiness come out even in the long run, morality rests on an ungrounded leap of faith. Demonstrated survival wouldn't settle that, but it would be a start — a seance that produced real results is, on this reading, the last remaining route to a cosmic guarantee. Which gives you the engine: the most scrupulous, most unbribable skeptic in England is also the man with the most to gain from being convinced.

Some plot material, take what you want. Cambridge, 1880s or 90s. A professor chairs a small society that investigates mediumship. To discipline the witnesses, the society has engaged a young conjuror who performs fake slate-writing seances for invited guests, collects their written accounts afterwards, and publishes the comparison — showing how confidently a dozen respectable people will record in detail things that never happened. (That's a real methodological move the society's people made, and it's the heart of what I want.) The professor's own private position is that a field which can only ever produce exposures is a field that can never produce evidence: fraud is repeatable, so it accumulates, whereas a veridical case is a one-off event you can't replicate without the dead person's cooperation, so it can't accumulate. The whole discipline is structurally doomed to be a debunking operation, and he knows it. Then, in the middle of one of these rigged demonstrations, something happens that the conjuror did not do.

What I actually want the story to be about is not whether it's real. Please don't build to a reveal. The interesting thing is what the evidence could and could not buy him: even if survival were demonstrated to his full satisfaction, that's evidence about the continuation of persons, not evidence about a moral order that pays virtue its wages, and I want him to feel that gap open underneath his feet. Second problem worth getting at — if he accepts a phenomenon produced inside a demonstration designed to show how easily phenomena are faked, what does that acceptance do to the standards he spent his life defending, and is the belief he ends up holding still the belief he wanted? I don't want him to loose either horn of that, and I don't want the story to flinch from the fact that he'd rather be duped than be dry.

Form. I'd like it built partly out of documents — committee minutes, the witnesses' written reports, the conjuror's own plain list of what he actually did that evening, one or two private notes. Let the divergence between the documents do a lot of the arguing, and let the prose sections carry the interiority.

Constraints:
- 1,800 to 2,500 words of story.
- Nobody is allowed to say "the dualism of the practical reason" or any other philosopherly label out loud. If a character has to voice the idea, they have to do it in the flat, hedged vocabulary of a committee minute.
- No jump scares, no weeping ghosts, no ouija boards. Keep it dry, slightly bureaucratic, cold rooms and gaslight and paperwork. The creepiness should come out of the epistemology.
- Don't resolve it. End on the professor deciding what he is going to write down, and make it genuinely arguable afterwards whether that's integrity or self-destruction.
- The professor can be invented; he doesn't have to literally be Sidgwick, he just has to have Sidgwick's problem.

Before you draft, think carefully and spend half a page sketching: (a) what the conjuror actually did that night versus what the witnesses reported, item by item; (b) the single anomalous detail, plus the two or three innocent explanations the professor can't quite kill; (c) how the minute-book's language bends itself around that detail. Then write the story. No bibliography, no list of sources, and don't explain your choices at the end — I'll take the story on its own terms.
