I'm about halfway through my third novel and there's a stretch of maybe forty pages where everything goes wrong for the narrator — his brother drowns, the old dog has to be put down, that kind of thing — and my editor loved it, and so did the two friends who read it early, both of whom said some version of "it absolutely wrecked me and I couldnt put it down". Which is the bit I can't get my head round, and I want a proper conceptual unpacking rather than craft advice.

Here's the distinction I keep tripping over. When people explain why we enjoy tragedy they usually say something like: the sadness is the price of admission, and what you're really after is the payoff on the other side — catharsis, or the pleasure of admiring how well it's written, or the comfort of watching dreadful things happen to people who aren't you. On that picture the sadness is instrumental, a means to something separately pleasant. But there's another picture where the sadness is somehow part of the good thing itself rather than a toll you pay to reach it — where the reader is pleased *at* being grieved, or the grief gets folded into the pleasure instead of traded off against it.

I can't tell whether that second picture actually says anything, or whether its just the first one in fancy dress. If I'm glad that I'm sad, isn't that a second attitude about a first one, and haven't I only relocated the puzzle one level up instead of solving it?

So: could you explain what genuinely separates the instrumental account from the constitutive one, and what would have to be true for the constitutive version to be a real alternative rather than a redescription? Give me your bottom line in a sentence or two up top, then the explanation in prose I could read aloud — no bullet points — and keep the whole thing under 350 words. I want to be able to hold it in my head while I'm redrafting, and I don't want to be sent away with a reading list.
