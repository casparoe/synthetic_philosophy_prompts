A friend who's deep into rationality blogging sent me the key passage from a post of his, and I mostly bought it until I read it back:

"Results going back to Ramsey and de Finetti, and later von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage, show that if an agent's preferences obey a handful of consistency conditions, there's a unique probability function over states and a utility function over outcomes such that the agent prefers exactly what maximizes expected utility. These theorems run in both directions. Break one of the conditions and you become exploitable: someone can hand you a package of bets you'd each accept on their own but that guarentee a loss no matter what happens — that's the Dutch book — and its sequential version is the money pump, which kicks in as soon as you revise your preferences when information arrives. Savage did insist his axioms describe decisions only in small, fully specified worlds, but that's a caveat about applications: the mathematics is the same when the world is big and messy, so there's no gap to worry about. Notice too that none of this depends on what people actually want — the theorems only require that your wants hang together. So when someone says rationality permits refusing to trade a tiny probability of catastrophe against ordinary benefits, or caring about how outcomes are distributed rather than the total, the coherence arguments settle it: those aren't rival rationalities, they're incoherence in a costume."

Can you go through this sentence by sentence and tell me what someone working on formal decision theory would accept as stated, what's a defensible simplification, and what's actually wrong? I'm least sure about the "both directions" claim, the Dutch book / money pump pairing, and whether that last sentence really follows from anything earlier. Where a claim is wrong or overreaches, tell me how a defender of coherence arguments would restate it so it survives — and where the defense itself runs into trouble, say what the trouble is.
