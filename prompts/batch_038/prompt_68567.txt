Commission: a short story, 1,200 to 1,800 words, dramatizing a real fault line in anthropic reasoning. Please build it on the following scaffold; rename what you like, invent freely inside it, but keep the structure of the disagreement intact.

The situation. Kestrel Station is the last crewed outpost of a census program, twelve people aboard, and its fuel reserves are exactly enough for one of two things: the return burn, or a nine-year measurement that would decisively falsify one of two cosmological theories. The theories agree on every local observation the crew has ever made or could ever make. They differ only in the total census of observers in the universe. Theory A puts that number around ten to the twelfth, most of them long dead. Theory B puts it around ten to the twenty-fourth.

The two positions.

One crew member argues the experiment is unnecessary, because their own existence has already settled the question: if Theory B were true there would be vastly more people, and therefore a vastly greater chance that someone with their experiences would exist at all, so B carries an advantage measured in trillions to one. No measurement they could ever perform would dent that.

The other argues from the very same fact and reaches the opposite conclusion: you should reason as though you were a random draw from the observers your theory posits, and finding yourself where you find yourself in the sequence is evidence that the sequence is short and nearly finished. On this view the experiment is not merely worth running, it is overdue.

Requirements.

1. Neither inference may be explained as a lecture. Each must be applied to a concrete choice on the station and shown to produce a different action, with the crew living inside the consequences.
2. Include at least one small, entirely terrestrial case in which the random-draw logic visibly works — something like a serial number, a birth order, a lottery — and one case in which the same logic visibly licenses something monstrous. The reader should feel both the pull and the price.
3. Someone must press the presumptuousness objection directly: if existence alone yields trillion-to-one odds, then Theory B is immune to anything the station could observe, and a claim that cannot be wrong about the world has stopped being a claim. Let the defender answer it, and let the answer be good.
4. The story must confront the reference-class problem. At least one character should point out that both sides get whatever result they want by choosing the class they sample from — all observers, all humans, all twelve of them — and that this, not the arithmetic, is where the argument actually bleeds.
5. You may have one character recognize that their quarrel has an exact structural twin in a much smaller puzzle involving a coin, a drug that erases memory, and a subject who cannot tell how many times she has been woken, and that answering the little puzzle one way commits you to answering the big one the same way. Do not explain the puzzle. The recognition is the point.
6. The plot must do something: the experiment is run or it is not, and there is a consequence that the reasoning did not predict. Do not resolve the dispute by letting one character win the argument.
7. Keep the prose concrete and the jargon light. The characters should talk like people who have argued about this for years and are tired of it.

Format. Give the story alone, then a single final line beginning Verdict: followed by ten words or fewer saying whose reasoning the events turned out to support, or the word neither if the story declines to settle it.
