In US capital trials, prospective jurors get screened for the content of their moral views: someone whose opposition to the death penalty is strong enough that they couldn't apply the law as instructed gets dismissed for cause, and in principle the same goes for someone who says they'd automatically vote for death. Then the penalty phase turns around and hands that filtered jury a decision with no rule attached to it -- weigh aggravation against mitigation -- where the sentencer's own moral judgment is treated as the whole point of the exercise. And seperately the system leans on juries to nullify when the law would produce something unjust, while judges are told not to inform jurors they can.

So what picture of moral judgment does this arrangement presuppose? I want the implicit theory of when a moral conviction counts as a bias to be filtered out and when it counts as the faculty doing the judging. Does it assume convictions can be bracketed at will, that conviction only distorts when it points one way, or that there's a difference in kind between applying a rule and making a value call that licenses both moves at once? Then tell me which parts the practice strictly needs versus which are just convenient fits -- maybe it's all administrability and finality with no theory underneath at all -- and where it's underdetermined, so that several rival accounts of the preference/bias line would license exactly the same courtroom procedure. Finally, what would selection have to look like if we took seriously the view that a juror's moral commitments shape what they can even see in the evidence, so there's nothing there to bracket?
