For something like fifteen years I have read the rationalist blogosphere and its offshoots — forecasting, economic history, the recurring thread about whether we are living in a local optimum — and one rhetorical move has accumulated enough instances that I now notice it reflexively. I have never found a name for it, and I would like to know whether that is because I have failed to look in the right places or because the thing is genuinely under-theorized.

The move works like this. Someone points to a feature of the present that no longer has a visible justification: the layout of the standard keyboard, the fact that one half of the world's electrical grids runs at one frequency and the other half at another, the permanent membership of the Security Council being a roster of great powers as it stood in 1945, a capital city sited as a compromise between two rivals that has since grown into a location nobody would choose. The original rationale, where there ever was one, is obsolete or unrecorded, and the arrangement persists regardless, because an enormous mass of complementary equipment, training, law, and expectation has grown up around it. The writer then draws a conclusion about the past from this picture of the present: the thing must have been an accident, a coin flip at some branching point, which is why we can still be stuck with it.

What bothers me is the direction of the inference. The arbitrariness being cited as evidence of past openness is itself manufactured by the entrenchment. Once enough complements have accumulated, the original reasons are overwritten and unrecoverable — that is largely what entrenchment consists in — so the surviving arrangement is guaranteed to look unmotivated, no matter what the branching point actually looked like. A choice that was well-motivated and effectively forced at the time and a choice that was pure luck leave exactly the same present-day appearance, and that appearance is then read as evidence for the luck. If this is right, the two halves of a path-dependence story are not independently supported: the evidence for the increasing-returns half is what produces the apparent evidence for the open-branching-point half, and the more thoroughly something is locked in, the more contingent its origin looks. I also notice the mirror image, in arguments about whether the same outcomes would have arrived anyway. The convergence-minded make the reverse present-to-past move: because the surviving arrangement works, there must have been a reason it won, so the same thing would have turned up regardless. In both cases the present supplies the evidence, and in both cases the present has been shaped by the very process under investigation, which seems to me to be the whole difficulty.

I know the neighbourhood, and none of it fits cleanly. Hindsight bias concerns individuals revising their estimates after learning an outcome; here nobody is revising anything, because the estimate is being formed for the first time, decades later, from the shape of the present. Survivorship bias is a sampling defect, and this is not a sampling problem — it happens when every relevant case is in view. The narrative fallacy, about coherence imposed after the fact, is closer, but the defect I have in mind is not that the story is too tidy; it is that a single present-tense property is doing double duty as evidence for two claims that ought to require separate evidence.

So: does this have a name? I am not looking for a label so much as a carving, and I would like the answer to do the following. First, identify the established concepts that come closest — from philosophy of history, philosophy of biology, the methodology of the social sciences, or anywhere else you think relevant — and say for each one precisely where the fit breaks down. Second, if nothing fits, propose a term and define it so that it has real boundaries, and give me the boundary cases: in particular, when is it legitimate to read present arbitrariness as evidence that a past choice was open? There must be cases where the inference is sound, and I want to know what distinguishes them from the ones I have described. Third, say what would count as independent evidence that a branching point really was open — evidence that does not run through the present appearance of the outcome at all. And fourth, tell me plainly whether this is a recognized failure mode with a literature I have simply missed, or a piece of amateur epistemology that happens to be correct, since the answer determines whether I should stop re-deriving it every few years.
