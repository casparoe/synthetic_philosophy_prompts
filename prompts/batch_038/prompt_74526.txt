A colleague of mine who works on what, if anything, makes human extinction bad over and above the deaths it involves has accumulated a fairly precise track record of judgments:

- She says Scheffler's doomsday scenario — the Earth destroyed by an asteroid thirty days after she dies — is far worse than her dying of a stroke tomorrow while humanity carries on, and she insists the gap cannot be reduced to the number of additional deaths.
- She says that if everyone were annihilated painlessly and instantaneously tonight it would be a catastrophe, even though nobody would experience it as a loss and nobody would be left to grieve.
- She denies that the eventual uninhabitability of the Earth, or the heat death of the universe, undercuts the point of what she does now; the distance of the endpoint seems to matter to her.
- She says that a humanity which survived intact but lost every record and all memory of its own past, so that no future person could ever learn what had been built or done, would suffer a grave loss — but of a different and lesser kind than extinction.
- She says that non-biological successors who carried on our sciences and arts, remembered us, and finished what we left unfinished would make the disappearance of human beings considerably less tragic; pressed, though, she adds that she would still rather it were human beings who survived.

Three cases are coming up in a seminar she is convening:

(a) Humanity survives indefinitely, healthy and clever, but a hereditary condition erases each generation's memories and archives every eighty years, so nothing accumulates and no undertaking spans more than a single lifetime.
(b) Humanity is destroyed outright, but a self-sustaining archive of its culture is launched first and is recovered ten thousand years later by beings who understand it, admire it, and build on it.
(c) A pathogen leaves a few thousand sterile survivors who live out full, culturally rich lives and are gone within fifty years.

Predict her verdicts on (a), (b), and (c) — above all, how she would rank them relative to extinction and to untroubled survival, and whether she would rank (a) above or below (b). Then lay out the two or three rules that would account for her existing verdicts equally well, say exactly where they come apart on these three cases, commit to a best guess for each with your reasons for preferring that rule over its rivals, and name one further case that would settle which rule she is in fact running.
