I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend who is writing a dissertation on seventeenth-century natural philosophy, and there was one move he kept returning to that I could not dislodge. I would like a careful audit of it.

His position, as nearly as I can reconstruct it: the controversy over whether there was such a thing as a Scientific Revolution is downstream of a single analogy, and that analogy is doing more work than anyone admits. The moment a historian calls the transformation of natural knowledge in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries a revolution, she has imported the whole schema of a political revolution — a discredited old regime, a self-conscious party of insurgents, a threshold after which the old order cannot be restored, and a new settlement that redefines what counts as legitimate authority. He thinks the schema fits well enough to earn its keep: scholastic-Aristotelian natural philosophy was the old regime; Copernicus, Kepler, Galileo, Descartes and Newton were the insurgents; they knew what they were about; and after Newton there was no going back. When I pressed the standard constructivist complaint — that the period label is a much later coinage, manufactured by historians who wanted a heroic origin story for modern science — he said it missed the point entirely. A period is not made real by the date of its name; it is made real, or at least defensible, by whether the analogy that picks it out actually fits. If the political-revolution schema fits, the Scientific Revolution is a genuine historical unit rather than a convenient imposition; and the same test, he added, is what disqualifies the Renaissance, for which he cheerfully concedes there is no such schema at all.

What I want is an assessment of the analogy itself, not a survey of the debate.

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly. What in that period is playing the part of the old regime, the insurgents, the threshold of no return, the new constitution, the counter-revolution? Flag any slot that can only be filled by stipulation rather than by anything a contemporaneous observer could have pointed to.

2. Separate the disanalogies that genuinely threaten his use of the comparison from the ones that are merely interesting. Candidates I can think of, on which I have no idea how to adjudicate: nothing was declared or signed by anyone; the insurgents mostly understood themselves as recovering a lost ancient wisdom rather than founding anything; there was no institutional body whose authority had to be seized, since the universities carried on much as before; the changes are spread across roughly two centuries and were wildly uneven across places and disciplines; natural knowledge was not yet a bounded social domain with members and outsiders but was tangled up with theology, medicine, alchemy, astrology and workshop practice; and on the continuity reading, medieval work on impetus and on uniformly accelerated motion had already done much of what gets credited to the insurgents. Which of these bite, and which can he absorb without damage?

3. Tell me what the analogy quietly imports that the target case lacks — particularly anything about the transfer of legitimacy, about the direction of change amounting to improvement, about a revolution being an event rather than a process, and about the existence of a party whose members could have been identified as such at the time.

4. A thought occurred to me halfway through that he waved away as etymology. The word he is leaning on did not carry the sense he needs when the period is supposed to begin: the book that conventionally opens the sequence used revolution in its astronomical sense, as a circuit or a returning, and the political sense was layered on afterward. Is that trivia, or does it bear on whether the analogy is doing descriptive work rather than rhetoric?

5. Does his meta-claim survive the audit — that a period is real rather than imposed provided the analogy that picks it out fits? And if the political-revolution analogy fails, is there a better one? I have in mind something like a phase transition, or speciation, or the drift of one stage of a language into the next: cases where the boundaries are undateable and the agents nonexistent, but the difference between the endpoints is nevertheless real and not a matter of convention. Would any of those carry the weight he wants, what would each commit a historian to, and would any of them let him keep his differential verdict on the Renaissance?

Please be explicit about which of your conclusions you are confident in and which turn on contested readings of the evidence.
