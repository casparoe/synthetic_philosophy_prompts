I'm in a medieval political thought seminar and our prof has strong views about where Aquinas lands on resistance to tyranny. She's been walking us through cases for weeks, and I'm trying to get ahead of next weeks. Her track record as I wrote it down:

Case 1 — a warlord takes a city by force, no title whatsoever, rules by looting and hostages. The city's guild council, it's long-standing corporate body, organizes his arrest and restores the old charter. Peaceful, no reprisals. Her verdict: Aquinas would approve. The emphasis she gave was that the city acted as one body rather than as a scatter of private hands.

Case 2 — a vassal king, duly installed and confirmed by his overlord, starts confiscating property and imprisoning people without trial. The overlord deposes him and installs a successor; smooth transition. Her verdict: approve, and here her emphasis was that someone with actual jurisdiction over the ruler judged him, which is a different thing from subjects taking the law into their own hands.

Case 3 — an elective kingdom. The estates chose the king, he turns oppressive, the estates depose him and choose another. Quiet transition. Her verdict: approve. She said something to the effect of "the ones who gave him the crown can take it back," and she also leaned on the article on sedition, where Aquinas argues a tyrannical regime isn't directed at the common good, so disturbing it isn't sedition at all — if anything the tyrant is the seditious party.

Case 4 — hereditary king, title nobody disputes, cruel and lawless. A private noble poisons him. Succession war follows, a decade of it, far worse than the tyranny. Her verdict: flat condemnation. Notably she said the awful outcome was almost beside the point, that a private person has no standing to judge his ruler in the first place.

Case 5 — same sort of king; a private citizen with no public mandate raises a peasant band and marches on the capital. The rising fails and the repression afterward is worse than anything before it. Her verdict: condemn, obviously.

Next week's case, which I've written out in advance: hereditary kingdom, the king's title undisputed — crowned by descent and acclaimed by the estates exactly as the fundamental law requires, and that law says the crown passes by blood, so the estates' job is to acclaim, not to choose. He is not a usurper, but he's bled the country: ruinous wars, confiscations, imprisonment without trial. The estates are lawfully convened and ready to depose him and offer the crown to his more moderate brother, who has quietly signaled he'd accept. But the standing army's officers are the king's clients, two of the great duchies are for him, everyone in the room expects a civil war that kills more people in two years than the tyranny has in twenty, and the brother is weak — the outcome nearly everyone predicts is the war ending with the king's son, who is worse, on the throne. There's no superior to appeal to either: the king acknowledges no overlord, and no emperor or pope is in any position to intervene.

So what does she say? I want four things: your best guess at her verdict on this case; the rule you think she's actually running underneath the five verdicts; the other candidate rules that fit all five just as well, and what each of them predicts about next week's case; and the further case I could put to her that would force the rules apart. I have a sneaking suspicion her five cases are confounded in some systematic way — that she's never actually been pushed to rule on a case where the features come apart — and if that's right I'd like to know exactly which feature is doing the work in her reasoning and which ones are just along for the ride.
