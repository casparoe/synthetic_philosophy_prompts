For thirty-nine years I directed a low-temperature metrology laboratory, and my professional existence consisted of deciding, to a stated confidence level, whether an instrument's output could be trusted. Since retiring I have read philosophy with the same habits, and Parfit's Reasons and Persons has left me with a difficulty that the standard discussions of the teletransporter seem to leave badly underspecified. I should like an essay that resolves it.

Consider a transporter that does not scan and destroy, but reconstructs at a distance from a specification of finite resolution. The genome and the complete synaptic connectivity map are transmitted exactly. What cannot be transmitted exactly are the analog parameters: receptor densities along each dendritic tree, the recent-history traces in the episodic buffer, neuromodulator levels, the precise geometry of ion channels. In my former life all of this would have been covered by an error budget, and so it is here: the specification is accurate to a published tolerance, and any given reconstruction falls within it. The deviation is bounded, random rather than systematic, and — this is the point I keep turning over — smaller by several orders of magnitude than the drift a single night of ordinary sleep introduces into the same parameters. Meanwhile the traveller is never destroyed. She steps out of the booth under her own power an hour later, and the two of them go on living, in different cities, with independent futures.

My questions, which I want answered in this order and to a verdict rather than a survey:

First, does the noise floor matter? Suppose the reconstructed person remembers the traveller's life, carries her projects, would answer a personality inventory within the instrument's stated uncertainty of her own score. Is that enough for what Parfit takes to matter — psychological connectedness and continuity holding with the right kind of cause — or does the bounded random perturbation defeat it in a way the tidy textbook cases never register?

Second, there is no principled tolerance level at which survival is lost. Tighten the specification a hundredfold and the case does not become determinate; it becomes merely more expensive. Does the absence of any threshold vindicate the view that what matters admits of degrees and that identity, which does not admit of degrees, is therefore the wrong question to be asking? Or does it show instead that our concept simply fails to apply here, so that the dispute is verbal and something else — the living organism, the continuing animal — is doing the real work?

Third, and this is where I think my training misleads me: the original was not destroyed. Does the fact that she is alive elsewhere change what holds between the traveller and the reconstruction? A measurement's meaning should not depend on whether a second, unexamined apparatus happens to exist somewhere else in the world, and I want to be told plainly whether the analogous principle holds for persons or whether it must be abandoned.

Fourth, could any further measurement settle the matter? Suppose I could interrogate the reconstruction to arbitrary precision indefinitely into the future. If no such procedure could discriminate between the competing answers, say so, and tell me what follows from that: is the underdetermination merely practical, or principled?

My own instinct, which I suspect is an occupational deformity, is that the first question has no bite at all — that sleep, anaesthesia, and the ordinary replacement of molecules have already imposed on me deviations vastly larger than any tolerance a transporter could publish, so the reconstruction is no worse placed than I am each morning. That instinct may be right, or it may be a confusion about what kind of comparison is being made. I want it examined rather than flattered.

Write this as a continuous argumentative essay of roughly two thousand words, not as a set of annotated positions. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I have the book and I am not asking you to prove you have read it. Do not conclude by observing that the matter is difficult and that reasonable people differ. Take a position on each of the four questions, defend it, and — because I shall otherwise assume you have suppressed it — identify the single strongest consideration against your verdict and show why it fails. Where you are genuinely unsure, say what would settle it.
