I'm in a weekly reading group working through Nāgārjuna in translation, led by a guy who's spent decades on Madhyamaka and who likes to run a little game on us: he reads out a claim in plain English and we each have to say whether he thinks Nāgārjuna would let it stand as conventionally acceptable or swat it down. Here's his own verdicts so far (my paraphrase of what he said, not quotes):

- a chariot exists, and so does the person riding it → yes
- there's a self that owns these experiences → no
- effects get produced: from themselves, from something else, from both, or from no cause at all → no, all four branches are dead
- the Tathāgata exists after death / doesn't / both / neither → no on every one of those
- fire burns fuel → yes
- there is a path that leads out of suffering → yes
- things lack intrinsic nature → yes
- emptiness is a real something that things possess → no
- nirvāṇa is something you can attain → no
- nirvāṇa is not something you can attain → also no
- dependent origination is how things really work → yes

Next week's case is one of our members' own line: since nothing has intrinsic nature, being without intrinsic nature is what everything ultimately is. Half of us think that's just the "things lack intrinsic nature" verdict wearing a disguise, the other half think its the "emptiness is a real something" verdict with the serial numbers filed off.

What do you reckon he says? What I really want is this: which candidate rule he could plausibly be running, since I think the list above fits more than one — something keyed to whether a claim posits intrinsic nature at all, something keyed to whether the claim survives being analyzed to death, something keyed to whether saying it loosens or tightens your grasping, maybe something keyed to whether you can say it while openly flagging it as merely conventional. Then run each of those rules on the new line and show me where they come apart, tell me which one you'd bet on and why his past verdicts favor it, and name one extra case you'd toss at him that would split your top two rules cleanly.

First line: just your prediction — yes, no, or yes-with-a-correction — plus a rough confidence. Then the rest. Hard cap of 450 words; I'd rather have one tight answer then a survey of the literature.
