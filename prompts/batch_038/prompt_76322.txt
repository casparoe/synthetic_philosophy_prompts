I am a staff writer at a magazine of science and ideas, and most of my reporting is long-form and interview-driven: a single feature may involve fifty recorded conversations, conducted over weeks, in laboratories, hospital corridors, living rooms, and over the phone at inconvenient hours. Scattered through that material are people whose agreement to be quoted is, on reflection, not obviously valid. A graduate student says yes while her principal investigator sits two feet away and does not speak. A father talks to me nine days after his daughter's death and gives me the passage the whole piece will turn on. A man with an early, fluctuating dementia is lucid on Tuesdays and less so by Friday, and I interview him on a Tuesday. A sixteen-year-old's mother signs a release. A nurse on a ward agrees because the press officer who arranged my visit has already agreed on her behalf and everyone else in the room is agreeing. A researcher agrees because he has decided the piece will vindicate him in a dispute I have no intention of adjudicating.

I make these calls several times a day, usually mid-sentence, with no ethics committee, no time to consult counsel, and only afterwards a standards editor who must sign off on anything I have flagged. The stakes are asymmetric and unglamorous: a retraction, a subject who reasonably says he never understood what he was agreeing to, a career damaged by a quote he would have given differently had he known the frame, or a piece I have to kill three weeks before the issue closes.

What I want is a decision procedure I can run on a candidate quotation, not an essay about consent. Work from the element analysis familiar in research ethics and in the philosophical literature on valid consent — capacity, disclosure, understanding, voluntariness — and from the places where that analysis is genuinely contested rather than settled: whether consent extracted under a coercive background (the person who needs the job, the visa, the reference, the continued access to a clinic, the continued good standing with the person who introduced us) is invalid or merely compromised; whether an offer that only improves someone's options can be coercive at all, and if not, what does the work in the cases where we want to say the yes was not free; the difference between deception about the nature of what one is agreeing to and deception about incidental facts, and which of the two voids consent; the scope question, which is my daily problem — what exactly has a person agreed to when they agree to be quoted, and does agreement to a sympathetic profile carry over into a piece in which they are one of the parties under scrutiny; capacity as relative to a decision and a moment rather than global; the weight a signed release actually carries, if any; what happens in group settings where the consent is individually given but socially manufactured; and whether consent can be withdrawn after the fact, and what follows if it is.

The deliverable should be a sequence of gates I can pass a quotation through: the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops with a flat do-not-use, and the points at which it hands the decision to a named human being — and says which human, and precisely what that person is being asked to decide rather than what I am hoping they will ratify. Tell me what evidence of understanding looks like when you cannot quiz a source without turning an interview into an examination; borrow whatever is usable from clinical practice on that score, but adapt it. Mark explicitly every step where the procedure is judgment wearing a procedural costume. Give me a version short enough to hold in my head during a conversation and a version detailed enough for the standards editor.

Then run it on three cases: the graduate student interviewed in her advisor's presence, who emails a month later asking that certain passages be removed; the man with fluctuating capacity, quoted from a good day, whose daughter objects after publication; and the bereaved father, who understood exactly what he was doing and consented without ambiguity, and then asks to withdraw three weeks in. At least one of these should come out against my interest, and I want you to say which and not soften it.

Two further things. The magazine's current house rule is that any source who has been a patient within the past twelve months gets their quotes read back before publication. I suspect it is simultaneously over- and under-inclusive; tell me whether the procedure explains why, and what should replace it. And I have been privately using an unprincipled heuristic of my own, which I want you to assess rather than flatter: if I would be uncomfortable describing how I got the quote to the person who introduced me to the source, I treat the consent as defective. Does that track anything real, or is it merely a measure of social awkwardness that will misfire in both directions?

Finish with what the procedure cannot do — the categories of case it will get wrong or cannot reach, including anything involving fixers and translators working abroad on my behalf. Argue from the concepts; do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to anything.
