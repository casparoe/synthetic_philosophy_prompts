I am a third-year doctoral student in philosophy, and I am applying for a four-month residential fellowship at an institute that pairs philosophers with machine-learning researchers. The application requires a project proposal of roughly 1,500 words, and I would like you to draft it from the half-formed idea below.

The idea concerns Goodhart's law and its cognates in the specification-gaming and reward-hacking literature. My worry is that the field's dominant analytic tool — the four-way taxonomy of Goodhart effects into regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial kinds — buys its clarity from a silent assumption. In that framework one posits a system with a set of states, a true goal understood as a fixed function from states to value, and a proxy measure that imperfectly tracks it; the four categories then classify the mechanisms by which the statistical relationship between proxy and goal collapses under optimization pressure. But the goal is taken as antecedently determinate, and the proxy's job is merely to estimate it. I think a large and undertheorized class of failures does not fit that picture at all, because in those cases what changes under optimization is not the correlation between measure and target but the conditions that fixed the target's extension in the first place. When a hospital mortality figure, a journal impact measure, a wait-time standard, or a code-review score is used only descriptively, a surrounding practice of measurement, coding, reporting, and peer correction determines what the term picks out; once the figure becomes an incentive-bearing target, that practice is itself reorganized by the incentives, and the term survives with a different extension. On this reading Goodhart's law is not a claim about instruments degrading but about a measurement losing the practice that made it a measurement of that thing. I want to develop this as a third reading alongside the incentive-design reading favoured by economists and the proxy-fidelity reading favoured by the alignment literature, and to argue it explains a puzzle the other two do not: why some Goodhart failures are in principle repairable by better measurement — more data, better proxies, regularization, hold-out monitoring — while others cannot be repaired by any improvement in measurement fidelity, only by changing what is being asked for. I want a principled criterion separating the repairable from the constitutive cases, and I want the proposal to confront the obvious objection that the constitutive cases are just causal or adversarial Goodhart under a new name.
