I'm a novelist — one book out, and I'm maybe sixty percent through a second one whose narrator can't hold onto anything new for more than a few minutes, which I know sounds like a gimmick, and everyone in my workshop keeps saying "so, Memento." I finally went back and rewatched it properly last weekend, twice, the second time paying attention to the black-and-white strand instead of just the reverse-order stuff, and rather than settling the workshop argument it sent me down a decision theory rabbit hole of all places, and now I can't stop seeing the film in those terms. So: can you do a real reading of Memento through updateless decision theory for me? Not a plot summary, I know it cold now, and no bibliography please — I wont chase references, I'm sitting at my desk and I just want your thinking.

Let me say what I mean by the updateless idea, because I got it secondhand from a forum thread my brother in law sent me and I might be mangling it: roughly, the thought that when you choose an action you shouldn't just condition on everything you happen to know at the moment of choice, because the decision you're making now and the decisions your better-informed future selves will make are all outputs of one policy that you're effectively selecting from an earlier standpoint, before the evidence came in. So in cases with predictors, or copies of you, or where you've found out how the coin landed and would have been richly rewarded for committing in advance to pay up anyway, the right move is to act as though you hadn't updated — evaluate from the earlier information state. Related to that is the imperfect-recall business, Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver, where the guy can't tell the first junction from the second, so the plan he'd have made beforehand and the choice he'd make on the spot come apart; and Kavka's toxin, where you're paid for the intention rather than the act. Leonard looks to me like a walking version of all three: the notes, the Polaroids with captions, the tattoos — those are precommitment devices, little Odysseus-and-the-mast technologies for a man who can't be trusted with information. Even Sammy Jankis is relevant here, since the tests showed he couldn't learn from repetition at all, which is what an agent with literally zero updating capacity looks like from the outside.

So what I want is this. Is Leonard an updateless agent, or the ruin of one? Take the ending: Teddy hands him the truth — that he killed the real John G a year ago, that the Sammy story is his own story — and Leonard responds by burning the photos and writing down Teddy's plate number so his next self has a fresh target. Is that a refusal to update in the technical sense, and can it be defended on updateless grounds, meaning the Leonard-who-knows would just collapse or drift, so a policy picked from the earlier standpoint is better even though it costs him the truth? Where does Sammy fit — a parable he generates to bind himself, a toxin-style intention he'll never have to cash out, or something else? And I keep thinking the reverse chronology is doing philosophical work and not just formal cleverness: we meet Leonard-with-all-the-evidence first and Leonard-in-ignorance last, so the audience arrives at the earlier information state only after seeing what it leads to, which feels like the film is either forcing us into the updateless standpoint or making us commit exactly the error the theory warns against. I'd really like your take on that one. Then be honest about where the analogy breaks. Leonard can't model his own decision procedure the way the theory assumes he can, and worse, his "policy" is being edited by other people — Natalie writes on his photos, Teddy feeds him targets, Dodd gets invented — so it isn't even clear whose payoffs the whole system is optimizing, or whether Leonard counts as a single agent across time at all. Does that wreck the reading or is it the interesting part? Point me to specific scenes (paraphrase them, dont quote, I won't have the film in front of me), and end by telling me what the film shows about precommitment and self-deception that the decision-theory writing would never think to say — because if there's a real answer there it changes what I do with my own narrator, and if Memento is just a handsome puzzle wearing a philosophy costume, I'd rather hear that too.
