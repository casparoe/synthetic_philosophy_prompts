The question, as set (25 marks; word limit 1,200):

Question 3. Answer all three parts. You may not appeal to cases other than the one described here.

Pain asymbolia, sometimes called pain dissociation, is a condition usually following focal brain injury — insular lesions are the best documented, and the dissociation was historically described after leucotomy and cingulotomy — in which patients report pains that they can localize and characterize (sharp or dull, burning or aching, here rather than there) while showing none of the distress such reports ordinarily occasion. They do not guard the affected part, they do not avoid the stimuli that provoke the pain, and they say that they have the pain but are not bothered by it. The dissociation is mostly, and perhaps not always, complete; it can also be produced transiently by opioid analgesia.

Adopt, for this question, the following labels:

(i) The Indifference Datum: it is possible for a subject to feel a pain and not to mind feeling it.
(ii) Separatism: unpleasantness is a component of pain distinct from its sensory-discriminative character, and the two can come apart.
(iii) Internalism: necessarily, any subject who feels a pain is thereby motivated, to some degree, to make it stop.
(iv) Hedonism: how well a life goes for the person living it depends solely on the balance of pleasant and unpleasant experience in it.

Recall also the two families of account of what unpleasantness consists in: felt-character accounts, on which unpleasantness is a way experience feels, and attitudinal accounts, on which it consists in a negative attitude — paradigmatically a desire that the experience cease — directed at the experience.

(a) [8 marks] Does (i) entail (ii)? Set out the reasoning that would carry you from one to the other, and identify the further assumption that reasoning requires.
(b) [11 marks] Assuming (i) and (iv), construct the best argument you can for the conclusion that some pains are not bad for the people who feel them. Then say which premise a hedonist who wished to deny that conclusion would have to give up, and what giving it up would cost.
(c) [6 marks] A fellow student remarked in section that Internalism is false and that pain asymbolia proves it. Assess.

The answer submitted:

(a) Yes. Nothing here is mysterious. The patients are in pain: they localize it, they grade it, they distinguish burning from aching, they use pain language without correction. And they are not bothered by it. That is a case in which the sensory-discriminative side of pain is present while the side of it that makes pain a thing to mind is absent. Where two aspects of a single state can be present without each other, they are distinct aspects. Hence Separatism.

The reasoning requires one assumption, and it is an empirical one: that the reports are to be trusted. The patients may be confabulating, may have acquired an idiosyncratic use of pain words, or may be saying what an examiner with a clipboard appears to want; if what they report is not what they have, we have a misunderstanding rather than a dissociation, and (ii) loses its support. Once testimony is granted, nothing else is doing work. The dissociation is then an observation, and the observation settles the metaphysics: unpleasantness turns out to be nothing over and above the minding, the desire that the sensation cease, which is what the attitudinal account says. The felt-character account is left holding something felt as bad in a subject who is not bothered by it, and owes an account of what that feeling consists in once the minding has been subtracted. It has none to give.

The contrast with congenital analgesia points the same way. In both conditions noxious stimulation fails to produce suffering and the body is left undefended; the difference is only that the one never acquired the capacity the other has lost.

(b) 1. Some subjects feel pains they do not mind. [(i)]
2. A pain that is not minded is not an unpleasant pain.
3. How well a life goes for the person living it depends solely on the balance of pleasant and unpleasant experience in it. [(iv)]
4. Therefore some pains are not bad for the subjects who feel them.
5. And therefore pain is not bad in itself. Its badness is borrowed. Pain is bad insofar as it is suffered, and suffering is what pain does to us: it intrudes, it commandeers attention, it makes a life go badly. That is the whole of the badness there is in it.

A hedonist who wants to resist (4) must reject (1) — must say that the reports mislead, or that what the patients describe is not pain, or that the phenomenon is an artifact of damaged tissue and so tells us nothing about pain as such. There is no other move available, since (2) and (3) are precisely what the hedonist holds. The cost is high. It is an empirical concession made from the armchair, against patients describing their own experience; and it leaves the hedonist unable to say why the phenomenon is possible at all, because if unpleasantness were built into the sensory character of pain, no lesion could remove the one and spare the other. Such a hedonist has kept the theory by surrendering the datum that was its best evidence.

(c) The remark is right in its conclusion but overstated in its evidence. Internalism is a necessity claim, and the asymbolics look like the counterexample to it: a single genuine instance suffices against a claim of that form, and here we appear to have several. But the thesis is hedged — to some degree — and the internalist will reply that an asymbolic who permits a nurse to move his arm, or who accepts an analgesic when one is offered, is motivated to some degree, however faintly. On that reading the datum shows only that motivation can be attenuated almost to vanishing, which Internalism never denied. So asymbolia does not prove Internalism false. It shows that Internalism, hedged as it is, is close to unfalsifiable; and a thesis that survives every counterexample by shrinking is worth less than it appears.
