I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am reporting a long feature on the machinery by which regulators turn observed behaviour into money: what people actually did at the pump, at the pharmacy counter, or in the checkout queue treated as evidence of what their health, their time, or their clean air is worth. Reporting has also surfaced a wrinkle the standard account handles awkwardly. A growing share of those consumer choices are no longer executed by the consumer at all, but by replenishment algorithms, subscription defaults, and recommendation engines acting under mandates set once, years ago, and never revisited.

For the piece I want a decision tree that sorts sources by the view they actually hold about the relationship between choice, preference, and welfare, rather than by the label they reach for on the record. Please construct it as follows.

First, derive the space before you populate it. Lay out the load-bearing questions that divide the field: whether a choice is evidence of a preference that preceded it or is partly constitutive of it; whether consistency across choice situations is a discovery about minds or a definitional convenience; whether framing-dependence disqualifies a choice from entering welfare analysis, is itself informative, or is a reason to abandon the whole enterprise; whether preference is the right bearer of welfare at all, or whether something like experienced well-being should displace it; whether a choice made against the chooser's own considered judgment reveals a preference, two conflicting preferences, or no preference. Only then place the named positions.

Second, give me eight to ten branch nodes, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, phrased so that a working economist with no philosophy training could answer it on the spot during a recorded interview. No node may name a position, invoke a theorist, or ask anyone to characterise utility in the abstract. Among the cases I want used: the person who signs an annual gym contract in January and never swipes in; the identical clinical trial result presented once as a ninety per cent survival rate and once as a ten per cent mortality rate; the shopper whose weekly basket is assembled by an algorithm she configured three years earlier; the customer who pays more to avoid a supplier she disapproves of; the diner whose ranking of the menu shifts with the order in which items are read aloud; and the patient who chooses a course of treatment decisively and reports being wretched throughout it.

Third, the leaves must include, at minimum: Samuelsonian revealed preference as an operationalist programme on which utility carries no mental content; the representation-theorem orthodoxy on which preferences are real states whose existence the axioms license us to posit; Daniel Hausman's preference-satisfaction account in *Preference, Value, Choice, and Welfare*; the behavioural welfare economics of Bernheim and Rangel's 2009 unambiguous-choice proposal; Sen's 1977 argument that choice does not map onto preference once commitment and sympathy are admitted; the judgement-and-decision-making position that preferences are constructed at elicitation; hedonist revisionism built on Kahneman's separation of decision utility from experienced utility; libertarian paternalism as a policy stance rather than a theory of mind; and outright eliminativism about preferences. Mark any further leaf you think the space demands, including unnamed ones. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment and one sentence identifying which node did the sorting work.

Fourth, tell me what is missing. Identify the answer combinations no one occupies and diagnose each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Then flag every pair of leaves my tree cannot reliably separate, and say for each whether the failure is verbal or substantive — I specifically want to know which of my sources' disagreements would vanish at the level of policy recommendation.

Deliver it as a nested outline of roughly sixteen hundred words, followed by a compact scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. Close with the single node that, if I had thirty minutes with a source and could ask only one question, would do the most sorting.
