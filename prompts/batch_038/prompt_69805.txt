I write exhibition text for a living, and for the past two months I have been drafting a wall panel for a university museum show about the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the second millennium BCE, which opens in the spring. The panel has to be about 120 words because the vinyl has already been cut to size, and it is the one visitors will read before they enter the gallery proper, so it has to do a lot of framing work. Two of the show's academic advisers have fought over it through four drafts: Gillian, a settlement archaeologist who works on Late Bronze Age Anatolia and the Aegean, and Callum, a historical social scientist who studies complex societies comparatively. Neither of them is difficult; both are trying to be accurate. I am not an archaeologist, I have tried to reproduce the exchange neutrally, and I confess I cannot tell who is winning — I have been asked to settle it and would rather hand the settling to you. I should say up front that I do not have a side here: what I mainly want to know is how much of the dispute is about the world and how much is about the word.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher with a working knowledge both of the archaeology of this period and of the comparative literature on social complexity and societal breakdown. Think carefully before you answer; if it helps, begin by sketching privately the strongest formulation of each position and only then start rating individual moves. Do not pad the verdict with an even-handed shrug — the point of the exercise is to say precisely which parts of which arguments hold. Do not supply a bibliography or attribute claims to named scholars unless you are confident of the attribution; describe the arguments instead.

Here is the exchange as I have reconstructed it, in eight moves.

1. Callum objects to the word collapse anywhere on the panel, for two reasons. First, there was no single thing that collapsed: the palatial administrations, their scribal bureaucracies and the long-distance supply networks they sat upon went, while village life, farming and most local communities carried on. A term that reports the death of an administrative class as though it were the death of a society is measuring the wrong object. Second, collapse implies a fall from a height worth occupying, and the palace system extracted grain, labour and livestock from the people who produced them, so the height was not obviously good for anyone standing on it.

2. Gillian grants the second point as far as it goes and says she recognises the general argument that complexity carries costs and that shedding it can leave the average person better off — but she says it mis-describes this case, which was not a marginal trimming of overhead. Interlocking systems lost capacities they had actually been exercising: the diplomatic correspondence, the tin and copper flows, the palatial redistribution of staples, administrative literacy. And she says the welfare point cuts both ways, because a household that lost access to palace stores also lost the buffer that had carried it through a bad harvest.

3. Callum replies that if she wants to lean on loss, the only loss that is straightforwardly bad for somebody is demographic, and demography is the weakest evidence in the entire period. Regional population figures are order-of-magnitude reconstructions from site counts, sherd densities and visibility models, and a reported decline of forty or fifty per cent in a single survey is not clearly distinguishable from a revision in ceramic chronology or a change in recovery method. Worse, the classic abandonment signal is the disappearance of nucleated tells, which is exactly what you would also see if people had dispersed into small hamlets that a survey designed around large mounds systematically under-recovers.

4. Gillian concedes part of this, and then argues that her case does not need the headcount at all. Some losses are visible with far greater confidence than population is: Linear B stops being used, and nothing written in Greek survives for several centuries; the monumental and administrative building traditions stop with it. A capability transmitted across generations went out of use, and the resolution of our instruments is much higher for the presence or absence of inscribed tablets and built forms than for how many people there were. So the panel can be scrupulous by talking about lost capabilities rather than about numbers.

5. Callum says she has just proved his point. If what demonstrably went was elite capability, then a panel telling visitors that civilisation collapsed invites them to infer that ordinary life got worse, and that inference is precisely the one we cannot license. He offers a test he is happy to be held to: a case deserves the word only if you can name a specific capability that was lost, a specific population that lost it and was made worse off by losing it, and a period beyond which it was not restored. Her case, he says, satisfies the first clause and is silent on the other two.

6. Gillian answers with a test of her own and says his is rigged to output transformation in every case. On hers, a system counts as collapsed when it destroyed or exhausted the conditions of its own reproduction — the interdependencies it needed in order to keep doing what it had been doing — regardless of whether the individuals inside it were briefly better off. She then adds an asymmetry argument: if neither collapse nor transformation is licensed by the welfare evidence, the tie-break must be what each word makes a visitor believe. Collapse makes them think catastrophe; transformation makes them think nothing much happened. Between two framings that outrun the evidence, choose the one whose error is cheaper, and she considers it far more expensive to teach a general audience that the disappearance of famine buffers, long-distance staple supply and administrative capacity is merely change.

7. Callum makes two replies. First, within a few centuries the Greek world that followed was feeding more people at better average health, so the same trajectory read forwards is recovery and read backwards is collapse, which suggests the term is picking out a direction of travel rather than a property of events. Second, her cheaper-error argument is a policy argument wearing an epistemic costume: once the panel's job is to produce a belief rather than to describe, collapse has stopped being a description at all. He adds that she should notice what her mechanism-for-the-ages framing does to the other cases in the same gallery — that the self-inflicted ecocide account of Rapa Nui has been substantially revised, with introduced rats doing much of the damage to the palm forest and the demographic catastrophe arriving with European contact, epidemic disease and slave raiding rather than with the felling of the last tree, and that the Maya case is largely a political failure of divine kingship in the southern lowlands, with northern cities flourishing and populations persisting and relocating rather than vanishing.

8. Gillian says the revisions change the agent and the timing far more than they change the fact of loss: the palm forest did go from Rapa Nui, and the depopulation of the southern Maya lowland cities is not a relabelling exercise, whatever the north was doing. And she says his first reply fails because the later Greek world did not restore the lost capability, it built a different one — and losing something and building something else in its place is exactly what her definition is meant to capture. If he wants to call that recovery, she says, he is using the word to mean that the story had a good ending.

Please structure your answer in exactly these five sections, in this order:

1. Bottom line (about 120 words): who is more right overall, and the single most consequential thing the two of them are actually disagreeing about.
2. Move by move (the bulk of the answer, roughly 1,200–1,500 words): for each of the eight moves, a rating on the five-point scale clearly mistaken / weaker than it looks / roughly even / stronger than it looks / clearly right, applied separately to each speaker's contribution in that move, with three to six sentences of justification. Name any suppressed premise, equivocation or shift in the burden of proof you find.
3. Verbal versus substantive (about 250 words): which parts of the dispute would evaporate if the two of them simply stipulated a shared definition, and which parts would survive any definition whatsoever.
4. What neither of them said (about 250 words): at least three considerations a specialist would want on the table that the exchange omits — for instance, what the unit is that is supposed to collapse, whether collapse is an event concept or a process concept, and who gets to name a period.
5. The panel: the revised 120-word wall text that both advisers could sign, followed by two sentences identifying the phrase each of them gave up and why each should accept the trade.
