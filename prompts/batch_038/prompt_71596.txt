I help run the methods working group at a small meta-research nonprofit — mostly systematic reviews, and lately a lot of adjudicating disputes between two labs that looked at the same data and came out differently. Two of our senior people got into it after a talk last month: Ruth, a statistician who does this for a living, and Dan, ex-clinician, self-taught methodologist, reads the epistemology stuff on his own. I was in the room, I didnt take a side, and nobody won. Here's the exchange as close to verbatim as I can get it:

1. Dan: if two people are equally well informed and equally competent and they land on opposite conclusions, at least one of them is making a mistake, and from inside your own head you can't tell which one it is. So you have to cut your confidence way down. That's the whole answer.

2. Ruth: fine, conciliate. But notice what you've just committed yourself to. You now think the confidence you had ten minutes ago was irrational for you to have. And you're going to keep believing the thing anyway, because you can't believe P and also believe your believing P is irrational — that's a contradiction dressed up as sophistication.

3. Dan: no it isn't, and it's not a contradiction, it's just incoherent, and those are different complaints. Plenty of things are incoherent without being self-contradictory. Weakness of will is incoherent. Nobody thinks the weak-willed person literally believes contradictory things.

4. Ruth: okay but here's the part that bugs me. Take the Ambian case. You take a sleeping pill, and an hour later you're cheerfully telling your partner you're certain you had a dog named Biscuit as a kid. Next morning you remember the packet said it causes false memories. Are you required to drop the Biscuit belief? Obviously not — the higher-order evidence is about the pill, not about the dog. So you rationally think your confidence is irrational, and you rationally keep it. Dan: exactly, and that's my point. Ruth: no, that's my point. Those cases only work because the higher-order evidence is misleading — the pill didn't actually corrupt anything. Real disagreement isn't like that. There's no pill. The evidence that you got it wrong is just true.

5. Dan: there's no reason to think that. Rationality governs what you believe about the world and what you believe about your own believing separately, and there's no guarantee those two line up. Expecting them to line up is expecting rationality to be transparent to itself, and it isn't.

6. Ruth: on your view there is nothing anyone could say to you. Every piece of criticism becomes higher-order evidence, which you absorb as "I know, I shouldn't believe this, but I do," and you keep going. A notion of rationality that can't be derailed by any argument isn't a notion of rationality, it's a get-out clause.

7. Dan: except you'd keep your mouth shut about it. You wouldn't assert it, wouldn't publish it, wouldn't recommend it to a colleague.

8. Ruth: and the fact that you'd be embarassed to say it out loud is the argument against you, not for you.

9. Dan: then your enkratic requirement isn't about rationality at all, it's about agency or self-presentation or something. Fine — call it a norm on being a certain kind of unified knower, and stop telling me I'm irrational.

10. Ruth: also, your view runs a regress and you know it. Conciliate once, then you have higher-order evidence that your opponent conciliated, so you conciliate about that, then about that, forever.

11. Dan: it stops, because eventually you run out of new information about your own reliability. And yours doesn't stop either — on your view I conciliate, you see me conciliate, you revise, I see you revise.

12. Ruth: mine stops when we agree.

13. Dan: then you owe me an explanation of why your regress converges and mine diverges, and "because mine agrees with itself" isn't one.

Could you go through this move by move and rate each of the thirteen — clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, clearly wrong — with a sentence or two on each? I want to know specifically who's ahead on the incoherence-vs-contradiction point, on whether the drug cases and peer disagreement are really the same shape, on move 6, and on whether either regress argument does any work at all. Then say what both of them missed that a third person in the room should have raised, and who was more right overall.

And please be straight with me about where the field actually is: which of these claims are settled enough that I could put them in a newsletter, which ones are live arguments between people who know this literature cold, and which side (if either) is closer to the mainstream. Don't sell me a contested view as the consensus, and if there's a place where you're reconstructing the debate from principle rather than from knowing how it's actually gone, mark it.
