I'm a senior engineer at a mid-sized freight brokerage, and for the past year our internal Autonomous Systems Review Panel has sorted deployed and proposed systems into one of two categories: Tier 1, which it treats as a tool subject to ordinary software change control, and Tier 3, which it treats as an agent requiring a named human owner, a written risk assessment, and specific conditions on deployment. There is no Tier 2; the panel skips it deliberately, and people grumble about that. I've just submitted a proposal that comes before the panel next month. I'm also in a reading group on the tool-versus-agent distinction in AI, and I've become convinced the panel isn't tracking that distinction — or not straightforwardly — though I can't pin down what it is tracking instead. Its chair is on leave, so a deputy from Legal is running the meeting, and my manager wants the system live before the quarter closes. Here is the panel's complete record to date.

One: a code-review assistant that proposes diffs to internal repositories; a human reads and merges every one. Tier 1. Two: a customer-support model that judges refund eligibility on its own and issues refunds up to fifty dollars to named customers, with no per-case approval. Tier 3. Three: an open-ended research agent given a one-line brief; it plans its own subgoals, browses the public web, and writes files in a sandbox, but it cannot email anyone, spend money, or reach any production system. Tier 1. Four: a trading bot that executes a deterministic strategy with parameters set by humans under change control; nobody reviews individual trades, it turns over tens of millions of dollars of notional a day, but every position is exchange-cleared against an anonymous counterparty and the book is flattened nightly. Tier 1. Five: an internal operations helper whose every action was trivial and reversible — wiki edits, ticket updates, nothing touching the outside world — but which could register new API credentials for itself at runtime. Tier 3, and it was reclassified to Tier 1 only after the vendor disabled runtime registration entirely. Six: a contract-drafting system with frozen weights, no self-extension, and complete logs, whose drafts were formally signed by a staff lawyer; the panel found that the lawyer could not realistically read three hundred drafts a day and treated the signature as a rubber stamp. Tier 3.

My proposal is an exception-handling system for our invoicing pipeline. The operations lead hands it a one-paragraph objective in ordinary language — keep the dispute backlog under two hundred items and cut average resolution time — and it decomposes that into subgoals itself. Its action set is fixed at deployment and cannot grow: seven approved tools, being reassign a ticket, apply a credit of up to $250 against a carrier's account strictly from a published schedule of enumerated reasons, write to an internal owner from a template library, place or release a hold on an outbound shipment, escalate to a human queue, annotate a shared adjustments log that downstream systems read as authoritative, and open a corrective-action record. Weights and prompts are frozen and any change goes through version control. No human approves individual acts; instead a team reviews a five percent random sample weekly, every action is logged, and anything it did can be rolled back for thirty days. Two features are why I expect trouble. First, the pilot showed that placing a hold is felt by the shipper as a delivery delay, even though no notice is sent and no contractual entitlement changes. Second, the system keeps a persistent working memory of what has worked, which is fed back into its context on every run; over six weeks of pilot that memory measurably changed its behavior in directions the team cannot fully explain, despite nothing else about it changing.

Working only from these six decisions as the evidence — I'm not asking for references, and I don't want a survey of the literature on tool AI versus agent AI — please do the following. Identify which decision rules are consistent with all six verdicts, and separately which plausible-sounding rules the record actually refutes; I suspect at least one rule about autonomy of ends and one about scale of money are dead on arrival, and I'd like to know whether I'm right. Then predict the panel's verdict on my proposal together with the conditions you expect it to attach, say how confident you are, and say which surviving rule you think is driving the prediction rather than merely compatible with it. Identify the closest analog among the six and say precisely where that analogy strains. Then tell me what the panel ought to decide if it wants to be consistent with itself, and state plainly which principle it would be committing to if it went the other way instead — I want to know what I'd be conceding by accepting a Tier 1 classification, and what the panel would be conceding by imposing Tier 3. Finally, design one further hypothetical case that I could put to the panel which would discriminate among the surviving rules, with each rule's verdict on it written down in advance, and answer this: on the evidence of its own record, is the panel's Tier 1/Tier 3 line tracking anything like agency at all, or is it tracking something else — reviewability, attribution, exposure to outside parties — that merely correlates with agency in the cases it has seen so far? If it's the latter, name it as precisely as you can. Please write the whole response in American English.
