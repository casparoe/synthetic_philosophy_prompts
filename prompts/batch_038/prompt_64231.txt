You are an expert in archaeological method and theory — someone with real training in site formation processes and taphonomy, and a secondary competence in the philosophy of scientific inference. Please think carefully before answering, and treat this as an analytic problem to be solved rather than a survey of positions in the field.

**Context.** I have spent a decade reading rationalist blogs and forums, so I have acquired a habit: when an argument persuades a room, I want to know which premises were actually load-bearing and which were decoration. My younger sister is finishing a PhD in Near Eastern archaeology, and last month she came home from a departmental panel in which her supervisor disposed of a junior colleague's paper with an image rather than a measurement. I would like you to audit the image.

**The paper, as my sister describes it.** At a small hilltop site there is a burnt horizon. Within it: nine skeletons in a courtyard, several still articulated; a scatter of metal points in the collapse debris above them; crushed storage jars in a blocked doorway; and a charred roof beam short enough to give a dendrochronological date to the individual year of felling. The authors argue four things — (i) that the horizon records a single episode rather than an accumulation of episodes, (ii) that the episode was a violent raid, (iii) that the distribution of the points relative to the doorway and the collapse indicates how the attackers entered, and (iv) that all of this can be placed within a specific decade.

**The supervisor's response.** Roughly: this is a palimpsest. What you are looking at is not a raid but a surface that has been scraped and rewritten many times over — trampling, rodent burrowing, later pits cut down through the horizon, colluvial movement, plough action near the top, roof material that kept shifting for years after it fell. Each rewriting smears whatever was underneath. You may read the long-run average of a place; you cannot read an afternoon. Event-scale claims are the Pompeii premise dressed up in field photographs. His verdict was that the paper should be rewritten to make claims about a phase of destruction and reoccupation, and drop the raid, the attackers, and the decade.

**My sister's counter-image.** Roughly: it is not a manuscript, it is a crash site. Nobody tells a forensic investigator that a single event cannot be reconstructed because leaves blew across the scene afterwards. Disturbance is not always noise laid over a signal; often the disturbance *is* the record — the way the beam fell tells you what had been holding it up, the way a jar broke tells you whether it was full or empty at the time, and conjoining fragments prove that pieces of one object are still lying where they landed.
