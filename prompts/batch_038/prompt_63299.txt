I have been reconstructing the standard resolution of Maxwell's demon, and in the course of it I produced an extension that appears to entail a conclusion I cannot accept. Each step looks defensible to me in isolation, so what I want is a careful diagnosis rather than reassurance. Please work through the argument premise by premise, identify where it goes wrong, and price the alternatives. Please write your answer in American English.

Setup. A Szilard-type demon works on a gas in thermal contact with a heat bath at temperature T_h. Each time it acquires and uses one bit of information about the gas, it can extract up to kT_h ln 2 of work from that bath.

1. Landauer's principle: the only unavoidable thermodynamic cost in such a cycle is incurred at the reset of the demon's memory. Resetting one bit while the memory is coupled to a bath at temperature T dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat into that bath. Measurement, feedback, and the work-extraction stroke can all be carried out reversibly in principle.
2. Consequently, if the demon resets its memory into the very bath at T_h from which it drew the work, it pays kT_h ln 2 and gains kT_h ln 2. The books balance, and the second law is vindicated. This is the standard resolution, and I accept it.
3. But nothing in (1) requires the reset to be performed at the temperature of the gas being sorted. A demon is a machine with two thermal contacts, and it is at liberty to make those contacts different from one another.
4. So let the demon sort a gas at 300 K and reset its memory radiatively into deep space, whose effective temperature is roughly 3 K. Per bit, it gains kT_h ln 2 of work and discharges only kT_c ln 2 of heat, about one percent of the gain. The net work per cycle is k(T_h − T_c) ln 2, strictly positive, and the entropy withdrawn from the hot bath is exactly matched by the entropy deposited in the cold one, so the cycle produces no entropy anywhere else. Idealize every step as saturating the relevant bounds.
5. The third law forbids attaining T_c = 0, not approaching it, and nothing in (1) through (4) privileges any particular value of T_c. So for every T_c > 0, however small, the cycle in (4) is legal, and the heat discharged into the cold sink can be made arbitrarily small while the work extracted remains essentially kT_h ln 2.
6. Hence there is a family of legal cycles whose sole net effect approaches, in the limit, the conversion of heat drawn from a single reservoir entirely into work, which is precisely what the Kelvin-Planck formulation of the second law declares impossible.
7. A prohibition that can be approached to within an arbitrarily small margin is not functioning as a prohibition. What (1) through (6) really show is that the content of the second law is a contingent claim about how cold the coldest sink available to us happens to be: a cosmological boundary condition, not a constraint built into the dynamics.
8. Therefore the second law is either false, in that its usual formulation does not hold as a strict constraint on possible processes, or true but not a law.

For what it is worth, I would rather surrender (7) and (8) than (1) and (2), but I cannot tell whether that preference is principled or merely conservative. In answering, please address each of the following:

(a) Which numbered step is the first to fail, and why did it look acceptable at the time? If more than one fails, say which failure does the decisive work and which are only downstream of it.

(b) Give a correct thermodynamic accounting of one complete cycle of the device described in (4), listing everything gained, everything discharged, and every resource that is drawn down during the cycle but never appears in my per-cycle balance. Where does the net work come from: the demon's information, the temperature difference, or the depletion of something?

(c) Does the device in (4) fall within the scope of the formulation of the second law invoked in (6)? State that formulation's conditions of application precisely, and say whether they are met here, and at what stage.

(d) Is my inference in (6) from a family of processes to a limiting process legitimate? In particular, an idealized reversible heat engine already operates at exactly zero entropy production and has done so in textbook treatments for a long time, so explain what, if anything, my family adds to that familiar situation.

(e) Suppose instead that I retain (3) through (8) and reject (1), by insisting that the acquisition of the bit carries an irreducible cost of its own, payable at T_h and independent of any later reset. Is that route open to me, and what would it cost me elsewhere, in the thermodynamics of measurement, in the theory of computation, or in fluctuation-theorem accounting?

(f) A colleague proposes a different escape: a demon that never resets at all, recording its measurements into a memory that grows without bound. Does that avoid the accounting in (b), or merely relocate the cost? If it relocates it, identify what is being consumed and where the bill eventually arrives.

(g) Is there any legitimate residue in (7)? Is there a defensible sense in which the status or the applicability of the second law depends on boundary conditions, and if so, how does that differ from the conclusion drawn in (8)?
