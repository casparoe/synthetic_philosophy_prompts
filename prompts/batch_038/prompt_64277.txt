I work on pricing at a small specialty insurer — we write first-of-kind stuff, vertical farms, drone inspection fleets, that sort of thing, where there's essentially no loss history. We see maybe two or three new exposures a month. Right now, when there's no data, our committee picks a default prior by literally saying let's stay neutral and split the difference between the optimistic and pessimistic case, and it blew up on us a few years back: someone on the rooftop greenhouse book assumed loss ratio was uniform between zero and one, a colleague re-ran it applying the same "neutral" reasoning to log loss ratio, and the indicated rate moved something like 40%. Nobody could say which of the two was the neutral one. Its obviously the chord problem — I remember enough to know that "no reason to prefer one over the other" doesn't actually pick out a distribution, because it depends on how you've sliced up the parameter space, and there's more than one honest way to slice.

What I want is a procedure my team can run, not an essay on why the problem is hard. So: the questions to ask, in order, when you're standing in front of a brand-new exposure with nothing to go on; what counts as a satisfactory answer at each step; where the procedure terminates in a number; and where it hands the choice to a human because the question turns out to be about what we care about rather than about what's neutral. Tell me how to choose the parameterization — what makes one slicing defensible rather than arbitrary, whether invariance under reparameterization is a real constraint or just a slogan people wave, what to do when two plausible invariances conflict, what to do when you can't defend any of them, and how wide to make the resulting distribution. Flag clearly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It has to be runnable in about an hour by someone who's had one stats course, it has to survive a filing examiner asking "justify this choice," and it needs to end in one number for the rate plus something honest about how far that number could move if we'd sliced differently.

Then run it on three cases. One: a drone-inspection fleet hull and liability book, about eight operators, nobody has a credible estimate of annual total-loss frequency. Two: a cultivated-meat facility contamination and business-interruption exposure where we have plenty of data on adjacent food industries and none on this one. Three: you pick it — an exposure where the procedure spits out something I won't like, either a book we should stop writing or a number that shows we've been badly underpricing. And be explicit about what the procedure can't do: where it manufactures false precision, and where a determined underwriter could game it to land on the answer he wanted anyway while still looking principled.

No reading list, I can't chase references and neither can my team. But do tell me how confident you are in the load-bearing claims — especially anything you assert about invariance arguments actually settling the choice rather than just relocating it — and single out the one step in the procedure you're least sure of.
