I've been down a rabbit hole on the Year Without a Summer and I want to use it to pressure-test a little scheme I made up. Be honest about whether the scheme earns its keep — I'd rather you break it then flatter it.

Facts as I have them, correct me where I'm wrong: Mount Tambora on Sumbawa goes off in April 1815, biggest eruption in recorded history, something like ten thousand killed outright and tens of thousands more from famine and disease across the islands afterwards, and enough sulfur in the stratosphere to knock roughly half a degree off global average temperatures the following year, much more in some places. Then 1816: snow in New England in June and killing frosts right through August, grain prices spiking, families pulling up stakes for Ohio; a cold, soaked summer across western Europe with failed harvests, bread prices doubling or worse, food riots and hoarding panics; a typhus epidemic in Ireland that starts in 1816 and runs for years; and, famously, the Byron/Shelley/Polidori party at the Villa Diodati getting rained in until they come up with Frankenstein and the vampire story. But the same window in Britain also gives you the Corn Law of March 1815, deliberately engineered to hold bread prices up by shutting out imports, and the demobilization after Waterloo dumping a huge number of men onto a labour market with no room for them.

Here's the scheme. I think a non-human cause can sit in four distinct roles in a historical narrative, and they're not the same claim: trigger (the exogenous thing that starts the chain), terrain (a standing condition that reshapes the option space without settling anything), amplifier (something that speeds up or intensifies a process already under way), and alibi (something invoked afterwards to take responsibility off the human actors).

So: sort each of those 1816 consequences into the four roles, and flag where the sorting is genuinely contested rather than obvious. Does calling Tambora a 'historical actor' assert anything beyond 'it was a cause with a big coefficient' — and if it does, what exactly does it add? Where does the alibi role start doing political work in this episode? I'm thinking of Sen's point that famines are usually failures of distribution and entitlement rather than of food supply, which would make the Corn Law the interesting variable and the volcano the convenient one. Which of the stock Tambora claims hold up as more than story — the running machine invented because horses were dying for want of oats, the Bengal cholera, the Gothic novel — and what would it take to show they did? And then run it backwards: does 1816 vindicate the critique of environmental determinism, expose a version of it nobody had in mind, or is it just a case where my four roles tangle up? Say so if the scheme its self is too blunt to be worth keeping.

No bibliography, I don't need one — just argue it out.
