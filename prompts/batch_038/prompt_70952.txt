Getting asked by the organizers of a workshop I'm in next month to circulate a "where I stand" sheet beforehand — just a bare list of commitments, no arguments, so that everyone can spend the weekend taking swings at it. Before I send mine off I want a sanity check, because I'm fairly confident there's a crack in it somewhere and I cant locate it myself. This is my patch (computation and epistemic norms), which is exactly the situation where you stop seeing the obvious.

Here's the list.

1. Feasibility is normative. I hold a computational version of ought-implies-can: if there's no polynomial-time algorithm for a task, no agent — human, machine, whatever — can be epistemically required to perform it. I take polynomial time as the right rough cut on feasibility (the Cobham-Edmonds line), while admitting it's a crude instrument.

2. Idealization in epistemology is legitimate rather than a mistake. When I teach Bayesianism I tell students that logical omniscience is an idealization on a par with a frictionless plane: the norm is stated for an ideal agent and we are the approximations. Nothing incoherent about that, and I don't think the idealization needs a computational apology.

3. I'm a plain realist about complexity-class questions. P vs NP has a determinate answer, and it's whatever it is regardless of whether anyone can prove it or exhibit the relevant algorithm.

4. Relatedly: a nonconstructive proof that P = NP would be a big metaphysical deal and a computational nothing. No algorithm, no new capacity to solve anything. You'd learn that something exists without being able to do anything with it.

5. I take the natural-proofs barrier (Razborov and Rudich, very roughly: a proof strategy with the combinatorial features we'd naturally expect would, if it worked, also destroy the pseudorandom generators we believe exist) to be a philosophical result and not merely a technical one — it says an entire style of conceptual explanation in mathematics is unavailable for this question.

6. I accept computer-assisted proofs as proofs. Four color, Kepler, whatever: if the machine-checked artifact is right we have knowledge, even though no single human surveys it. Understanding can be distributed across a community and across tools; it doesn't have to fit inside one skull.

7. Where I get heterdox: I think the intractability of exact probabilistic inference in general Bayesian networks — I'll just say NP-hard and worse in a handout like this, I'm not going to get the precise class right — is not merely an implementation nuisance. It licenses a substantive revision of the norm. The norm can't be "hold coherent credences and conditionalize on them"; it has to be something like "use a procedure that reliably does well in the environments you actually inhabit." That's why I'm broadly sympathetic to the Gigerenzer-style heuristics program, and I take it to be doing epistemology rather than psychology.

8. But I also insist, against a certain kind of pessimism, that worst-case complexity is the wrong measure of what an agent can actually do. When people tell me learning is hard or planning is hard or inference is hard, I say: yes, in the worst case over all instances, which is not the world any of us lives in. Real instances are structured, and average-case or smoothed behavior is what you should look at. That's my standing answer to people who think complexity theory has shown cognition to be impossible.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? I'd especially like to know whether 1, 2 and 7 can all stand up at once. And more generally, whether any item on the list is doing double duty in a way I haven't noticed — several of them are supposed to be doing load-bearing work in different arguments and I may not have checked that the loads are compatible.

Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what the cheapest revision is and what it costs me philosophically — I want to know if the fix is local (drop a hedge, restate a claim) or whether it takes out something I care about. Then tell me which single change buys the most overall consistency. And please don't give me a diplomatic "it depends how you define idealization"; if that really is the answer, say so and tell me which definition I'd have to adopt for the set to cohere.

Two last things. Be as committed as you can in the verdicts — I'd rather be told flatly that 7 has to go than be handed a menu. And then tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to break down: which of your findings is most fragile, and what argument or consideration would make you reverse your call on the worst tension you identify.
