I evaluate behavioural interventions for a regional government, and over several years I have kept encountering a configuration that I can describe precisely but cannot name. In each case the intervention is defended as preserving freedom of choice because the exit is trivially easy, and in each case the exit really is trivially easy at the point where it is offered. What has changed is that the costs of taking it have been moved elsewhere in the process, to a place where no user sees them and no evaluation counts them.

Three instances, all real in structure if not in detail.

A utility auto-enrols households into a new tariff. Declining is one click on the enrolment page, and the click registers immediately. That decline option, however, exists only within that page and that session; afterwards the portal presents the tariff as an active service, and the only route out is a paper form. No subsequent bill or letter mentions that the one-click route ever existed. The evaluation report records the opt-out as frictionless, and by the letter it is.

A welfare-to-work programme lets claimants decline a default appointment slot with a single tap in an app. The tap works. What the app does not say is that declining routes the file to a different processing queue, so that those who tap out wait eleven weeks for the next substantive contact rather than two. Formally free exit; in substance a queue penalty that is nowhere disclosed.

A hospital cafeteria moves confectionery to a low shelf and keeps it fully available. The shelf label shows the shelf price. At the till, a bundled levy is added to certain items, so the price a patient sees at the point of choosing is not the price that determines the choice. Nothing has been removed; the cost has been relocated from the shelf to the receipt.

What I am after is the concept, if one exists. I know the neighbouring vocabulary well enough to know it does not cover this. There is a term for friction deliberately piled onto people trying to claim something they are entitled to, and there is interface-design language for subscriptions that are easy to start and hard to stop — but in my cases the visible decision point is genuinely frictionless and, so far as I can tell, nothing is being concealed there. The designers have not added an obstacle; they have relocated an existing one to a different stage, a different medium, a different agency, or a different moment in time. What worries me is that any compliance test inspecting only the moment of choice scores all three as clean. The criterion appears satisfied at the point it examines while being defeated by everything that happens elsewhere in the process.

So: does this configuration have an established name, and in which literature? If it does, where does the description above strain against it? If it does not, what are the nearest concepts, and for each, what exactly distinguishes my pattern from it — I want the discriminations spelled out, not a list. And if you conclude that nothing fits, coin a term and define it properly, with edges rather than just a label. In particular: does the concept require the relocation to be deliberate, or does it apply to the structure alone? Is it still the same phenomenon when the relocated cost is smaller than the visible one? Where does it shade into ordinary poor service design, and where into outright deception? Does the familiar requirement that opting out be easy need restating so that it survives this, and if so, how? Finally, what would an audit capable of detecting it have to examine, given that any inspection confined to the decision point will miss it by construction?
