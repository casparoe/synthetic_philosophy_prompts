my uncle signed up with a cryonics outfit that sells two packages, whole body or head only, and he went head only — cheaper, and the pitch is that the body is just the part they'll be able to regrow or print later, while the head is where he actually is. whats keeps coming back in their literature is a distinction between being clinically dead and something they call information-theoretic death: the claim that you aren't really gone until the structure encoding your memories and personality is damaged beyond any conceivable recovery, so that dying is a long process with an endpoint much later than the time stamped on the death certificate. On that view the point of the standby team and the ice bath isn't to cheat death, it's to stop the record from being erased before it can be read.

Meanwhile the same legal system that happily lets him sign that contract says two things that look like they pull in opposite directions. Most states have adopted a uniform statute under which you're dead once all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, have irreversibly ceased — so a warm, ventilated body that still digests food, fights off infection, and heals wounds is legally a corpse, and there are cases where one was kept going for weeks so a pregnancy could be carried to term. But somebody in a persistent vegetative state, whose cortex is destroyed and who will never have another experience, is legally a living person: killing them is homicide, they keep their rights, and the only route by which they die is a surrogate speaking on their behalf and asking for the feeding tube to come out.

So what theory of persons does the head-only package presuppose, and does the law's patchwork presuppose the same one or a rival? Im especially stuck on the fact that these two look like opposite bets about the same quantity — the cryonics customer treats the psychological record as the person and the body as swappable, while the PVS ruling keeps a person in residence in a body whose psychological record is already gone, and then lets the earlier person's wishes (via a surrogate) be what ends them. Reconstruct the implicit views as carefully as you can, and separate what these practices strictly require from what merely sits comfortably with them: does buying neuro preservation commit you to a psychological-continuity theory of identity, or only to something weaker, like a claim about where the evidence of a person is stored? Is there a single account of what persons are that could license both the cryonics contract and the PVS ruling, or is one of them just an unrevised holdover from an older way of thinking? Tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined and several theories would equally license it, and then tell me what would actually have to change — in death statutes, in the dead-donor rule, in how hospitals and families treat a body — if we adopted the cryonics picture of when a person ends.

I realize this is roughly the setup of that Dennett story where the brain gets decanted into a vat and radio-linked to the wandering body, with a software duplicate as backup and a switch to choose which one is driving. But here people are paying real money for a version of it and the state is taking a position too, so I want the philosophy of the actual practice, not a summary of the thought experiment.
