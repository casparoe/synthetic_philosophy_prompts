I serve as staff biologist for a state listing committee that decides which taxa are added to our endangered-species register. Because the statute extends protection to recognized species and not to merely local populations, the question of whether a candidate is a species tends to decide the petition before any biology is discussed. Last month's hearing concerned two populations of a streamside salamander, one confined to a high-elevation basin and one occupying the drainage below it, which now meet and hybridize along a short stretch of creek where their ranges have come into contact over the past century. Our consulting taxonomist recommended against listing, and he closed his remarks by reciting the textbook formulation of Mayr's biological species concept — a species is a group of "actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations which are reproductively isolated from other such groups" — and observing that, since these animals demonstrably interbreed where they meet, the definition is satisfied and there is nothing left to decide. A committee member answered that under the phylogenetic concept each population is diagnosable by fixed character differences and the two are therefore distinct species; the taxonomist replied that she was simply using the word differently; and the chair tabled the petition pending a written clarification of terms, which I have been asked to provide.

What I need is a menu of readings, not a survey of the species problem. Please enumerate the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, and for each one state it explicitly, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false, indicate what evidence would bear on it, and say whether it actually delivers my taxonomist's conclusion. I would like particular attention to the following pressure points.

First, the modal in "potentially." Lay out the candidate readings separately — would interbreed if brought into contact; would interbreed if the geographic barrier between them were removed; is genetically capable of producing fertile offspring; would interbreed at some future date if ranges shifted further — and say for each whether it would, taken generally, collapse allopatric sister taxa into a single species or instead exclude taxa everyone counts as one species.

Second, "natural." What does it rule out: captive pairings, laboratory crosses, human-mediated translocations? And does a contact zone produced by climate-driven range expansion count as natural on any of these readings?

Third, "reproductively isolated." Does it require the absence of gene flow, or merely its reduction? Must the isolation be intrinsic rather than a matter of distance? Is it a property of the groups or of individual matings?

Fourth, the function of the sentence. Is it offered as a definition supplying necessary and sufficient conditions, as a diagnostic test, or as a claim about which properties make populations into independent evolutionary units? Does the disjunction "actually or potentially" make it true of almost any two populations, and therefore empty?

Finally, tell me which reading my taxonomist needed in order to end the discussion, whether that reading is one the salamanders satisfy, and what the clarification should say about whether he and the committee member are genuinely disagreeing or using one word in two different senses — and, if the latter, whether that settles the petition or only relocates it. A numbered list suitable for attaching to the minutes would be ideal.
