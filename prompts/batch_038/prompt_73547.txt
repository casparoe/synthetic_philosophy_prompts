I'm prepping a two-hour workshop for a mixed crowd — about half work on AI welfare / moral status of artificial systems, half work on invertebrate and farmed-animal welfare — and the two halves have already decided in advance that each other's favorite candidates are unserious. I want to open the day with a sorting quiz so everyone finds out, on paper, what they actually believe before they start winning arguments. So: ten to twelve questions, each one a concrete case or a claim they can answer without knowing which position it leads to, two or three options apiece, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named views.

Ground rules for the questions. Nothing may ask whether some creature is sentient — that's the thing under dispute, and everyone will just answer it tribally. No question should be answerable by recognizing a label. I want cases that pull apart things people normally run together, e.g.:

- a fruit fly that swims away from a heated zone and then, once it's safe, spends a while grooming at the spot that was heated
- a robot vacuum that makes a distressed noise when stuck under a cabinet — and then a second question where you learn the noise wasn't programmed, it came out of a system nobody designed to make it
- a copepod, where the real content of the case is that there are more of them than anything else
- a brain organoid in a dish showing coordinated bursting, where the researcher's stated reason for wanting to shut it down is that she can't rule out that it's having a bad time
- a honeybee that stings a handler and dies from it
- a person under propofol for a routine operation, where the question is about the two hours nobody can observe

Please build the tree out of three axes and tell me which axis each question is probing. First: what property does the work — capacity for any experience at all, valenced experience specifically, having interests, selfhood or narrative, agency? Second: where does the gradation live — in the property itself being a matter of degree, or in our uncertainty about whether it's present at all, or in moral weight being separable from status? Third: what's the default when you genuinely can't tell, and what's the shape of the function — a step, a smooth ramp, a cliff at one end with a slope at the other — and does the shape stay stable when you multiply it by very large numbers?

Then the part I care about most: walk the logical space before you locate the actual positions in it. Which combinations are occupied, which are empty, and for each empty cell say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's gotten around to it. Two cells I suspect are empty for very different reasons are (a) status comes in degrees but there's no fact of the matter how much any given being has, and (b) the precautionary default points toward exclusion — nobody's wronged if you leave them out. Tell me if I'm wrong about either.

Also flag every pair of leaves your instrument can't separate, and don't paper over it with a tiebreaker question — say why they come apart only somewhere the quiz can't reach. And when you name a leaf, use an established name if there is one; if there isn't, call it unnamed and describe it. Please don't attribute any view to a specific living researcher unless you're confident, because half this room reads the literature and will spend the coffee break checking me.
