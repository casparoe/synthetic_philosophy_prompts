I teach an upper-level interdisciplinary seminar on probability and rational choice, and I need one two-part question on the Kelly criterion for the final exam. Students will have 45 minutes and may use a calculator but not notes.

Part (a) should set up a specific repeated gamble — for instance, a coin with a 0.55 chance of heads, where staking a fraction f of current wealth returns 2f on heads and forfeits f on tails, with wealth reinvested each round — and require students to derive both the expected-wealth-maximizing stake and the long-run growth-rate-maximizing stake, then explain what the divergence between them shows about the status of expected-value reasoning for a single agent living through one trajectory.

Part (b) should present a compact argument I supply to you: because Kelly betting maximizes expected log wealth, and because the strong law of large numbers makes expected log wealth equal the almost-sure long-run growth rate in the limit, and because log is simply one admissible von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function among many, expected utility theory already mandates Kelly betting as uniquely rational. Students must locate the flaw rather than applaud the conclusion.

Please write the question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves each must contain — including the distinction between optimizing a distribution over hypothetical copies of the agent and optimizing the trajectory of one non-replicable agent, and why the bridge principle in part (b) fails even though its premises are individually true.
