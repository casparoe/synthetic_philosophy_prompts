I teach an MA-level philosophy of mind seminar — twelve students, one hundred minutes, a room with three movable tables and a whiteboard, and a printer down the hall. This session opens our unit on naturalizing mental content. None of them has had philosophy of language or cognitive science beyond the intro sequence, and I want the technical vocabulary held back while they work: no talk of covariance, proper function, or teleosemantics until the debrief. They should hit the problem structurally first and collect the names afterwards.

Design a paper-and-pencil game, playable in that time by twelve people in three teams of four, in which each team must settle what a simple detector mechanism is *about* and then be scored on how well its verdict handles the cases where the mechanism goes wrong. Four constraints I want met:

1. Teams are given a record of how the mechanism came to behave as it does, not a designer's statement of what it is for. Make that record a printed log of past episodes — what was present, what the mechanism did, what followed — long enough and messy enough that it genuinely underdetermines the answer, and underdetermines it at more than one level of grain.
2. At least one team's log contains a change of regime partway through: the mechanism was shaped under one set of circumstances and then acquired a later, second use. That team should face a real question about *which* history is the relevant one, not just a harder version of everyone else's puzzle.
3. The payoff scheme must make an error-proof assignment worthless. If a team's content verdict is one on which the mechanism could not possibly have malfunctioned in any of the test cases, that team scores zero on those cases no matter how it argues. Build this into the scoring arithmetic itself rather than leaving it to facilitator judgment.
4. Finish with an adversarial round: teams swap verdicts and are paid for constructing a case that the other team's verdict misclassifies, then paid again if the other team cannot repair its verdict without collapsing into something error-proof.

By the end I want them holding three things: that what a state is about cannot simply be read off what it currently co-varies with in the room; that repairing this by appeal to history generates a fresh problem, because a history can be described at competing grains and sometimes runs out before the case in front of you does; and that any account on which the mechanism cannot err has not accounted for anything worth calling content.

Deliver: a minute-by-minute timeline; exactly what goes on each printed page and in what order, including the log entries themselves; a facilitator's master key listing the test cases and the correct verdict under each of the two or three candidate content assignments, so I can score at the whiteboard quickly without adjudicating from scratch; the debrief, mapping particular moments of play onto the distinctions the literature draws and only then supplying the names; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to recover inside the room; and a frank statement of which features of the view the game cannot reproduce and I must supply myself. I already suspect that handing the shaping history over as a readable document is one leak — tell me where else the analogy fails.

No bibliography, no suggested readings, no quotations from the literature: I want something I can print tonight and run.

Commit rather than offer options. One design, one set of logs, one scoring table. Where there is a genuine fork — whether any team should be able to win outright, whether the log should expose the mechanism's internal states or only its inputs and outputs, whether the adversarial round is scored or purely rhetorical — pick a side and say why you picked it. And flag every point at which your design leans on a contested or idiosyncratic reading of how history fixes content rather than on something broadly agreed in the area, because twelve students will treat whatever I run as what the view says.
