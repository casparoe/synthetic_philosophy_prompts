For about two years now I have been reading about prisons on my own initiative — no degree, no coursework, just sustained curiosity — and I keep coming back to the contrast between the Norwegian prison everyone writes about, the one that opened in 2010 with a recording studio and no bars on the windows, where the officers spend their shifts talking to the men they are guarding, and an American maximum-security facility. What interests me is not which of the two is more pleasant. It is that the people arguing about them seem to be measuring a sentence in different units and never notice that they are doing so.

Here is the idea I have worked out for myself, and I would like it put to work in a piece of satire. A sentence is pronounced in years, but years are not what is actually delivered. Some people believe the state owes a certain quantity of hardship and that time is merely the meter it runs on; some believe it owes a stretch of life kept as close as possible to the ordinary life outside, so that the prisoner walks out through the same door he came in; and some believe it owes a reduction in future risk, which means the sentence is really over whenever an assessment says so. Call these three ways of denominating a sentence: suffering, normalcy, and risk. My suspicion is that each unit answers the question of when a man is finished in a different way, and that a great many quarrels about prisons are quarrels about which unit the speakers are secretly billing in.

Please write a parody of the "compare our plans" page that a subscription company puts online — plan names, a headline price, a short list of what is included, and fine print in smaller type underneath. The company is a private prison operator that has noticed the disagreement among its customers and has decided, without a trace of embarrassment, to sell all three denominations as separate products.

The response should consist of exactly five numbered plan entries and nothing else: no introduction, no framing paragraph, no closing line. For each entry, give the plan name, a price, two or three sentences of marketing copy in the operator's chipper corporate voice, and a single line of fine print. Roughly six hundred words in total.

The humor must come from the operator being clear-eyed about the trade-offs rather than from the operator being stupid. Each plan should curdle by the internal logic of the view it embodies, not because the seller is a villain: the suffering-denominated plan cannot say when enough hardship has been delivered and starts offering discounts it does not believe in; the normalcy plan has to answer the person who points out that the prisoner's room is nicer than his own, and discovers that keeping a man's life ordinary is expensive in ways no brochure admits; the risk plan has no fixed end date and finds that the safest person to release is the one no assessor will ever put a signature to. Anyone who has thought hard about punishment should recognize these as real dilemmas and consider the satire fair. Do not state the objections — put them in the small print, where a reader has to lean in.

Two further constraints. Invent everything: no real prison names, no real operators, and no statistics dressed up as research; any number that appears should read as obvious marketing nonsense. And do not let the operator's voice break character to make a moral point about itself — if the piece has a conscience, it should be visible only in the arrangement of the plans.
