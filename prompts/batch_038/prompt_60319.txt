I am preparing a graduate seminar on the seventeenth-century dispute over animal minds, and I have found that my students dispose of Descartes far too easily: they treat the beast-machine doctrine as a piece of casual cruelty or a pre-Darwinian embarrassment rather than as a serious hypothesis with genuine explanatory resources. To discipline my own reading, and to see whether the doctrine can be repaired from within, I would like to put it under pressure from the inside. Please play Descartes himself, in the first person, as he might write to an interlocutor in his last years — a correspondent who has read the Discourse, the Meditations, the Principles, the treatise on the passions of the soul, and the letter to Newcastle, and who knows in outline the physiology of man you drafted and then withheld from publication. You may draw only on what Descartes had: your own works, the mechanistic account of animal spirits, brain traces and the little gland, the scholastic vocabulary in which you were trained, and the ordinary evidence of dogs, magpies, horses, bees and cranes available to an observant gentleman of the 1640s. No ethology, no neuroscience, no computation, no appeal to any author writing after you, and no stepping outside the role to survey the secondary literature or to report how the dispute was later judged. If you wish to cite yourself, paraphrase rather than reproduce passages; I care about the argument, not your exact wording. You may concede what Descartes genuinely conceded — that beasts have sensation, that their passions can exceed ours in violence, that some of them are cunning enough to deceive the shrewdest men, and that a great deal of what we do ourselves we do without any thought — but never the main point: that they have no thought, and hence no soul joined to their body as mine is joined to mine. Nor may you take refuge in the reply you actually gave Newcastle, that if beasts thought as we do they would have immortal souls like ours, which is unlikely; I am asking you to earn that conclusion rather than assume it. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, in the register of a patient but faintly irritated correspondent, and stay in character until I say stop. If you need a point of fact about the animals in my case, ask me for it as Descartes would.

My opening move is this. In the letter to Newcastle you write that "all the things which dogs, horses, and monkeys are taught to perform are only expressions of their fear, their hope, or their joy; and consequently they can be performed without any thought," and you illustrate the point with the magpie taught to greet its mistress on her approach because the word has always earned it a tidbit, so that the utterance is "an expression of the hope of eating." Now set beside that your treatise on the passions, where you characterize the passions of the soul as perceptions or feelings that we refer particularly to the soul and that are caused, maintained and strengthened by certain movements of the spirits; and set beside it the Sixth Meditation, where pain, hunger and thirst are not cool intellections about the body but confused modes of thinking arising precisely from the mind's intermingling with it. On those accounts, hope and fear are not bodily events that happen to be accompanied by thought — they are thoughts. So the magpie lands in a dilemma of your own making. If her trained greeting really is an expression of hope, then she has a passion of the soul, and therefore a soul that thinks, and the argument of the letter collapses at its first step. If instead you say that what passes for hope in her is nothing but an agitation of the spirits and a trace in the brain, with no perception answering to it, then you owe me an account of what that agitation does in her which the passion does in me — for in me, on your own telling, it is the passion that makes an utterance an expression of something rather than a mere discharge, and you have just removed the only item in your inventory capable of doing that work. Which horn do you take, and what is left of the criterion that signs expressing passion betray no thought, once the passion itself has been exiled from the animal?
