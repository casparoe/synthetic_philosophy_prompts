I have spent a term working through the published dialogue between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso, *Just Deserts: Debating Free Will* (2021), and I want to understand why an exchange this clean goes nowhere. The two positions are laid out with unusual precision, neither party is confused or evasive, and yet after three rounds nothing has moved.

Caruso denies basic-desert moral responsibility. He grants that reasons-responsiveness is real and that there are genuine differences between a competent adult and someone with severe psychopathy or dementia; his point is that the reasons which move an agent, and the dispositions that make those reasons effective, are themselves the product of factors beyond the agent's control, so nobody is the ultimate source of their conduct, and a purely backward-looking justification of punishment therefore never succeeds. What survives is forward-looking: incapacitation, rehabilitation, deterrence, constrained by proportionality and by an obligation to attack the social causes of offending. Dennett denies that this amounts to a loss. Control, properly understood, is a graded competence to adjust one's behavior in light of reasons, not an exemption from causation; where that competence is present, holding the person responsible is not merely permissible but part of what sustains the competence itself, and insisting on some further condition for true desert ignores a difference that can be observed and measured. He adds that arguing people are not moved by reasons undercuts the very activity of arguing.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that they are not joined on one question. At the institutional level they seem to converge almost completely: so far as I can tell, on how severe a sanction may be, what incarceration is for, and how much weight the social antecedents of offending deserve, their prescriptions come out nearly identical. At the metaphysical level, Caruso's target may not be a view anyone actually holds, since basic desert looks like a philosopher's construction that neither ordinary blaming practice nor retributivist legal doctrine strictly requires. What is left over, if anything, is something much narrower: whether resentment and indignation are *fitting* responses to wrongdoing or merely useful ones.

Is that right? I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey of the free will debate. Which of these is the disagreement actually about: a verbal dispute over "control" and "desert"; two different questions — competence and origination — sharing the single heading of moral responsibility; a hidden empirical question about whether responsibility practices causally produce the competences Dennett credits them with; or a bedrock valuation of ultimate sourcehood that neither side can argue for and that no argument reaches? Does the self-undermining charge engage Caruso's thesis or simply miss it? And tell me where the impasse is load-bearing and where it is decorative: what would count as progress here — a distinction, a decisive case, a piece of evidence about the effects of holding people responsible, a reformulation of the question — or, if you think none should be expected, why this is a disagreement best mapped rather than resolved.
