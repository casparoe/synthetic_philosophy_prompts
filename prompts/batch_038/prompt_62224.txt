You are a political philosopher who also writes occasional speeches: you know the Foucault literature closely, but you write for rooms where almost nobody does. Think carefully about the architecture of the thing before you draft it.

I teach philosophy at a state secondary school and have been asked to give five minutes of opening remarks at Monday's whole-staff training day, at which the school rolls out a new classroom analytics platform. It logs lesson time, which pupils are called on, how quickly work is marked, and it places each teacher on a distribution of all the others. The headteacher is in the room and has staked real credibility on the purchase; roughly a third of the staff are genuinely enthusiastic, because they think it will surface the pupils who are quietly slipping, and they are right about that; the rest range from wary to openly hostile; the union representative will be listening for anything usable.

What I want the speech to transmit is a distinction, not an attitude. The distinction is between power that says no and power that first produces the objects it then measures — and, with it, the way an examination turns a person into a comparable case rather than judging them against a rule. The specific point I want the room to leave with is that the dashboard's real object of examination is not the pupils but us: it does not merely describe teaching, it manufactures categories of teacher, and those categories become real through what is done to the people sorted into them. Say that plainly, without apocalypse, and end in something a teacher could actually do differently in September — not in a call to refuse the system, which is neither available to us nor what I believe.

Constraints: about five minutes, so 600–750 words. The speech may not use the words Foucault, panopticon, or power/knowledge, and it may not quote anyone or any text — paraphrase instead. It must contain one concession to the enthusiasts that is true and that I would defend, not a piece of rhetorical throat-clearing. And it has to land for the two or three staff who know exactly whose ideas these are without turning into a lecture addressed to them.

Begin by stating, in three or four sentences, the single distinction the speech turns on and the concession you intend to make; then write the speech.
