Been going around the textualism/purposivism fight for a while and it feels totally frozen — same moves recycled since the 80s, nobody converting anybody. My hunch about why: the labels no longer carve the actual dispute. Textualists have converged on "ordinary public meaning fixed at enactment," and purposivists have largely retreated to statutory context, structure, and what problem the provision was demonstrably there to solve. So both sides now agree that meaning is a linguistic fact about the enacted text and its context, and what they really disagree about is which empirical facts settle it — what a competent reader at the time would take the words to convey versus what the legislature's public record shows it was aiming at. Plus there's a second, unnamed disagreement about judicial role that gets smuggled in under semantic vocabulary.

The case I keep coming back to as a test: the big 2020 Title VII decision, where the self-described textualists split and each side accused the other of doing purpose-driven reasoning in textualist clothing.

So: is my diagnosis right, or am I flattening a genuine substantive disagreement about meaning that's really there? I want the diagnosis argued from what each camp actually says, not a survey. And then tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a corpus-linguistics style result, a decision procedure, a change of question — or make the case that none is coming and why.
