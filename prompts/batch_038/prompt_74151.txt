I am in my fourth year of a PhD in jurisprudence, and this term I am teaching a five-week optional seminar on the concept of law to eighteen students — mostly second-year law students, three of whom have taken one undergraduate philosophy course. Week three is the Hart–Fuller exchange, and I have a single 100-minute session. When I have taught those readings as a discussion, the class reliably collapses the debate into "positivists hold that valid law must be obeyed, natural lawyers hold that unjust law is not law," which misdescribes both sides and makes the argument look like a quarrel over slogans. I want to replace the discussion with a structured adjudication exercise in which the students generate the real disagreement out of their own conduct before anyone names it.

Here is the skeleton I have drafted. Please complete it into a runnable session, and tell me candidly where the skeleton is wrong.

The class sits as first-instance panels of three in a fictional post-authoritarian state; I will supply the invented names and the regime sketch. Panels hear two cases under an identical written instrument I will call the Grounds of Decision, which lists only source-based grounds (proper enactment by the recognized organ, publication, prospectivity) and craft-based grounds (generality, intelligibility, absence of contradiction, feasibility of compliance, stability over time, and agreement between what officials actually do and what the rules declare). Manifest injustice is not an admissible ground at first instance: a panel that relies on it must say so expressly and accept that its judgment is struck on appeal. Grounds invoked are recorded by a student clerk and tabulated on the board. Round one is an enactment that is procedurally immaculate and substantively abhorrent. Round two is a measure the students will morally favour — relief for people punished by the old regime — that is retroactive, unpublished, internally contradictory, and impossible for many applicants to comply with. Each panel also seals a one-word answer to whether the instrument before it is law at all, and the sealed slips are opened only in the final ten minutes.

What I want participants to come away seeing: that both theorists agree a legal system can be efficient and wicked; that the craft standards do real normative work in round two without any appeal to justice, and do no work at all in round one, so the dispute is about whether that difference in kind is a moral difference; and that how one uses the word law changes what one is able to say in each round.

Please give me the two case files at the right level of detail, the Grounds of Decision instrument, role cards that carry no school labels, a scoring and accountability scheme that needs no money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps recorded moments — this panel's grounds, that panel's sealed slip, the pattern across rounds — onto the two positions, then onto the further claim that a regime pursuing wicked ends tends to corrupt its own legality, which is where I want the last fifteen minutes to land. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it: the student who identifies the exercise as a displaced version of the postwar German denunciation cases, the panel that strikes both measures on the ground that it is the court and no one can stop it, and the panel that launders a justice argument into the intelligibility or congruence ground. Say how to counterbalance case order across panels so the debrief can compare them. Also tell me whether round two as described serves the purpose better than a second case in which the craft defects are themselves the symptom of bad faith, and why.

Finish with an honest statement of which features of the debate this exercise cannot reproduce and I must supply myself — including, I suspect, the fact that a real rule of recognition is a social fact accepted by officials rather than a list handed to students on a card.

Do not set either case in Germany between 1933 and 1949, do not use real statutes or real decisions, and do not propose any bibliography: everything the session needs must be in the materials you write.
