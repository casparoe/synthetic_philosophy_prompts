I help run an evening reading group attached to a community library in Leeds, and every spring we stage a short revue of invented documents read aloud on a single theme; this year's theme is Sartre on radical freedom and bad faith. The piece I have been asked to contribute parodies a genre I know uncomfortably well from my day job in personnel: the corporate Performance Improvement Plan. I would like you to write one in full, as a self-contained document of roughly 850 to 950 words, complete with the usual apparatus — a header block giving the employee's name, job title, branch, line manager, date of issue and review date, followed by numbered sections with headings of the customary kind (Statement of Concern, Required Behaviours, Support Available, Review Arrangements, Consequences of Failure to Improve), a sign-off block, and a short appendix listing the logged incidents in bullet form.

The employer is a regional chain of garden centres in the East Midlands, and the employee is a customer assistant on the outdoor plants counter at one of the larger branches. Three incidents have been logged over a single season. In the first, a customer hesitating between two varieties of lavender was told, helpfully, that she was already choosing to stand where she was standing, and that her indecision was a decision she had made and was concealing from herself; she left without buying. In the second, a colleague asked whether he would cover a Saturday shift and he agreed, but wished it recorded that nothing obliged him to agree and that covering shifts was not what he fundamentally is. In the third, at the monthly team briefing, when the regional manager remarked that this was simply how the trade worked, the employee observed that the trade could work otherwise and that everyone present was, each morning, the reason it did not. The plan should be issued by a line manager and an HR business partner who are entirely sincere, entirely reasonable, and entirely convinced that they are helping, and it should be written in impeccable British HR register — "colleague", "line manager", "occupational health referral", "the Business", the reminder that he has the right to be accompanied at any review meeting by a colleague or a trade union representative. The company's stated values should be invented by you, but must include one about bringing one's whole self to work, and the plan should present itself throughout as a faithful, good-humoured application of that value.

The satire has to be aimed at the practice rather than at any person: at the way institutional life requires that the world present itself as a settled, natural order that nobody is responsible for, and at the way managerial discourse converts claims about what a person is into measurable behaviours. Please keep it rigorously deadpan — no authorial winking, no asides, no footnotes decoding the jokes, and nothing in the document may name Sartre, existentialism, philosophy or any philosopher. Do not recycle the two most anthologised illustrations of bad faith, the café waiter and the woman who lets her hand be held, or any beats recognisably derived from them. It must be funny to a reader who has never opened Sartre, and devastating to one who has: a specialist should be able to identify, purely from what the document does, that the plan demands a sincerity the document's own logic makes unattainable; that a ninety-day "sustained improvement" target is a category error about a stance renewed at every instant; that occupational health has misfiled as work-related stress something structurally different from fear of an external cause; that the line manager's repeated appeal to "the process" and to what the Business expects is precisely the manoeuvre the plan is condemning; and that facticity has been turned into a fixed inventory of the employee's strengths and development areas. The Consequences section is the one the reader should recoil from, and it should do so by following the plan's logic to its end rather than by breaking tone — a firm that has removed every alternative and then attributes the outcome wholly and solemnly to the colleague's own free choice. Write the whole thing in British English, spelling and idiom included.
