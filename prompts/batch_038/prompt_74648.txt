I teach an upper-level philosophy of language seminar — 16 students, one 75-minute session — and I want to build a class around Hofstadter's "Person Paper" piece. You know the move: write in the voice of a fastidious stickler for correct usage who defends the traditional generic pronoun and the traditional honorifics and treats anyone who objects as a squeamish masher of grammar, except that every one of his examples is about race instead of gender, so you suddenly feel the sting of conventions you've stopped hearing.

I dont want to run it on race in my actual classroom, though. Too heavy, and it turns whoever in the room belongs to the targeted group into the exhibit. So build the whole thing on an invented social division — you pick it, and tell me why you picked it and what the choice costs you.

What I need: a house style guide, roughly ten rules, each with its own perfectly reasonable-sounding rationale (consistency, brevity, avoiding ambiguity, respect for tradition) and none of them ever stating the point. Hand the rules over one at a time rather than as a printed list, with a beat for objections after each. Plus a short, boring, real-feeling source text for them to copyedit under those rules. Materials, roles if you want them, and a minute-by-minute. The payoff I'm after is that they produce something they'd happily sign off on as clean prose, and only then get asked to transpose the same rule list back onto men and women. I want to say nothing beforehand, and I want Hofstadter's name to land last.

Then the debrief: map each rule onto the convention it stands in for, and onto the philosophical machinery underneath — what a generic term's literal content is even supposed to be versus what it gets used to convey, marked versus unmarked terms, the asymmetry in titles, and how much work the "it's just a convention and conventions are arbitrary" defense can really do.

Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and what I say when it does: someone clocks the pattern in minute five, someone refuses to apply rules they find gross, the exercise collapses into a prescriptivism brawl, that sort of thing. And be straight with me about what an invented group can't reproduce — I suspect the whole thing stays intellectually cold, and I want to know how much of the point survives that.

Last thing, and it matters to me: separate out which of this is settled and which is genuinely contested among people who actually work on it. I don't want to stand in front of sixteen students asserting that the generic masculine pronoun is mentally represented as male, or that pejoration of marked terms is exceptionless, as though those were facts rather than live arguments. But I equally don't want to hand the "it's only grammar" line a free pass as the neutral default. Show me where the fault lines are and flag anywhere I'd be overclaiming if I said it flatly.
