"In The Querist, Berkeley argues that money is not wealth. Explain (a) what he thinks money actually is, (b) where money gets its value and what determines the value of things generally, and (c) why he nevertheless thinks increasing the circulating medium could relieve Irish poverty -- including what, if anything, limits how much a bank may issue."

And here's the student's answer, roughly as submitted. Typos are theirs, and the quotations do check out against the edition we assigned, so dont spend any time on that part:

"Berkeley wrote the Querist as a series of rhetorical questions in the mid-1730s while he was Bishop of Cloyne, and his target is the mercantilist habit of treating a nation's wealth as identical with the gold and silver it holds. His alternative is that wealth is really the produce of industry -- the food, clothing and lodging people actually consume -- and that money is only an instrument for getting that produce moving. He asks 'Whether there be any vertue in gold or silver, other than as they set people at work, or create industry?' and his answer is no: money is 'only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour.' So the true idea of money is 'altogether that of a ticket or counter', a token for reckoning, recording and transferring claims, with nothing valuable in the metal itself. That's why he's happy to go paper: keep the denominations and the bullion could disappear entirely, and things would still be rated, bought and sold and industry still promoted. He also gets close to velocity -- 'less money swiftly circulating' is equivalent in effect to 'more money slowly circulating' -- so what matters is how much circulation happens, not how big the hoard is.

On where the value comes from, Berkeley's answer is labour. A ticket is worth whatever work it lets you command, so the value of things tracks the labour that goes into them; he's a labour theorist of value before Smith, and money is just the receipt for work already done. This also means the ticket is worth nothing apart from what people agree to give it, so the denomination itself is what fixes value: a pound is a pound because the state calls it a pound. On that basis Berkeley proposes a national bank issuing paper credit, and since the notes have no worth except by agreement there's no internal limit on the issue -- the only real constraint is that people keep accepting them, so his argument shades into a confidence theory of paper money. For Ireland this is the whole point. The country isn't poor because it lacks land or labour, it's poor because there aren't enough tickets moving around to set people at work, so multiplying paper credit through a bank would raise industry, and therefore real wealth, with no need for mines."
