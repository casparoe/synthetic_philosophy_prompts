For about twelve years I have run a volunteer peer-support group. It started as a folding-table meeting in a library back room and is now mostly a set of chat channels, with roughly two hundred regulars and a waiting list for the in-person evenings. Almost everyone who comes to us is an adult who has spent most of their life being characterized by other people as lazy, careless, unreliable, or not trying hard enough. Somewhere around the middle of those twelve years, a particular label for adult attention difficulties became common talk outside clinics, and the way our members described themselves changed in a way I did not anticipate.

What I saw was this. In the early years people arrived with scattered complaints in borrowed language: lost keys, missed appointments, a stomach ache on Sunday evenings, a run of jobs they had been let go from. Later, people arrive already fluent in a checklist, and they volunteer items that nobody in the earlier group ever mentioned, because nobody had ever given them the words. Long-standing members begin reporting difficulties that did not appear in anything they said for years. Some of those members say the difficulties are genuinely new. Others say, after reflection, that they had always been there and simply had no name. I cannot reliably tell the two apart, and I have stopped trying.

From this I built an argument, and it now seems to me to reach much further than I can follow it.

1. A description of a person is accurate only if it would have been accurate whether or not that person ever heard it.
2. A psychiatric diagnosis is a description of a person that is, as a matter of routine, delivered to that person; the delivery is part of what the diagnosis is for, not an accident that befalls it.
3. Delivery changes the person described: once my members have the label, they report the label's items, they redescribe old difficulties in the label's vocabulary, and some of them acquire difficulties they are confident are new.
4. So a psychiatric diagnosis is not accurate of the person it is delivered to, since the act of delivering it alters what it was meant to track.
5. Nothing about my group is peculiar in this respect. Whatever follows from a diagnosis reshaping the person it is delivered to follows for every diagnosis, because every diagnosis is delivered.
6. So no psychiatric diagnosis is accurate of anyone.
7. So a group whose entire purpose is to help people describe themselves truly should stop handing out these labels, and should probably not exist in its present form, because the labels are the reason people find us.

The same route appears to reach a conclusion I have direct evidence against. It would seem to show that the single most useful word I know in this whole area, the one that members of twelve-step fellowships apply to themselves out loud in front of strangers, is never accurate of anyone, on the grounds that it is delivered to the person it describes and reshapes that person more thoroughly than anything a clinic manages. I have watched that word hold a family together. Either the argument is unsound or my own eyes are unreliable about the thing I know best, and I would rather not accept the second.

So: which step do you reject? I would like you to say what that step was doing that made it look safe, and whether it is acceptable on one reading of its words while the next step requires a different reading. Then price the rejection for me. If the step goes, what am I no longer entitled to believe about description in general, and what am I supposed to say on a Saturday evening when someone arrives and asks me what they have? Finally, tell me whether a weakened version of the chain survives, and if so whether its conclusion is worth having. Of the seven, the one I am least willing to surrender is (1); it is the reason I ever thought that describing people accurately was a real activity rather than a form of decoration, and I have built twelve years of practice on it.
