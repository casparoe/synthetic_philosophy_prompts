At the end of next month I'm giving the closing talk at a three-day workshop on the philosophy of set theory. The room is small and technical: three faculty who actually work in set theory — one a forcing specialist and an unapologetic universist, one who defends a pluralist or multiverse line but understands it as a thesis about set-theoretic practice and truth-ascriptions rather than about what exists, and one who is broadly sceptical that there is any philosophy of set theory to do and who measures a talk by whether it would change how a working set theorist spends her afternoon — plus roughly a dozen graduate students, half in logic and half in philosophy, and one philosopher of language who has been telling people for some years that Putnam's model-theoretic argument was decisively answered in the 1980s and who is looking forward to saying so again in person. My slot is thirty minutes of talk and twenty of questions, with no anonymous feedback form afterwards; whatever happens will happen out loud and by name.

The thesis I'm defending is that the deflationary resolution of Skolem's paradox is correct — the countable model contains no bijection with ω for the sets it calls uncountable, so nothing has gone wrong except the expectation that a first-order theory should pin down a structure up to isomorphism — but that once you accept it for the right reasons it does not leave metaphysical realism intact, and instead commits you to something like height-relativism about the set-theoretic universe. My argument, in the form it will appear on the board:

1. Begin not with the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem but with forcing. For any infinite cardinal κ of V there is a forcing extension V[G] — same ordinals, still a model of ZFC — in which κ is countable. So the failure of "countable" to be absolute is not an artefact of selecting a countable model from the outside and squinting at it; it is exhibited by extensions that set theorists themselves routinely treat as legitimate objects of study.
2. The standard reply is that V and V[G] are different universes, that only V is real, and that nothing about V's cardinals has changed. That reply is available only if "the universe" denotes a single determinate totality we are somehow already standing outside of — but any characterisation of V that we can actually write down is itself a first-order or second-order description and is exposed to the same non-absoluteness, so the reply is not a resolution but a promissory note.
3. Retreating to second-order ZFC does not help. Under Henkin semantics the theory is a two-sorted first-order theory in disguise and Löwenheim–Skolem applies exactly as before. Under full semantics determinacy is restored — Zermelo's quasi-categoricity result leaves models related as rank-initial segments of one another — but only by making the semantics parasitic on a background universe that fixes what "all subsets of x" ranges over. The indeterminacy has been relocated into the meta-theory, and step 2 then applies one level up.
4. Therefore the lesson of Skolem's 1922 address is not that reference is indeterminate — I think Putnam's way of pressing the point fails, and I say so explicitly — but that there is no perspective-independent answer to how many sets there are, and that this is a claim about the subject matter of set theory rather than about our language for it.
