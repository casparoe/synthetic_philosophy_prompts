So I'm on the little commitee redrafting our firm's parental leave and childcare package — about 600 people, mostly salaried, in a country where there's a statutory scheme we top up. Reading the current rules end to end, I keep getting the feeling that they add up to some definite view of what a family is and what a worker is, a view that basically nobody on the committee would endorse if you asked them to say it out loud. I can't state what it is, though, and I'd like help reconstructing it.

Here's the design as it stands:

- 20 weeks of paid leave per birth or adoption, paid at 90% of that parent's own prior salary, subject to a cap that roughly a third of our staff hit.
- Each parent individually gets 8 weeks that are theirs alone and simply vanish if unused. The remaining 4 weeks are a shared pool the couple can split however they want.
- The leave has to be taken in one continuous block within 12 months. Coming back at 60% time for a couple of months exists on paper but needs manager sign-off, and in practise almost nobody asks.
- You have to be a regular employee on the date of birth. Someone on a fixed-term contract that ends in month four gets nothing; contractors get nothing.
- Separately, we subsidize 30 slots at a daycare down the street. Slots are allocated by length of service, so a person who joined two years ago and just had a baby queues behind a person who's been here 14 years and has no kids.

Officially we describe all of this as gender neutral — identical rules and identical weeks, and the words mother and father appear nowhere.

What I'm after: what theory of the family, of care, and of the employee does this package presuppose? Some specific things I'm stuck on:

1. Why index the payment to your own earnings at all? Is leave here a wage-replacement insurance benefit against interrupted earnings, or is it pay for care labor? The rules seem to hedge, and the answer changes who the policy is really about.
2. If it's earnings-replacement, then two parents doing the same weeks of childcare get paid different amounts for it. What does that presuppose about why we're paying, and about whether care counts as work?
3. What view of the household does the "use it or lose it" quota need? It seems to assume the point is to move behavior — get the second parent to actually take time — rather than to serve the child. Is that a claim about the employer's or the state's legitimate interest in how families divide things up?
4. The continuous-block requirement treats care as a one-off transition. The seniority queue for daycare treats a childcare slot as a reward for service to the firm rather than something indexed to a child's need. Those two seem to point in opposite directions — is one of them just an administrative accident, or do they rest on inconsistent ideas about whose need the package exists to meet?
5. Is calling it gender neutral coherent, given that household-income arithmetic plus the block rule predictably push the lower earner into taking nearly all of it? Which notion of neutrality does the policy assume is the right target — same rules, or same outcomes?
6. Which rival views does the design quietly rule out: children as a public good that everyone has a stake in, care as labor owed its own wage, the household rather than the individual as the unit of distribution? For each, tell me concretely what would have to change — flat rate per week regardless of salary, fully transferable weeks, care time counting toward vesting and pension accrual, daycare slots allotted by the child's situation rather than the parent's tenure.
7. Where is all this philosophically underdetermined? I want to know which rules are actually compatible with several quite different theories, so I don't walk into the meeting and claim our rules imply something they don't strictly imply.

One of my colleagues insists the whole thing is just an insurance product and that bolting a childcare subsidy onto it is a category mistake. Is there a principled line between the two, or does taking the insurance reading seriously already commit you to something about who counts as a parent and what a parent is for?
