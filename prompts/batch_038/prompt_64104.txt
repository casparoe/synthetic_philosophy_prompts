The question we set was:

"Plato seems to change his mind about drink. In the Republic drunkenness is no fit state for a guardian, and elsewhere intoxication is treated as a mark of the worst kind of soul; but in the Laws the Athenian Stranger writes supervised drinking parties into the constitution of the new colony. Explain the positive case the Laws makes for regulated wine-drinking, and say whether it can be reconciled with the Republic's attitude or whether one of the two has to give."

500 words, primary texts only, no footnotes expected, no secondary reading was assigned, and we told them they don't need to hunt down Stephanus numbers. Here's what came in:

---

Plato's two political works take opposite attitudes to wine, and the difference is not just a change of taste in old age but a change of argument. In the Republic the guardians must be kept away from drunkenness, because a guardian who cannot hold himself together cannot be trusted with the city; Socrates there describes how sleep and drink stir up the appetites that lurk even in respectable men, the beast within that reason keeps drowsy, and the man who gives himself over to drink is already on the road to the tyrannical character. The Laws accepts the danger and draws the opposite conclusion. Rather than removing wine, the Athenian wants to administer it under supervision.

The core of the argument is the thought experiment of the fear-potion. The Athenian asks us to suppose that some god had given men a draught that made them afraid of everything; even the bravest man would lose his nerve for as long as it lasted, and recover himself once he had slept it off. Nobody, he says, would deny that such a potion used as a touchstone would be worth having: you could test a citizen's courage without exposing him to any real danger, and train him in small repeated doses. The Athenian then points out that wine is in fact just such a potion. It is cheap, its effects pass off, and it reveals a man's nature reliably, so the legislator should use the symposium as a testing ground: give the citizens wine, see which of them hold up and which turn shameless, and honour or punish accordingly. Because the training is graduated by age — nothing at all before eighteen, moderation with no intoxication between eighteen and thirty, and full participation in the common meals only after forty — a citizen spends his whole life building up a resistance, so that by the time he is a magistrate the drink has lost its power over him. On this reading the colony inoculates its citizens against pleasure in just the way Sparta inoculated its young against pain, which is the one thing the Athenian credits the Spartan and Cretan lawgivers with having done well. The virtue being trained is still courage: a man who has practised resisting the cup will also stand firm in the line.

There is a second and softer benefit as well. Wine loosens the tongues of old men who would otherwise be too dignified to sing in the choruses, and drinking together produces friendship among the citizens. These are real gains but they are incidental to the main point, which is the test and the training.

So the two dialogues can be reconciled. Both agree that drunkenness is dangerous and that most men carry bad desires in them; the Laws' image of the soul as a puppet pulled by a golden cord of reason and iron cords of pleasure and pain is simply the Republic's tripartite soul in different dress. What differs is the remedy, and the difference is explained by institutional capacity. The Republic removes the temptation because it cannot supervise it — the guardians live communally and drink in the open, with no machinery to watch them — whereas the Laws, which has magistrates, common meals and a sober president over every drinking party, can supervise and therefore use wine as an instrument of education. Plato did not change his mind about the soul, only about what a lawgiver can realistically control.

---
