I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and in three weeks I am giving a forty-minute talk at a workshop on history and development held at a public-policy school. The room will be about thirty people: applied development economists and economic historians who work in instrumental variables, colonial-border discontinuities, and long-run persistence regressions; a few political scientists who code regime datasets for a living; and one economic anthropologist who is likely to be my only natural ally. None of them reads my magazine as a source of ideas, and several will have come specifically to catch a journalist garbling their field. The 2024 prize in economic sciences went to the comparative-prosperity program I am about to question, which is precisely why I think the piece is worth writing, and precisely why the room will be primed.

My argument has three moves, and I want the objections aimed at them rather than at a straw version.

First, that institutions as this literature measures them — constraints on executives, security of property rights, bureaucratic quality, and the composite indices built out of them — is not a variable but a label for a bundle, and a causal claim about a bundle is informative only if the bundle can come apart. I want to press a specific test: can anyone name a component of the package that has been built in a low-income setting without the rest arriving alongside it, or without nothing arriving at all?

Second, that the two identification strategies everyone cites do not break the tie between institutions and their rivals. The settler-mortality instrument was designed to hold geography fixed, but what it actually varies is the demography of European settlement, which was itself a function of the disease environment — so instrument and geography hypothesis share a common cause rather than adjudicating between them. And the divided-city and divided-country comparisons hold physical geography fixed only at the scale of the fence; the reason the fence runs where it does was settled by wars and treaties whose causes are the very thing under dispute, which pushes the causal question back a step instead of answering it. Relatedly, the reversal of fortune — the places that were prosperous in 1500 being poor today — is routinely read as evidence for institutions, but it is equally the signature one would expect if extractive arrangements had been chosen because geography made extraction pay.

Third, that the estimand itself is out of reach. The claim is about a society's trajectory across a century or more, each country received exactly one draw, and there is no sense in which these estimates average over replications of a treatment.

My conclusion is deliberately modest: not that institutions don't matter, but that the sentence "institutions cause prosperity" has been carrying weight it cannot support, and that the live question is separability — whether any part of the package can be built without the rest.

Give me a prep sheet of roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words, numbered in descending order of damage. For each entry: the objection in two or three sentences, phrased the way these people would phrase it — including the polite, concessive register, and including at least one that arrives disguised as a compliment; one sentence I should concede immediately; a reply that fits in ninety seconds; and a single line saying where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections are substantive and which are a research program defending sunk cost, and flag separately the one that, if it lands, kills the piece rather than merely embarrassing me.

They will come at me with specific results, so tell me for each whether I have to answer it or can distinguish it: the settler-mortality work and its robustness checks against geographic controls; the legal-origins tradition comparing common-law and civil-law systems; studies comparing outcomes on either side of arbitrarily drawn colonial borders in Africa; work on forced-labor mining regimes in the Andes and their present-day traces; persistence estimates tied to the Atlantic and Indian Ocean slave trades; the two divided-Korea and divided-Germany cases; and Botswana as the existence proof that institutions can transform without geography changing. Where I cannot distinguish one, say so plainly rather than handing me a bluff.

Two charges I already expect and want ranked among the rest: that I am converting an empirical hypothesis into a quarrel about definitions, and that non-identifiability is not non-existence — an effect can be real even where no design can reach it.

Then three closing items. What I should soften or drop from the thesis before I walk in, because it is indefensible and will cost me the room for nothing. How I should open the first five minutes so that the frame is not a generalist telling specialists they are confused. And the question I am hoping nobody asks: your guess at what it is, why it is the dangerous one, and what my fallback is if it comes anyway.

Please do not give me a bibliography, a literature review, or any citations; I will add references myself. Refer to designs and findings by description, as I have above. And spare me the familiar geography-versus-culture-versus-institutions summary — I know the truisms. What I need is what people who actually run these regressions will say.
