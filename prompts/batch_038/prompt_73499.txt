Writing a policy piece on constitutional routes to independence and one of my coauthors wont let go of the divorce analogy. Her argument runs: marriage law moved from fault-based divorce (you have to prove adultery or cruelty) to no-fault, and that was obviously progress; the secession debate is stuck in the fault era, because the "remedial right only" crowd want a region to document oppression before it's allowed to leave, which is about as archaic as making a wife prove cruelty. So a clear majority on a clear question should trigger a legal duty to negotiate terms, and nothing more is needed. A second colleague says the same analogy supports him instead, since even a no-fault divorce still requires division of assets, support arrangements and custody deals -- so a bare referendum gets you precisely nothing and the real work is all in the terms.

Can you audit this thing? I want the correspondence table: who plays the spouses, the children, the house, the alimony, the court, the marriage itself, the period of separation before you're allowed to file. Then the disanalogies, but sorted -- which ones actually bear on her conclusion and which are cosmetic. In particular, what does the analogy quietly import that secession doesn't have: two already-separate persons with independent standing? an umpire that survives the split? assets that can be sold and divided rather than a homeland that can't? And what happens to the people inside the seceding territory who don't want the divorce -- the analogy makes them children in a custody fight, which feels like a category error, and I want to know what category they actually belong in.

Finally: is there a better analogy, and does it do more work? I've been toying with a corporate demerger, or a town de-annexing itself from a county, or maybe a tenant breaking a lease. Tell me which of those survives contact with the hard cases, and whether my coauthor's conclusion survives the audit at all or whether the analogy was carrying it the whole time.
