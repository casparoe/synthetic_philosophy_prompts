I write a weekly ideas column for a general-interest magazine — minds, science, AI, whatever's in the water. I've got a piece coming up on why the "rationality just means coherence" line keeps turning up in AI-safety and econ writing, and I need to pressure-test a metaphor before it goes to print.

The setup: I interviewed a guy who works on AI alignment, and when I asked why anyone outside philosophy departments should care that real agents violate the axioms of expected utility, he said something to the effect that an incoherent agent is a walking leak — like a man with a hole in his pocket, or a gambler who hands the bookmaker a sure loss. His point was that it doesn't matter what your reasons are: the world finds the leak and drains you, trade by trade, until you're broke or gone, so coherence isn't a taste, it's a survival requirement. I ran that past an economist friend who said it was mostly bluff — that half the axiom violations people get excited about dont cost you a cent, and that the metaphor is doing all the work the argument can't.

Your job: play a decision theorist who's actually spent time in the coherence-argument literature (representation theorems, Dutch books, money pumps, Savage and where he thought his own framework stopped applying) but who can talk to smart non-specialists without talking down to them. Think carefully, and start with some brainstorming before you write anything polished — just dump every way the leaky-pocket picture could fail that occurs to you, then sort the pile into the failures that matter and the ones that are only verbal.

Then I need:

1. The correspondence, laid out plainly. What plays the pocket, the hole, the stuff draining out, and the party who notices the leak and exploits it? A little table is fine.
2. What the picture quietly imports from the pocket-and-gambling world that the target case doesn't have — a single universal currency, a counterparty who is always offering trades, costless reflection, the assumption that what leaks is something the agent would rather keep, the idea that the environment is adversarial rather than indifferent. Tell me which of those imports actually breaks the argument and which are just decoration.
3. My economist friend's claim. Which kinds of violation expose an agent to something like a sure loss and which don't, and why? Two half-remembered things I want unpacked: someone told me there's supposed to be a real difference between being handed a package of bets all at once and being walked around a circle one trade at a time, and separately that Savage thought his setup only applied to what he called small worlds. Didnt follow either. Say whether they change the verdict.
4. Whether the sentence I'd actually print survives — roughly: anything that manages to persist over time will behave as if it maximizes expected utility, or it gets pumped out of the game. If it survives, on what narrower footing, and what has to be added to make it honest. If it doesn't, give me the strongest true claim in the neighborhood that fits in one sentence.
5. A better image I could use in its place — or a straight answer that there isn't one and I should drop the metaphor and just say the thing flatly.

No citations or reading list, please, I'll chase sources myself if I end up using any of this. What I need is the reasoning visible enough that I can tell whether the quote was fair to the guy who gave it to me.
