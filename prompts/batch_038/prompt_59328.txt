I'm a commercial litigator, and contract doctrine is my native language: offer and acceptance, consideration and its nominal substitutes, promissory estoppel, conditions and gap-fillers, expectation versus reliance damages, voidability for incapacity, duress, mistake and impracticability, formalities such as seals and the statute of frauds, and the standing of third-party beneficiaries. Restate the philosophical debate over why promises bind in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can, taking the main accounts in turn: Hume's line that no bare act of the will can create an obligation and that promising binds only as a feature of a human convention; the practice conception, on which the duty arises from invoking a constitutive social practice; the expectation-based account, on which the wrong consists in deliberately leading someone to plan on your performance and then letting them down; and the view that a promise transfers to the promisee a right you previously held, including the right to release you. For each, name the doctrine that comes closest to carrying it, and say where that doctrine's machinery resists the philosophical claim.

Then give me the residue report in three lists: what translates cleanly; what you could render only by distorting the philosophy, the law, or both; and what has no expression at all in my vocabulary. Address the asymmetry head-on — consideration tells me a gratuitous promise is unenforceable, while a seal or a peppercorn makes binding something morality may never have made binding. Does that divergence show lawyers holding a concept of obligation the philosophers lack, or does it show that the philosophers' "bindingness" is something my vocabulary was never built to express? Close by naming the residual item you judge most damaging, and say whether it counts against the account that cannot be translated or against the framework that cannot translate it.
