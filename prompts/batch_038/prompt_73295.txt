I am a third-year doctoral student in philosophy, and in three weeks I am giving a departmental talk on the limits of expected-value reasoning. A friend on my programme helped me draft the argument below, which was meant to show that expected-value fanaticism — call it the view that you ought always to take the available act with the highest expected value, however small the probability that carries the payoff — refutes itself. The difficulty is that on reflection it appears to prove a good deal more than we wanted. I do not wish to present it until I know where the leak is.

Here is the argument as it currently stands.

(1) A decision principle is correct only if an agent who follows it cannot be made predictably worse off, by following it, than she would have been by following some alternative that was available to her.
(2) Expected-value fanaticism says: among the acts open to you, take the one with the highest expected value, whatever the shape of the probability distribution behind it.
(3) In a Pascal's mugging, a stranger demands a modest payment and promises an astronomically large reward with an astronomically small probability of delivery; the reward multiplied by that probability still exceeds the payment, so the fanatic hands over the money.
(4) Each encounter can be arranged so that the promised reward rises and the probability falls together, leaving the product in favour of paying; a fanatic who keeps meeting such strangers therefore pays again and again.
(5) So the fanatic is made worse off, predictably, than she would have been by refusing — worse off, indeed, in all but a vanishing proportion of branches.
(6) By (1), expected-value fanaticism is not a correct decision principle.
(7) And since (1) states a condition any decision principle must meet, the same reasoning shows that no principle may be sensitive to the far tail at all, however heavily it discounts it.

It is (7) that frightens me. Read so as to convict the fanatic, (1) appears also to convict anyone who funds a research programme, buys a long-shot option, or spends a decade on a conjecture with a small chance of a very large payoff: in each of those cases the safer alternative was better in all but a vanishing proportion of branches, and we do not think those people have made a mistake of principle. Read so as to spare them, (1) seems to spare the fanatic too, since the branch in which the mugger actually pays is precisely the branch in which paying wins. My suspicion is that "predictably", in (1) and again in (5), is doing more work than we noticed, but I cannot tell which of the things it might mean the argument actually needs, nor whether the failure sits inside (1), in the move from (4) to (5), or in the move from (6) to (7).

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me which step I should reject — pick one, say why it is the load-bearing failure rather than merely an available one, and explain why that step looked perfectly acceptable to us when we wrote it down. Then price the rejection: what is given up along with it, and what is left of the argument against fanaticism once the price has been paid? In particular, tell me whether (7) can be blocked without also blocking (6), and whether there is a repaired argument that starts from the same case and arrives only at the falsity of fanaticism rather than at the falsity of tail-sensitivity as such. For what it is worth, I would much rather surrender (7) than (1), because (1) is the only reason I have for thinking that decision principles can be incorrect at all rather than merely unfashionable; but I am not attached to that preference and I would like to be told if it is what is forcing the problem. If your honest view is that the argument cannot be repaired and should come out of the talk altogether, say so plainly and tell me what I ought to put in its place that will still support a talk about where expected-value reasoning runs out.

Please be committed throughout: take a definite position on each question, give your reasons for it, and where a step is genuinely debatable tell me which side of it you are on rather than leaving the options weighed against one another for me to choose between. Write the answer in American English, as the handout will go to a largely American audience, even though I have drafted this note in my own idiom. Do not append a bibliography, a list of further reading, or citations of any kind; I am after the diagnosis itself.
