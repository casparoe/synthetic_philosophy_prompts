I want a number, and the arithmetic behind it, on a version of Molyneux's problem that I think is sharper than the one usually debated.

First, the facts I propose we fix so that we are not arguing about them. Take a person blind from birth who knows cubes and spheres well by touch, and who acquires sight in late childhood or adulthood after the removal of congenital cataracts. The relevant clinical work reports, in broad terms: for the first days after the eyes are opened, such patients cannot name or recognize ordinary objects by sight; their spontaneous descriptions are of colored expanses without boundaries, without depth, and without evident connection to the things they know by handling; and when asked which of two solids in front of them is the cube and which the sphere, they do not perform better than guessing. Over the following days and weeks performance improves substantially, and it improves fastest when the patient is permitted to move, to look while handling the object, and to be given structured practice. Treat all of that as granted.

Note what is granted and what is not. The negative answer to Molyneux's question is, on this stipulation, no longer in dispute. What I want your credence on is the explanation of it.

The proposition: the failure occurs for the reason given by those who first posed and answered the question — that the ideas of shape the patient possesses are tactile ideas, that sight supplies a wholly new and initially uninterpreted set of appearances, and that the correspondence between the two is established only by accumulated experience — rather than for the reasons a contemporary vision scientist would give, such as that prolonged deprivation leaves the visual system unable to segment figure from ground, to recover three-dimensional form from shading and contour, or to bind color, texture, and motion into a single object.

In short: is the classical empiricist mechanism the true mechanism of the failure, or a correct prediction resting on a wrong account of why the prediction comes out true?

Please decompose rather than assert. Specifically:

(a) List the candidate explanations you take to be genuinely in competition, including any I have not named. At minimum consider the association-of-distinct-ideas account; the low-level visual deficit account; the account on which what is missing is a learned coupling between movement and what is seen, so that no amount of passive looking would suffice; and Reid's suggestion that two-dimensional figures might be recognized at once while solid ones could not, since the visible figure of a cube is a flat projection bearing no resemblance to the object felt.

(b) Assign each a rough probability that it is the operative explanation of the observed failure, with one sentence saying why.

(c) Give a separate probability for the prior question of whether these candidates are rivals at all. An explanation framed in terms of ideas and one framed in terms of cortical processing may be descriptions at different levels that cannot conflict, in which case my proposition is not false but ill-posed. I want to know how much of your final number is really a verdict on that.

(d) Show how (a) through (c) combine into your number for the proposition as stated.

(e) Identify the term your number is most sensitive to, and name the single observation, argument, or experiment that would move it furthest — including, if it applies, a result that would show the question cannot be settled by patients of this kind at all.

For calibration: a colleague puts the proposition at roughly 0.7 and I put it at roughly 0.25, and neither of us can say what the other is denying. Tell us which term in your decomposition most plausibly accounts for that gap, and whether one of us is answering a different question from the one asked.

References are not wanted. I want the reasoning laid bare, not the literature.
