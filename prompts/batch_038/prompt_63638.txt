You're a philosopher of mind who also happens to write decent idea-driven SF — think the cerebral end of the genre rather than the explosions end. I want a short story, call it 2,000–2,500 words, that dramatizes Putnam's old triviality worry about computationalism: if you're free to map physical states onto computational states however you like, then every ordinary open system — a rock, a cooling cup of coffee, a city's traffic grid — realizes every finite-state automaton, and the claim that the brain is a computer stops carrying any content at all. The standard repair is that a genuine implementation has to respect the causal interface: which state transitions are actually triggered by which inputs, and which ones actually go on to produce which outputs. A gerrymandered mapping doesn't have to answer for any of that, which is why it can be conjured up for anything.

Setting I'd like you to run with: a near-future licensing office that issues "instantiation certificates." When someone dies and a preserved mind-file has to be run on some hardware, the hardware has to be certified as really executing that file rather than merely being describable as doing so — the certificate is what makes the person legally alive again. Your protagonist is a tired auditor who has denied hundreds of these and is very good at spotting a cooked mapping. One day a claimant walks in with something genuinely ridiculous as the substrate — your pick, but make it a physical object that is clearly not a computer in any intuitive sense, and make the mapping document technically flawless. Then the turn: the auditor discovers that the stricter interface standard she has built her career on also fails for someone she certified years ago and loves.

Before writing, spend a short paragraph brainstorming out loud: what's the smallest possible tampering that converts an honest implementation into a fake one, what physical evidence would betray that tampering, and how does an auditor who can't run the system get at it? Then write the story. Keep the philosophy in the situations, the paperwork, and the arguments between characters — no lecture passages — and have the ending commit to an answer about whether implementation is a real feature of the world or just a way of describing it, rather than shrugging.
