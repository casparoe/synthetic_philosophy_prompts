I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar of twelve students, and last night I spent four hours arguing with a friend about Kripke's dogmatism paradox without either of us moving an inch. I have decided the disagreement is better spent on the final than on us, so I want to hand it to students who encounter it cold.

The background, so that you know what the question has to do. My friend is a clinical epidemiologist. Her view is that the dogmatist inference is not a paradox but a description of sound method: once you know that a treatment works, setting aside a small, badly powered trial that appears to contradict you is discipline rather than closed-mindedness, and any rule forbidding it would oblige you to reopen every settled question at every contrary appearance, which would make knowledge unusable. My reply was that her case only looks like the paradox because her knowledge is grounded in a body of evidence entirely separate from the trial she proposes to dismiss. Where the knowledge rests on nothing but the very evidence now under suspicion, dismissing the evidence dismantles the knowledge, and the permission she claims evaporates. She said I was just restating the problem. I could not show her otherwise, and that is exactly the crux I want examined.

Here is the material I want the question built out of. Please treat both of the following as stipulated by me and stated inside the question stem itself, so that nothing outside the stem is needed to answer it.

The first is the response my friend was making, formalized. Call it the Independence Response: it is permissible for S to disregard a piece of evidence E that suggests not-p only if S's knowledge that p does not depend on E, where dependence is understood counterfactually — had E been misleading, S would not have known p.

The second is the case I think defeats it. Call it the Cumulative Case. A research programme knows that p on the strength of a large body of results. Each individual anomalous result is dispensable: the knowledge rests on the body, not on any one study, so dismissing any single one satisfies the Independence Response. Yet the programme dismisses a hundred of them in sequence, each dismissal locally licensed, and is left claiming to know p on a body it has itself dismantled. The response is thus piecewise sound and globally self-defeating.

What I want from you is a single essay question on this material, with the following specifications.

It must be answerable in three hours, by hand, and worth thirty marks. The stem must restate the paradox compactly in its own terms — knowledge that p, knowledge that if p then contrary evidence misleads, closure, and the step from what one knows to what one may disregard — so that a student who has forgotten the original presentation is not thereby penalized. It must state the Independence Response and the Cumulative Case explicitly, with the counterfactual clause spelled out. It must not be answerable by reproducing the paradox, cataloguing the familiar blocking moves, or narrating the debate; those should be worth nothing. The real task should be to decide whether the Cumulative Case refutes the Independence Response or merely shows it has been scoped wrongly, and then to say what a scope fix would cost. In particular, I want students forced to confront the possibility that indexing independence to the total body of evidence rather than to individual pieces restores the permission globally only by regenerating the paradox at the second order, since an agent who knows p thereby knows that her total evidence supports p. Finally, the question should ask them to say which premise has to give — closure, the move from knowing to being permitted to disregard, or the thought that knowledge licenses a standing policy rather than a disposition — and to say why the epidemiologist's original trial is either a counterexample to the paradox or an instance of it. Do not ask for citations or for engagement with any named literature; my students have read the standard responses, but the question should stand on its own.

Then supply a model answer of roughly nine hundred words that a strong student could plausibly write in the time available, taking a definite position rather than surveying options.

Then supply the marking scheme, out of the same thirty marks. I want five or six named moves, each with the marks it carries, each described in terms of what the student must actually do rather than what topic she must mention. State explicitly what a first-class answer does that a solid upper-second does not — my expectation is that the difference lies in whether the student notices that the scope fix reimports the paradox rather than merely observing that the Cumulative Case is a sorites-shaped problem, but tell me if you think the line should be drawn elsewhere. For each move, add one sentence naming the way students most commonly botch it and how many marks that failure costs. Close with three brief notes on answers that look sophisticated but should be marked down, for instance a student who simply denies closure to make the trouble disappear, or who treats the paradox as a mere artefact of idealization without saying which idealization does the work.
