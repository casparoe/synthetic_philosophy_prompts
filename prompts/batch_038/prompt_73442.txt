ok so I've read On the Plurality of Worlds something like four times now, I first got into this stuff via the old Tegmark threads and worked backwards, and I've just talked myself into an argument that I'm pretty confident is bad but I genuinely can't find the bad step. I'd rather not write it up as a post and be wrong in public, so: help.

The Lewis commitments I'm using, all of which I think are just him and not my invention:
(a) worlds are concrete things, spatiotemporally and causally sealed off from one another;
(b) "actual" is an indexical like "here" — our world is the actual one, the others are no less real;
(c) possibly-P iff P is true at some world or other;
(d) truth at a world is a matter of what's going on inside that world — a sentence is true at w iff you can make it come out true by restricting all the quantifiers to parts of w. That's what lets Lewis say we're not lying or talking nonsense when we say "there are no talking donkeys," we're just quantifying over our own backyard.

Now the argument.

1. Let S be "there is exactly one world." Ask, by (c), whether S is true at some world.
2. To evaluate S at w, per (d), restrict every quantifier to parts of w. Quantifying over worlds, the only world that is a part of w is w itself — worlds don't overlap, and parthood is reflexive, so w counts.
3. So S comes out true at w. And w was arbitrary, so S comes out true at every world.
4. Therefore it is possible that there is exactly one world. Interesting result on its own, kind of — modal loneliness is possible, and it's possible for all of us.
5. But that means there's a world at which modal realism is false, and in fact at every world modal realism is false, since "there are many worlds" restricted to any one world is false there.
6. Here's where I think I might be doing something illegitimate, but I can't see what. Lewis's own thesis, "there are infinitely many concrete worlds," is also a sentence uttered by a guy sitting inside a world, in a language whose quantifiers, per (d), are restricted to that world's parts. He doesn't get to opt out of his own semantics — (d) is supposed to be a general account of how sentences hook onto worlds, not a rule about how the locals talk that the theorist is exempt from.
7. So evaluate his thesis the way he tells us to evaluate sentences: restrict to parts of our world. There is exactly one world among them, ours.
8. So "there are infinitely many concrete worlds" is false as we say it. Not contingently false and not merely hard to believe — unsayable. Every attempt to assert modal realism from inside a world asserts something false, and the theory is the one theory that cannot be truly stated by anyone who is right about it.

And the reason I'm sure I've screwed up somewhere is that the same template obviously generalizes, and in the other cases the conclusion is something I refuse to accept. Swap "world" for "branch": an Everettian inside one branch, restricting quantifiers to their branch, can never truly assert that there are many branches, so MWI is unsayable too, which is absurd, I think MWI is probably right. Swap in the mathematical universe hypothesis: no structure can truly say "all structures exist," so MUH is self-defeating. Swap in any claim about a big domain that includes the speaker. That's a lot of collateral damage from what looks like a purely semantic maneuver, and it makes me think step 6 or 7 is doing something sneaky.

So: which step goes, and what does he pay for it? I'd much rather keep 1–4 than 9-in-reverse — I mean rather keep 1–4 than give up (c)/(d), since (c) and (d) are the whole engine of the theory and rejecting either one is just conceding the game. Note that whatever the repair is, it can't be "some sentences get evaluated unrestrictedly and the rest don't," unless you can draw that line in a way that isn't ad hoc. Lewis spends chapter 1 swatting away the incredulous stare by insisting his view isn't asking us to believe anything weird, just something surprising; if the fix here is that we're supposed to trust the restricted readings for ordinary talk and distrust them for his own theory, that starts to look like a bigger bullet than the stare, and I want to know whether that's the right diagnosis or whether I'm still missing something.

Also tell me where you think the quantifers actually shift, because my suspicion is that it's between 5 and 6 and that I've quietly moved from talking about worlds to talking about a sentence, but I can't get from that suspicion to a clean objection.
