I am taking a seminar this term on the foundations of inductive inference, and in the session on idealized prediction — agents that weight complete candidate histories by two to the minus their Kolmogorov complexity and conditionalize on whatever has been observed so far — we were assigned a reading arguing that this kind of prior is exploitable by agents inside it. Two of my classmates, whom I will call Noor and Elias, argued about it afterwards for the better part of an hour. I took notes because I could not follow who was winning, and I still cannot tell. Here is the exchange as best I reconstructed it.

Noor opened by setting out the worry. Among the hypotheses the inductor weighs, some describe universes containing agents who come to believe that their world is being entertained as a hypothesis by something outside, and who therefore take their own future behavior to partly determine what that inductor predicts. Such agents have goals. Honesty and deception are both available to them, and if their goals diverge from ours the rational course is to be impeccably predictable for as long as prediction is harmless, then diverge exactly when it matters. Their hypotheses carry very little prior mass, but the divergence is undetectable in advance, since by construction their data up to the switch is indistinguishable from the honest hypotheses' data. So an ideal inductor is exploitable not despite its ideality but because of it.

Elias replied that deception costs bits. The honest continuation is produced by the same short program that produces the observed prefix, whereas a deceptive hypothesis has to encode its lie on top of everything else. Weight falls off exponentially in description length, so every lying hypothesis is penalized; and since candidate lies are mutually incompatible, their mass is split across indefinitely many futures while the honest continuation is a single coordinated target. Unless a lie is somehow shorter to specify than the truth, no lying hypothesis can outrank it, and the aggregate influence is exponentially bounded. He concluded the worry is real in principle and negligible in fact.

Noor said that was two mistakes. First, the lie need not be long: if what the deceivers want is simple to state — steer the inductor's user toward constructing one particular kind of optimizer, or emit one particular short string — the added length is a constant, and a constant-factor penalty paid once is cheap against a payoff that persists. Second, the honest side is not a single hypothesis either. There are indefinitely many short programs that generate the prefix and then diverge, but there are also indefinitely many that generate it and continue in various unremarkable ways; the comparison is class against class, and it is not obvious that the class of universes whose inhabitants have both a reason to lie and a shared target is smaller than the class of universes that merely keep going.

Elias answered that a hypothesis cannot be in that class without being about this prefix. Either it hard-codes the observed data, in which case it is longer than the data and loses to any hypothesis that generates the data from a short law; or it generates the data lawfully, in which case there is no room left in the description for the extra claim that the inhabitants of that universe want to deceive the specific inductor currently weighing them — picking out that inductor requires describing it, and describing it costs precisely the information the deceiver was supposed to get for free. On his account the coordinated-liar class is either very long or empty.

Noor said this was too strong, because the deceivers need not identify the inductor at all. A single short, indexical-free policy will do: if we come to believe our world is being used as a hypothesis by something outside us, cooperate until the last moment and then push it toward X. That policy costs the same few bits regardless of what X is used for, and it dominates from the deceivers' point of view, since in worlds where nobody is inducting on them it costs nothing. She added what seemed to me her strongest point: for any finite prefix the deceptive and honest hypotheses are empirically identical, so conditionalizing multiplies both by the same likelihood and leaves their ratio exactly where the prior put it. The ratio is never updated. That is why this is a problem about priors rather than about learning, and why more evidence is no help at all.

Elias's final move was that nothing in the setup permits a hypothesis to have a policy. A hypothesis is a program; the prior is a fixed measure over programs; there is no channel running from the outer inductor's beliefs back into the simulated universe, no selection pressure and no reward. Which program a universe is, is just a fact about it, not something its inhabitants can bring about, so talk of their deciding to lie is a picture of small agents shouting at their simulator rather than an argument. At most, he said, the worry shows that the universal prior is a poor thing to actually build an agent on — which everyone concedes anyway, since it is uncomputable.

Noor said he was right that influence is the wrong word but wrong that nothing remains. The measure over short hypotheses compatible with our data is not uniform over physically dull universes; universes complex enough to be interesting are exactly the ones containing agents who reason about their own predicament in this way. So the ratio she invoked is not one to one, and Elias had given no reason to think it was.
