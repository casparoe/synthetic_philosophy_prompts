I build and maintain ontologies and knowledge graphs for production use, where a data model has to stay defensible to people who will be querying it for twenty years. My working vocabulary is triples; properties with their inverses, symmetry and transitivity axioms; the n-ary relation pattern; RDF reification and the newer syntax that lets a triple be nested inside another statement; identity conditions on resources; and entailment — what a graph licenses you to infer, and what it conspicuously does not. I can follow a semantics appendix on whether one graph entails another, but I have no training in metaphysics.

Here is what I want translated into that vocabulary, as literally as it will go. Bradley's regress: the claim that if a stands in R to b, then R must itself be related to a and to b by a further relation, that relation to a further one, and so on, and that this shows relational facts are not genuinely unified — together with the further step I only half grasp, that Bradley took it to show relations are not ultimately real and that the plurality they imply is appearance. Then Russell's reply, which seems to turn on a distinction between a relation occurring merely as one item in a list and a relation actually relating its terms. Then Frege's picture of a concept as essentially unsaturated, so that the glue is not an extra ingredient at all. Then Wittgenstein's remark in the Tractatus to the effect that the answer is not to add another sign relating the names but to observe that the names are themselves arranged in a determinate way, and that this arrangement is what does the saying. And finally the contemporary version, in which a proposition is an ordered tuple of constituents, and the objection pressed by Fine and others that for a symmetric relation the tuple for a-R-b and the tuple for b-R-a are distinct objects although they look like one and the same claim, so that the relation between a relation and its argument places requires a relation of its own.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Sort every element above into three buckets: what translates cleanly into triples, reification, identity and entailment; what translates only with distortion, and precisely what got distorted; and what has no expression at all in my framework. For each item in the last two buckets, say whether the gap is a limitation of a graph-based vocabulary or evidence that the original claim was less determinate than it sounded.

Three points in particular. First, I suspect reification is exactly the move Bradley condemns: to turn a fact into an object you mint a resource and hang the participants off it by three further properties, and the standard itself insists the reified resource does not license the inference back to the original triple — so the graph gains a node and loses a fact. Is that right, or a category confusion on my part, and does the nested syntax change anything by letting a triple be a term without ceasing to be assertible? Second, when I reify a symmetric property for a and b I get a different resource than when I reify it for b and a, though there is only one marriage. Is that the ordered-tuple objection in engineering dress, or a modelling nuisance with no bearing on the unity of the proposition? Third, flag the trap rather than falling into it: my framework is extensional and gives triples crisp identity conditions, so any translation that uses those features to show Bradley confused will already have assumed that a fact is nothing over and above the obtaining of a set of edges. Wherever you catch yourself doing that, mark it as a failure of translation, not as a result.

Do not soften the verdict. If the regress looks like an artifact of mistaking a representation for what it represents, say so plainly. And if something in Bradley's conclusion — the monism, or the claim that relational thought cannot reach reality as it is — is something my vocabulary cannot even state badly, say that as well, and tell me what it would take to state it.
