**Question.** In approximately 500 words, explain Sen's question — equality of what? — as it bears on Dworkin's proposal that the currency of egalitarian justice is resources rather than welfare. Identify what the capability metric is meant to capture that a resource metric misses, and assess whether the capability approach is vulnerable to the charge that it merely relabels welfare in non-psychological vocabulary.

**Student's answer.** Rawls and Dworkin agree that a theory of distributive justice needs a metric: an answer to what it is that should be equalized. Rawls proposes the primary goods; Dworkin argues that these should be replaced by resources, and he supplies a procedure for working out what an equal distribution of resources amounts to. Everyone begins with equal purchasing power at an auction of the island's resources, and the resulting distribution is equal when it clears the envy test — no one would prefer anyone else's bundle to their own. That test is what makes the metric ambition-sensitive and endowment-insensitive: people are held responsible for the tastes and commitments they have formed and compensated for the disadvantages they did not choose. The hypothetical insurance market extends the same idea to cases the auction cannot settle, by asking what premium people would have paid against a given handicap and redistributing accordingly.

Sen's objection is that the enterprise is aimed at the wrong thing. Resources are means, and what matters for justice is not the means people hold but what they are able to do and be. A capability is a functioning a person has actually achieved and has reason to value — being adequately nourished, being literate, being able to appear in public without shame, taking part in the life of the community. The capability set of a person is then the collection of functionings she in fact realizes. On this picture two people holding exactly equal bundles can stand in very different relations to their holdings: the wheelchair user needs more resources to achieve the same mobility, the person in a cold climate more to be adequately sheltered, the chronically ill more still. Conversion rates vary with personal heterogeneity, and heterogeneity is neither chosen nor registered by the envy test — the auction prices the wheelchair, not the impairment — so a distribution that satisfies the test can leave the disabled person with less freedom than her neighbours. Sen's point is that the ambition/endowment distinction therefore cannot carry the weight Dworkin places on it, because the differences that matter most are differences in what resources convert into rather than differences in what people want from them.

The same reasoning disposes of welfare as a metric. Satisfaction adapts to deprivation, so a measure built on it will systematically flatter the worst off, who have learned to want less. Capabilities track real freedom rather than how people feel about their situation.

But does this not simply relabel welfare? One worry is that, because capabilities are plural and resist a single index, Sen has no way of ranking distributions and so no guidance to offer — which is why Nussbaum had to supply the list of central capabilities that Sen declines to give. I think this gets things backwards: the refusal to complete the ordering is deliberate, and a partial ordering is still an ordering. As for the welfarism charge itself, Sen distinguishes well-being freedom from agency freedom but treats the second as derivative of the first, since pursuing goals one has set for oneself is part of doing well; capabilities are on that reading opportunities for well-being, and the charge half lands. The real answer to it is the faster: someone who has the capability to be well nourished and declines to exercise it for religious reasons is not judged worse off by a metric that counts her freedom, though she would be by one that counts her functioning. That shows the metric is tracking freedom, not welfare.
