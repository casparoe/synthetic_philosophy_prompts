I was at a leaving do for a colleague last week and two people I know, Nadia and Owen, got into a long arguement about a mutual friend of theirs, Elias, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it. I'm not on either side and I honestly couldn't tell who was arguing better, so I want you to go through it move by move.

The background they both agreed on: Elias's brother, who he fell out with as a teenager, is dying somewhere up north. Elias knows all the facts — he isn't confused about the prognosis, isn't in denial, and isn't depressed (they both insisted on this, and said he'd have been picked up by people who'd know). He did drive up once, sat in the hospital for about an hour, felt, in his own description, nothing at all, and came home. He's told them he won't go back for the funeral and has no intention of phoning.

Here's the exchange roughly as I remember it:

(1) Nadia: He has a reason to go. It's his brother and the man is dying. The reason doesn't stop existing just because he doesn't feel it.

(2) Owen: So tell me what "has a reason" is doing in that sentence. It doesn't explain anything — no act of Elias's, and nothing he'd do, is explained by it. It isn't a consideration he could weigh, because he's already weighed it, in person, for an hour. It's your disapproval wearing a fancy hat.

(3) Nadia: He hasn't weighed it. He visited as a tourist. He hasn't imagined his own sixtieth birthday with that call still unmade. Deliberation includes imagination, and imagining something properly can change what you want — so there can be a route from what he cares about now to going, even if he can't see the route from here.

(4) Owen: Careful. If "he hasn't imagined it hard enough yet" always rescues the reason, then anybody has a reason to do anything, because there's always some vivid enough imagining that would produce the desire. Your claim stops being that Elias has a reason and turns into the claim that somebody could manufacture one in him. Those aren't the same claim, and only the second is defensible.

(5) Nadia: Fine — then here's the part you're dodging. Suppose nobody ever manufactures it. Suppose at 60 he looks back and says he should have gone. That judgement isn't reporting a new desire of his, it's a judgement about what he had reason to do all along. And on your account nothing anybody says to Elias can be criticism: he can't be wrong about his own reasons, not even once, and "should" just means "I wish you were more like me."

(6) Owen: Why is 60-year-old Elias the correct one? He'll have his own set of wants, same as he does now. You're privileging the future one because you like what he says. And your criticism point cuts against you — what actually happens when you criticize Elias is that he starts wanting not to be thought badly of by you, and then he has a reason. Criticism works, but it works by changing him, not by reporting a fact he'd been ignoring.

Then right at the end Nadia said something I think was a third claim, and I couldn't tell wether Owen even heard it: she said Elias has a reason to want to go — that even if there's no reason for him to get on a train, there's a reason for him to be the kind of person who'd want to. Owen said that was either incoherent or empty: you can only have a reason to want something if wanting it serves something else you already want, in which case it's an instrumental reason in disguise; and if it isn't, she's back at (1) and hasn't answered him. She said that "you can only want things that serve your other wants" is itself exactly what's in dispute and he'd assumed it.

Somebody afterwards told me this is a named debate in philosophy, something about internal and external reasons, but please don't hand me the standard summary of that debate — I want you to work from what Nadia and Owen actually said.

- Go through (1)–(6) and the final exchange, and say who's right at each point, flagging where a move is basically sound but overstated.
- Does (4) land? Is Nadia entitled to treat imagination as a genuine deliberative route without her position collapsing, and what would it take for her to be entitled to it — is there a version of (3) that survives (4)?
- Is (5) a real objection to Owen, or does it quietly concede that Elias doesn't have the reason yet, only that he might come to think he did?
- Is "a reason to want to go" a coherent third thing between the two of them, or is Owen right that it reduces to either a disguised instrumental reason or a restatement of (1)? And if it is coherent, does it help Nadia or hurt her?
- Who's more right overall, and what did both of them miss — is there something about what "has a reason" is even for that neither of them said out loud, and would saying it dissolve the fight or just relocate it?
