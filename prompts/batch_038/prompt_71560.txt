I am preparing a chapter for an edited collection on foresight and responsibility in narrative, and before committing anything to print I want to stress-test a somewhat unfashionable reading of Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818; revised 1831) against the unilateralist's curse.

Let me specify the idea exactly as I intend to use it, so that the reading does not depend on your recalling any particular paper. The curse concerns a class of agents, any one of whom can perform some action, each holding a private and imperfect estimate of whether that action is on balance good. If each agent acts whenever their own estimate is favorable, the probability that a harmful action gets performed rises with the number of agents in the class, since only one favorable estimate is needed to trigger it. The standard prescription is deference: act only if some collective judgment — a majority, a designated body, or the most pessimistic well-informed party — also favors the action. Two corollaries matter here. Deference is not free: it suppresses actions that would in fact have been good and biases groups toward the status quo. And the prescription presupposes something the formal model takes for granted, namely that the agents share enough of a value framework that their estimates are estimates of the same quantity; where they disagree about what counts as harm, deferring to a collective judgment does not aggregate information but substitutes one party's values for another's.

Against that specification, I would like a critical reading that answers the following.

1. Does the curse get a grip on Victor at all? He looks less like one agent among many than like the only one: his class of possible actors is a class of one. If the right diagnosis is not failed aggregation but the total absence of a decision procedure, say so plainly, and explain what the novel is then diagnosing.

2. Consider instead the distribution of information as the locus of the problem. Victor cuts himself off from family and friends while working, keeps Clerval ignorant of the project until after the catastrophe, and discloses the secret to Walton only in the frame, once Walton's own voyage has already gone wrong. Read the epistolary structure — the letters to Margaret, the nested first-person accounts — as a device for deference after the fact: chains of testimony standing in for contemporaneous review. Does the frame bear that weight, or does the novel show testimony arriving too late to change anything, which would make it a story about the cost of secrecy rather than about aggregation?

3. Treat the destruction of the unfinished female creature as the mirror image. Here Victor acts unilaterally to withhold, on the strength of his own pessimistic projection about what the pair might propagate, against the settled expectation of the only other party to the arrangement. Is this the type-II error side of the curse, and does the novel thereby suggest that the prescription of deference cuts both ways — that deferring to the gloomier estimate is itself a unilateral act with identifiable victims?

4. What of Walton's decision at the end? Recall its shape: the crew demands to turn back once the ice frees the ship; Walton gives a conditional undertaking under pressure; the dying Victor harangues the men to press on and not be cowards; Walton honors what he promised anyway. I want to know whether this is the novel's one genuine case of deference — a pre-commitment to a collective judgment adopted in advance precisely so that it will not be renegotiated in the moment, when sunk costs and passion distort one's own estimate — or whether the crew's judgment is not about the ice or the passage at all but about who bears the risk, in which case Walton has not aggregated information but handed the decision to the party with the most at stake. Which of these the novel supports is decisive, because it determines whether Shelley dramatizes a solution to the curse or dramatizes the curse's inapplicability.

5. Adjudicate the strongest objection to the whole project: that Victor's estimate is not merely noisy but motivated, and that the novel's real subject is not the ethics of acting on incomplete information but the ethics of creation and abandonment — bringing a being into existence without its consent and then withholding the care it was owed. If that objection is right, the curse lens mislabels a value problem as an epistemic one. Tell me how much of the reading survives, and where the two diagnoses are genuine rivals rather than complements.

On the deliverable: an argumentative essay of roughly 1,500 words with a stated thesis and an explicit verdict, not a survey. Do not quote the novel or any secondary source, and include no bibliography, notes, or citations; identify scenes and episodes by description, as I have done above. Where a claim depends on differences between the 1818 text and the revised edition — in particular on how far Victor is presented as freely choosing rather than driven by necessity — flag that dependence and say which version the argument needs, but do not build the essay on details you are not confident about. I would far rather have a reading that concedes what the lens cannot handle than one that conscripts the novel into illustrating a model.
