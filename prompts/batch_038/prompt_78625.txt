I am a second-year graduate student serving as the reliability lead on a multi-site observational study, and I have ended up in an argument with my PI that I cannot resolve without some outside perspective. We are building a new clinician-rated severity scale — a dimensional coding instrument for agitation and distress behaviors, scored from short video segments — to be used as a primary outcome. The pipeline we pre-registered runs like this: we wrote a coding manual with behavioral anchors at each level; we ran two rounds of coder training on archived footage not drawn from the study; we released a coder to real study material only once that coder reached a threshold level of chance-corrected agreement both with the trainer and with the other coders on practice tapes; and we then report, as the scale's reliability estimate, the agreement obtained on a randomly drawn double-coded subsample of actual study material. That number is what our protocol treats as licensing us to use the scale as an outcome measure at all. Separately, the case for the scale measuring what we say it measures rests substantially on its correlation with an older instrument in the same domain, an instrument whose own original evidence base was, so far as I can tell from reading its manuals, the same kind of consensus among raters trained on the same kind of manual. A friend of mine who studies philosophy of science looked at my description and said we were bootstrapping, though I am not sure she meant that in the technical sense.

What makes me uneasy is that the same research group is scrupulous about independence elsewhere, and the two attitudes look irreconcilable. We rejected a candidate self-report measure for the study on the grounds that a study nurse walks participants through the items at baseline, so that consistency across participants is produced by the administration procedure rather than discovered by it — one of our reviewers wrote that up as coaching, and we agreed with him. Yet when uniformity is produced by our own training pipeline we file it under evidence. Likewise, we go to considerable expense to keep the double-coded subsample genuinely blind: coders cannot see one another's scores, cannot see which treatment arm a participant is in, and cannot see the earlier timepoint when scoring the later one. We are fastidious about independence at the level of the individual rating while the raters' judgment-forming dispositions have been deliberately homogenized by design. I keep trying to articulate why the first kind of manufactured agreement is fatal and the second is a virtue, and I keep coming up with something that sounds like a distinction without being one.

So here is what I would like you to do. Reconstruct the epistemology this practice presupposes — the account of evidence and justification that has to be in place for training-to-criterion followed by a blind agreement check to count as warrant for a claim about a latent behavioral property. Be explicit about whether the practice is committed to the strong thesis that agreement among appropriately trained judges partly constitutes the property rather than merely tracking it, or whether a weaker, externalist reading is available on which training simply improves the instrument and the agreement statistic genuinely samples an independently settled fact. Then separate, carefully, what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, and point out where it is underdetermined — which rival accounts of measurement would license exactly the same protocol. Address the circularity worry head on: whether this pipeline lets us acquire justification for the scale without ever collecting evidence that could have come out the other way, and what it implicitly assumes about the availability of a non-circular check on a property that is only accessible through trained judgment. Then diagnose the inconsistency I described — does the coached-participant objection commit us to an independence requirement that our own training procedure violates, or is there a principled asymmetry between what a rater's judgment is sensitive to and what a participant's report is sensitive to that lets both attitudes stand? Finish concretely: if we adopted the independence-demanding view, which steps of the protocol would have to be added or dropped, which of our reported numbers would cease to function as evidence, and whether anything capable of validating the scale would be left standing.
