I've basically bought the Dutch book line: a set of credences is irrational just in case it leaves you open to a sure loss, so coherence isn't some mysterious norm hanging over us, it's the cash value of being rational. Tell me what would actually count against this — I want defeaters, not a restatement of the argument. A few places I suspect it's soft, press on them:

- Real markets are full of people with demonstrably incoherent beliefs (conjunction fallacy, badly miscalibrated confidence) who never get cleaned out. If exposure to sure loss is the substance of the irrationality, why doesn't selection eat them?
- The gap between credence and betting disposition. Someone can be incoherent and simply never bet, or bet in ways that don't reveal their numbers. Nothing is then exploitable — is the view's silence there a bug or a feature?
- Sub-threshold incoherence: my credence in P is 0.5 and in P-and-Q is 0.5 minus 10^-13, and any bookie who could construct the winning book needs more decimal places than an actual transaction has, with fees swamping the loss. Coherence fails but bookability doesn't. Do they really come apart, and if so which one was doing the work all along?
- Imprecise credences can apparently be non-bookable yet still accuracy-dominated. If a stronger dominance theorem is doing the real work, is the Dutch book just a heuristic wrapper I should trade in?
- The diachronic arguments: are bets across time genuinely a separate line, or does every dynamic book collapse into a synchronic one plus a standing commitment to your future prices? What's lost if it collapses?
