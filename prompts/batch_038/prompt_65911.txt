Write a dialogue between two researchers debating whether the strategy-stealing assumption supports pushing ahead in the development of advanced AI. Set the scene concretely: their organization is months away from a system its own leadership expects to confer a decisive strategic advantage, and the two are arguing about whether the safety-minded camp should want to be the ones who get there first.

One of them argues yes, on strategy-stealing grounds: any careful, slow, alignment-first course of action the cautious camp would have pursued is still available to whoever arrives first, who additionally keeps whatever the lead buys — the same reasoning that lets the first player in Hex steal the second player's strategy, since in that game an extra move of your own color can never hurt. The other denies the transfer, and must identify precisely which premise breaks rather than simply insist that racing is reckless.

Hold both to a high standard. The skeptic must answer the objection that the leading party need only be capable of executing the careful strategy, not disposed to, since capability is what the assumption is about. The defender must answer the objection that the game is not fixed while the players move: some choices made quickly and irreversibly delete options on which the careful strategy depended, and unlike Hex, a player can be harmed by the mere passage of turns.

Let neither win outright. End with the two of them isolating the single premise on which their disagreement now turns, and stating what evidence would settle it.
