I am in my third year of a philosophy PhD, drafting the second chapter of a dissertation on whether Hume's is–ought passage supports anything as strong as the thesis usually called Hume's law. Before I write another paragraph, I want to know what principle, if any, my own verdicts add up to, because they currently look inconsistent to me.

Below are the inferences I have been sorting into piles, with my gut verdict on each. Treat the verdicts as data rather than as commitments I will defend at any cost.

1. Premises: this well water contains cholera bacteria; you want to avoid cholera. Conclusion: you ought not to drink from this well. Legitimate — but I take the ought to be entirely on loan from the desire, so that the inference dies when the desire dies.

2. Premise: this well water contains cholera bacteria. Conclusion: you ought not to drink from this well. Illegitimate. What interests me is that I know exactly what I would add to repair it, namely the desire I just deleted.

3. Premise: he is a sea captain. Conclusion: he ought to do whatever a sea captain ought to do. Valid, but no victory for the naturalist: the ought was already sitting inside a relative clause in the premise, so nothing crossed anything.

4. Premise: tea drinking is common in England. Conclusion: either tea drinking is common in England or all New Zealanders ought to be shot. Valid by disjunction introduction, and I regard the people who wave this inference around as scoring a point about syntax rather than about normativity. It does not show that anyone has a reason to shoot New Zealanders.

5. The familiar institutional derivation: Jones utters, in appropriate circumstances, words to the effect that he hereby undertakes to pay Smith five dollars; therefore Jones has promised; therefore Jones has placed himself under an obligation; therefore Jones ought to pay Smith five dollars. I want to say the first two steps are unobjectionable and the last is where the argument fails — and that it fails for a reason unrelated to 3 and 4: it compels only an audience that already treats being under an obligation as a reason to act. That is a different species of failure, and I cannot characterize it.

6. Premise: she moved her king two squares toward the rook. Conclusion: she castled, so she may not have left her king in check, so she ought to have made sure her king was not in check. Legitimate, and not degenerate: the ought is real and she has a genuine reason, given that she is playing. This is my problem, because 6 and 5 have exactly the same shape — a purely descriptive fact about a move inside a rule-governed practice issuing in a real ought for participants — and yet I reject 5 and accept 6.

7. Premise: this heart moves three liters a minute when the organism needs five. Conclusion: it is a bad heart. Legitimate. I accept the Geach and Foot point that good and bad are attributive here, with the standard supplied by the kind.

8. Premise: human beings are by nature social and cannot flourish in isolation. Conclusion: human beings ought to live in communities. Illegitimate, even though it has the same shape as 7 and even though I am broadly sympathetic to an Aristotelian account of human flourishing.

9. Premise: pleasure is the only thing people desire for their own sakes. Conclusion: pleasure is the good. Illegitimate. But notice that my test here is different: I ask whether it still seems an open question whether pleasure is really good, and if it does, I call the inference fallacious. That looks like a claim about what good could mean rather than a claim about which inferences are valid.

My tentative principle is this: an inference from wholly descriptive premises to a normative conclusion is legitimate only if the normativity in the conclusion is already available in the premises, either explicitly, as in 3, or as part of the rules of a practice that the premises describe and that the addressee of the conclusion already accepts, as I want to say in 1 and 6. I suspect this misfires, because it appears to license 5 — Jones accepted the rules of the practice by making the move — and it appears to license 8, since I arguably already accept the standards attached to human nature. So it either overturns two verdicts I want to keep or needs amending, and I cannot see how to amend it without making it either trivial or a bare restatement of Hume's law.

What I want from you:

(a) A principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have never seen. For each clause, say which of my numbered verdicts motivates it.

(b) An honest accounting of which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, and why. If the cheapest way to save the set is to give up 5 and 8 together, say so and tell me what that costs; if the pair 5/6 can be split, tell me what does the splitting, and whether the distinction survives the obvious objection that making the moral ought non-conditional simply declares the gap uncrossable.

(c) Your principle's verdicts on two cases I have not listed: from these conditions of formation were coercive to this contract ought to be void; and from he caused her a serious injury for no reason to he acted wrongly, which a Foot-style naturalist would insist is not a gap-crossing at all, since the normative work is already done by injury and by for no reason.

(d) A judgment on whether 9 belongs in this chapter at all: is the distinction between an argument about the definability of good and an argument about validity one I should be enforcing, and does collapsing the two explain any of my other verdicts?

Aim for about 1,200 words. Argue rather than survey, and do not pad the answer with a history of the passage. You may name positions and philosophers by their familiar labels, but do not build the argument on citations I would have to check, since I am working offline. End with one line, and only one, in exactly this form, naming the single verdict you think I should abandon first if the set cannot be saved whole:

Verdict: <case number>
