The question, as set by the instructor:

> Some philosophers hold that if perceptual knowledge can be basic — knowledge that does not presuppose any prior warrant for trusting the perceptual source — then a subject can acquire knowledge that her source is reliable by reasoning that is far too easy. Reconstruct the bootstrapping argument in detail, making explicit every epistemic principle the argument requires. Explain why its proponents present it as a reductio rather than as a discovery, and say what they think has to go. Then discuss one of the following two responses: (i) that the reasoning is not epistemically defective after all, and the conclusion should simply be accepted; or (ii) that the reasoning is defective, and the defect can be identified. Say which you find more convincing and why.

The student's answer:

> The bootstrapping argument comes from Vogel, and Cohen develops it into a general attack on any epistemology that allows basic knowledge. The core thought is that if a source can give you knowledge without your first having to know that the source is trustworthy, then you can use that source to establish its own trustworthiness, and that is far too cheap.
>
> Vogel's case is Roxanne and the gas gauge in her car. Roxanne has no independent way of checking the gauge and has no view about whether it is reliable. She glances at it and forms the belief that the tank is full on the basis of its reading F. If the gauge is in fact reliable and her vision is reliable, then on reliabilism she knows the tank is full, and she needs nothing further in order to know it: that is what makes the knowledge basic. She also knows, from looking, that the gauge reads F. Putting the two together, she is in a position to know that on this occasion the gauge read F and the tank was full — that is, that it was right. She repeats the procedure on Tuesday and Wednesday, and accumulates a list of occasions on which the gauge's reading matched the level of fuel. Then she generalises inductively: the gauge has been right on every occasion I have examined, so the gauge is reliable. Roxanne now knows the gauge is reliable without ever having compared it to anything independent, which is just what she could not do at the start.
>
> Cohen takes this to be a reductio rather than a discovery. The upshot is not that Roxanne has genuinely learned something about her gauge; it is that the premise which licensed the reasoning must be false. Basic knowledge has to go.
>
> It has to go because the reasoning is question-begging. Every step assumes the very reliability the argument sets out to establish: Roxanne's premises already take it for granted that the gauge reports the truth, so the conclusion that the gauge reports the truth is smuggled in rather than earned. On this way of putting it the inference is simply invalid, or at any rate circular in the vicious sense, and no number of accumulated instances will repair it. It is now generally accepted that bootstrapping reasoning is defective, and the live debate concerns only the best account of the defect.
>
> The most influential account is Wright's. Wright's treatment of Moore's proof of an external world is that warrant fails to pass from the premises to the conclusion, and the same diagnosis applies here, since Roxanne's argument is the same kind of argument as Moore's: in both cases the subject lacks the antecedent warrant that the inference would need in order to do any work. On Wright's picture the failure is a failure of closure — knowledge is not closed under known entailment in arguments of this shape — which is why the bootstrapping derivation cannot be run. That is also why Roxanne's perceptual warrant turns out not to be basic at all, since it depends on an entitlement to accept that perception is reliable, and it is that requirement which blocks the derivation.
>
> Finally, it is worth saying where the reliabilist is in the deepest trouble. If the gauge is unreliable, Roxanne ends up with a false belief about its reliability, produced by the very same procedure that in the good case produced knowledge. The reliabilist owes us an account of how identical reasoning can be knowledge-conducive in one situation and not in the other, and meeting that demand is the real cost of the view.
>
> On balance I think the argument succeeds against reliabilism and against any view on which knowledge of the external world requires no prior assurance about its sources, but that it does not touch views which demand antecedent justification for trusting a source, since on those views the reasoning never gets started. The cost of such views is a regress, but a regress that terminates in an entitlement looks to me preferable to knowledge this cheap.
