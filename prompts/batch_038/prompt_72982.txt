I'm circulating a short statement of my position on behavioral public policy at a workshop next month, and I want a consistency audit before I send it out. As I would put it to colleagues:

1. There is no neutral choice architecture. Every policy frames, orders, and defaults some options, so the live question is never whether government shapes choice but how.
2. Paternalism is presumptively wrong only when it shrinks the option set or makes choosing otherwise more costly. Nudges do neither, which is why I think the liberty-based objection to them fails.
3. A nudge is permissible when it selects what the person would choose for themselves under good conditions — attentive, informed, and unhurried.
4. Preferences are largely constructed in the act of choosing. Presentation determines what people want, and there is no stable, presentation-independent ordering underneath it.
5. Nudges must be open: any nudge ought to survive being announced and defended in public without losing its bite.
6. On the cases, I favor automatic enrollment in retirement saving with an opt-out, and presumed-consent organ donation; I think graphic health warnings on cigarette packaging are impermissibly manipulative; and I don't think government should nudge electoral conduct at all, not even in the direction of turnout.

I genuinely don't know where the fault lines are here. Tell me which of these commitments actually pull against one another and which only look as though they do, rank the real problems by severity rather than listing them as equal worries, and for each say what would have to be given up and what I would lose by giving it up. Please write the response in American English.
