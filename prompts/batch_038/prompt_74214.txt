I write novels, and for four years I have also taught a first-year seminar at a small liberal arts college: a cross-listed course on constraint and invention, whose first half concerns rule-bound composition and whose second half concerns formal systems, with Hofstadter's MU-puzzle — the one in Gödel, Escher, Bach — serving as the hinge between them. It is the cleanest case I know of a set of rules whose products can be characterized exhaustively from a standpoint the rules themselves cannot occupy, which is more or less the situation a novelist occupies with respect to a form, and the students tend to feel that immediately.

Our take-home midterm has come back. About a third of the seminar are writers with no mathematics beyond secondary school, and the question below was set with that in mind; the standard I want applied is the standard of the question, not the standard of a logic course.

The question, worth twenty points, read as follows.

Question 3. Consider the MIU-system. Its only axiom is the string MI, and it admits four rules operating on strings built from M, I and U: (i) if a string ends in I, you may append a U; (ii) from a string of the form Mx you may infer Mxx — that is, you may duplicate the entire remainder of the string following the initial M; (iii) any occurrence of the substring III may be replaced by a single U; (iv) any occurrence of the substring UU may be deleted. Hofstadter asks whether MU is a theorem of this system. Answer in two parts.

(a) [12 points] Give the argument that settles the question. Your argument should make explicit what it is an argument about, and at what level it is conducted; 7 points are allotted to the argument itself and 5 to that account of its status.

(b) [8 points] Explain what is meant by jumping out of the system in this connection — the phrase is Hofstadter's, and we adopted it as seminar shorthand — and say whether introducing a new rule that delivers MU would constitute such a jump, giving reasons. 4 points for the explanation, 4 for the verdict and its support.

One student's answer is reproduced below exactly as submitted, including its hesitations.

(a) MU cannot be derived. The reason is that the number of I's in any string the system produces is never a multiple of three. We begin from MI, which has one I. Rule (i) appends a U and does not touch the I's. Rule (ii) copies everything after the M, so it doubles the number of I's, and doubling a number that is not a multiple of three cannot make it one. Rule (iii) exchanges three I's for a single U, so it lowers the count by three, which likewise cannot produce a multiple of three out of something that was not one. Rule (iv) deletes a pair of U's and leaves the count alone. So the counts available to us run 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11 and never 3, 6, 9 or 12; and MU has no I's at all, and zero is a multiple of three, so MU is not a theorem. It is not that we have failed to find the derivation. There is no derivation, and there never will be one, however long we work.

What strikes me is that the argument is carried out inside the system. We appealed to nothing but the four rules: we went through them one by one and generalised over what they do. So MIU is able to establish its own limit from within, which is the shape of Gödel's first incompleteness theorem — a system containing a statement that is true and that the system cannot prove. MU plays that role here. It is a string we can perfectly well write down and think about, and it is true of it that it is what we were after, and still the rules cannot reach it. That is incompleteness, not mere difficulty.

(b) Jumping out of the system is what you do once you see that the rules are not the weather. Since MU cannot be reached under (i) to (iv), the only way to have MU is to alter them: add a rule permitting you to strike out any occurrence of III without substituting a U — no, that does not help, it leaves the same count — or, more simply, make MU itself an axiom, or license the inference from MI to MU. Then MU is a theorem and the puzzle is solved. This counts as a jump because you have stopped treating the system as given and started treating it as something you may revise, which is a different kind of act from any number of moves within it. Hofstadter's point, as I understand it, is that this is what people do and machines do not: a program set to run the MIU-system would enumerate theorems indefinitely, never noticing that MU was out of reach, while a person stops, looks at the whole arrangement from outside, and sees it at once. The jump is therefore a matter of the kind of mind involved, not of how much effort is applied.

I have read this once, quickly, and I would rather not hand you a settled opinion of my own to argue against; please treat your assessment as the first reading.

What I need from you is a mark out of twenty, allocated across the four components named in the question, together with comments itemized in the order of the answer and tied to particular steps rather than to its general tenor. For each item, say what the student has got right, and where something is wrong, say what the correct point is in terms the student could act on when revising. Where a conclusion is acceptable but the reasoning offered does not carry it, mark that separately from a conclusion that is simply unacceptable. Where the answer attributes a position to Hofstadter, judge it by what the answer itself offers in support; you are not being asked to reconstruct the book's argument, and if you think the attribution unsafe, say why in those terms. Style should not enter the mark one way or the other.
