Please think carefully and at length before committing yourself to a diagnosis, and write as a colleague corresponding with another colleague rather than as a textbook: I want your reasoning, not your references, so please do not supply citations, a bibliography, or a reading list. I run a graduate seminar on moral responsibility. To force my students to see what a Frankfurt-style case must commit one to, I constructed the argument that follows. It has worked considerably better than I intended: three of my students now maintain that it shows the debate since Frankfurt's original paper has been in pursuit of an object that cannot exist, and I have not been able to show them where it slips.

The argument refers to a case of my own devising, so let me state it first.

**The Deputy.** Bram has been offered a large sum to sign a statement that he knows to be false and that will end a colleague's career. He is deliberating. A device implanted in his cortex — the Deputy — monitors the deliberation. It is triggered by a distinctive neural signature, N, which would be the onset of a decision to refuse. My stipulations are these. (i) Bram's relevant neural activity is indeterministic; N is not causally sufficient for refusal, but no refusal can occur unless N has appeared. (ii) If N appears, the Deputy seizes Bram's motor system and produces the signature itself. (iii) If N does not appear, the Deputy remains wholly inert and Bram signs on his own. (iv) The interval between the earliest moment at which N could appear and the moment of signing is ample — say three seconds — for the Deputy to complete its work. (v) In the actual sequence, N never appears. Bram signs, for reasons of his own, and the Deputy does nothing at all.

I built the Deputy in order to escape the familiar dilemma for such cases, by keeping the interval between sign and decision indeterministic while nevertheless guaranteeing the outcome. Here, then, is the argument.

(1) **Sufficiency Requirement (SR).** An agent is unable at t to do other than A only if there is some state of affairs G, obtaining at or before t, that is causally sufficient for his A-ing at t. Inability must be grounded in something real, and causal sufficiency is the only grounding relation that will bear that weight.

(2) **Inertness.** Nothing that does no work in the actual sequence can be, or be part of, G.

(3) In a Frankfurt-style case, the intervener's device acts only upon a prior sign; in the case as designed, the sign fails to appear and the device stays dormant.

(4) So by (2), G in such a case cannot include the device, and must be sought in the agent's own prior states together with the laws of nature.

(5) Either the prior sign, together with the laws, is causally sufficient for the agent's decision, or it is not.

(6) If it is, then the decision is determined, and the case can be pressed against the principle of alternative possibilities only on the assumption that determination does not undermine responsibility — precisely what the incompatibilist denies. The case begs the question.

(7) If it is not, then no G of the kind (1) demands is available in the case at all, and so by (1) the agent can do otherwise. The case is no counterexample.

(8) The Deputy was meant to slip between (6) and (7). But the guarantee it supplies is disjunctive: either Bram signs of his own accord or the Deputy signs for him. No single disjunct is causally sufficient for the signature, and a disjunction is not a state of affairs of the kind (1) requires. The Deputy therefore also falls to (5)–(7).

(9) Hence in any Frankfurt-style case either the agent could have done otherwise or his decision was determined. No case of the intended kind can be coherently described.

(10) SR is not, however, a thesis about exotic neurosurgery. It is a general account of inability, with general consequences. It entails that I am unable to leave a room only if something is causally sufficient for my staying — so a man whose only exit has been bricked up is able to leave it, unless his staying happens to be determined. It entails that a swimmer who cannot swim is able to cross the lake unless his failure to cross is determined. It entails that in an indeterministic world no threat, no absent means, no forgotten obligation and no locked door has ever removed an alternative possibility.

(11) So either the principle of alternative possibilities is immune to counterexample and a great deal of ordinary talk about what people cannot do is false, or SR is false. I cannot accept the first disjunct, and I do not know what to put in place of the second.

My students located the trouble variously in (2), in (6), and in the remark about disjunctions at (8); I found each diagnosis unsatisfying, since each leaves other steps of the argument unexplained and unchallenged. For what it is worth, I am most attached to (1) and least attached to (9). If you think that attachment is misplaced, say so plainly.
