Consider the standing dispute over shared intention: whether it reduces to a web of individual attitudes — each party's intention that we J, interlocking subplans that mesh, mutual responsiveness, mutual knowledge of all this — or whether mutual expressions of readiness constitute a joint commitment and hence a plural subject whose intentions are not distributable across the members. Produce the joint statement that a committed partisan of each side could sign after a genuinely adversarial collaboration rather than a negotiated truce.

Then a table of residual disagreements, each row worded so that its own side would accept it, with a column marking whether the row is substantive or merely verbal — and if you think some of the disagreement dissolves under paraphrase, say which part and why.

Then the cruxes: sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side. For each, give a concrete case both would agree bears on it and record each side's prediction in advance. Build at least one crux around a case neither camp standardly uses: two people who exchange mutual assurances of readiness to renovate a house and deliberate as a unit, but whose subplans demonstrably do not mesh, each of whom would cheerfully take the whole project over solo if the other quit. State whether the two theories issue divergent verdicts there, whether the rebuke-and-obligation data can be factored into an assurance component and a meshing component, and what each camp predicts about which component the folk intuitions actually track.
