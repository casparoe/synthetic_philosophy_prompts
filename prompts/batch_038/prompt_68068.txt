I teach a graduate seminar in philosophy of religion, and this term's unit is the problem of evil. The difficulty I run into every year is not that my students lack views but that they hold combinations of views they cannot articulate and would not endorse if they saw them laid out together. One will insist that God must be able to state a reason for each instance of suffering he permits, and then, two sentences later, insist that we are in no position to say what any such reason would look like — without noticing that the second claim drains the first of its content. Another will happily say that evil is nothing in itself, a mere absence, and then treat particular atrocities as positive data demanding explanation. I want an instrument that forces these commitments into the open.

Please write a decision tree that sorts a person by what they are actually committed to when they respond to the problem of evil, so that a student who has never seen the labels can work down the tree from concrete questions and arrive, surprised, at a name.

The following constraints matter to me, roughly in order.

1. Begin downstream of the logical problem. My students already accept that a being's permitting evil is logically compatible with perfect goodness so long as that being might have a morally sufficient reason to permit it; treat the incompatibility argument as settled and dead. Do not spend a branch on whether the argument is logical or evidential. Every node should be a question that is still live for someone who has granted that much.

2. Build from the space, not from the roster. Before drawing anything, give me an ordered list of the questions you intend to use as branch nodes, each with one line explaining why it belongs at that depth rather than deeper or shallower — that is, what it separates most cleanly at that point in the tree and what it would muddy if asked earlier. Then present the tree itself. I want to see that the questions were derived from the structure of the responses rather than reverse-engineered from a list of positions you already knew.

3. Phrase every node so that it can be answered without knowing where the answer leads. In practice this means concrete claims about cases, not questions about labels. "Do you think God's reasons are beyond our comprehension?" is useless to me. A usable node asks something like: when a fawn is trapped in a forest fire and dies in agony over five days, and we can discern no good that would justify permitting it, is our failure to discern such a good evidence that there is none, or only evidence about the limits of the discerner? Another usable node turns on a claim about heaven: if it is possible for created free agents to coexist with God and yet never sin, why did God not actualize that possibility from the start?

4. Distinguish carefully between two questions that my students routinely collapse, and make sure they land on different nodes. The first is a question of capacity: could we in principle understand the reason God has, if there is one? The second is a question of entitlement and adequacy: would God's having such a reason be the sort of thing that could answer the complaint of the person it was permitted against? These come apart in both directions, and a tree that cannot tell them apart will be of no use to me.

5. Make the tree sensitive to the kind of suffering at issue, because the standard responses do not distribute evenly across kinds. At some depth I want nodes that turn on: suffering inflicted through the free choices of other agents; suffering undergone by beings who never develop the capacities that any character-formation story presupposes — the profoundly impaired infant, the non-human animal, the child who dies before forming a character at all; and suffering of the sort Marilyn McCord Adams calls horrendous evils, where participation in the evil gives prima facie reason to doubt whether the victim's life could be a great good to them on the whole. Two responses that handle the first kind but fail the second must not share a leaf.

6. Also make room for two structural questions that most treatments bury. First: is God subject to agent-centered restrictions — barred from bringing about a good by means of an evil even where the outcome would be better — or is divine goodness a matter of optimizing? Second: must a perfect being actualize the best world available, and if so, is there such a thing, or is the series of possible worlds such that there is always a better one to be had?

7. The leaves should include at minimum: skeptical theism in its "we should expect not to see" formulation; greater-good theodicy of the free-will and the soul-making (Irenaean) kinds, as two separate leaves; afterlife compensation; the felix culpa strategy; privation theory; kenotic theism; open theism; process theism; the Adams-style project of defeating horrors rather than justifying them; and anti-theodicy, on which the project of justifying God is itself the morally objectionable move. Where a single response strategy can be deployed either as a defense (God might have such a reason) or as a theodicy (God has such a reason, and here it is), send these to different leaves: they carry very different costs and the tree should make the reader choose. Each leaf gets one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the thing its defender least wants to have to say out loud.

8. Name your collisions. Where two distinct paths down the tree terminate in what is in practice the same position, say so explicitly rather than smoothing it over, and say whether any further question would separate them. I suspect the skeptical-theist and the mere-defense branches will end up entangled. I also suspect that privation theory will turn out to be orthogonal to nearly everything else on the tree, since it is an account of what evil is rather than of why it is permitted; if that is your judgment, report it as a limitation of the tree rather than forcing the view onto a branch where it does not belong.

9. Enumerate the unoccupied cells. Some combinations of answers are perfectly available and have no defenders; for each, tell me whether the cell is empty because the resulting view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply tried it yet. I would rather discover an interesting empty cell by enumeration than stumble on it by accident, and if you think a given empty cell is worth occupying, say what a defender would have to give up to occupy it.

Two housekeeping rules. Use the standard technical vocabulary where it exists — CORNEA, middle knowledge, trans-world depravity, and so forth — but give me no bibliography and no reading list; I want positions sorted, not people cited, and where a name is attached to a claim, mention it only if you are confident of the attribution. And keep it handout-sized: the tree itself no more than two pages, with a further page for the collision and empty-cell notes in items 8 and 9.
