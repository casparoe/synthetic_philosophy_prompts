I cover mathematics and the sciences of decision-making for a general-interest desk, and I am drafting a piece on the perennial misuse of Aumann's agreement theorem. Argue the following position as strongly as the material permits: that the theorem's content lies in the common-knowledge operator rather than in the common-prior assumption, so the familiar gloss — that rational people may legitimately disagree because they began from different premises — inverts what the result actually establishes. Please build the case on the further claim that an apparent difference of priors is best understood as a difference of indexical location within a single prior, and therefore as something the agents are obliged to treat as corrigible rather than exculpatory. Address two objections squarely along the way: that common knowledge of each other's posteriors can in practice be achieved only by pooling evidence, which would make the conclusion trivial; and that once priors genuinely differ, agreement becomes a matter of degree and the theorem falls silent on every case anyone actually cares about. British English, please.
