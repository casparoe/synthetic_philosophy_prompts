You are an expert in formal epistemology — particularly Bayesian accounts of disagreement, common knowledge, and aggregation — who has also spent real time with market microstructure, and who has designed diagnostic teaching instruments before. I would like you to help me build one. I co-teach a graduate reading group called "Markets as Knowers," half philosophy students and half economics students, and every year the same argument derails the third session. One side says that Aumann's agreement theorem and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem jointly show that a price you would bet against is itself evidence that you are the one who is wrong: under a common prior, a willingness to trade is a public announcement of private information, so an informed participant's offer should move the price before anybody accepts it. The other side says that the same two results show that any actual trade proves somebody's beliefs are mistaken, so a market with volume is a market containing fools — and that talk of a market "having" a credence is a category mistake in any case, since the common prior the theorems require is not even a well-defined object for a heterogeneous crowd of institutions, algorithms, and hedgers. Both sides cite the same papers. They have never managed to say which assumption each of them is actually dropping, and I want an instrument that settles it, so that each student can locate themselves at the start of the session before they have read anything.

Please write me a decision tree that takes a student from one concrete predicament — you hold credence *p* in a proposition, you observe a market price implying a different credence *q*, and you are trying to work out what, if anything, that gap licenses you to conclude — to one named position on the epistemic authority of prices. Aim for nine to twelve branching nodes and eight to twelve leaves. Every node must be a question about the situation rather than about the student's affiliations, phrased so that it can be answered honestly without knowing where either answer leads; nothing of the form "do you think markets are wise?" or "are you a conciliationist?" belongs on the tree. Build the instrument from the logical space first and from the names second: before drawing any branches, give me a short brainstorming section that lists the assumption slots in the two theorems' machinery — common prior, common knowledge of rationality and of the information structure, the absence of non-epistemic motives to trade, a well-defined resolution event, the absence of feedback between the price and what the price is about — together with what happens to the conclusion when each slot is relaxed or refused outright, including the standard relaxations, such as substituting common high-probability belief for common knowledge, and substituting priors that are merely close for a prior that is shared. Then locate the actual positions in that space, and mark the combinations of answers that no live view occupies, saying for each whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because nobody has bothered to explore it.

The leaves must include at least the following: full deference, on which the price is a sufficient statistic for what you should believe, handled together with the Grossman–Stiglitz objection that such deference is self-undermining because it destroys the incentive to acquire the very information being deferred to; a conciliationist leaf on which the price functions as an epistemic peer and the gap should be split; a steadfast leaf on which you may rationally retain *p*; a leaf on which the gap is evidence about other participants' irrationality rather than about your own; a deflationary leaf on which prices are not credences at all and the framing mistakes a distribution of dispositions for a belief state; and a reflexivity or performativity leaf on which the price partly causes the event it is supposed to track, so that tracking is the wrong relation. Four specific cases must be used somewhere as nodes or as tests hanging off nodes: a counterparty who is legally or contractually obliged to trade — a hedger, a fund meeting redemptions, a dealer satisfying a regulatory requirement — so that the volume carries no information about anyone's beliefs; a contract whose payoff depends on a decision that the price itself influences; a situation in which you know that every other participant's information set is a subset of yours, and yet the price still sits where you would bet against it; and a market whose resolution event will never be publicly observed by anyone. Flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and say what further question would separate them, if one exists. Close with a compact table mapping each leaf to the assumption of the theorem machinery it gives up, its nearest neighbour among the leaves, and the single question that discriminates between them. Two house rules: no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations of any kind — the students will not have library access during the session, and I do not want them sent chasing references; and where a position has a standard name, use it, but where you are not confident about an attribution or a label, describe the view rather than inventing one. Take the brainstorming section seriously before you start branching, because the entire value of this instrument lies in whether the questions genuinely come apart.
