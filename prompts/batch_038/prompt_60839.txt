I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political theory on defeat and collective memory — eighteen students, nearly all seniors — and I am writing their three-hour, closed-book final. Over the term we worked through four cases in which a military or political disaster was converted into a usable national story: the Confederacy's Lost Cause, Masada, the Battle of Kosovo in 1389 and the myth that grew up around it, and Dunkirk. The final has two essay questions; the one I need from you is worth 25 of the 100 marks, and students will have roughly fifty minutes on it. They will have nothing in the room with them, so the question must supply whatever historical detail it depends on, and neither the question nor the model answer should require or provide citations. Please do not attribute positions to named scholars or books; if a view has an author, describe the view instead.

Here is the difficulty I want the question to sit on. The distinction my students arrive with, and the one our reading tends to reinforce, is between tragic memory and redemptive myth: a defeat is either remembered as loss and warning, or reframed as a moral victory that was worth the cost. That distinction is useless for sorting these cases, because it puts Dunkirk and the Lost Cause on the same side — both redemptive, both converting catastrophe into proof of character — and puts Masada and Kosovo on the same side, both binding sacrifice to a covenant and a promised future. That is precisely the wrong grouping, since in ordinary liberal usage the first pair and the second pair are supposed to be the tolerable and the intolerable cases. So I have been working with a candidate criterion of my own, which for seminar purposes I call the creditor test. Every redemptive myth of defeat is a story about a debt the dead left outstanding, and the test asks whom the myth makes a debtor. An inward-debt myth binds the living only to their own future: endure, rebuild, keep faith, do not let it happen again. An outward-debt myth generates claims on other people — on land, on status, on submission, on redress, on revenge. I think this sorts the cases better, but I am not confident it survives contact with them, and I want the question built exactly where it strains. Four pressure points, which you may use, sharpen, or replace: Dunkirk looks purely inward-facing, yet it was pressed into service as a justification for holding an empire and for treating a rout as a moral triumph, and its most beloved image, the flotilla of little ships, sits on top of the fact that most of the men came off naval vessels and along the harbor mole; Masada looks inward and is usually retold that way, while resting on a single ancient account whose defenders are a band of Sicarii rather than a national army; the Lost Cause looks outward in its claims against Black citizens and against the federal government, yet it also supplied a defeated region with a genuinely inward-facing self-image of endurance; and Kosovo looks outward in its modern nationalist uses while apparently having functioned under Ottoman rule as a fatalistic accommodation to that rule rather than a claim against it.

What I want is one essay question, not a set. It must be answerable from argument alone — a fluent narration of any one of the four cases should not carry a student past the midpoint — and it should force the student either to reconstruct a criterion of their own or to take mine apart, and to say what case or what evidence would falsify whichever criterion they defend. Build the creditor test into the stem, stated crisply enough that a student who has never encountered it can work with it in fifty minutes, and leave room for a strong answer to conclude that the test grades the wrong feature entirely. Then give me: (1) the question exactly as students would see it, including any supplied detail; (2) a model answer of about nine hundred words that commits to a definite verdict on the test and argues for it rather than inventorying positions; (3) a marking scheme as a table with exactly these columns — band and mark range out of 25; the moves an answer must contain to earn that band; the characteristic failure that caps an answer at that band; the comment I would write in the margin; and a one-line diagnostic question I could ask in a viva that would reveal whether the student genuinely has the move or has merely landed on the right vocabulary; and (4) after the table, a short JSON object with the fields crux (one sentence on what the question really tests), recitation_ceiling (the highest mark a purely recitational answer can reach, and why), test_verdict (the model answer's committed verdict on the creditor test), discriminating_moves (an array of strings, each naming a move that separates a first-class answer from a merely good one), and strongest_objection (the best objection a student could raise against the framework built into the stem, and how the scheme credits it). I would much rather be told that my framework is wrong and given a better question built around a better criterion than be handed a hedge between the two: take a position and give your reasons for it. Please write everything in American English.
