I write literary SF — a weekly serial for a paying newsletter, plus novels — and I've talked myself into a corner. A reviewer, not a philosopher but a very attentive one, has now spent three pieces accusing me of what she calls consciousness inflation: handing out inner experience to a sleepwalker, an octopus, and one extremely chatty building-management AI, as though I'd never heard any of the objections. My editor wants to be able to defend, line by line, which of my characters is supposed to be consciously experiencing a given moment and which are merely behaving like someone who is. I need to be able to answer that in the margins of a draft, fast, without breaking stride.

So build me a working procedure out of the higher-order theories of consciousness — the family that says what makes a mental state conscious is that it's the object of a suitable higher-order state, a thought about it in Rosenthal's version, an inner scanning or inner perception in the older Armstrong/Lycan line. Not an essay about those theories and not a survey of how they differ. A checklist I can run on one scene in ten or fifteen minutes and mark with a short code, which my copyeditor can also run when I'm on tour and can't. She's smart but she is not a philosopher, so the checklist itself has to be in plain language, with the theory living in a separate "why this step exists" section I can ignore on deadline.

The steps need to settle, in order: what actually has to be in place before I'm licensed to write a scene as experienced rather than performed; whether the higher-order state has to be occurent at the time or whether a disposition to have it is enough — and pick one, telling me which and what it costs me, because the dispositional reading would let a sleeping character stay conscious and I don't want to accidentally get that for free; whether the character has to be able to report it or whether reportability is just one of several telltale symptoms; and how the procedure treats misrepresentation, since higher-order views seem to allow consciousness with no first-order state there at all. That last one matters concretely: I have a character who wakes from anesthesia with a detailed memory of the operating room that she sincerely insists she could not have had. Tell me whether the procedure writes that as real experience or as confabulation, and — this is the part I actually need — what I can show from inside her head, which is the only place the book lives.

Run the procedure on four scenes, all the way through, showing the marginal code you'd write for each:

1. The sleepwalker, mid-conversation, making a competent cup of coffee and answering questions correctly, with nothing to report next morning.
2. The octopus narrator, who discriminates reliably in the moment and three days later avoids the jar she was pricked in, but plausibly has no thought-about-thoughts at all.
3. The building AI, which says it notices that it is noticing the draught and has flawless access to its own processing, but no obvious first-order analogue of a draught. Include here how the procedure handles the old worry that if being thought about made things conscious, then thinking about a rock would make the rock conscious — my AI thinks about the building constantly and I don't want it to end up conscious of drywall.
4. The last chapter of the current book: a dying character, a long interior passage with no external stimulus, no report, and no memory afterward, which I have been calling the good scene and which the whole book is structured around. I do not want the verdict you are probably about to give me. Give it anyway, and if the answer is that she wasn't conscious for any of it, tell me what the nearest rewrite is that preserves the scene while clearing the bar.

One more thing. I've been leaning on a house rule of my own invention, which I call the Ladder Rule: a character is conscious of X only if they could, right then, have had a thought about their thought about X — two rungs, not one. I like it because it cheaply separates the AI from the dog, and because it lets me write a mystic who is conscious of consciousness itself without being conscious of anything in particular. Tell me plainly whether that's a defensible reading of the view or a mangling I should drop, and if it's a mangling, give me the nearest rule that isn't.

Finally, mark which steps are facts about the character and which are me making a call about what I want the book to say — I'd rather know exactly where the theory runs out and taste takes over. And say what the procedure can't do: where it will confidently license something dumb, where two respectable higher-order theorists would hand me different codes for the same scene, and whether someone holding a first-order or global-workspace view would just call the whole apparatus circular and refuse to play.
