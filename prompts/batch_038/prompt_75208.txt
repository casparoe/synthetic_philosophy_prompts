I've got a draft chapter on the demandingness objection that I've now rewritten twice, mostly because my own verdicts won't line up, and I want to use you as a sparring partner rather than a summarizer. So: skip the literature tour entirely, I've taught this material for years and I know the pond and the envelope and the whole fair-share/partial-compliance business. What I want is the machinery — principles stated precisely enough that I could tell whether they actually generate my intuitions or just decorate them.

Here's what I'm currently defending, more or less. A demand is too much iff meeting it would leave you worse off than a reasonable-compliance baseline: the life you'd have if everyone in your position did their fair share, plus a sliding margin that grows with how bad things get if nobody else steps up. Second clause: costs made of goods that money cant buy back — a vocation, a relationship, an ability, a person's history with an object — count heavier than their welfare equivalent. Third: the baseline is measured at the moment of the demand, not across a whole life. Now the cases, and where I think this thing starts to leak.

Drowning child, ruined suit, and ninety-nine other bystanders standing around with their phones out: wade in, required, no hesitation — and note the free riders are right there, which I'd have thought should trigger the margin clause. But: a hundred strangers will die unless someone sends $100,000 today; ninety-nine people exactly as rich as me refuse their $1,000 share; the agency asks me for the whole amount. I think I owe my $1,000 and maybe $1,500, and I do not think I owe $100,000, even though refusing it means ninety-nine preventable deaths that my $1,000 does nothing about. Those two look like the same structure and I judge them differently, and I can't tell whether that's a principled difference or a flinch.

Then the drip case: a well-run charity emails me every day asking for $20. Each request is trivially cheap, and I think each one is required. But I also think that a person who answers all of them for twenty years has had their life eaten by morality, and I wouldn't call them admirable, I'd call them erased. So I'm committed to "obligatory, one at a time" and "unacceptable, taken together," and my third clause explicitly refuses to look at the aggregate. Related, and worse: tutoring a kid every Saturday for two years, which costs me nothing but hours I'd otherwise spend doomscrolling, and which plausibly does more good than the $20-a-day donations. I think it's not required. Money is demanded of me in a way that time simply isn't, and clause two doesn't explain that, because nothing irreplaceable is lost when I give up a Saturday.

Cheaper pairs, same problem. Selling my grandmother's ring for $4,000 to buy nets: not required. Selling my second car, also $4,000, mild inconvenience: required. My reflex explanation is grief, and grief is just welfare, so maybe clause two is doing nothing — except that a kidney for a stranger is not required either, and that's a far bigger welfare hit, so now I've got two different-looking reasons giving the same answer. And then: a 1-in-50 chance of permanently losing the ability to enjoy music, taken to save a stranger's life. I think its required. Which surprises me, because in the ring case I exempted a much cheaper loss of exactly the same kind of good. Apparently probability launders identity-costs and I have no account of why.

The one I keep going back and forth on: I can give $5,000 now, or wait ten years, when I'll be a full professor with a bigger salary and a paid-off house, and give $25,000. Waiting seems permitted and not required, but I honestly don't know what my baseline clause says about it, since it measures at the moment of the demand.

So: give me the two or three candidate principles that come closest to my verdicts, each with a name and a one-sentence statement, then walk through it clause by clause telling me which case each clause is there to handle. Then tell me which of my verdicts each candidate has to overturn, and pick — I'd rather have one honest recommendation than a menu that saves everything at the cost of being unfalsifiable. Apply the survivor to the deferral case and to two cases you invent yourself where the survivor and its closest rival come apart, because I only trust a clause when I can see two principles disagreeing. And if you think one of my verdicts is just a tenured, comfortably paid person protecting himself, say which one and show me where it shows up in the principle. My co-teacher for the seminar thinks the entire demandingness debate is a rich-country excuse, so I want the version of this I could defend to her without blushing.
