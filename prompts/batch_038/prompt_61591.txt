I am drafting a chapter for an edited volume on philosophy and literature, in which I argue that Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day is best read as a novel about the metaphysics of causation by omission, and I should like your help stress-testing that claim before I commit to it in print.

Let me set out the philosophical terrain as I am framing it. Whether an omission can literally be a cause is disputed. Lewis's counterfactual account appears to make absences causes, at the price of promiscuity: if the butler's silence caused the dismissal of the maids, then so did the silence of every other member of the household, of every guest at the conference, and of every person anywhere in the world who did nothing. Beebee denies that omissions cause anything while insisting that they remain central to causal explanation. Schaffer makes causation contrastive, so that an omission causes an outcome only relative to some specified alternative. Dowe's conserved-quantity account leaves omissions outside causation proper, treating them as a close relative he calls quasi-causation. And Hart and Honoré, approaching the matter from law, tie absence causation to departure from a norm or standard of normal functioning: the omission causes the harm because the agent was supposed to act and stood in a position where acting was the expected condition.

Now the novel. The episodes that matter for my thesis are these: the international conference at Darlington Hall, during which Stevens's father dies upstairs while Stevens continues to serve the guests; the dismissal of the two Jewish maids at Lord Darlington's instruction, with Miss Kenton's protest, Stevens's compliance, and his subsequent decades of minimization; the informal conference of the 1930s and the appeasement project it advances, in which Stevens's contribution consists largely in not objecting, not warning, and not passing anything on; the sustained failure to acknowledge Miss Kenton's feelings, culminating in the 1956 journey to the West Country, the meeting with the now-married Mrs. Benn, and the bus-stop parting; and the closing scene on the pier, where Stevens resolves to cultivate the art of bantering.

What I want from you is analysis, not summary, and I would rather have objections to my framing than agreement. Specifically:

1. Taking the episodes in turn, does the narrative's own causal bookkeeping treat the omission as a cause of the outcome, in a sense precise enough to be philosophically interesting? Where does it treat it as merely causally explanatory, and where as neither?

2. Which of the accounts sketched above best fits the novel's practice, and what in the text discriminates among them? I am especially interested in whether a contrastive account is already covertly at work in the narration. Stevens's characteristic move is to name one particular alternative he declined rather than the open class of everything he failed to do, and I want to know whether that is a genuine datum for contrastivism or only a stylistic habit with no metaphysical import.

3. The Hart and Honoré line looks tailor-made for a world organized around duties, standards, and professional role. Does the novel vindicate a norm-based account of absence causation, or does it expose that account as an attempt to smuggle moral and legal judgment into metaphysics? Bear in mind that Stevens's entire self-conception consists in maintaining that the operative standards are those of the great house rather than his own, which seems to cut both ways.

4. A complication I have not been able to resolve: many of Stevens's silences look more like actions — compliance, deference, the performance of a role — than like omissions. Does the novel blur the doing-and-allowing distinction, or does it depend on it? If his failures to speak are really things he does, does the book forfeit its claim to be about omission causation altogether?

5. The narration is systematically evasive, so the causal inventory reaches the reader only through a narrator with strong reasons to suppress it. Does that pose a problem for the metaphysical accounts, which are supposed to concern the world rather than reports of it, or is it a resource — for instance, for the thought that which contrast is relevant is not fixed until the narrator supplies it thirty years after the fact?

6. Tell me plainly where my reading overreaches. Identify at least two respects in which the novel is doing something other than taking a position on negative causation, and where an honest critic must retreat from the thesis.

7. Finally, what does the book contribute to this debate that argument has not? My suspicion is that it has to do with the fact that the agent discovers only retrospectively that what he did was an omission, and that the alternatives he declined become visible to him only once they are no longer available. Please either develop that thought into something defensible or dismantle it.

Please do not reproduce extended passages from the novel; I have the text in front of me and want your judgment rather than your recollection of it.
