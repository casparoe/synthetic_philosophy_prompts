So — odd request. I'm fourth year in philosophy, mostly decision theory, and I got talked into giving a paper for a literature-and-philosophy session on Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, and now I'm worried the lens I picked doesn't actually fit.

The lens is coherence arguments for expected utility maximization: Dutch books, money pumps, Savage-style representation theorems, the whole package that says if your credences and preferences violate these axioms you're either exploitable or not interpretable as an agent at all. My hunch is that Jekyll is a case study in what happens when the unit of agency the theorems assume just isn't there — no single locus whose credences and utilities are stable enough across time to be a target for coherence in the first place.

Can you work through this with me? The bits I keep circling:

- The will. Jekyll has drafted a legal instrument handing everything to Hyde if he disappears or is unaccounted for more than three months. Is that a precommitment device, an insurance policy against a state he can't price, or a sign he already knows the exchange rate is going to run against him? It's odd that the thing that most horrifies Utterson is the most rationally self-protective act in the book.
- The cheque Hyde gives the girl's family, drawn on Jekyll's account, and Utterson's hunch that Hyde must be blackmailing him. Utterson is doing a two-agent exploitation analysis of what is really a one-agent pump. Does that matter for the coherence story — can you be Dutch-booked by yourself, and does the theorem-generating worry about exploitability even get off the ground when there's no bookie and no external party?
- The ending, where it turns out the first batch of salt was impure and the pure stuff doesn't work. That reads to me like an unforeseeable state, not incoherence — so does the novella's climax actually escape the lens I want to apply, and if so should I say that outright?
- Whether Hyde himself is incoherent or just coherent about different things. If Hyde is decisive, consistent and goal-directed, then the book is making the point the coherence theorems can't: form without content, coherent and monstrous. Does that reading survive contact with how impulsive and ape-furious Hyde is on the page?

Two structural worries. First, basically everything about Jekyll's reasoning arrives in his own final written confession, which is self-exculpatory and self-dramatising, so the calculus I'd be auditing is a motivated reconstruction by the agent being audited. Foreground that, or is it a distraction? Second, is Jekyll's London even a small world in Savage's sense, or is the whole tragedy that he's applying small-world decision theory to a grand-world problem about his own future selves?
