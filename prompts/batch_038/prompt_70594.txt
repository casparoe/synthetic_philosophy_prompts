For several years I have convened a monthly reading circle of about a dozen people — two academic philosophers, a palliative-care nurse, several programmers, and a retired classics teacher — who work through a text and then argue about it, sometimes for hours. Last month's text was Hofstadter's account of selfhood, in particular the claim he develops out of his wife's death: that a person's "I" is not bounded by a single skull but is distributed, in varying degrees of fidelity, across the minds of everyone who knew them well, so that after a death a great deal of the person is genuinely still present in others. Two members, Miriam and Julian, disputed this for most of the evening. I was chairing and did not intervene; I am now trying to write up the session honestly, and I cannot tell who had the better of it. Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

(1) Julian: Taken seriously, this view removes the metaphysical premise of grief's characteristic thought — that the person is simply gone. What is gone is one high-resolution instance of a pattern; the pattern itself persists, degraded but real, in the widow, the children, the colleagues. So the felt absoluteness of the loss rests partly on a false picture of what a person is, and correcting that picture is not a consolation but a claim about what is and is not the case.

(2) Miriam: This confuses resemblance with persistence. What survives in you after your wife's death is your model of her — a representation shaped by years of interaction, superbly useful for prediction, but a representation nonetheless, and maps do not keep their territory alive. If you want the standard framework: on Parfit-style accounts what matters is psychological connectedness and continuity with an appropriate kind of cause, realized in a continuing brain. The carriers are downstream effects of the original person, not continuations of her. An effect is not a survivor.

(3) Julian: Parfit's own conclusions cut the other way. He says identity is not what matters and that survival comes in degrees; in fission cases he is willing to say both resulting people survive the original. If degrees of survival are coherent there, they are coherent here, and the difference between a fission branch and a grieving husband is one of fidelity, not of kind. Your effect-versus-continuation line would rule out Parfit's fission survivors as well, since they too are caused by the original.

(4) Miriam: No. In fission, each branch is the ongoing operation of a single psychology: it perceives, forms new beliefs, is surprised, has plans that can be thwarted, and can be harmed. What the husband carries is inert in exactly those respects — it acquires no new beliefs, it cannot be mistaken about anything, it has no interests that can be set back. A pattern that cannot be disappointed is not a subject. So the two cases are not on one scale, and "degrees" is being used equivocally across them.

(5) Julian: Then your criterion is agential rather than metaphysical, and it builds in the assumption that only what can be frustrated counts. That assumption is costly: a patient under general anaesthesia satisfies it no more than your carried pattern does, and we do not say her self has been suspended. Meanwhile the carried pattern is not inert in the way you describe. The widow finishes her husband's sentences, avoids the road he hated, takes up the translation he left unfinished, corrects strangers who misremember him. Those are actions performed in accordance with, and sustained by, the pattern — not merely by a representation of it.

(6) Miriam: Those are precisely the actions a good representation predicts. The direction of explanation runs from my model to my behaviour; it does not run from him to my behaviour. Attributing my conduct to him is a courtesy we extend to the dead, not a report of a causal fact.

A third member, Theo, then put a case to them, and their responses diverged in a way I found more confusing than clarifying. A woman dies; her partner builds a conversational system from her years of text messages, voicemails, and emails. It answers in her idiom, recalls shared events, and occasionally produces something she might plausibly have said that the partner did not anticipate. Julian said this is a strictly worse carrier than the spouse, because it is a statistical average with no shared history and no ongoing causal coupling to the world she inhabited, whereas the spouse's version of her is embedded in a life that continues to interact with her absence. Miriam said the system is the more honest case, because it makes visible that what Julian has been describing all along is a representation — and if the system does not count as survival, neither does the internalized pattern, since they are the same kind of thing at different levels of polish.

Finally our classics teacher offered a distinction she said both had been ignoring, and I would like your assessment of whether it helps. She said "pattern" was doing two irreducibly different jobs: in one sense a pattern is like a melody, a type that can be instantiated with equal legitimacy by different orchestras, none of which continues any of the others; in the other sense a pattern is like a flame or a whirlpool, a single ongoing process that persists precisely by exchanging its matter. On the first sense the widow instantiates the husband's pattern but does not continue him, and Miriam wins. On the second sense there is no process left to continue once the brain is gone, and Miriam wins again. The only way Julian can win, she said, is by claiming that the carried pattern is a third thing — neither a type-token instance nor a continuing process — and then he owes everyone an account of what that third thing is.

What I want is a verdict, not a survey of the personal-identity literature and not a list of further reading; I have read enough of it and it did not settle this. Specifically:

(a) Work through moves 1 to 6 in order and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right. For each rating, say what justifies it, and where a move is partly right, isolate which part survives criticism and which part does not.

(b) Identify the single crux: the point at which, had one side conceded, the rest of the evening would have followed automatically. Then say whether that crux is metaphysical, semantic (turning on what "the same pattern" means), or evaluative (turning on what makes a loss bad for anyone).

(c) Say whether Miriam's inertness objection in (4) and Julian's anaesthesia reply in (5) genuinely meet, or whether the two are arguing past each other. If the latter, reconstruct the strongest version of each that would actually conflict, and adjudicate that reconstructed pair.

(d) Rule on Theo's case. Does the conversational system favour Julian, favour Miriam, or is it neutral evidence? State what feature of the case is supposed to do the work, and then say whether a system built from the same corpus *before* the death would score differently — and if it would, why the timing of construction should matter at all to a question about persistence.

(e) Say whether the melody/flame distinction dissolves the disagreement or merely relocates it. If it relocates it, state as precisely as you can what the third notion Julian needs, and assess whether that notion can be made coherent without collapsing back into one of the two.

(f) Diagnose what neither of them said. I suspect something important went unraised all evening — perhaps about why anyone would want the distributed view to be true, or about whether the question they were contesting is even the question a bereaved person is asking when she says he is still with her — but I want your diagnosis rather than my hunch, and I want it argued for rather than gestured at.
