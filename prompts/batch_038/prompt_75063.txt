TA'ing a first-year minds-and-machines seminar and I want a one-page diagnostic handout: a decision tree that sorts students by what they actually DO when they run into the MU-puzzle, not by which ism they say they like.

What they'll have in front of them: the MIU-system — axiom MI, and the four rules (if a string ends in I you may add a U; you may double everything after the M; you may turn any III into a U; you may delete any UU) — with the target MU. MU isnt derivable, and a few of them will work out why: the number of I's can never become a multiple of three. The rest will flail, or cheat, or declare victory wrongly. I can get to 1, 2, 4, 5 or 8 I's from MI, but 3, 6 and 9 never show up, which is the whole ballgame.

I want the tree built around a distinction I've been pushing on them, because I think most talk of jumping out of the system — joosting, in Hofstadter's ugly coinage — equivocates between two different things. Call them the vertical jump and the lateral jump. Vertical means you go up to a level that talks about the object system: the I-count invariant is exactly that, you've stopped pushing strings around and started doing arithmetic on strings. Lateral means you quit the system and start a different one with different primitives: "let me delete U's wherever I like", "actually the goal was MUU", "rules are made up anyway". Both feel like stepping outside. But only the vertical one buys you knowledge, and only the vertical one is what Gödel's theorem is about. My suspicion is that people who like the Lucas-Penrose line tend to smuggle in the lateral version: they want the mathematician doing something no machine can do, but when you look closely it's either a vertical jump a machine could in principle make too, or a lateral jump that isn't an achievement at all, just rule-breaking dressed up as insight.

Requirements for the handout:

- 6 to 8 questions. Every node is about something a student could plausibly have done or said at the blackboard, phrased so they can answer it without knowing where it leads. No node may name an ism or ask anything as blunt as "do you think machines can think".
- Leaves are positions. Use the real names where names exist (the Gödelian anti-mechanist, the formalist, the Wittgensteinian who says there's no outside to jump to, the deflationist who says all of this is just ordinary informal maths with extra drama, plus whatever else you think belongs) and write "unnamed" where nobody actually holds the view. One sentence per leaf on its most awkward commitment.
- At least two leaves should be slots I suspect are empty or unstable. Tell me which ones, and diagnose each: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's bothered?
- Flag every pair of leaves your questions fail to seperate. My guess is you'll find that "proved the invariant in a meta-theory" and "just has a strong informal sense that no finite sequence of moves will ever get there" land on top of each other. If they do, say so rather than inventing a fake branch to look thorough.
- If you want a leaf for the program that rewrites its own rules — did it jump out, or is the rewriting just one more rule? — put it on the tree, but tell me which kind of jump it counts as.

Then, after the tree, answer me one thing straight: does the vertical/lateral distinction survive contact with the tree you just built? Specifically, where does the student who sits there muttering "I keep getting 1, 2, 4, 5, 8 and never a multiple of three, hmm" belong — is that a jump at all, or is it just decent problem-solving inside one enormous system that nobody ever left? If your answer is that it's all one system and there's no genuine outside, fine, but then tell me what work the word "system" is still doing on my handout, because if nothing is ever outside then I've been selling these students a distinction that doesn't buy them anything.
