Consider the standard anatomy of an official state apology for a historical wrong: a sitting head of government apologizes for enslavement, dispossession, mass expulsion, or atrocity carried out by officials of an earlier regime — often a regime the present government defines itself against, and whose members are nearly all dead. The speech is delivered by executive act alone: no legislature votes on the text, and no citizen is asked whether he assents to it. It acknowledges the facts, declares the acts wrongful, expresses sorrow or shame, and frequently promises commemoration and revised school curricula. It is addressed to a group rather than to identifiable victims, and it is accepted or rejected by a small number of people who claim to speak for that group. Almost always, a further clause states that the apology creates no legal liability and no entitlement to compensation for anyone. And in the same weeks, the same officials reassure the public that guilt is personal: no living citizen did anything wrong, the young cannot answer for their grandparents' deeds, and the apology imputes nothing to anyone's ancestors. Nothing in the practice provides a procedure for withdrawing an apology once given; if an incoming administration distanced itself from a predecessor's, it could only decline to stand behind it.

Reconstruct the theory of agency, identity, and obligation this bundle presupposes, and assess whether its parts presuppose consistent things. In particular:

1. For the utterance to be anything more than a report of the current officeholders' private sentiments, what must the state be? Is bare legal continuity — same juridical person, same treaties, same treasury, same archives — enough to make a first-person-plural expression of remorse apt, or does that require the state to be an agent with intentions of its own? State the minimum metaphysics the practice needs, and distinguish it from a richer one it merely fits comfortably.

2. Can one apologize for what one did not do? Sort apologizing out from acknowledging a wrong, condemning it, and grieving over it, and say whether a "we are sorry" issued by a non-culpable speaker is an apology at all or a different speech act wearing apology's grammar. Note which of the practice's other features — the promised commemoration, the admission of persisting effects — may be doing the real justificatory work.

3. In the same state's private law, accepting responsibility for a wrong is precisely what grounds a duty to repair. What distinction must be in play to accept the former while refusing the latter: expressive versus material responsibility, attitude versus debt, responsibility as ownership versus responsibility as liability? Is any such distinction available here, or does the disclaimer simply mark the apology as cheap?

4. Is "the nation is sorry" consistent with "no citizen is guilty"? Give at least two views that hold both together and at least one on which the pairing is flatly contradictory and survives only because nobody reads the two statements side by side.

5. Where all the victims are dead, the apology is addressed to people who were never victimized. What theory of harm does that presuppose: that the dead can still be wronged, that a group persists as a victim across generations, or that the descendants are harmed by non-recognition rather than by the original acts?

6. Identify which features are philosophically underdetermined — equally well explained by a purely strategic account (legitimacy, international standing, closing off a standing grievance) that requires no corporate moral agency whatsoever — and which features resist that deflationary reading.

7. Finally, say what would have to change under replacements. If only individual human beings were moral agents, would these apologies become impermissible, merely reworded, or untouched? If instead a state persisted as a debtor across regime change in the fullest sense, which existing clauses would have to go and which new machinery — legislative authorization, a compensation schedule, a standing right of descendants to refuse the apology — would have to appear? And what does the absence of any procedure for retraction presuppose about the kind of act an official apology is?
