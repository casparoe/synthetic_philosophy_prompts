I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy of mind working at the interface with comparative cognition, and in three weeks I will deliver a forty-minute paper at a half-day workshop hosted by a national animal-welfare research institute. I would like you to prepare me for the question period. I want that preparation to be specific to the room rather than generic, so let me describe both the audience and the thesis with some care.

The audience is roughly twenty people. The large majority are comparative physiologists and behavioural ecologists who work on arthropods and cephalopod molluscs, and whose professional currency is experimental control and paradigm validity. Three are veterinarians doing analgesia and welfare-assessment research; their concern is which indicators can be scored reliably and repeatedly in a husbandry or clinical setting. Two are philosophers of biology, one of whom is a deflationist about sentience and holds that the construct should be replaced by talk of behavioural regulation and allostatic cost. One works in science policy and regulatory drafting. There is no philosopher of mind present and nobody who does formal modelling. Accordingly, please do not give me the objections a philosophy-of-mind audience would raise: no zombies, no higher-order theories, no panpsychist priors, no relitigation of the explanatory gap. Those will not come. What will come is methodological, empirical, and regulatory, delivered by people who are broadly sceptical that philosophers understand how this evidence actually works.

The thesis of the paper. My target is what I call convergent weighting, the field's default standard, on which a taxon is credited with sentience only when several independent evidence classes line up — nociceptors and ascending pathways; aversive conditioning and its attenuation by analgesia; self-administration of analgesics; wound-directed protection and grooming; trade-offs in which the animal pays a real cost to avoid a noxious stimulus; behavioural flexibility that a reflexive account struggles to absorb — with the weight assigned to each class then adjusted by how closely the taxon's neural organisation resembles the vertebrate case.

My argument runs as follows. (1) The similarity adjustment carries far more of the load than is typically acknowledged, and it is applied asymmetrically: behavioural evidence treated as near-decisive for octopus and cuttlefish is treated as ambiguous for insects even where the paradigms and the controls are broadly comparable, and the discrepancy is reported afterwards as a difference in evidential strength rather than as a difference in weight assigned to evidence of the same kind. (2) The criterion should therefore be disjunctive rather than conjunctive: demonstration of any single marker under controls adequate to exclude non-associative alternatives — habituation, sensitisation, peripheral modulation of nociceptive signalling — suffices to place a taxon in a presumptively sentient category, because the markers pick out distinct functional capacities each of which is independently sufficient for negatively valenced experience on any broadly functionalist account, and no such account makes them jointly necessary. (3) The main rival, a graded view on which taxa are ranked by evidential strength and receive protection in proportion, is unstable: the ranking tracks our confidence rather than the degree of the property, and using the first to allocate protection for the second is what allows insects to fall off the map while decapods remain on it. (4) The policy upshot is a shift in the burden: once any one marker is established under adequate controls, continued invasive use or intensive husbandry of that taxon requires positive argument rather than mere absence of argument.

Two further commitments I should flag, since they are obvious targets. I use as a concrete case the fact that the definition of animal in the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 covers vertebrates, decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs, leaving insects and most other invertebrates outside its remit even though, on my account, the relevant evidence classes are already available for several insect orders. And I lean on a line of neuroethological work holding that the insect central complex implements a unified, egocentric model of the animal's position in space, and that this integrative role is a plausible candidate substrate for a minimal subjective point of view — a claim I treat as suggestive rather than load-bearing, though I suspect the room will not accept that qualification.

What I want back is a prep sheet, not a defence of my view and not a restatement of it.

- Eight to ten objections, ordered by the product of how likely each is to be raised in this particular room and how much damage it would do if I fumbled it.
- For each: whose objection it is (a crustacean physiologist, an analgesia veterinarian, the deflationist philosopher of biology, the policy drafter), phrased the way that person would actually phrase it, in their vocabulary and at the length they would actually speak it, including the interrupting asides and the my-system framing that experimentalists use.
- Which of steps (1) through (4) it targets, and whether it attacks the diagnostic claim, the constructive claim, or the policy claim.
- What I should concede outright and early, where the concession costs me nothing and buys credibility; what I should hold; and a reply that fits in roughly ninety seconds of spoken delivery.
- Where that reply is softest — the single follow-up that would expose the softness if the asker pressed once more.
- At least one objection that is simply correct: a genuine defect in the disjunctive criterion that I should repair in the paper before the talk rather than attempt to talk my way out of live. Identify it and sketch what the minimal repair looks like.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks. Say why it is the one I most want to avoid, and what the least damaging answer is if it comes anyway.

Two closing constraints. Do not encourage me, and do not tell me my view is interesting. And flag any point at which my characterisation of the evidence looks to you as though it is leaning on results a specialist would consider contested, thin, or drawn from a handful of laboratory species — I would far rather hear that now than have it said from the third row in three weeks.
