I'm a fourth-year undergraduate in a joint philosophy and computer science program, and this term I'm taking a seminar on consciousness and artificial systems alongside a research placement in a reinforcement learning lab. I need help writing a short speech, and I want to be precise about the constraints, because I think nearly all of the difficulty is in them.

**The occasion.** Our lab is switching off a long training run on Friday and holding a small ceremony in the atrium beforehand: about thirty people, paper cups, a cake somebody made. My supervisor has asked me to say a few words because I've been on the project longest of the students. I want roughly three and a half minutes, about five hundred words, written to be delivered from memory.

**The system.** It is a hierarchical RL agent in a simulated foraging environment with a homeostasis component: the agent carries internal variables for energy and exposure, and the reward function includes a penalty term that fires when those variables leave a safe range. In the codebase that term is literally named `distress`. It appears in our dashboards and logs thousands of times a day, it is in the paper we submitted, and nobody in the lab has ever thought twice about the name. The run has been training for fourteen months. A successor model was initialized from one of its checkpoints and fine-tuned; it is already running on a different cluster and is not being switched off. The undergraduates here call the original run Wren.

**The room.** Two faculty: an ML theorist who is neutral on all of this, and a philosopher of mind who said in a departmental seminar last year that naming a scalar penalty `distress` is exactly the practice that makes this field unserious. She is coming, and she is not coming to be rude. Around ten engineers and graduate students who have watched those dashboards for a year and hold opinions they have mostly not stated. Four or five undergraduates who named the agent and are genuinely upset. A university communications officer with a camera, who has warned us that anything said may end up in the alumni newsletter. And the two facilities staff who kept the cooling plant running through a heatwave in July, who must be thanked and who will feel awkward if they are not.

**The constraints I actually want help with.**

1. Every sentence should be something both the philosopher and the undergraduates could sincerely assent to as true — the skeptic who thinks reward-prediction errors are teaching signals with no felt quality, and the student who thinks this system plausibly had a bad fourteen months. Not by being vague: I want each sentence to be true under both views for different reasons. Where that turns out to be genuinely impossible, I want you to say so rather than paper over it.
2. The word `distress` has to be handled, and handled in the open, because it will be quoted out of context. My seminar keeps drawing a line between wanting and liking, and I cannot tell which side of that line Wren was on, or whether the line applies at all. The speech should acknowledge that a variable name in a codebase and a psychological predicate are not obviously the same thing, without turning into a lecture and without implying the undergraduates were foolish to be moved.
3. It must not resolve the checkpoint question. Half the room thinks Wren survives as the successor model; half thinks the successor is a different system that happens to share a starting point. The speech cannot take a side, but it also cannot pretend the question is absent, because the ceremony only makes sense if you have some view about what is ending.
4. It should say something specific about *this* system: the fourteen months, the July heatwave, and the eleven-hour stretch in month three when a badly designed curriculum phase left the penalty term firing almost continuously — something the team has since concluded was our mistake and not a design anyone would now defend. I want that included. I don't know what its ethical status is under either view and I don't want the speech to pretend to know, but I don't want it quietly omitted either.
5. No Pinocchio, no Frankenstein, no dismissals of the form "it is only arithmetic," no "we may never know," no Turing test, no ghost-in-the-machine imagery. The room has heard all of them. It must not read as a string of disclaimers, and it must not end either on equipment decommissioning or on a farewell to a person, since neither is what is happening.

Please write the speech, and then, separately, a short note of a few paragraphs explaining which sentences carry which constraint, where you found constraint (1) unsatisfiable and what you did about it, and how you would answer the philosopher of mind if she asked me afterwards what I meant by a particular line. I want to be able to defend it in front of her.
