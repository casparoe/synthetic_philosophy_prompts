ok so I'm in my school's philosophy club (12 of us, we meet in a room that smells like gym socks) and we've burned three straight meetings on whether a group can literally have a mind. It started because I made everyone read the Ant Fugue dialogue from Gödel, Escher, Bach over the summer, the one where they have a conversation with the anthill Aunt Hillary. Half the club thinks it's a cute metaphor and nothing more. The other half thinks group minds are obvious and that we're standing inside one right now — their favorite example is our group chat, which they say "was in a bad mood" for the whole of exam week and which apparently knew about a breakup before the two people in it did.

For next week I want to build something that sorts us. Like a flowchart or a short quiz, 8 to 10 questions, each one a concrete situation with two or three answer options, and then a key at the end that says which pattern of answers lands you on which position. The whole point is to catch people by what they say about cases, not by what they call themselves, so no question can be anything like "do you think a colony has a mind," and someone who already knows the labels shouldn't be able to steer themselves.

Can you write it? Stuff I care about:

- Use cases where the positions actually come apart. Some I want in there: our ant farm, where the tunnels and the whole daily routine just stopped when the queen died; those little ants that pick a new nest by having scouts pile into a candidate cavity until enough of them agree and then the whole colony moves; the slime mold in bio that finds the shortest route thru a maze even though nothing inside it is doing arithmetic; a leafcutter colony plus the fungus it farms; and then the people cases — Wikipedia, our group chat, and that thought experiment where an entire country passes notes around to imitate a brain. I'd also like at least one about an AI built out of separate modules that message each other, because my friend Maya thinks all the insect stuff is doing sneaky work by assuming ahead of time that ants are dumb.

- Make the branch points be the criteria that do the real work, not names. Like: do the parts have to be smart on their own; does it matter whether the information is spread out versus sitting in one place; does the group need a single point of view, something it's like to be it; does it survive if you swap out every member; does it have to be able to be wrong about itself.

- The key should name each position it sorts people into, or say "unnamed" if nobody actually defends it, plus one line on the weirdest thing that position is committed to.

- Tell me which of your questions can't really separate two of the positions, so I know which ones are pulling their weight and which are just decoration.

- And if some combination of answers lands in a spot nobody occupies, say so, and say whether that's because the view is incoherent, or just unappealing, or nobody's thought of it yet.

No reading list or citations needed, we're not writing a paper. Just the questions, the options, and the key.
