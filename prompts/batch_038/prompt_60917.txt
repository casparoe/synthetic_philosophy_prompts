My roommate thinks moral foundations theory settles the left-versus-right debate and I can't quite break his argument. Here's how he puts it:

(1) If one person's moral intuitions are responsive to a wider range of the considerations that actually matter morally, that person's moral judgments are likelier to be right.

(2) The data: conservatives respond fairly evenly across care, fairness, loyalty, authority and sanctity; liberals respond strongly to care and fairness and barely at all to the other three.

(3) So conservative moral judgment is likelier to be right than liberal moral judgment.

(4) Nothing in (1)–(2) turns on that particular list, and the list isn't even fixed — a liberty/oppression foundation got bolted on after the original five turned out to describe libertarians badly, and fairness has since been split into equality and proportionality. Someone whose intuitions also fire on honor, purity of lineage, deference to elders, and the sacredness of the soil is responsive to a wider range still.

(5) So the same inference makes him likelier to be right than the conservative, and it keeps going: the more traditionalist and disgust-prone, the closer to moral truth, all the way up to the blood-and-soil nationalist.

(6) Nobody should sign onto (5), so (1) is false, and the psychology of left and right tells us nothing about who's actually right.

He says (4)–(5) is just guilt by association dressed up as a reductio. I don't buy that, but I also can't point to the step where it slips. And I'm reluctant to dump (1), because it looks like the only thing that makes the charge of moral blindness mean anything — if being responsive to fewer considerations isn't a defect, what's the complaint against the other side supposed to be? So which step goes, why did it look fine when I first read it, and if the answer really is (1), what am I giving up and is there any weaker version of it I can keep?
