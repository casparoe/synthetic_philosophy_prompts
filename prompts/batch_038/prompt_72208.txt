I have been a backend and ML-infrastructure engineer for about twelve years and have no formal training in philosophy, though I have been reading on my own about determinism, agency, and machine intelligence. I have accumulated a set of views that I now suspect do not all fit together, and I would like an audit rather than a lecture. Please identify which items below genuinely conflict with which others, rank the conflicts by severity, state for each what I would have to give up and what that revision would cost me, and separately flag the pairs that only appear to conflict and dissolve once they are stated precisely. I am not asking which position is correct in the literature; I am asking what my own set commits me to.

1. I am a physicalist about minds in the plain sense that I do not believe anything nonphysical happens inside a skull. Deliberation is a physical process, and physical processes are either deterministic or deterministic plus noise. I do not see a third category.

2. Free will, if it amounts to anything, is a matter of an agent's actions issuing from its own reasons-tracking machinery in a way that tracks what the reasons actually are. I do not think it requires being able to do otherwise in some metaphysically loaded sense. The alternative picture looks to me like wanting a miracle rather than wanting an agent.

3. I find the digger wasp story genuinely illuminating. As I understand it, roughly: the wasp paralyzes a caterpillar, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, goes inside to check the nest, comes back out, drags the prey to the entrance again, and repeats the routine indefinitely if an observer keeps moving the prey a few inches while she is underground. My reading is that she is not stupid but rigid: her behavior is exquisitely fitted to a normal environment and blind to an alternative sitting right in front of her. I use that as my working test for whether a system is choosing at all — a system that cannot register an available alternative is running a routine, however elaborate. (I have heard the underlying ethology is shakier than the retellings suggest, but I am using the story as an illustration, not as evidence.)

4. Flexibility is a matter of degree, not of kind. There is a continuum running from a thermostat through the wasp, a crow, a dog, and a person, and I do not believe anything sharp happens anywhere along it.

5. The language-model agents we ship are flexible by any measure I can operationalize. They handle inputs nobody designed for, they revise a plan mid-task when I raise an objection, and they will abandon a course of action they were halfway through. I would not describe them as sphexish.

6. That said, the wasp test only probes the perturbations I happen to think of. Last month one of our agents re-issued the same failing tool call twelve times in a row with the error output sitting in its own context window, which looked to me exactly like the wasp loop. I explained it to my team as a defect in our scaffolding rather than a property of the model, but I can see that this splits the system at a joint I chose for convenience: the harness is what lets the model's flexibility show up, and the model is what the harness is made of.

7. When I want to explain away an impressive behavior, I say that it is just a fixed array of parameters evaluated by a fixed decoding procedure: given the parameters and the prompt, the output is what it is, and the system could not have produced anything else. On that description it is a wasp with an astronomically large repertoire of routines.

8. I do not think stochastic sampling rescues anything. Raising the temperature makes an agent less predictable, not more of an author. Noise is not agency.

9. I nevertheless hold that there is a sharp line where responsibility is concerned: a person can step back on a reason and reject it, and that is why praise, blame, and obligation attach to people and not to software. I also accept that nobody bootstraps themselves — the way you respond to reasons is the product of a genome and a history you did not choose — and I do not think that gets anyone off the hook.

10. On the practical side I have argued in design reviews that questions of freedom are beside the point for deployment, because accountability sits with the people who build and ship the system regardless. In those same reviews I have also said that the agent decided to do something, when that framing helped explain a failure or argue against adding a human checkpoint.

A few things I would like addressed specifically. Is item 7 an argument against item 2, and if so, what entitles me to run it against our models and not against my colleagues? Does the model-versus-harness split in item 6 mark a real boundary in the world, or is it a place to park evidence I do not like? Can the test in item 3 and the continuum in item 4 jointly support the sharp line item 9 needs? Does the second half of item 9 do to persons what item 7 does to machines, and if it does not, why not? Is item 8 doing independent work, or is it a restatement of item 2? And is item 10 a genuine inconsistency or merely instrumental talk that commits me to nothing? Please give me the tensions ordered by severity, the cheapest repair for each, and an explicit list of the pairs that look contradictory but are not.
