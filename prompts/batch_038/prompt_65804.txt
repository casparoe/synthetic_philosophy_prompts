I am preparing the week on superrationality for a graduate decision-theory seminar, and there is a difficulty in Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma that I have never seen handled with the precision it deserves. I should like your help getting clear on it.

The case, as Hofstadter set it up in his Scientific American column and then again in Metamagical Themas: an eccentric trillionaire gathers twenty people, forbids any communication among them, and announces that whoever is the sole sender of a telegram by noon the following day receives a billion dollars, while if more than one telegram arrives, or none at all, nobody is paid. The recommendation he draws out is that each of the twenty should send with probability one in twenty, which gives the group something on the order of a thirty-seven percent chance that exactly one telegram arrives.

As I read the underlying game, that recommendation cannot be a Nash equilibrium. On the usual independence assumption, if each of the other nineteen sends with any probability below one, then sending is strictly better for me than abstaining, since abstaining pays nothing in every state of the world whatever, whereas sending pays a billion whenever nobody else does. So the profile in which everyone mixes at one in twenty is not stable against unilateral deviation, and the only symmetric equilibrium is the one in which everybody sends and nobody is paid. The equilibria that do pay are all asymmetric, and settling which player is to be the sole sender is precisely what the rules of the dilemma forbid. Superrationality therefore does not look like a refinement of the standard solution concept in this case; it looks like a repudiation of the assumption that makes dominance reasoning go through. My first question is what that assumption is, stated exactly, and whether surrendering it is a move within the theory of individual rational choice or a covert substitution of a group's interest for an individual's.

My second concern is a strain inside the recommendation itself. It requires the twenty reasoners to be identical at the level of policy yet statistically independent at the level of realization. If they genuinely are running the same procedure on the same information, they arrive at the same verdict, and the two verdicts available to them — all send, all abstain — both pay nothing. So the superrationalist needs sameness of algorithm together with independence of random seeds, which is an oddly specific metaphysical package. I want you to say what would have to be true of the twenty people for that package to be available at all, and whether a principle of rationality is entitled to assume it or can merely hope for it.

To sharpen the matter I have invented a variant, and I should like you to work out what it implies, in words rather than in algebra. Suppose the trillionaire privately and truthfully assures each of the twenty that exactly nineteen of them are superrational thinkers who will reason as you do, and that the twentieth — you are not told which — will simply toss a fair coin to decide whether to send. What is the superrational recommendation now? My own hunch is that it is not one in nineteen, and that it in fact collapses into abstention, because the coin-tosser's fifty percent chance of being the sole sender already exceeds anything the nineteen could jointly add. Tell me whether that hunch survives scrutiny, and if it does, tell me what it shows: whether superrationality has any content that is not an empirical wager on the composition of the group, and whether something whose deliverance swings this violently with an arbitrary piece of population statistics deserves to be called a principle of rationality at all.

That is where I want the analysis joined to what actually happened in the Luring Lottery, the contest Hofstadter ran through Scientific American in 1983, in which a reader wrote a number of entries on a postcard and the million-dollar prize was divided by the total number of entries received. The superrational advice was to randomize so that, were everyone to follow it, the magazine would receive a single postcard bearing a single entry. But that advice was published to a readership of unknown and demonstrably non-uniform composition, and entrants duly wrote down numbers so vast, several of them googolplexes, that the prize was divided into a minuscule fraction of a cent and the magazine could not identify a winner. I should like an account of whether that collapse was an unlucky implementation of a sound principle, or the principle's own presuppositions failing in public, where they were always going to fail.

Finally, please tell me how you think the idea is best classified. Is superrationality an early and informal relative of the view that one's own decision is evidence about, or logically entangled with, the decisions of others reasoning the same way — the family of views now discussed under evidential conditioning on one's own act, or under functional and updateless approaches — or is it something categorically different, namely a rule for teams that has been mistaken for a rule for individuals? Whichever reading you favor, I want it to earn its keep by explaining the fragility identified above, rather than merely labeling it.

Please write all of this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter rather than a report: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, just paragraphs running one into the next. Somewhere between twelve hundred and fifteen hundred words would be right. Where you think a hunch of mine is mistaken, say so plainly and show me where it goes wrong, rather than writing around it.
