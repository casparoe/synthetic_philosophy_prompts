ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on this anthropic reasoning stuff and I have nobody to argue with about it, so you're it.

Here's my understanding of the two camps. The self-sampling idea says you should reason as if you were randomly drawn out of the set of observers in your "reference class" — so if one theory says there's a trillion observers and another says only a million, the bare fact that you exist doesn't tilt you toward either one; you just work out what fraction of observers in each world would see the evidence you actually see. The self-indication idea sort of flips that: your existing at all *is* evidence for the bigger-universe theory, because there were more tickets in the hat with your name on one of them, so you should weight theories by how many observers they imply before you even get around to looking at your evidence.

The classic complaint against the second one is that it makes you ridiculous-ly presumptuous: some guy could sit in an armchair, invent a theory that predicts a googol observers, and claim he's now overwhelmingly justified in believing it without running a single experiment. And then apparently the two effects (biasing toward big worlds, and then diluting yourself inside them) mostly cancel, which people say rescues us from doomsday-style doom — but that cancellation smells like a coincidence to me.

Now here's my half-baked third option, and I want you to take it seriously rather than just dunk on it: instead of a hard in-or-out reference class, weight the candidate observers by *similarity to you* — same species, same memories-ish, same situation — on a gradient, not a switch. Call it similarity-weighted sampling. The thought is that the reference class problem, which everyone admits is fatal-ish for the first view, mostly dissolves because similarity comes in degrees you can actually cash out.
