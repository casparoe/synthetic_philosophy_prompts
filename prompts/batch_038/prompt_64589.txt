so I've been in a rabbit hole on al-Ghazālī's occassionalism for a paper and I've ended up with a package of views that I cant tell is coherent anymore. Reading group next week and they will absolutely go for it. Here's what I hold:

1. Full occasionalism, seventeenth-discussion style: created things have no causal powers whatsoever. When fire touches cotton it isn't the fire that does the burning — God creates the burning on that occasion. Contact is an occasion, never an agent.

2. This doesn't wreck knowledge or induction, contra everyone who says Ghazālī has to end up a sceptic. God keeps to a habit, the ʿāda, and the stability of those sequences is exactly what our projections track when we reason about the future.

3. But God's keeping to that habit isn't grounded in any plan, promise, wisdom, or standing intention. Nothing in God's nature and nothing about God's earlier choices explains or constrains the next choice. At every moment God wills fresh, and that is the whole story — divine freedom is not hedged in by a disposition God merely happens to have.

4. Counterfactuals about unobserved cases are literally true or false, not useful fictions. "If you'd dropped that glass it would have shattered" is true, full stop.

5. A miracle is a real suspension, not merely a novelty. There's a genuine difference between God declining to do the thing he habitually does and God doing something we happen never to have seen, and that difference is in principle available to us — which is why miracles can be evidence for anything at all.

6. Kasb: God creates the act, the human being acquires it, and that acquisition is enough for the human to deserve praise or blame for it.

7. Occasionalism doesn't force you into instrumentalism about science. Laws can be true descriptions of how the world behaves even though nothing in the world has a power that makes them true.

Go through this as an audit, not a lecture: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, how badly, and what has to be surrendered in each case. Rank them by severity instead of treating everything as equally fatal, and be straight with me where a tension is only apparent — I don't want five alarms if two of them are nothing. I'm deliberately not telling you which of these I'd be happiest to lose, so don't guess at my priorities, just trace what commits me to what.

Format: exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with nothing before or after the list. Items one through four are the pairs (real or only apparent) in order of severity, each saying what would have to go. Item five says which single change to the list repairs the most damage and what it costs me to make it.
