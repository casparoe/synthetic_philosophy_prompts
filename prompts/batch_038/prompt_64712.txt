My colleague writes a monthly column for a small design mag where she rates one everyday artifact per issue on a single question: does this thing have politics, yes or no. She never states her criterion, she's just an STS person so obviously Winner's old essay is floating around in the back of it, plus a lot of skepticism about artifacts "causing" anything. I've been reading it for two years and I still can't predict her calls, so I want to actually work it out before next month's issue lands.

Her record so far:

- Robert Moses's Long Island parkway overpasses, built with clearances too low for buses: YES. She calls it the entry-level example and complains everyone thinks reciting it settles the whole debate.
- The mechanical tomato harvester that came out of the UC agricultural engineering work, together with the tougher-skinned tomato bred to survive it: YES.
- A bench with the armrest welded across the middle so nobody can lie down on it: YES.
- The British three-pin plug, fuse inside the plug itself, shutters over the live holes: YES. Two whole paragraphs on the decision to put the cost of safety on the manufacturer rather than the wiring.
- A nuclear power station: YES. But a photovoltaic panel on its own: NO — her gloss was that the panel is politically promiscuous, and the fight lives in the tariff and the ownership model, not the silicon.
- The QWERTY keyboard: NO. This one shocked me. I figured lock-in was exactly her thing — one layout wins by accident, everybody's stuck with it forever, alternatives are unrecoverable. She said it was a coordination problem with a very long memory and nothing more.
- An ergonomic mesh office chair: NO. Comfort isn't politics.

Now, next month's subject is a food hall, and I have the details because the developer did a talk here. Two layouts were costed and both fit the same budget and the same footprint. Layout A was a normal mix — two-tops, four-tops, a handful of long tables. Layout B is nearly all long communal tables, benches fixed to the floor, no loose chairs anywhere. They built B. The architect's design statement says the whole point was that strangers should have to share a table, and that a food hall shouldn't be a place where you can eat alone. The developer's own feasibility memo notes that A would have turned roughly 15 percent more covers per hour, and they went with B anyway. Same prices, same vendors either way. No membership, no cameras, no data collection, no reservations — it's open to anyone and nobody seats you, you find a spot. Under B nobody loses access to anything and no group comes out ahead of any other group; the only difference is that you might end up elbow to elbow with someone you don't know.

So: what does she say, yes or no?

Lay out the candidate rules that are all equally consistent with the seven verdicts above — I can see at least a couple, and I suspect one of them is a version of "a contested question got settled by a design choice," another is "somebody predictably gains at somebody else's expense," and there's probably one about whether the thing only runs under a particular kind of standing authority. Then tell me which of them you think she's actually running, and why the food hall splits them. If the record genuinely doesn't decide it, say what she'd be committing herself to with each possible answer, because I'd like to be able to call her on it either way. And propose the one artifact I should pitch for the issue after next that would discriminate between your top two rules most cleanly — I want something where they point in opposite directions, not something where they both shrug.

Don't bother with references or a reading list, I've got the essays, I just want the reasoning. And be honest about the epistemics: give me how confident you are in the verdict you land on, roughly, and single out the step in your reconstruction of her rule that you're least sure of — I'd rather have a shaky rule with the shaky part flagged than a confident guess that turns out to be reading my own views into her column.
