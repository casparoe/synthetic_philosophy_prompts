I run a small office at a mid-sized city — civic engagement, so it's me and two part-time staff. We keep a registry of recognized community organizations, and recognition isn't just a certificate: it gets you a guaranteed slot to speak at planning hearings, first crack at the seats on advisory commissions, and a couple thousand dollars in operating money each year. We get around sixty applications a year and we have maybe 45 minutes per file between the two of us, then a volunteer panel of five residents signs off. They meet monthly, and their decision can be appealed to council, so whatever we write down has to survive being read out loud by someone who disagrees with it.

The applications have gotten harder lately because a lot of what shows up is frankly political: a national advocacy group's local chapter, a tenant union, a homeowners association organized entirely around stopping fourplexes, a mutual-aid network that insists it takes no positions on anything. We have no framework for any of it, and I don't want to invent an orthodoxy test — we cant screen on ideology, and if we start rejecting groups for having strong opinions or for being loud we'll be in a bad place both legally and morally.

What I've got instead is Simone Weil's short essay arguing for the abolition of all political parties, which a staffer put in my hand. My understanding of it, roughly: she says the criterion for public affairs is truth, justice and the public interest, and that majority rule is only ever a means toward those and not good in itself; she says a party in the continental sense is three things at once — a machine for generating collective passion, an organization built to exert pressure on the minds of its own members, and something whose first and final aim is its own unlimited growth, so the party ends up being the end and its stated purpose the means; and she also says the word means something different in Anglo-Saxon countries, where parties carry an element of sport. Her claim seems to be that these features don't make people stupid, they make it expensive to say what you actually see.

Turn that into a procedure my office can actually run. I want the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each (we only have the application packet, the bylaws, the last six months of their public postings, and a 30-minute interview — we never get to observe their internal meetings, so anything that depends on seeing how they treat a dissenter from the inside has to be marked as unanswerable), a threshold for each question, the points where it stops with a no, and the points where the issue is really about values and has to be handed to the panel rather than decided by staff. Tell me which of Weil's distinctions absolutely have to survive the conversion into checkboxes and which ones can be dropped, and be honest about where the thing misfires: what kinds of group it wrongly rejects, what kinds it wrongly waves through.

Then run it on three: (a) a tenant union whose members pledge to show up for each others eviction defenses, meet in closed session, and take direction from two paid organizers; (b) the local chapter of a national advocacy organization that runs endorsements and get-out-the-vote drives but is also the only group in town providing translation and childcare at our hearings; (c) a small association of retirees who read staff reports, file very detailed comments, and refuse on principle to endorse anything or recruit new members. One of these should come out against us — I mean the procedure should honestly tell us not to recognize the group we most want to recognize — and if it does, say what we're supposed to do about that, not just shrug.

Last thing: Weil's own conclusion is to get rid of parties altogether, and we obviously can't do that from a city office with two part-time staff and a spreadsheet. If turning her argument into a screening tool already betrays what she's arguing, say so and say where the betrayal bites.
