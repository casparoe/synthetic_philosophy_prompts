Forty-odd years in an analytical chemistry lab, retired three years now, and I've stumbled into philosophy late — there's a reading group at the library and I go mostly for the company. We're currently in Dennett's paper Quining Qualia, and specifically the bit with the two coffee drinkers, Chase and Sanborn: same cheap supermarket coffee both of them used to love, both of them have stopped loving it, but they describe the change in opposite ways. One says the coffee tastes exactly as it always did and what's changed is his reaction to it — his judgment, his standards, his mood about it. The other insists the taste itself has changed for him, that it genuinely presents differently now, and it's his reaction that's stayed loyal.

Here's my problem. I'm the one who keeps dragging my old working life into the discussion, and last week somebody finally told me to go and find out whether my analogy means anything at all. So: please audit it, honestly, and tell me if I've been arguing from a lab coat.

The analogy is a drifting gauge. When one of my instruments started giving a different readout on the same reference standard, nobody in the lab ever said the standard had changed. We said the instrument had. And crucially we could work out *which*, and where the drift sat, because we had things to compare against: a second instrument off the shelf, a certified reference material, a spec sheet, a blank run. Drift is only a diagnosis because there's an independent handle on the quantity. So I tell the group: the coffee in the tin hasn't changed, therefore whatever changed is in the man, and the only remaining question is whether it moved in the sensing part or in the judging part — which is precisely what Chase and Sanborn disagree about. Which means, I say, there *is* a fact of the matter here, we've just not done the calibration work to settle it. Half the group thinks that's a category error wearing a lab coat, and one of them says the whole thing collapses because qualia are supposed to be private, intrinsic, ineffable and directly given — so there is no second gauge, no reference material, nothing to check the readout against, and my analogy assumes exactly the thing that's missing.

What I'd like from you:

- The correspondence table. What in the coffee case plays the gauge, the readout, the quantity being measured, the drift, the reference standard, the second instrument, the spec sheet. Be explicit where something in my lab has no counterpart over there — that's presumably where the trouble is.
- Where the mapping actually breaks, and please separate the breaks that matter for the use I'm putting it to from the ones that are just cosmetic. For instance: an instrument can be wrong in a way its owner can't detect, and that's a big part of why drift is a real thing. Can a person be wrong about how the coffee tastes to them in that same sense? If not, does the analogy still earn its keep?
- What my version quietly imports. I suspect it sneaks in a third-person vantage point on a first-person state, and possibly a split between "the sensing" and "the judging" that is itself just the qualia posit in new clothes — i.e. that I've assumed there's a readout separable from the dispositions to report and react, when that separability is the very thing under dispute. If that's right, say so plainly.
- Whether my conclusion survives. Not "is the analogy colourful" but: once the disanalogies are on the table, is there still a determinate fact about whether it was Chase's tasting or Chase's judging that changed, or is the question one that has no answer because nothing could count as evidence either way? And which of the two coffee drinkers, if either, comes out better on the analogy — including the possibility that the analogy actually favours Dennett against me.
- Whether there's a better analogy from measurement practice, if there is one. I can think of cases where we had no external standard — self-calibrating instruments, or a whole lab's worth of kit drifting together so the drift is invisible from inside — and I don't know whether those help or whether they're just the same hole with more machinery in it.
- And a plain-language account of what Dennett himself takes the coffee case to show about qualia as a notion, since I want to check I'm not arguing against a straw man of my own making.

One more thing, and I'd take it as a favour: at the end, tell me what would change your verdict. Which consideration, if I or someone in the group pressed it, would make you say the analogy does hold after all — or make you say there is a fact of the matter even without any calibration story. And point at where you think your own reasoning here is weakest or most likely to be leaning on a reading of Dennett that's contested. I've spent a career being wrong in public about small things, so I'd rather hear it from you now than from the group on Tuesday.
