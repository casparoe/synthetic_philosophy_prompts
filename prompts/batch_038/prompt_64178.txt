I'm a novelist — third book, currently out next spring if I ever finish this one scene — and my editor and I are stuck on a single line of dialogue. Vera is a retired neuroscientist in her late sixties. Her daughter Marguerite has spent three hundred-odd pages assembling a case that Vera lied about the circumstances of Marguerite's father's death, and the confrontation ends with Marguerite saying: "You knew he was drinking again. You told us he wasn't. That's a lie, and you knew it was." Vera's answer is the line I need help with: **there's no such thing as belief — nobody has ever believed anything, not me, not you, not the imaginary jury you're pleading to.** Marguerite fires back that Vera has just stated a belief, which my philosophy friends tell me is the thing called a performative contradiction, and then I cut away, because I had no idea where to go next.

I know the neighbourhood vaguely — the Churchlands, Stich, Dennett and the intentional stance, Fodor pushing back the other way, the phlogiston comparison — but I can't tell whether those people are even disagreeing about the same sentence, and that's exactly my problem as a writer: one flat line of dialogue is doing several different jobs and I don't know which. So: I'd like you to act as a philosopher of mind who has genuinely worked through the eliminativism debate about propositional attitudes *and* who reads fiction closely enough to care what a character is trying to do with a sentence. Think carefully and brainstorm the space first before you commit to anything. Give me a menu — not an essay on one favourite reading — of the precise claims that "there's no such thing as belief" could be expressing, sorted roughly from tamest to most radical. I'd like to see the axes made explicit rather than just a list of -isms: is the claim about what exists internally, about whether belief has determinate content, about whether beliefs are the real causal explainers of behaviour, about whether "belief" picks out one natural kind or several, about first-person authority, about the truth of ordinary talk versus the truth of psychological explanation, about scope (all attitudes, or belief only, or conscious belief only, or narrow content only). For each reading, give me two or three plain-English sentences a smart non-philosopher could follow, what would have to be the case for it to be true, and a verdict: platitude, substantive but defensible, probably false, or self-undermining. Please also flag which readings are ones working philosophers actually hold and which are just things people say at parties. Don't send me a reading list or any citations — I can't chase them and I don't want to.

Then do the craft part. Which reading, if any, actually earns Vera what she wants from the line: not "you can't prove it," but the accusation being empty, so that there's no fact of the matter about what she believed in 1994? Does the daughter's comeback defeat that reading, all the readings, or only the loud ones — and is it really a contradiction, or something weaker, like the sentence being true while the speech act is fine? Is there a version where Vera is right *and* Marguerite still lands the blow? Compare it with the softer variant I almost wrote instead, "I never believed any of it," and tell me what's philosophically different about those two lines, because I suspect that difference is the whole scene. Finally, I want you to commit rather than hand me a balanced buffet: tell me which reading Vera is most plausibly making given who she is (a wet-lab person who read around, not a specialist), which one she *should* be making for the scene to survive, why those might not be the same, and what the smallest change to the dialogue would be if you think the scene as it stands is broken. Close with a short note on where your menu leans on a contested way of carving up the debate, or where a working philosopher would tell me you've been too generous to Vera.
