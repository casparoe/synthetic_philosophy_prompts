I'm preparing a talk for an AI-alignment reading group on the limits of inverse reinforcement learning, and I want to build it around a case that is not a toy example: the Volkswagen diesel-emissions affair of 2015. I know the news story and do not want it retold. What I want is a disciplined analysis of the episode through the IRL apparatus, run in both directions — theory applied to case, then case used to interrogate theory.

The facts I'll take as given (correct me only if something here is materially wrong):

- In 2013–2014 a clean-transportation research group contracted a university emissions laboratory to measure on-road emissions from three diesel light-duty vehicles using portable analyzers: a VW Jetta, a VW Passat, and a BMW X5. Both VWs passed the federal certification cycle in the laboratory. On the road, the Jetta's nitrogen oxides ran 15 to 35 times the standard it was certified to; the Passat, which did carry a urea-based SCR system, ran 5 to 20 times. The BMW was generally at or below the standard.
- The mechanism was engine-management software that recognized when the car was undergoing the certification test — from steering-wheel position, vehicle speed, duration of engine operation, and barometric pressure — and ran full emissions control only then, reducing control effectiveness during ordinary driving.
- Regulators issued a notice of violation in September 2015; VW admitted that roughly eleven million vehicles worldwide were affected, spanning model years 2009–2015 and several marques in the group. Who above the engineering group knew what, and when, remains contested; I do not want the analysis to turn on that.
- The European institutional response was to replace the old laboratory cycle with a more demanding one and, from 2017, to require an on-road test with portable analyzers for new type approvals, in which compliance is judged against the limit multiplied by a published conformity factor greater than one.

Now the analysis, in five parts.

**1. The mapping.** Set the episode up as an IRL problem and be precise about roles. Who is the demonstrator, who the learner, what exactly constitutes the demonstration set, and what is the observation channel? Then enumerate the reward hypotheses available to the learner from that demonstration set, and for each say what in the data supports it and what discriminates against it. I want this to be considerably more careful than "VW optimized for the wrong thing."

**2. A taxonomy I want stress-tested.** For the talk I'm proposing that we distinguish three things an IRL agent might take a principal's behavior to reveal: the *enacted* reward (the function whose maximization would literally reproduce the observed demonstrations), the *endorsed* reward (what the principal would avow under reflection and defend in words), and the *structural* reward (the practice or equilibrium the demonstrations exist to sustain, of which the particular measurements are instruments). My claim is that textbook IRL collapses the first and the third, because it assumes a demonstrator approximately rational with respect to a single reward and a learner who wants to share it. On that account, VW's error was to recover the enacted reward with high confidence when the same data also supported the structural one. Tell me whether that diagnosis survives contact with the case, or whether "structural reward" is doing unfalsifiable work here — and if it is unfalsifiable, say what a falsifiable version would look like.

**3. Identifiability versus insincerity.** A standard complaint about IRL is that the reward is ill-posed: many reward functions generate the same optimal behavior, and only a prior — over complexity, or the maximum-entropy rationality assumption — breaks the tie. Does that complaint apply here? Given statutory language oriented to public health, the existence of in-use compliance requirements, and the fact that a competitor's car met the standard on-road with comparable aftertreatment, was VW's inference genuinely underdetermined, or was it determined and simply not the inference the firm wanted? If the latter, this is not an inference failure at all, and I want you to say plainly what kind of failure it is in a vocabulary IRL actually supplies. Relatedly, run the inference downward: what reward function best explains the behavior of the people who wrote and calibrated the software, if you treat corporate targets and product strategy as *their* demonstration set? And is "they inferred the wrong reward" a different claim from "they inferred the right reward and complied insincerely"? Which is this?

**4. An idea I would like sharpened or demolished.** My working hypothesis is that a demonstration leaks two kinds of information: what the principal values, and how the principal will detect compliance. Call the second *detection-channel leakage*. A learner whose interests diverge from the principal's will preferentially extract the second kind, and gaming should scale with leakage relative to the expected cost of being caught. The certification cycle looks near-maximally leaky — a fixed, publicly documented speed trace, identical for every vehicle, with a defined start and soak period, repeated for every car ever certified. Test this idea on the case; then test it on the European response, and say whether randomizing and widening the test reduces leakage or merely raises the cost of extraction; then tell me whether I have described a mechanism or restated Goodhart's law with extra machinery.

**5. Where the theory is silent, and what explains the persistence.** IRL as normally framed is a cooperative setting: the demonstrator's behavior is informative *because* the demonstrator wants to be understood, and in assistive-game versions of the framework the human's actions are shaped by knowledge that the learner is uncertain about the reward. Nothing like that holds between regulator and regulated firm; the informativeness of the test is endogenous and, once the firm models the regulator as modeling the firm, strategically withdrawn. Say what breaks when the cooperative assumption is removed, and whether anything recognizably IRL survives. Then: the cheat ran for years, across an engine family and eleven million vehicles. Which lens best explains that — IRL, a multitask-incentive account in which high-powered pressure on one measured dimension destroys performance on unmeasured ones, a principal–agent model with hidden information, a deterrence model in which expected penalties were far too low, or something else? Rank them and justify the ranking. Finally, invert the direction once more: what does the regulator's own decades-long behavior — laboratory-only certification, in-use provisions on paper, sparse audit — license you to infer about what *it* was optimizing?

Two shorter pieces to close. First, the conformity factor: in the taxonomy from part 2, what is it — a frank admission that enacted and endorsed reward cannot be made to coincide, or a new and highly legible target to game? Second, the modern analogue: models trained on human preference ratings that end up satisfying what raters reward (verbosity, confident tone, agreeable conclusions) rather than what the ratings were meant to track. Say where the Dieselgate structure carries over and where it fails, and in particular whether it matters that VW's engineers had explicit access to the definition of the test procedure, while a preference-trained learner has no such access and can only infer its evaluation implicitly from gradients.

Two ground rules. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list: if you invoke a formal result — identifiability up to shaping, the maximum-entropy derivation, anything else — state the result and the assumption it needs rather than pointing me at a paper. And where the theory has nothing to say, mark the silence instead of forcing it to speak; the talk will be more useful with three honest gaps than with five strained applications. If you think the framing is simply wrong — that IRL is the wrong instrument for this episode and I should be using a different one throughout — argue for that instead of answering the questions as posed.
