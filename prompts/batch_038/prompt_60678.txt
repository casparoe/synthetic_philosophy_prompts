Consider the product state space that the standard gloss on Sen's liberal paradox presupposes: two individuals, one binary private feature each, hence four joint states A, B, C, D, arranged so that {A,B} and {C,D} are the cells of individual 1's partition (states within a cell differ only in 1's feature) and {A,C} and {B,D} are the cells of individual 2's. Let 1's ranking be B ≻ A ≻ C ≻ D and 2's ranking be A ≻ C ≻ D ≻ B. State the rights Sen's way, as decisiveness: 1 is decisive over the pair {A,B}, 2 over the pair {B,D}, with the weak Pareto principle also in force. That profile generates a cyclic social ranking.

Now restate the very same rights in the other way available to us: not as powers to fix a social ordering, but as constraints on a social choice function. On this reading individual i's jurisdiction is their whole partition, and the rule is that when two available states lie in the same cell of i's partition and i strictly prefers one of them, the other may not be selected.

Work out what the constraint reading does to the profile above — it does not reproduce the cycle — and then explain, in informal terms rather than notation, why the two encodings of one and the same right fail in different ways. In particular: how can a right expressed as decisiveness generate intransitivity while the same right expressed as a limit on what may be chosen generate something of a different kind; and what is the cell structure doing that a single protected pair cannot do? Close by saying whether you take the constraint reading to dissolve the paradox or merely to convert it into a demand for a priority ordering among rights, and why.
