I'm a backend engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and a few weeks ago I read a long general-interest essay on the ethics of border control that I have been arguing about with two colleagues ever since. I lean toward open borders, but my suspicion is that the essay is unfair to the position it is summarizing: it sounds sympathetic, and that is exactly what worries me, because a badly executed steelman is harder to spot than a hostile one. Before I write this up for the informal reading group I run at work, I would like an accuracy audit rather than my own guesswork. Below is the passage that matters. I have transcribed the substance faithfully but changed the wording in places so that I am not reproducing the article verbatim, and I have kept the author's order of moves:

"The cleanest defense of a state's right to exclude does not come from economics or from cultural preservation; it comes from freedom of association. The version everyone cites now runs roughly as follows. A state is legitimate only if it actually performs the function of protecting the basic rights of the people inside its territory, and a legitimate state is entitled to political self-determination. Self-determination includes freedom of association, and freedom of association includes the right to refuse to associate. So just as you may decline to marry someone who wants to marry you — and just as your right to stay single is not outweighed by the fact that the other person would be much happier married to you than to anyone else — a legitimate state may decline to admit anyone at all. Notice what this does to the usual framing: borders turn out to be a question of liberty, not of property. The state is not a landowner keeping trespassers off its land; it is a collective project deciding who joins. Three consequences follow, and they are where the view earns its bad reputation. First, the right to exclude is close to absolute, because a basic liberty may be restricted only to prevent harm to others, and someone's being poor in another country is not a harm this state caused; so the state may turn away people whose lives would be much better inside. Second, aid and admission are interchangeable: whatever duties a rich state owes the global poor, it can discharge them by funding refugee agencies abroad rather than by admitting anyone, since what morality requires is that the suffering stop, not that the sufferer be let in. Third, the standard objection that this licenses racially discriminatory selection misses the point. The right belongs to the state as an association rather than to individuals, and a state that excluded on racial grounds would fail the legitimacy condition in any case, so the argument rules that possibility out from the start. The most interesting remaining reply — that freedom of association would equally entitle cities, neighborhoods, and private clubs to exclude — is answered by the observation that only states perform the function of securing rights. The most influential open-borders writer, Joseph Carens, accepts the logic of the analogy but argues it proves too little: marriage is intimate and chosen, whereas being born into a jurisdiction is neither, so birthright membership is as arbitrary as race and cannot be permitted to fix anyone's life chances."

Please go through this claim by claim and sort it into three buckets: what a defender of this line of argument would sign off on without qualification, what is a fair simplification of something considerably more complicated, and what is simply wrong. I do not know where the errors are; my instinct is that the three numbered consequences are the trouble and that the setup is fine, but I cannot say why, and I may have that backwards.
