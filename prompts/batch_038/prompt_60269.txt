I am a senior at a public high school, and for reasons I still find slightly surreal, I ended up chairing our school's Environmental Affairs Committee, which the county mosquito abatement district has invited to submit a short student recommendation ahead of its spring hearing on next season's control program. We have roughly fourteen weeks, twelve members, and almost no expertise: eight of us are in AP Biology and can read a toxicity label, two are genuinely interested in philosophy, one is a sophomore who thinks the entire project is a waste of time because insects are "just biological robots," and our faculty advisor has warned us that the district's staff entomologist is courteous but will not be impressed by sentiment. The committee's actual output is supposed to be a two-page public comment plus a primer we post on the school website. Before I divide up the work, I want to know what is worth working on at all, and I would rather get that wrong deliberately than spend fourteen weeks on a question whose answer changes nothing.

Here are the cruxes we keep circling, and I suspect some of them are far more important than the others. The district currently does both larviciding with a bacterial product in the storm drains and marshy ditches, and truck-mounted fogging with a pyrethroid adulticide a few nights a summer. Killing larvae seems gentler in that each one has lived less of a life, but if an insect's life is on balance worth living, then killing early removes the only good that individual was going to have, and it is not obvious which way that cuts. There is also the replacement worry: a sprayed neighborhood next season may simply hatch a comparable number of mosquitoes, so individual deaths might not change the total, whereas methods that suppress populations over time — the sterile-insect releases people keep mentioning in the news — reduce future numbers without killing anything directly, which makes them look almost cost-free until you ask what happened to all the larvae that were going to be produced. Then there is resistance, which means today's choice may determine how harsh the chemicals of 2035 have to be. And running underneath all of it is the question that makes my advisor roll his eyes: insects outnumber vertebrates so astronomically that even a very small probability that they experience something like pain rather than mere nociception seems to swamp every other consideration, and I cannot tell whether that inference is a genuine discovery or a piece of arithmetic that ought to embarrass us. I have also read, secondhand and possibly inaccurately, that bumblebees show something like optimistic or pessimistic judgement biases in choice tasks, which strikes me as the only kind of evidence that could ever bear on the question, and I do not know whether a high school group could design anything remotely comparable.

What I want from you is a defended agenda, not a survey. Take the cruxes I have listed, add any that I have missed entirely, and tell me which two or three our twelve people and one semester should actually prioritize, in what order, and why. For each, I want your reasoning made explicit along dimensions I care about in roughly this sequence: how much of the rest of the analysis depends on settling it first, whether a group with no lab, no statistics background, and no access to researchers can make any real progress on it rather than just summarizing other people's progress, whether resolving it would plausibly change what the district does next spring as opposed to merely sounding interesting, and whether it is underdiscussed relative to how often people already rehearse it in animal-ethics conversations. I would also like you to say plainly which of my cruxes are overrated, which are just the familiar trolley-problem furniture repainted with wings, and which are unresolvable by us in principle, so that we report the disagreement honestly instead of pretending to settle it. Please name the single question you would build the whole project around if you could only build it around one, describe what evidence or development over the coming year would move any question up or down in your ordering, and sketch roughly how the fourteen weeks should be divided among the priorities you keep. One constraint: do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations, because I have no way of retrieving them and guessed-at references would be worse than none; if you need to invoke a study or an argument to make a point, describe its content in a sentence and move on.
