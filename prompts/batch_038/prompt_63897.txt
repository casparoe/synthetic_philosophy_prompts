I hold a chair in practical philosophy at a Northern European university, and on the last Sunday in April I am to deliver the principal address at the dedication of a memorial in the Central European provincial city where I did my doctoral fieldwork thirty years ago. The stone stands on the site of a rail siding and adjacent brickyard from which, over a few weeks in the spring and early summer of 1944, roughly eleven thousand of the city's Jewish residents were assembled and deported, together with several hundred Roma families from the same quarter. The inscription has already been cast. It reads: *They were taken from this place. Civilization failed them. Never again.* I did not sit on the committee that chose it, and I have no standing to revise it.

The room, such as it will be: about forty people who are the children and grandchildren of those deported, several of whom read names aloud at this site every year and who will experience abstraction as an insult; the mayor, most of the council, and the county's director of procurement and compliance, whose working lives are budget lines, chain of approval, and the small task correctly executed; two classes of fifteen-year-olds from the district gymnasium, who have had one lesson on this subject and no more; three professional historians of modern Europe, one of whom has built a career arguing that these provincial deportations were driven above all by labor requisition and who will notice immediately if I flatten the historiography into a moral fable; two of the people standing closest to the stone at the unveiling are grandchildren of Roma families deported from the same siding in the same season, whose names are not on it, because a proposal to add a second panel was defeated by the committee five votes to four and both sides of that vote will be present; and a correspondent from the regional paper who will lift exactly one sentence.

I want the address built on the argument of Bauman's book on modernity and the Holocaust — that this was not a suspension of modern civilization nor a relapse into pre-modern barbarism, but something its ordinary apparatus made possible and, in places, efficient: the fragmentation of an act into tasks small enough that no one performs it whole; the substitution of responsibility toward a procedure for responsibility toward a person; the deliberate manufacture of distance, physical and administrative and psychic, between the one who acts and the one who suffers; the treatment of human beings as objects of classification and filing; the elevation of rule-following into a virtue in itself; and hence the conclusion that modern societies have not absorbed the lesson, because they have arranged the event as a picture hanging on a wall of the house rather than a window in it. The argument must be load-bearing, not decorative: the shape of the sentences, and what the address permits itself to say and refuses to say, should follow from it.

Several consequences I need you to manage rather than evade:

1. The stone says civilization failed them. On Bauman's argument, the consolatory reading of that sentence — that this was a breakdown, an eruption of something foreign to us — is very nearly false; what acted here was civilization functioning. I cannot repudiate a stone already cast by grieving people, and I will not repeat the sentence in a way that hands the room a monster story. Find a manner of inhabiting it.

2. The thesis places half of my audience inside the machinery. But this is a dedication, not an indictment, and I shall not accuse people who have come to mourn. The implication has to arrive without accusation, and without retreating into the harmless generality about bystanders that lets every listener classify himself as a rescuer.

3. Explanation of machinery must not dissolve agency. Persons decided; persons profited; persons wrote and signed and scheduled. One of the historians present has spent her life arguing against functionalist accounts that turn perpetrators into cogs, and she will be listening for the slide.

4. The Roma families are not named on the stone. The address cannot litigate the committee's vote, cannot pretend the omission away, and cannot confine itself to the dead it has been given — and it must not resolve this by the usual inflation of numbers or the phrase about all victims of all genocides, which will read, to the people nearest the stone, as a second erasure.

5. The fifteen-year-olds must leave holding something determinate — a task, not a feeling — and it must follow from the thesis rather than from the standard inventory of commemorative homework: tolerance, speaking up, remembering.

6. The address must nowhere assert that this is unspeakable, unrepresentable, beyond words, or beyond understanding. That is the move the thesis specifically forecloses, and it is also a way of closing the conversation while appearing to reverence it.

Formal requirements. My eleven minutes are inclusive of consecutive translation into Hungarian by a municipal interpreter, so the English must run about five and a half minutes — between 650 and 800 words — and must be translatable: no idiom, no wordplay, no irony, short clauses, no nested subordinates. Mark the two or three places where I should stop with a bracketed [pause]. Do not name Bauman, or any scholar, in the address itself; the historians will recognize the argument, and a citation at a graveside is a form of hiding. Supply no bibliography, no references, and no academic apparatus. If you want a line of scripture, poetry, or testimony, do not produce one from memory: leave a clearly marked placeholder with a note on what the line must do, and I will source it myself.

Deliverable: the address, then a separate note of no more than two hundred words identifying the two moments at which the thesis is doing genuine structural work; what a listener who has never encountered the argument will hear at each of those moments; the one sentence most likely to be quoted in the paper, and whether it survives being lifted; and your candid judgment of where the address is most likely to fail, and in which direction — tipping into sermon, or into a lecture this room cannot use.
