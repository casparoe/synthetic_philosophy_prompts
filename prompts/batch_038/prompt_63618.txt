I write the ideas column for a weekly that covers science and policy, and I am reporting a piece on the quiet return of deterrence as the organising assumption of great-power politics. Roughly half my interviews end with someone invoking Hegel, usually to insist that a long peace between heavily armed states is precisely the condition he thought would rot a people. Before I let that framing stand in print, I want it tested against his actual rulings rather than his reputation.

The track record I have assembled, paraphrased from his own texts:

- In the Philosophy of Right he assigns war an ethical meaning rather than treating it as a mere instrument, likening perpetual peace to a prolonged calm that would foul the sea and saying that corruption in nations would be the product of peace.
- He denies that there is any praetor or judge standing above states; they confront one another in a state of nature, and each is the power that decides which injuries it can absorb and which it cannot.
- He is sceptical of Kant's federation for perpetual peace, on the ground that a body able to hold particular wills in check would have to be a power above them and would itself become a source of enmity; treaties bind only as long as the parties will them.
- He supplies no criteria distinguishing wars of conquest from defensive wars, holds the welfare of the state to be the supreme law in its dealings with others, and allows that imminent danger can justify what would otherwise be prohibited.
- He treats the citizen's exposure to death as a universal duty, the moment in which the substantial tie between state and members becomes actual rather than notional.
- He treats recognition by other states as constitutive of statehood, and in his earlier work on the German constitution he said that Germany had ceased to be a state because it no longer acted as a whole.
- His 1806 letter describing the emperor entering Jena on horseback is rapturous rather than appalled: a world-historical individual's arrival with an army reads to him as the march of spirit.

The new case: two nuclear-armed great powers that have not fought each other since 1945. Both maintain survivable second-strike forces on continuous alert, both sign and then abandon arms-control agreements by particular decision, and both preside over settled, prosperous, administratively efficient civil societies whose citizens have not been asked to risk death in a general war within living memory.

What I need from you: which principles jointly fit the verdicts above, what each one predicts about this case, and whether Hegel would call it the stagnant calm he warned of or the state of nature among states working exactly as described — and, separately, whether he would count the deterrent powers as fully actual states at all.

Please give me the comparison as a Markdown table with exactly these columns: candidate principle (your own name for it, one line); grounds (which of the listed verdicts support it); prediction for the deterrence case (does it yield corruption-by-calm, legitimate state of nature, or something else, and what it says about the full-actuality question); confidence (high, medium, low); discriminating case (the specific historical or hypothetical case on which this principle and the strongest rival come apart). After the table, add three to five sentences giving your best single guess at his verdict and naming the one case that would settle which principle he was in fact running.
