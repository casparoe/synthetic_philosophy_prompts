Paul Benacerraf pressed two distinct challenges to platonism about the natural numbers, eight years apart. In the first, he argued that because two set-theoretic reconstructions of the natural numbers — Zermelo's and von Neumann's — are equally adequate, and nothing in mathematics or elsewhere singles one of them out as the progression the numbers really are, no individual set can be the number three; what a number is, is a position in a structure. In the second, he argued that a platonist semantics is what makes mathematical truth of a piece with truth in ordinary and scientific discourse, but that such a semantics makes mathematical knowledge inexplicable, since abstract objects are causally inert and knowledge of a subject matter seems to require some causal commerce with it.

Compare these two arguments. Are they independent challenges that a platonist must answer separately, or does one of them presuppose a conclusion the other has already established? Structuralism is conventionally offered as the upshot of the first argument and as no help whatsoever with the second; assess whether that division of labor is defensible. Finally, say which argument survives if the causal account of knowledge that the second relies on is simply dropped, and what, if anything, the platonist is then left to answer.
