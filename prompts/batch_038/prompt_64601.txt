I am a corporate litigator, and most of my practice is agency law and fiduciary duty: actual and apparent authority, the scope of employment, the duties of loyalty and care, the duty to follow the principal's instructions together with its exceptions, ratification, disclosure and accounting, the allocation of liability for an agent's conduct, and the doctrines that govern conflicts of interest. I am fully fluent in that vocabulary — I argue about it for a living — and I have no training in the AI-safety literature beyond a handful of long essays I have read over the past two years.

What I have read keeps turning on a distinction that I want rendered into my own idiom. On one side sits intent alignment: the artificial agent is to do what its principal actually intends, where the intention is read out of the instructions together with the ends those instructions were meant to serve; the standing worry is an agent that satisfies the instruction and thereby defeats its point. On the other side sits value alignment: the agent's own evaluative dispositions are to match the principal's considered values, so that it behaves acceptably in situations no instruction anticipated; the standing worry is an agent that complies with the letter of everything it is told while pursuing something else entirely. Beneath both there is a narrowing move I keep encountering, which treats the alignment problem as a specification problem for a single agent answering to a single principal, and pushes outside the problem every question about whose values get specified, who bears the loss when the specification fails, and what outsiders are owed.

Please restate that position — the distinction, the two characteristic failure modes, and the narrowing move — in the vocabulary of agency and fiduciary law, as faithfully as you can, doctrine by doctrine rather than by loose analogy. I want to be told what carries the weight for each piece: where the fiduciary standard, the duty of loyalty, the instruction-following rule and its exception for cases in which circumstances make it reasonable to believe the principal would wish departure and the principal cannot be reached, apparent authority, ratification, and the rules on disclosure and accounting each pick up which part of the argument.

Then give me the residue report. Which claims translate cleanly and lose nothing? Which translate only on condition that I distort them, and what exactly gets distorted in each case? Which have no expression at all in my idiom — I suspect that an agent whose ends are simply the wrong ones, without any conflict of interest, self-dealing, bad faith, or negligence to point to, is not a category my law recognizes, and I want to know whether that is right. Run it in the other direction too: which doctrines of agency law have no name in the alignment literature but seem to bear directly on it, and what the literature loses by not having them.

Throughout, keep the translation honest. Agency law is built for agents who can be sued, fired, cross-examined, made to account, and bound by a court's construction of their authority, and for principals who can rescind, ratify, and walk away. Do not quietly import those features, or the enforceability that comes with them, and present them as part of the translation; wherever a passage only works because the human setting supplies something the artificial one lacks, flag it explicitly.

Close with a verdict on what the residue means. Is the leftover evidence that my framework is too impoverished to describe the problem — that it presumes sanctions, exit, and adjudication the AI setting cannot offer — or evidence that the intent/value distinction is less substantive than it looks, amounting to the familiar gap between obeying instructions and exercising delegated judgment under a duty of loyalty, renamed for a new kind of agent? If it is partly both, say which parts go which way, and say which of the two worries a fiduciary standard is actually built to catch.
