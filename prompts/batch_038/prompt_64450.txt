I chair the Academic Integrity Board at a small liberal arts college, and I must circulate a decision memo before our hearing resumes on Tuesday. The case is unusually clean, which is precisely why it has split the panel three to three. Two students enrolled in different sections of the same first-year political theory course, taught by different instructors, submitted written responses to an unproctored take-home exercise that are identical to the character: 412 words, the same sentences in the same order, the same two typographical slips (a doubled article in the second paragraph, an idiosyncratic capitalization in the fourth), and the same improbable phrasing of a commonplace idea. The similarity software reports 100 percent overlap. Neither student appears in the other's residence-hall records, group chats, or shared cloud folders; they had no section meeting, no study group, and no mutual acquaintances in common. Student A produced a full document revision history showing eleven drafting sessions across six days, with deletions, reordered paragraphs, and the two typos introduced at different points in the process; Student B produced only a final file uploaded from a shared library machine, but supplied a spiral notebook containing a legible, corrected hand draft that matches the submission, together with a library entry log placing them at a carrel on two of the drafting evenings. Neither can identify the other, and neither has any motive to protect the other.

Article IV.3 of our Integrity Code governs, and reads in relevant part: "Where two submitted works are substantively identical, the Board shall presume that the identity arises from impermissible collaboration, and the burden of explanation shall rest upon the students." Read strictly, both are responsible unless they can affirmatively explain the identity, and one faction of the panel holds that no explanation has been offered and that the matter is closed. The other faction holds that the clause does no work at all in this case, because A's revision history and B's notebook each constitute exactly the sort of explanation the clause demands, and because the Code nowhere says what would count. At our last session the panel's lone philosopher member intervened with Max Black's 1952 thought experiment — the imagined universe containing nothing but two exactly similar spheres of chemically pure iron — to argue that qualitative indiscernibility does not entail numerical identity, and therefore that two indiscernible texts need not be a single text twice. I found the analogy alarming rather than reassuring: if it defeats the presumption here, it seems to defeat it everywhere, and textual identity is how nearly every case this Board hears is in fact proved.

Please draft the memo I will circulate under my name: a recommendation with a concrete disposition of this case and a concrete proposal for how Article IV.3 should be rewritten or applied at future hearings, argued in terms both factions could actually respond to. It should say what the presumption clause is committed to — whether it assumes that indiscernibility between two artifacts entails a single origin, whether that assumption is metaphysical in character or merely probabilistic with metaphysical clothing, and whether the disanalogy between generated things like texts and stipulated things like Black's spheres does any work for the inference. It should specify what standard of proof applies, what sorts of evidence can discharge a student's burden, and what the panel does when the evidence is symmetric and complete on both sides, so that a future board has criteria rather than intuitions. And it should treat the strongest case against my own inclination honestly: that a coincidence defense is cheap, unfalsifiable, and available to every actual copier; that the deterrent force of the presumption depends on its being close to conclusive; and that asking a board of faculty and students to weigh the probability of independent generation is to ask them to administer a standard they are not equipped to apply.
