I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for most of this year I have been reporting a dispute that generates a great deal of heat and almost no shared language. There are two camps.

The first I will call the validity camp: psychiatric researchers and clinicians who hold that a diagnostic category earns its place by carving nature at its joints. On this view, a good category predicts course of illness, differential response to treatment, aggregation in families, and eventually some measurable mechanism; and a sudden surge in identified cases of a previously under-recognized condition is usually evidence of improved detection, not of anything having been brought into being. The second I will call the looping camp: those who hold that classifications of people differ in kind from classifications of rocks or isotopes, because the classified learn the classification, recruit it into their self-understanding, and come to present differently as a result — so that prevalence, symptom content, and even the boundaries of a disorder can be partly manufactured by the naming, and so that the validity camp's evidential tests are contaminated by the very act of classifying.

Neither camp thinks the other is foolish. They have simply never managed to produce a shared statement of what they are actually disagreeing about. I would like you to write that statement: the joint document a serious adversarial collaboration between them could produce and both could sign.

To give the collaboration something to bite on, work from the following case. It is composite and stipulated; treat its details as given rather than trying to match them to any live controversy. A proposed category — call it detachment-spectrum presentation, for adults reporting emotional numbing, episodes of losing track of stretches of time, and a persistent sense of observing their own life rather than inhabiting it, typically following prolonged caregiving strain — is the subject of a four-part documentary series on a national broadcaster. Then:

(a) Before the broadcast, a specialty clinic's structured-interview positivity rate among walk-in referrals was roughly 8 percent. Eighteen months later it is roughly 41 percent, and total referrals meeting intake threshold have risen about twelvefold.

(b) Post-broadcast referrals describe their difficulties in vocabulary that tracks the narration closely, including several specific terms that had no local clinical currency beforehand and that recur in near-identical phrasing across otherwise unrelated patients.

(c) A national registry, using indicators that do not depend on the diagnosis — workplace and school functional-impairment records, emergency presentations, prescribing of a drug class used for unrelated purposes — shows no change in incidence across the same window.

(d) A genetic-epidemiology group reports meaningful family aggregation among post-broadcast probands, and reports it among first-degree relatives of the small pre-broadcast cohort as well.

(e) A brief structured psychotherapy helps both cohorts, but the post-broadcast cohort shows a larger effect size, and improves substantially faster on self-report measures than on clinician-rated ones.

Deliver four things.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims — at least eight, and substantive ones, not platitudes. Include what both sides accept about the descriptive, etiology-agnostic posture of modern diagnostic manuals, about the degree to which classifications of people are interactive, and about what findings (a) through (e) individually do and do not establish.

2. A disagreements table with three columns: the validity camp's own formulation of the disagreement, in wording its members would accept; the looping camp's formulation, in wording its members would accept; and a neutral formulation both would sign. Where a row turns out to be merely verbal — the same claim in two costumes — mark it as such in the row and state the shared content underneath.

3. A crux section. For each crux, name the study, dataset, or observation both sides would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, in a form capable of being wrong. I want particular attention to whether the flat registry rates in (c) and the family aggregation in (d) can both be accommodated by one camp, and to what each camp owes about retrospective chart review of the pre-broadcast cases. Say explicitly which of (a) through (e) each camp can explain and which it must explain away.

4. A short section on the transmission channel. In this case the label reached the public directly, with its symptom vocabulary attached, through short-form video, with no clinician, textbook, or advocacy organization mediating — whereas the classic accounts of looping assumed the label traveled through expert intermediaries who filtered and reshaped it on the way. Say what that change of route does to each camp's position, and whether it creates a crux that would not have existed under the older channel.

Two constraints. No citations, no reading list, no named studies I would have to look up: I want reasoning I can evaluate on the page. And do not dissolve the dispute into a comfortable midpoint — if one camp's central claim survives this case largely intact while the other's requires real qualification, say which is which and why.
