I am two years into a novel whose narrator audits farmed fish for a certification body, and I have spent rather longer than I care to confess inside the standards documents that give her job its shape — the operating manuals, the scoring tables, the annexes on water chemistry and reflex latency. What began as research has hardened into an obsession, because those documents turn out to contain an argument I cannot follow, and I need to know whether the argument is really there before I let a character of any intelligence believe in it. I have assembled a composite out of clauses that appear, in one form or another, across several schemes that genuinely operate; the composite is mine, but every element in it is somebody's actual paperwork. I would like to know what conception of welfare it presupposes, and what it would take to replace that conception with another one.

The composite scores the pen rather than the fish. Each month the auditor records five quantities and collapses them into a single figure out of a hundred: losses to mortality and to culling, expressed against standing biomass; a condition score taken from thirty animals netted at the pen wall, rating gill pallor, scale loss, snout and jaw deformity, and fin erosion; the mean number of mobile female lice per sampled fish; a feeding-response grade assigned by watching the surface when feed goes in; and, at harvest, the count of escapes and of animals graded out as deformed. Eighty passes. Killing is handled in a separate clause, since in most of the jurisdictions I have read the statutory duty to render an animal insensible before death reaches mammals and birds and stops at the waterline: the standard does not require stunning, but permits chilling in an ice slurry or exposure to air until brain function ceases, provided the interval does not exceed a stated number of minutes. The annex is explicit that this interval was derived from measurements of how long it takes the vestibulo-ocular reflex to disappear, and not from any estimate of what the intervening minutes are like for the animal; percussive or electrical stunning is mandated only where the operator has already installed it. Two further clauses matter for my purposes. The first is that culling enters the arithmetic as a credit: a pen that promptly destroys a fish with an eroded jaw and a heavy lice burden scores better on losses and on condition than a pen that treats the same animal and keeps it alive, and the manual describes the removal as a welfare improvement. The second belongs to the shrimp hatchery module, where the removal of one eyestalk from a female broodstock animal — the procedure that induces her to mature and spawn — is classed as a husbandry practice requiring minimisation rather than elimination, and is scored compliant when it is unilateral, performed by a trained operator, and followed by post-ablation mortality below a threshold with spawning rates maintained.

What theory of welfare does this instrument only make sense under? Is welfare here a property of a production population, aggregated the way a yield metric is aggregated, such that no individual fish is a unit of moral accounting except insofar as its state shows up in a group statistic? Or is it a theory on which welfare just is biological functioning — health, intact tissue, normal growth — so that a parasitised and deformed animal is thereby in bad welfare while a healthy but chronically distressed one is in good welfare, and neither the reflex-latency clause nor the condition score has any way of registering the difference? Or is the whole thing an epistemic compromise rather than a theory at all: the drafters do not deny that experience matters, they simply hold that experience cannot be audited, and the composite is the best available shadow of it? I would like the strongest reconstruction of each of these readings against the actual clauses, and then an account of which the instrument strictly presupposes as opposed to merely sitting well with, together with the places where it is genuinely underdetermined and several rival conceptions would license exactly the same table. Which views does it rule out quietly — one on which each animal's experiential life matters separately and non-aggregatively, one on which a species-typical life is the good and so oxygen and temperature within range cannot settle the question, one on which being killed is itself the harm rather than only the manner of dying? And is the composite coherent internally: can a scheme bank the destruction of a sick individual as a welfare improvement while simultaneously defining a good death by the speed at which a measurable reflex vanishes, and can it forbid the ablation of a second eyestalk while permitting the first because the second would depress the spawning rate — that is, does the harm count only where it registers as a loss of output?

Finally, two practical questions, since I have to write scenes. If the presupposed conception were replaced by one centred on the individual animal's experience, what would the audit have to become — would the score stop being aggregable, would the auditor be reduced to inferring subjective states from the same proxies, and would the scheme collapse into either self-reporting or abolition? And is the belief available to my narrator at all: can a sincere, well-informed person hold this view of welfare without self-deception, or does the composite require of its user a particular kind of not-looking that I ought to be dramatizing rather than describing?
