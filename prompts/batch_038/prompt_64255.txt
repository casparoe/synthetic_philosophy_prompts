I've read the standard dialectic here — the pinprick cases, the torture-versus-trillions-of-bliss intuition pumps, the Sorites complaint against any sharp boundary — and both sides always retreat to their familiar positions. I'd like to see the argument run under a setup that makes those moves unavailable.

Write a dialogue of roughly 1,100 words between two members of an institutional review board evaluating a proposed neural "hedonic governor." The device eliminates every aversive state above a given intensity and caps every appetitive state just below that same intensity, compressing a whole population's experience into a narrow band sitting immediately beneath a threshold T. The catch: T is not fixed by the hardware. The board sets it on a continuous dial, and the board's welfare metric treats T as lexically significant, so the two members have to agree where it goes. One is a lexical threshold negative utilitarian, holding that no quantity of sub-T pleasure can outweigh any above-T pain; the other is a commensurabilist who thinks all hedonic states sit on a single ratio scale with a determinate exchange rate.

Both are first-rate philosophers and neither may be a strawman. The negative utilitarian must answer the objection that a lexical threshold makes existence itself optional — that it licenses an empty world — and must say what she does when the dial is set so that T lands in a vague zone, where there is no fact about which side of it a given ache falls. The commensurabilist must explain the torture intuition without reducing it to scope neglect, and must defend the continuity of the trade-off function against the observation that people's judgments reverse between small and large stakes in a way that looks like a discontinuity rather than a bias.

Real back-and-forth: each takes up the other's last point instead of restating their own. End unresolved, but with one of them naming the single question that would decide the dispute. Don't name or cite any actual philosophers or papers; invent every case.
