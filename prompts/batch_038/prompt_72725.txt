I spent forty-two years in analytical chemistry, mostly running instruments — mass specs, HPLC, the usual. Retired about three years ago and have fallen down a philosophy hole, of all things. Right now I'm chewing on van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I want you to argue for a reading of it that I cooked up myself and haven't been able to find anyone defending, so I suspect it's either novel or dumb.

Quick version of where I'm coming from. In the lab, if you knew an instrument was going to drift — say a detector that loses sensitivity as it warms up — you never "corrected" for that by deciding in advance to believe whatever the drifted reading would say. You either fixed the instrument, swapped the part, or you threw the run out and logged it as invalid. The reading is downstream of the maintenance. Nobody would call a chemist irrational for declining to defer to tomorrow's badly-calibrated machine; they'd say he should have calibrated it.

So here's the thesis I want you to argue for, and I want you to argue it as strongly as you honestly can:

Reflection isn't a norm about credences at all. It's an engineering specification for agents. Read it not as "align your present beliefs with your anticipated future beliefs" but as "arrange yourself so that your anticipated future beliefs are the kind you'd want to align with." On that reading, the cases usually trotted out as counterexamples — the drug that will make you confident of something false, the interrogation, the conversion experience, the drunk-and-exhausted future self — are not violations of a belief-norm. They're maintenance failures. It's job in those cases isn't to adjust the credence now, it's to change the setup: don't take the drug, precommit, don't be in that room at 2am.

And there's a corollary I think is worth pushing, which is the interesting part: on this reading Reflection never actually fails, because whenever your future self is going to be compromised, what the principle demands gets discharged by an action rather than by a doxastic adjustment. That sounds suspiciously like making the principle unfalsifiable, so you'll need to walk straight into that worry rather than around it.

- Keep the notation light. One statement of Reflection, in words or symbols, is plenty. This shouldn't read like a journal article.

Structure it exactly like this, in this order:

1. "The setup in plain words" — about 150 words. State Reflection the way you'd state it to a smart non-philosopher, then put my maintenance reading next to it so the contrast is visible.
2. "Brainstorm: the rival readings" — about 200 words. Three or four other things Reflection could plausibly be read as doing, one or two sentences each, plus a line on why the maintenance reading beats thier alternatives. This is where I want to see you thinking rather than reciting.
3. "The argument" — about 500 words, the main event. Three or four numbered steps, each doing something genuinely distinct, not the same point four times over. At least one step should be about what the principle is *for* rather than about what it says.
4. "The three objections I'd raise if I were reviewing this" — about 350 words. Include the unfalsifiability worry, a worry about involuntary cases where the agent really can't change the setup, and a worry coming from the Dutch-book-style arguments, which seem to give Reflection standing as a belief-norm on its own. Don't strawman them.
5. "My verdict" — about 150 words. What survives, what I should give up, and one concrete case where the maintenance reading and the standard reading tell the agent to do different things.

Aim for roughly 1300 words total. One last thing: I'm a chemist, not a philosopher, so if my thesis rests on a confusion between two things I've run together, name the confusion outright instead of politely working around it.
