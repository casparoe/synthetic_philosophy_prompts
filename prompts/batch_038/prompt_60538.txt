im in the second half of a social ontology seminar and our final assessment is a creative piece — we each have to satirise one of the positions we read. im doing the shared agency unit (Bratman, Gilbert, Searle, that lot) and i've got a conceit i like but i cant get it to land on my own, so can you write the thing for me?

Conceit: it's the minutes of an ordinary monthly meeting of a small homeowners' association. Deadpan parliamentary prose, present/apologies at the top, numbered items, motions recorded as carried or lost, a treasurer's report nobody reads. Give the association an invented name, a date, and a venue (village hall, 7pm, tea and biscuits). About 750–900 words. The joke is that this HOA has unknowingly written a complete theory of joint action into its standing orders, and its members are split between incompatible theories — but they only ever argue about procedure, never about philosophy.

Beats i want, roughly in this order:

1. Repainting the clubhouse. Two members each announce they're completely on board with us doing it together, and it looks unanimous right up until the secretary notices that one of them has privately resolved it will be the sage green she's already bought twelve gallons of, and the other has resolved it's cream or he walks. The chair rules that this isn't a disagreement about paint at all, it's a failure of their individual sub-plans to interlock, and the remedy isn't a vote, it's renegotiating the sub-plans. Don't use the term meshing subplans — make it a point of order instead.

2. One member keeps insisting the entire business is going on inside each of their separate heads and there's no need for anything over and above that, and that he could perfectly well be party to the association's plan to repaint even if no one else ever found out. The chair and the secretary both lose patience with him, for different reasons, and i want a reader who knows the literature to be able to tell exactly what each of them is objecting to — one to the individualism, one to the dropping of the common-knowledge condition.

3. The barn dance. A motion to host a harvest barn dance carries unanimously, and the minutes record flatly that every single member personally would rather not, and that this makes no difference whatsoever to the association's intention. The secretary objects that this is an accounting error, since the association can't intend what nobody intends, and is overruled from the chair.

4. The rescission problem — this is the bit i want to be the funniest. A member who voted for the barn dance moves to withdraw from it on the grounds that he's changed his mind and never wanted it anyway. The chair rules him out of order: he cannot release himself by himself, the commitment runs to the others and only they can dissolve it, and if he fails to appear on the night he'll be fined fifteen pounds and owes them an apology. He asks how this is any different from a promise he's allowed to be let out of; the answer he gets should be confidently wrong in an interesting way rather than just evasive.

5. Any other business: a fallen tree across the shared drive, and two equally acceptable ways of clearing it that only work if everyone picks the same one. Every member is willing to do their bit provided the others do, nobody is willing to go first, and the standard procedure — poll each member for a preference, then vote — provably cannot get them there. A member who teaches economics proposes they stop asking what each of them wants and start asking what they should do as a group, and is shouted down for trying to rewrite the standing orders. Minute it as noted.

Ground rules: no philosophers' names anywhere, no jargon (non-summativist, plural subject, collective intentionality, we-intention — none of them appear), and nobody ever explains what the theory is. It all has to come through as bylaws, numbered standing orders, rulings from the chair, points of order, and fines. The chair stays internally consistent throughout: she never lets the association's intentions collapse into what the members each happen to intend, even when it costs her the meeting. Same for the secretary, in the other direction. Someone who has never read a page of this should still laugh at it as an accurate sendup of a parish council; someone who has should be able to identify each position from the procedure alone. Keep it completely straight-faced — no winking at the reader, no footnotes, no closing gag that tells us what it meant.
