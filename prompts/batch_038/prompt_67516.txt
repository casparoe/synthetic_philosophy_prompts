Suppose a new polity adopts a constitution with a bootstrapping clause. The initial electorate is fixed by a frankly non-democratic criterion — every adult resident on some specified date, no exceptions, no justification offered. That electorate's first act is to vote on the *rule* determining who counts for the second election. Each subsequent electorate inherits that rule and may amend it by supermajority, using the rule it inherited. So the boundary is never set by the boundary-setters in the first instance; it's set forward, one step at a time, with each generation ratifying the frame it was handed.

My claim is that the legitimacy deficit of the founding act decays: every round of self-amendment retroactively endorses more of the chain, so residual illegitimacy converges to zero and the bootstrapping objection loses its bite. Call the problem dissolved if a theorist who accepts that boundary-setting needs legitimation can no longer press the regress objection without smuggling in some independent premise.
