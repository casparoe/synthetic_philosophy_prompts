I spent thirty-eight years directing a small analytical laboratory, and since retiring I have been reading my way through the philosophy of self-deception, largely because of something I witnessed repeatedly in my own discipline and could never describe cleanly: a thoroughly competent colleague who had plainly seen a result, who then quietly arranged matters so that the result could not easily be encountered again, and who would have been sincerely indignant at the suggestion that anything of the kind had taken place.

I should like you to construct a thought experiment to a specification, rather than to illustrate a position you already find attractive. What follows states what the case is for, because that determines what would count as a successful construction and what would count as smuggling in the conclusion.

**Purpose.** One family of accounts, the deflationary or bias-based approach associated in recent decades with Alfred Mele, holds that a self-deceived person need not intend to deceive herself and need not hold two contradictory beliefs at once: it suffices that a desire or emotion biases the way she gathers, attends to, and weighs evidence, so that she comes to believe something false that suits her. The rival, more intentionalist approach, associated with Donald Davidson, holds that the mind must be divided in some fashion, so that one part knows what another part is kept from believing. I want to know whether the first account can absorb a specific kind of conduct — an action that is intelligible only on the assumption that the agent represents to herself the very evidence she has discounted — without quietly appealing to something like the second account's partition.

**Specifications.** Please satisfy all of the following, and satisfy them in a way that generates the verdicts from the theories rather than asserting them.

1. Setting: an ordinary working research environment — a laboratory bench, a field station, a data-analysis office. No clinical material of any kind: no dissociation, no amnesia, no hypnosis, no pharmacology, no dreams, no imaginings, no split-brain apparatus.
2. Length: the scenario itself in a single tight passage of no more than about three hundred words.
3. The subject must be a careful, above-average scientist in her ordinary reasoning. Her false belief may not be attributable to incompetence, haste, or ignorance of the relevant method.
4. She must end up holding a false belief that is contradicted by data she has personally seen and understood at the time she saw it.
5. At least one of her subsequent actions must be explicable only by attributing to her a representation of that disconfirming evidence — an act of avoidance, suppression, or re-description that would be pointless if she did not know what she was keeping out of sight. This is the load-bearing feature of the case, so please design it deliberately rather than let it emerge incidentally.
6. That action must not admit of an innocent reading. If a reasonable scientist might have done the same thing for ordinary methodological, resource, or safety reasons, it will not serve.
7. She must at no point entertain, express, or betray a second-order thought to the effect that she is managing her own beliefs. No self-catch, no hesitation, no trace of shame, no reflexive remark. The case may not be built on her nearly noticing.
8. The tension must not be produced by an interlocutor. Do not stage a confrontation in which someone presents her with the evidence and she reacts defensively; I regard that as too cheap a way of manufacturing the difficulty.
9. The motivating desire must be mundane and identifiable — a grant, a student's thesis, the standing of work she published years ago, the reluctance to reopen a settled question — and the belief must be one she would be embarrassed to hear a stranger assert.

**What I want back.**

- The scenario.
- A check against each of the nine specifications, taken in order, indicating where in the text each is met. If any specification turns out to be unsatisfiable alongside the others, say so plainly and identify the conflict; I would rather hear that than receive a case that quietly drops one.
- The deflationary account's diagnosis: which of the familiar bias mechanisms — motivated evidence-gathering, selective attention to supporting data, discounting of contrary data, the vividness or availability of supporting data — you take to be doing the work here, and why those mechanisms suffice to explain how she comes to believe what she does.
- The intentionalist account's diagnosis of the same case, and in particular how it handles the action required by specification 5 given that specification 7 forbids any second-order acknowledgment.
- Which single feature of the case generates the disagreement between the two accounts; what each account says once that feature is removed — for instance, if the deliberate act is replaced by a merely passive failure to seek further evidence — and whether the case then collapses into an unremarkable instance of motivated belief.
- How a defender of the deflationary account would attack the construction. I want her best reply concerning the act in specification 5, and an assessment of what that reply costs her elsewhere.
- A candid judgment: does the case as designed function as a counterexample to the deflationary account, or only as a hard instance of it, and what would have to be added to make it decisive?

Two further requests. Write for a reader who has not encountered the technical literature, defining any term of art on first use; I come to this from outside the discipline. And please do not supply citations, a bibliography, or attributed quotations — I have no access to the journals, and I would rather you reason from the positions themselves than reproduce references I cannot check.
