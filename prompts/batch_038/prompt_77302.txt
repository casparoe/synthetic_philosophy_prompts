I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been trying to sort out which of the regularities I write about are actually conventions in David Lewis's sense — coordination problem, arbitary, everybody conforms because everybody else does and everybody knows it — versus norms, habits, or rules someone simply imposed. Going by gut:

Driving on the right: convention, obviously. Which side of the escalator you stand on: convention, even though it differs city to city. The QWERTY layout: convention, though no one gains from switching alone and everybody would gain only if everybody switched — is that even a coordination problem anymore? Tipping 20%: not a convention, that's a norm; skipping it earns moral blame, not a coordination failure. Except driving on the wrong side of the road also earns moral blame, and arguably worse, so those two verdicts sit badly together and I don't know which one to give up. Sweden flipping the whole country to right-hand traffic by government decree: I want to call the outcome a convention even though nobody drifted into it. Singular "they" for a person whose gender you don't know: this is the one I keep going back and forth on. It looks like a textbook convention — arbitrary, coordinative, decreed by no one — but the arguments over it are conducted entirely in moral and political terms, and a lot of the participants insist the alternative just isn't available.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which case each clause is there to handle. Then give me its verdicts on cases I didn't list — a league's offsides rule, word order in a sentence, a newsroom's habit of writing headlines in the present tense. And be straight about which of my gut calls it has to overturn. I'd rather have one committed answer with reasons than a menu of options: pick a principle, defend it, and tell me where I'm wrong. Bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning — no preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
