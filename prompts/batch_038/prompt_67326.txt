I'm the associate dean for curriculum at a medical school, and for the past dozen years my working life has been competency frameworks: entrustable professional activities scored on a five-point supervision scale, milestones signed off by a clinical competency committee, blueprinting of high-stakes exams, constructive alignment of stated objectives to assessment tasks, and the learning-science apparatus I reach for constantly — cognitive load, spaced retrieval, deliberate practice, the revised Bloom taxonomy, programmatic assessment and its demand for accumulated validity evidence. I have never read Alfred North Whitehead. I don't want a general-reader summary of his philosophy and I don't want citations; I'll read him myself. What I want is a translation of his educational position into the vocabulary I just described, done as faithfully as it can be done, followed by an account of what the translation failed to carry.

Here are the claims I want rendered, one at a time. First, his diagnosis of inert ideas: knowledge received into the mind without being used, tested, recombined, or brought to bear on new situations — inert precisely because it was merely received. Second, his rhythm of education, the threefold pattern of romance, precision, and generalization, and especially his insistence that the characteristic failure of formal instruction is to impose precision on material in which the learner has not yet taken any interested delight, and to demand generalization before precision has been earned. Third, his claim that these are not age-graded stages through which a pupil passes once, but cycles that recur at every scale, inside a single lesson as well as across a whole course of study. Fourth, his hostility to examination-driven teaching, and his view that an examination measuring recall at a moment of maximum cramming can systematically certify the possession of ideas that are inert. Fifth, his claim that the aim of education is not the accumulation of knowledge but the art of using it, so that a curriculum which transmits a large, well-organized body of facts has still failed if it has not preserved a zest for the activity of knowing. Sixth, his warning that uniformity of curriculum and centralized inspection, pushed far enough, destroy the very capacities they exist to promote. Since I have not read him, tell me before you translate if any of these is not actually his claim, or if I have already distorted it in stating it — do not quietly translate something he never said.

For each claim, report what translates cleanly into my vocabulary, what translates only at the cost of a distortion you should name explicitly, and what has no expression in it at all. Where you had to choose among candidate renderings — for instance, whether inert ideas map onto knowledge that passes an item but never enters an entrustment decision, onto weak transfer, onto something describable in cognitive-load terms, or onto a construct my framework simply lacks — say which you chose and what that choice costs you. And do not smuggle: if one of Whitehead's claims turns out to be equivalent to something my learning science already says, show me the equivalence rather than assuming it, and do not hand my own framework's commitments back to me labelled as translations of his. Then give me the residue report proper, with a verdict on each of the two readings I keep going back and forth between. Is whatever fails to translate a genuine gap in a competency-and-entrustment vocabulary — something my instruments cannot represent because they were built to generate ratable evidence — or is it a sign that Whitehead's claims were less determinate than their rhetorical force suggests, so that the residue is exhortation that no assessment could ever confirm or disconfirm? Sort the items onto those two sides, and concede plainly where you think my suspicion is justified. Close with the one practical consequence: if the residue is real rather than rhetorical, what specific change would it force in the way a competency committee writes or reads an entrustment decision, and what would I look at afterward to tell whether the change had made any difference?
