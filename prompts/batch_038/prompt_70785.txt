I am midway through a third novel — a four-generation story about a drained wetland in eastern England, in which the water table is arguably the protagonist — and I have been reading Braudel for discipline rather than pleasure. What I want from him is his insistence that a life has three speeds at once: the nearly motionless time of a people in relation to their soil, climate and sea; the slower cyclical time of prices, wages, harvests, credit and demographic tides; and the surface time of events and persons, which he treats with something like contempt. My difficulty is a formal one. There is a chapter I cannot write, in which something close to thirty years pass and nothing anyone would call an event occurs, and I am coming to suspect that no novelist has ever managed Braudel's middle scale except by lecturing — which is what we do when we have no way to dramatize a conjuncture. So I have gone back to The Grapes of Wrath, the book everyone reaches for on this question, and I want a serious critical reading of it through Braudel rather than through the frames it usually gets: the intercalary chapters as biblical or Dos Passos-esque counterpoint, Steinbeck as sentimental socialist, the Dust Bowl as moral parable. Please keep the comparison structural rather than genetic — the novel came first and Braudel's Mediterranean a decade later, so there is no influence to trace, only a shared problem.

Here is the knot I want worked, and it is a hypothesis of my own that I would rather have tested than flattered. My claim is that Steinbeck's two registers leap clean over Braudel's middle time. The intercalary chapters are geological and climatic at one end — the dust, the rains, the turtle crossing the highway, the land itself — and juridical at the other, arriving at the bank and the owners as culprits; the Joad chapters are pure event, run almost entirely on accident: a grandfather who cannot bear to leave the soil and has to be drugged into the truck, dead before the first day is out; a son who walks away down a river and simply stops existing; a son-in-law who abandons a pregnant wife; a stillbirth; a flood. What seems to me to be missing is the conjoncture — the used-car lots, the tractor finance, the collapsing wages in Oklahoma, the California labour market and its handbills, the organised violence of the growers — treated as a temporal layer with its own momentum and its own periodicity, rather than as scenery for a verdict. If I am right, then the intercalary chapters are not a slow scale at all but a fast one: they are events of anger, narrated in the present tense, and the book's structural vision is really an ethical one wearing a geological costume. If I am wrong, show me where — identify the stretches, described rather than quoted, in which Steinbeck genuinely renders a cycle, an oscillation, or a price rather than an indictment, and say whether any of them do it through scene instead of through voice.

The second thing I want pressed is agency, because it is where my own draft is stuck. Steinbeck stages tenant men arguing with a tractor driver who is their neighbour's son, and he insists that no individual in the chain wants the evictions: the bank is a thing men made that has grown past them and now does its work through them, so that the man behind the wheel is less a person than a limb of the machine, unable to see or smell the land he is breaking. And yet the novel ends in fury at particular, nameable people. It is the dilemma Braudel's own method courts from the other direction — the moment a structure is described it wants to become an actor with intentions, and the moment an actor appears the structure dissolves back into event. Does Steinbeck know he is doing this? Is his monstrous bank functioning as Braudel's geography does, an inert condition nobody chose, or as a character, in which case the book has merely traded great-man history for great-institution history? And how should one read the final act in the flooded barn in that light: an event the structure cannot account for, the one place where surface time does something none of the slow times could, or a sentimental gesture that lets the reader off the structural hook? Please close by telling me where the Braudelian reading breaks down and Steinbeck resists it — I would much rather have the limits of the lens than a tidy fit, and I need to know whether the middle scale is unwritable in fiction or merely unwritten by him. Around twelve hundred words, argued through paraphrase and scene description throughout; please do not quote either author verbatim.
