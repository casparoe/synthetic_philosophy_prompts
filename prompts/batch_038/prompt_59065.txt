You are a philosopher who specializes in the ethics of war, particularly the debate over the moral equality of combatants and its revisionist critics. Please think carefully: what I want is not a survey of just war theory but a disciplined exercise in inferring an operative rule from a track record of verdicts.

I am profiling a prominent just war theorist for a magazine of ideas, and the spine of the piece will be a case she has not yet ruled on. Her settled verdicts, given repeatedly in print and on the record, concern who may permissibly be targeted in a war that is plainly aggressive on one side. She says:

- a nineteen-year-old conscript in the aggressor's infantry, frightened and politically indifferent, is a legitimate target;
- the general who planned the invasion is a legitimate target, and more obviously so than the conscript;
- a foreign volunteer who joined the aggressor's army out of ideological conviction is a legitimate target;
- a civilian contractor who pilots the aggressor's surveillance aircraft from a base in a neighboring country, wears no uniform, and never enters the theater of war, is a legitimate target;
- an intelligence officer who runs the aggressor's interrogation program, unarmed and far from any front line, is a legitimate target;
- a combat medic attached to the aggressor's forces is not a legitimate target;
- a machinist in a state munitions plant whose livelihood depends on the job is not a legitimate target;
- a radio presenter broadcasting propaganda in support of the war is not a legitimate target.

The new case my editors want me to press her on: a software engineer, a national of a neutral country, well paid by a private firm to maintain the target-selection model used by the aggressor's drone fleet. She has never worn a uniform, has never been near the fighting, and maintains that she believes the model is used only against military targets; her contribution is real but small, and she could be replaced within weeks by any one of dozens of equally qualified contractors.

Predict what she will say about this engineer — and note that I want two things separated: whether the engineer is liable to defensive harm at all, and whether that liability could extend to lethal harm given how marginal and replaceable her contribution is. Then tell me which rule her eight verdicts are actually running. At least four seem to fit the record: liability grounded in moral responsibility for an unjust threat; liability grounded in material contribution regardless of what the contributor believes; liability grounded in occupying the socially recognized role of combatant; and liability graded by the size and necessity of one's contribution. Show which verdicts each candidate explains cleanly, which it strains or must explain away, and which single rule best fits all eight. If the record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say what principle she would be committing herself to by ruling either way on the engineer, and specify one further concrete case that would discriminate between the leading candidates.

Open with two or three sentences brainstorming the candidate rules before you commit to one. Then lead with your bottom-line prediction on the engineer, follow it with a compact table giving each candidate rule, its verdict on the engineer, and the strongest case in the record that supports it, and only then give your reasoning. Hard limit: 800 words.
