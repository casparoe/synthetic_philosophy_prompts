I am the registrar and collections manager at a small university teaching museum — roughly fourteen thousand objects, two full-time curators, no conservator on staff, and technical examination contracted out through a partner institution's lab. Eighteen months ago the director asked me to convene a standing Attribution and Authenticity Review panel: two curators, a faculty art historian, a conservator from the partner lab, one graduate student member, and me as chair. We meet monthly, and weekly during a review cycle of a single collection. Our mandate is narrow but endlessly recurring: whenever an object's authorship, period, or status as an original is called into question — by the lab's imaging, by an outside scholar, by a journalist, by the donor, or by our own re-cataloguing — the panel must decide what the museum says about the object and what the museum does with it. We have no written method, so meetings dissolve into art-historical argument while the wall label, the accession record, the online catalogue entry, and the insurance valuation drift further out of agreement with one another. I need a procedure: a fixed sequence of questions the panel runs on any such object, in order, that a non-specialist can execute — my student member, or me acting alone between meetings — without being able to adjudicate connoisseurship disputes.

It has to be grounded in what the philosophical literature on forgery, copies, and the value of originals actually argues, but I want those arguments translated into questions rather than cited, and I want no reading list. Two things in particular we get wrong in discussion, and the procedure must not inherit our errors. First, the indiscernibility objection: whenever attribution is questioned, someone asks why it matters if the substitute panel would look exactly the same, and the panel then spends an hour trying to find perceptual differences it cannot find. The procedure must say, in operational terms, what is being decided when we downgrade an object that looks precisely as it did last year and looks just as good, and it may not make any step depend on a looking-based test. Second, the distinction between forgery and legitimate replication — posthumous casts from the sculptor's own model, studio replicas, workshop production with the master's hand in parts, later interventions that replaced original material, honest reproductions sold as such — where the operative facts concern the maker's intention, the object's history of disclosure and ownership, and how it came to be, not how it appears. I want that distinction kept sharp, and I want explicit handling of the hybrids: a nineteenth-century copy that was subsequently passed off as an original; a workshop panel that the master finished; a heavily restored object in which almost no original substance survives but which is nonetheless the very object that has hung in this building since 1931.

For each step, state what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as an insufficient one, which of a fixed set of dispositions the step triggers (retain attribution unchanged; qualify attribution in the label and catalogue record; withdraw attribution but retain and reinterpret the object; withdraw from display pending review; issue a public correction of the record; decline a proffered gift), who must sign off, and — flagged separately and conspicuously — the steps at which the panel is not uncovering a fact but making a value choice, together with a sentence naming what the choice is between. Also state what the procedure cannot settle (looting and restitution, legal title, market value and insurable value, whether an object is good, questions of cultural affiliation) and the ways in which it will predictably misfire on real files. Bear in mind our constraints: ninety minutes per object, and no authority to commission new technical work ourselves, so any step requiring new evidence must be marked as a referral out with an interim disposition specified.

Then run the procedure end to end on three files. (1) A small panel long catalogued under a named master's name; the partner lab's imaging suggests predominantly workshop production, with the master's hand confined to the faces. It is a visitor favorite and it is the donor family's namesake gift. (2) A bronze offered by an alumnus whose foundry marks and paperwork indicate it was cast decades after the sculptor's death from the sculptor's own model, and which the donor has described in print, in a local paper, as an original. (3) A drawing whose attribution to a well-known hand was withdrawn by a specialist fifteen years ago and which we have continued to display under the old name with no note anywhere. Take the third to be the case where the procedure delivers a result the museum will not like: I expect it to force a public correction rather than a quiet label change, and I want the procedure to tell me whether that expectation is correct or whether there is a defensible route to resolving it quietly. Finally, after the prose procedure, give me the whole thing as a table with exactly these columns: Step | Question put to the file or the object | Sufficient answer | Insufficient answer | Disposition(s) triggered | Sign-off | Judgment flag (Y/N), and if Y, what is being chosen between. Keep the table to one page and put everything else — the grounding of the questions, the hard cases, the exclusions, the misfire modes, and the three worked files — in prose above it.
