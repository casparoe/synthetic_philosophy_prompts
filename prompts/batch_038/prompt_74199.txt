My brother-in-law Ray is retiring next month after 33 years running the sleep lab at the county hospital, and somehow I got the toast. I'm not academic, I fix HVAC for a living, but he likes arguing with me at Thanksgiving so I think he asked me on purpose.

Here's the bind. Few years back I fell down a hole on the question of whether dreams are even things that happen. Malcolm's argument -- that all you ever really have is the impression on waking, so saying I dreamt I was back in my old school is only true of that impression, not of some private movie that ran while I was asleep. And then Dennett's version, where the dream might be more like a tape that only gets played at the moment of waking, so the remembering isn't evidence anything went on during the night. I honestly don't know which is right, maybe neither, but I can't say the plain thing anymore without hearing how much it assumes.

Ray's whole career is the plain thing. Waking people at three in the morning with the lights low, asking what they were just dreaming, writing the answer in a notebook. So if I stand up and say he spent his life listening in on other people's dreams, I'll be asserting something I think is probably false, in front of maybe fifteen of forty guests who are sleep techs and have read the same arguments. Ray definitely has. We've argued the tape thing twice.

So: write the toast. About five minutes spoken. It has to make a room feel something about that working life without once claiming that anyone actually had a dream -- including Ray, and including his dad, who punched through the headboard for years before anyone knew what was wrong with him, which is how Ray got into this field in the first place. And it can't turn into a philosophy lecture or a private joke that leaves the other twenty-five people -- his wife, his kids, a couple of old neighbors who only know him as the dream guy -- politely waiting for it to end. Affectionate, a little funny, and something Ray himself would call honest rather than evasive. I want him to hear the constraint and think I did it on purpose and did it well.

After the toast, give me a table with one row per moment that's carrying weight. Columns: Passage (just the first few words), For whom (lab people / family / both), What it leans on about dreams, Survives Malcolm? (yes/no), Survives Dennett? (yes/no), What we'd lose if I cut it.
