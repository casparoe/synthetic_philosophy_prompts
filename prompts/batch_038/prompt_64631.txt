I am in the second year of a three-year term as the graduate-student member of my university's Forum Standards Committee, a six-person body — two faculty, two staff, one graduate student, one undergraduate — that hears complaints about conduct on university-controlled channels: the listservs, the campus forum, the shared drives, and the alumni network. Our charter gives us almost nothing to work with; it says that we exist to protect the conditions of open exchange and leaves every operative term undefined, so in practice we reason case to case from our own minutes. The two members who joined this year know those minutes only as the chair and I have summarized them. Before Thursday's session the chair has asked each of us to circulate a short memo on the new complaint, and I would rather think it through against our record than from first principles.

Here is the record as I have it. (i) Two years ago, a tenured faculty member posted on a public account a claim about a clinical trial that was then recruiting patients; after consulting the investigators we found the claim demonstrably false and enrollment measurably depressed, and we suspended his listserv access for sixty days and required removal of the post, taking no position on his teaching or research. (ii) The same year, a student posted a screenshot of another student's private message — the screenshot authentic, its content accurate — and we upheld a one-semester suspension from the housing forum plus a restorative meeting, the majority rejecting two dissenters' argument that accuracy was a defense, on the ground that accuracy was not the question in front of us. (iii) A year later we declined to act against a recent graduate who, while still enrolled, had run an anonymous account urging the disruption of a scheduled lecture; the disruption never happened, the account had been deleted, and he was by then living abroad, and the minutes record the reason as there being nothing left to protect. (iv) A staff member made one badly phrased remark in a public comment thread read by perhaps a dozen people; a journalist with a large following quoted it, and the organization the remark concerned received several hundred hostile messages. He retracted and apologized within the hour. We issued a formal finding and suspended his posting privileges for a year, the majority holding that in choosing a public channel he had taken his chances on what the channel would do with his words; two members objected that the amplification was not foreseeable from a thread of eleven readers. (v) In a case that looks like (iv) from the other side, a graduate student said something comparably harsh inside a closed reading-group chat, another member of the group leaked it, and a pile-on followed; we declined to sanction the student, noting that the leak, not the remark, had created the public harm. (vi) In what everyone agreed was the worst matter we have seen — a sustained harassment campaign run through the alumni forum — we refused the complainant's request for permanent exclusion and imposed five years' exclusion with a right to petition for reinstatement, on the stated ground that we did not consider ourselves competent to make final judgments about people. We have also settled two points of procedure: we set aside a finding because the respondent had never been shown the underlying complaint, even though we thought the sanction substantively deserved, and we proceeded against a respondent who declined to participate, holding that declining to engage cost him nothing but the chance to be heard.

The new complaint is this. The dean of our college, eleven years in the post, has accepted a position elsewhere effective at the end of this month. Four days ago she sent a farewell note to the all-faculty-and-staff listserv, 4,100 recipients, which after a page of thanks said two things about a junior faculty member's recently denied tenure file: that the denial was correct because the work was derivative, and that the candidate had misrepresented a coauthor's contribution. Both appear to be true — the second rests on a research-integrity finding from last year that was never made public and was communicated to her in confidence under a process that promises confidentiality to those who receive it. Six hours after the faculty association president asked her to, she retracted and apologized, saying she should not have referred to a confidential matter in that forum. By the next morning the note had been forwarded to a national disciplinary blog, which published it; the junior faculty member has since received messages from strangers and has asked the university to act. She will not be our employee when we meet, but she keeps her alumni email, her access to the alumni forum, and her library privileges, and she has said through counsel that she will not participate in our process. The complainant asks for three things: a published finding that names her, revocation of the alumni email and forum access, and a public statement that the university does not endorse her characterization. Counsel has advised that naming her in a published finding carries defamation exposure, and the president's office has let it be known that it would prefer nothing published — a direction we have never taken from either.

So what should the committee decide on Thursday, and what should I put in my memo? Start by laying out the rules that are jointly consistent with (i) through (vi): I can see a forward-looking reading on which (iii) and (vi) are the heart of the record and a channel-risk reading on which (iv) and (v) are, and I cannot tell which one the majority has actually been running, or whether it is either. Then say what each candidate rule implies for each of the three requested remedies, identify the single closest analog in the record and say honestly where that analogy strains, and address whether her refusal to participate changes anything given our procedural practice. If the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, tell me what principle the committee would be adopting with each available choice. I do not want a balanced menu: commit to a recommendation on all three remedies — or to a fourth option you think the complainant has not thought of — and give me the reasons I could defend in the room, along with one further hypothetical case, of the kind we might see next year, on which the two rules you take most seriously would come apart and so reveal which one we are running. Finally, tell me where your recommendation is most fragile: which precedent it leans on hardest, and how the answer would change if I have described the grounds of that decision inaccurately. Please write in American English.
