I've spent a decade operating stateful distributed systems — live migration, checkpoint-and-restore, primary/replica promotion, leader election, split-brain, at-least-once versus exactly-once semantics — and I have essentially no training in philosophy. Take Dennett's 1978 first-person story "Where Am I?", in which his brain is removed and kept alive in a vat, radio-linked to his body so that it continues to steer it, after which a program is built to reproduce the brain's operation and take over control of the body, with a duplicate body held in reserve.

Restate what that story is actually doing in the vocabulary I use, as faithfully as you can: what is the process, what is the state, what gets serialized, what makes a restored instance the same service rather than a new one, and what the vat, the radio link, the emulation, and the spare body correspond to. Then give me the residue report, item by item. Which of Dennett's moves translate cleanly? Which translate only by distortion, or by quietly assuming what they were meant to establish — in particular, whether "the self is wherever the state is" is something his story argues for or something my vocabulary builds in by construction, so that the translation comes out flattering rather than honest? And which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all?

For that last category I want a verdict, not a shrug: is the gap a deficiency in how engineers talk about identity, ownership, and continuity, or evidence that the question he is asking is less well posed than it appears? I particularly want to know whether anything survives that cannot be reduced to a question about which replica is authoritative — for instance the asymmetry between his relation to the brain and his relation to the emulation, or the fact that only one of the candidates can be the one who is frightened at a given moment. If you think my proposed reductions are wrong, say so and show me why.
