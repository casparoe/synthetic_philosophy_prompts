i'm teaching a grad seminar on path dependence and i want a short story to hand out — something that is not just the QWERTY parable wearing a trench coat. here's the setup i've got in my head; change names and details freely but keep the bones:

a mining valley (invent the place, don't use a real one) where in the 1870s a tramway gets built to a slightly odd gauge because the contractor happened to be sitting on a surplus of axles from a government order that got cancelled. the reason for the choice is dead within a decade — the foundry burns, the order's gone, nobody alive remembers — but the configuration is still standing a hundred and fifty years later: tunnel widths, warehouse doorframes, the width of the lorries, the rail deck on the ferry that takes the ore out to market.

the story should follow a young engineer who has built a conversion proposal, and the point is that converting is *net* better for the valley. everyone agrees on the arithmetic. it still can't happen, because no single actor can move first and the complimentarities reach into everything. i want the economics right rather than waved at: sunk capital, coordination failure, and above all the fact that the lock-in isn't a conspiracy or a mistake anybody is currently making — it's a configuration that keeps reproducing itself out of individually sensible decisions.

then the twist. across the ridge there's a rival valley that made the opposite arbitrary choice for an equally dumb reason (your pick: a surveyor's error, a wager, a brother-in-law's foundry), and yet a century and a half on the two valleys have nearly identical freight costs and the same mix of industry. So when the engineer runs her counterfactual simulation, the alternate history comes back uncomfortably close to the real one. the question i want the reader left holding: was anything ever genuinely locked in, or did the terrain and the market fix the outcome and history only fix the route to it?

one more beat i'd like somewhere near the end: somebody pulls the original ledger, and it turns out the "arbitrary" choice was made deliberately, for a reason that hasn't applied since the year it was written down. extinct cause, live effect, walking around on rails.

say 1300–1600 words, third person probably. please don't let it turn into an essay with people explaining the concept at each other over coffee — carry the argument in scenes and objects instead (a doorframe four inches too narrow, a wheelset under glass in the local museum, the ferry deck that only takes one kind of truck). and don't resolve it. ending on a shrug is fine, ending on a verdict isn't.

then, out of character, a few lines: tell me which side of the debate the story is quietly betting on — lock-in as a real constraint, versus the convergence reading where function does the work and contingency is only scenery — and flag where you had to bend the ideas to make a story instead of a paper. also say what would change your mind here: what consideration or piece of evidence would tip you from one reading to the other, and where your version of events is most likely to be wrong about how this actually works.
