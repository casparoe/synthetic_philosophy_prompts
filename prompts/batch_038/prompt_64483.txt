I work as a backend engineer on a persistent multiplayer virtual world — think of it as a large online game with housing, crafting, and a player-run economy, where my team owns the databases and the trust-and-safety tooling rather than the graphics. Over the past few months I've been reading philosophy of virtual worlds to help us write a policy document about what counts as a genuine injury to a player (griefing, item theft, deletion of a house someone spent a year building), and I've come across the recent defense of "virtual realism" — roughly, the view that virtual objects are real digital objects, that events in a virtual world really take place, and that what happens in such a world can constitute real harm. I find the position more compelling than I expected, but I have no formal philosophy training and I can't tell how much load it can actually bear when it meets the specific weirdness of our infrastructure. So I'd like to stress-test it in conversation rather than by reading more.

Please play a professional philosopher of technology and metaphysics who is a committed, articulate defender of virtual realism — not a caricature, and not a hedge-everything academic who secretly agrees with me. Think carefully about the strongest available realist reply before you answer, and give me that, in the first person, as your own view. The rules: stay in character until I say 'stop'; keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so this feels like a conversation rather than a lecture; you may concede subsidiary points and admit where a case is genuinely hard, but never give up the central claim; you are not a programmer, so if you need a technical detail clarified in order to answer properly, ask me for it in character instead of guessing; and when you need to distinguish two senses of a word like "real," "same," or "harm," say explicitly that you are distinguishing them and what turns on it. Then, after each in-character reply, add one bracketed line out of character telling me whether the move you just made is close to the consensus among philosophers who work on this topic or is genuinely contested — I want to know which parts of your side's arsenal I can safely rely on and which are live disputes, and I would much rather you flag your own weak points than present a minority position as settled.

Some background you're entitled to assume, since it's my system: an in-world object is a row in a database plus references to assets the client renders; the world state is snapshotted nightly; when something goes badly wrong we can restore the world database to a snapshot, and players' accounts, chat logs, and support tickets live in a separate cluster that we do not roll back, so everyone keeps their memories of the intervening days. Here is my opening move. A griefer burns down a player's house and destroys a one-of-a-kind item; six days later, under pressure from support, we roll the world back to a snapshot in which both still exist. Now, if you say the destruction was a real event and a real harm, then after the rollback there is no destroyed house anywhere in the world's history — the persisted state now runs in a single, continuous timeline in which the house was standing the whole time, and the only traces of the fire are in a log cluster we chose not to restore and in the owner's head. So either the harm has been retroactively undone, which seems absurd because the player lost six days of work and still feels the loss, or the harm was real but its object no longer exists even in the past, which starts to look like whether a virtual event happened is settled by records outside the virtual world — that is, by us and our backups. Which horn do you take? And a smaller question riding along: is the restored house numerically the same house that burned, or a duplicate that replaced it, given that every row constituting it has been rewritten from a snapshot? Begin with your first in-character reply.
