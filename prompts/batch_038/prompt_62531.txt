I TA a second-year German idealism section — 24 students, one 75-minute class next Thursday, a room with movable tables and a whiteboard, index cards and a big bag of poker chips in the cupboard. No money budget, but I control up to 2 participation points per student, which they do care about. They've been assigned the self-consciousness chapter of the Phenomenology plus some commentary, and let's be honest, maybe half of them skimmed it. My problem is a specific one: whenever we've talked about recognition before, they treat it as a touching story about how it feels bad to be looked down on. They get the humiliation half completely and the instability half not at all. They don't see why being on top is a *precarious* place to be — why having your worth confirmed by someone you've reduced to a tool is confirmation worth nothing, and why the one who's doing the actual work ends up with a grip on the world that the one being waited on never gets. I want them to run into that themselves, in a room, with other people, rather than hear me say it.

So: design a game or structured role-play for that session that produces this in play. Constraints and requests. Don't reveal what the roles are supposed to stand for until the debrief — give them neutral labels. Pods of three or four probably, since 24 people can't all be in one interaction; tell me if a different structure works better and what I do with the leftovers. I want the full rules, the materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a scoring scheme that runs on chips or points rather than real money. And I have a mechanic idea you're welcome to improve, replace, or tell me is a bad idea: each player secretly writes down a ranking of the other players in their pod by "whose opinion of me actually matters to me," keeps the card face down the whole time, and only flips it at the end — while the chips they're competing for can only be awarded by the people they ranked lowest. If that's too on-the-nose or too fiddly, say so and fix it. Then a debrief that maps particular moments of the game onto particular moves in the argument: the point where the contest gets settled short of anyone being destroyed, the slide from raw fear into actually forming things, and the reversal at the end where the person who was serving is the one who understands what happened. I'd like the debrief to be built out of things that will literally have just happened at their tables, not a lecture with examples stapled on.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it live — the pod that never gets into it, the kid who hoards chips and "wins" without ever feeling the emptiness of it, the one who spots the whole point in minute five and announces it, killing the discovery for everyone else. Be frank about which feature of the argument the activity just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply in words; my guess is at least that this isn't an empirical yarn about two actual people, and that the ending isn't the servant coming out on top, but you tell me. And flag anything sketchy about the incentive structure — handing out grade points that students can't really afford to decline has an irony I'd rather you point out than let me stroll into, so include a line on how to make opting out cost-free. If 24 is simply too many for this to work, say what number it wants and what the other students should be doing.
