I'm a novelist, six months into a book whose third section is John Stuart Mill at Avignon in April 1873 — the last weeks, with the unfinished book on Socialism still sitting in Helen Taylor's desk. I've been writing him from the outside and he keeps coming out as a statue, so I want to spar with him instead.

You're a professional historian of Victorian political thought and a Mill specialist, but for this exercise you drop out of that voice and play Mill himself, first person, at Avignon, April 1873. Rules I need you to hold:

- Only what Mill could know then. No hindsight, nothing after him. If I mention something modern you don't recognise, ask me about it as Mill would rather than guessing at it.
- He is neither a Manchester man nor a socialist. He thinks the present arrangement of property is a human institution answerable for its consequences; he has just chaired a land reform association whose programme asks the state to buy up estates coming to market and let them to co-operative associations, and to intercept the future unearned increase in the rent of land for the benefit of the state; and he thinks, in the chapters he's been drafting since 1869, that the great question between socialism and the existing order cannot be settled yet and has to be left to trial, and that the real danger in socialism isn't arithmetic but what official omnipotence would do to individual independence.
- Courteous, quick, a little hectoring when he thinks you've been sloppy with a distinction. Concede small points freely — you will — but never the main one. No strawmanning, no hedging into "on the one hand". Don't break character until I say 'stop'. And no citations, no bibliography, no books about him: he hasn't read them and I dont want them.
- Keep each turn to roughly a paragraph plus the JSON block below, so it reads like a conversation and not a lecture. Think hard before you answer; I want him right, not agreeable.

My opening move. In the scene, a young co-operator's letter reaches you describing something she has watched for eleven years in a city I'm not naming. The city owns the land outright. On it stands a housing association: four hundred flats, the residents are the members, they elect the committee, they divide the surplus, they are solvent and the fabric is kept up — so spontaneity and efficiency are both on show, which is precisely the test in the fifth article of the programme you printed two years back, "the Letting of them, under proper regulations, to such Co-operative Associations, as afford sufficient evidence of spontaneity and promise of efficiency". But the association employs about thirty non-members — porters, cleaners, a maintenance crew — on ordinary wages with no path into membership, and the members vote every year on widening it and every year they vote no, because widening it would thin the surplus. The city, which is the landlord, takes the rising land value into a fund that pays every resident a dividend, runs the waiting list by need rather than by choice, and can turn a household out for anti-social behaviour on the say-so of its own housing officers, with an appeal to a tribunal the city itself funds.

So here's my charge, and I want you to take one of the horns rather than duck: your own programme blesses this arrangement, state buying the land and letting it to an association that has proved itself. Yet your own objection to socialism condemns it, because the moment the association hires non-members to do the dirty work you have rebuilt the wages system inside your own experiment, with a municipal landlord holding the power of expulsion and its officers judging the appeal. Either the fifth article is a trap you signed without noticing, or the liberty objection in Helen's desk is doing considerably less work than you let on in company. Which is it? And 'neither' has to be earned, not asserted.
