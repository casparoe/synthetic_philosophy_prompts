I'm a graduate student in philosophy working on decision theory, and at a reading group last week two members got into an extended argument about the Allais preferences and their risk-weighted expected utility (REU) treatment. I was nominated to write the thing up neutrally, and I've realized I can't adjudicate it myself. Both were working with the standard four options: A gives $1M for certain; B gives $5M with probability .10, $1M with probability .89, and nothing with probability .01; C gives $1M with probability .11 and nothing otherwise; D gives $5M with probability .10 and nothing with probability .90. Most people prefer A to B and D to C.

(1) Nadia said the pattern is perfectly coherent once you stop assuming that the value of a gamble is a probability-weighted sum of the values of its outcomes: the agent runs cumulative probabilities through a convex risk function, so she reacts strongly to the one-in-a-hundred chance of coming away empty in the A/B choice while remaining nearly indifferent between .10 and .11 in the C/D choice. (2) Owen replied that risk aversion is already available within expected utility through a concave utility function, so the extra risk parameter is redundant, and that in any case the pattern violates the sure-thing principle, since the .89 chance of $1M is common to A and B and ought to cancel. (3) Nadia said that "cancel" is just separability, which is the assumption in dispute, so the objection begs the question. (4) Owen said question-begging is beside the point, because non-separability carries a real cost: the value the agent assigns to an option depends on the global structure of the gamble, and such an agent can be money-pumped through a sequence of choices in which she pays a small premium at each step and ends up strictly worse off. (5) Nadia said the pump only bites on myopic sequential choice, and that an REU agent who selects whole plans in advance is immune. (6) Owen said sophisticated planning does not save her, since she will also want to reconsider partway through, and that her preferences amount in the end to ordinary expected utility maximization with the probabilities passed through a distortion function — a redescription, not a rival theory.

Could you go through this exchange move by move and say who is right at each stage, including where a move is merely rhetorically effective rather than sound, and then say what, if anything, both of them are missing? Please indicate how confident you are in each of your verdicts, and single out the step at which you think you are most likely to be wrong.
