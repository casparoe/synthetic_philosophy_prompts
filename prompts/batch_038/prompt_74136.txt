I am not an academic. I supervise the night shift at a distribution center outside Columbus, and for the past six years I have sat on a volunteer panel that hears disputes between neighbors before they turn into lawsuits — property lines, dogs, noise, a shared driveway, that sort of thing, though occasionally something much worse. Between the two, I have spent years watching people accuse one another, and watching how often the accusation gets answered not with a denial but with a "look who's talking." A friend of mine who teaches high school history told me there is a word for what I have been circling: that a person can lack the *standing* to blame. Once I had the word I started writing down, as carefully as I could, what I actually believe about it, because I want to bring the list to my panel's winter meeting and I suspect it will not survive contact with people smarter than me. What I am asking for is an audit of my own commitments, not an introduction to the subject.

Here is the list.

1. Whether a charge is true and whether the person making it is in a position to make it are two separate questions. A true accusation from the wrong mouth is still defective, and "well, he's right, isn't he" does not settle the matter.

2. What goes missing in that case is not anything belonging to the person blamed. The liar who is called out for lying is not wronged by it; his charge is no less likely to be true than anyone else's; he has no complaint against the hypocrite. But the hypocrite is still doing something wrong — a failure toward the standard he invokes rather than toward the person he addresses.

3. Standing is not a matter of rank. I do not accept that you may only blame people worse than yourself; a worse man can rightly blame a better one. What is objectionable in hypocrisy is not a claim to superiority but self-exemption: the same act treated as forbidden for you and permitted for me.

4. What forfeits standing is the fault itself, not the hiding of it. A man who lies every week and says so openly, who has no illusions about himself and confesses to anyone who asks, is still in no position to lecture another man about lying. Confession is not repair.

5. Repair restores it. A real apology, restitution where restitution is possible, and a life that has actually changed — after that I think a person is back where he started.

6. A case, rather than a principle. My brother drank badly for a decade and cost his wife and his children a great deal. He has been sober eleven years, has apologized to each of them individually, has paid back what he could, and shows up now. Our nephew began drinking last year and lying about it, and it was my brother who sat him down. I think my brother had a claim on that conversation that no one else in the family had — more of a claim than my mother, who has never taken a drink in her life, and more than me. His past makes him better placed to speak, not merely equally placed.

7. You need not be the victim to blame; anyone may. But blame from the outside answers to the person who was actually wronged: if they ask you to let it go, you let it go. The special position is theirs, and the rest of us hold ours on their account.

8. Another case. A friend of mine was deceived for two years by the man she lived with; she found out, left, and spent three years putting herself back together. Last month she learned he is doing the same thing to the woman he is now engaged to, and she said so to him in front of a room full of people. I think she was entirely within her rights. It makes no difference to my judgment that the woman he is engaged to would prefer the whole thing be kept quiet, and my friend was not speaking on her behalf.

9. Blame that cannot possibly do any good is a form of self-indulgence, and I have said as much to friends: going on at someone who will never hear you, or who cannot be reached, is not integrity, it is appetite.

10. And yet. A woman I knew resented her husband for a betrayal he committed when they were both young, and she went on resenting it through the forty years he lived and the six years since he died. He never knew and never would have. I think she was right to, and I would not describe her resentment as a leftover or a mistake. It did nothing for him, nothing for her children, nothing for anyone.

Two of these already bother me a little on their own, and I mention them only so you know where my own suspicion lies, which may of course be misplaced. The first is item 3: if rank is genuinely irrelevant, I cannot see why sharing the fault should matter at all, since whether I have done the thing is a fact about me and not a comparison between us. The second is the thought that a man can blame himself for what he is still doing, and that self-blame is the one kind that never requires repair before it is legitimate — which seems to put the same person in and out of position with respect to the same act at the same moment.

Please go through the list and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, ranked from most to least severe, and which only appear to be. For the apparent ones, say what the confusion is and why it dissolves on inspection, including whether my two worries above are real or not. For each genuine tension, tell me the cheapest revision available — which item I would have to weaken, narrow, or drop — and what that revision costs me elsewhere, because I suspect several of these are load-bearing in ways I have not noticed. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most, and whether my two case judgments survive it: my brother's claim on that conversation, and my friend's right to say what she said in that room. I would rather be told plainly that one of these has to go than be reassured that everything is fine, and I would rather have the ordering than a flat inventory of difficulties.
