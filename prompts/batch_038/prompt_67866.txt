I'm TA-ing an upper-level PPE seminar and I have to write this year's take-home exam question. The trouble is the prof assigns the same trio of readings on repugnant markets every year and the students all hand in the same essay about kidney sales or vote selling or surge pricing after a hurricane, a good chunk of it lifted from summaries online. I want a question that forces them to build something instead of reciting, and my plan is to make them design a case where the two standard camps swap places — but before I put it on an exam I want to know whether it can actually be done. So could you construct the case for me?

The two camps, roughly: camp A is the corruption/degradation camp (Sandel-ish), which holds that some goods have a proper mode of valuation and that pricing or selling them degrades them or expresses the wrong attitude toward them, over and above whatever harm anyone suffers; camp B is the market-legitimacy camp, which holds that a voluntary, informed exchange between the parties is presumptively fine and that repugnance is a taste that has to be argued for rather than assumed (Roth treats repugnance more as a constraint that keeps markets incomplete than as a moral argument, which is the flavor I want). Normally camp A says block it and camp B says let it happen. I want a realistic case where it comes out the other way: camp A concludes a market in the good ought to be allowed, or even created where none exists, and camp B concludes it ought to be blocked or heavily restricted.

Constraints, because otherwise my students will cheat and I assume you might too:

- One paragraph for the scenario itself. Realistic institutional setting — a city, a school district, a fishery, a hospital, a court, a public utility. No sci-fi, no future tech, no invented scarcity gadget.
- None of the usual suspects: no organs, no votes, no babies, no surrogacy, no sex work, no blood, no carbon permits, no scalping or queue-jumping, no post-disaster price gouging.
- The reversal has to be generated by the structure of the case, not stipulated. Nobody has a gun to their head, nobody is described as so destitute that the coercion is doing the work, the good isn't declared sacred or priceless by fiat, and nobody simply feels disgusted. If I can delete one sentence and the verdicts flip back to normal, tell me which sentence.
- Don't lean on the gift/sale asymmetry (the intuition that it's alright to give something away but not to sell it). I think that asymmetry is interesting but it's a different question and it short-circuits this one.
- Camp B's objection must be internal to camp B: voluntariness, information, exit, third parties who never agreed to anything, or the market not actually being the market it's advertised as. It can't be a corruption argument wearing an economist's coat. Camp A's support must be internal to camp A: about what pricing this good in this institutional setting expresses, or does to how the good is valued — not just that the poor will lose out, which is a harm argument they share with camp B anyway.

After the scenario, walk me through both verdicts in each camp's own reasoning; name the single feature of the case that's doing the work; say what happens if you remove it, i.e. whether the case collapses back to the normal direction and what that collapse would tell us about whether these two camps are even disagreeing about the same thing. Then give me the best attack each side would launch on the case as rigged — I expect camp B to say A's support is a welfare argument in disguise, and camp A to say B's objection is corruption talk with the word externality stapled on — and tell me which attack lands harder. Finally be honest about whether the case is really about commodification at all, or whether it dissolves into ordinary institutional design; a case that only looks like a reversal because I picked a badly specified institution is a failed case, not a clever one.

No bibliography, no reading list, no page numbers — you can name people if it helps but don't invent references. One more thing, and this matters for how I'll grade the answers: mark clearly what's settled versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. My sense is that there's broad agreement that objections to markets come in distinct families (harm and exploitation, corruption and degradation, legitimacy and democratic equality) and that only some of those families are controversial, whereas whether corruption is a free-standing moral consideration or just harms and tastes repackaged is very much live. I'm also not sure how much of the crowding-out story is empirically nailed down. Don't present a contested view as the consensus, and flag anywhere you're extrapolating past what's actually established.
