So I've been going down a rabbit hole on welfare biology and I built an argument that looks valid to me but has a conclusion I can't live with, and I genuinely can't tell whether there's a hidden equivocation in it or whether I just have to swallow something. Here it is:

(1) For any being that can be harmed, a life that is on net bad for it is worse for that being than never existing at all.

(2) Most wild animals are born in huge numbers and die within days or weeks of it — starvation, predation, parasitism, exposure — so the large majority of sentient lives any given ecosystem produces are net-negative for the animal living them.

(3) Destroying or degrading a habitat reduces the number of animals that ecosystem will go on to produce.

(4) So habitat destruction makes things better. It prevents net-bad lives from being lived, and there's nobody it fails: nobody is deprived of anything, because the animals who would have lived those lives never exist to be deprived.

(5) The same reasoning scales. If a big enough majority of sentient lives are net-bad, we have decisive reason to shrink ecosystems wherever we can — sterilize the soil, drain the wetland, crash the insect populations — and a barren rock is better than a flourishing forest.

(6) And notice what (4) already commits me to. To get from "these lives would have been bad" to "things are better" without a subject, I have to treat value as just a quantity sitting in an outcome, to be totaled up. But then individuals drop out entirely: I can't say draining the wetland wrongs any of the animals in it, only that it improves the totals. That's true of every action on this picture.

(7) (5) and (6) are things I refuse to accept, so something above is false.

Where I'm stuck is that I can't figure out which. (4) feels like its doing the dirty work — the phrase "worse for it" in (1) has a subject attached, and I can't see how to reuse it in (4) where the subject has been deleted. But maybe the problem is really in (1), which might only ever apply to beings who already exist and can be made worse or better off, and if I restrict it that way I'm not sure what's left of it. Or maybe the slide is between (4) and (5): perhaps I'm allowed to say a particular act of destruction prevents bad lives without being allowed to generalize that into a program, and if so I need to know what blocks the generalization, because it looks like the same premises both times.

The other thing is I really dont want to lose the ability to say that preventing suffering in animals who are already around is good — that seems like the one solid thing in this whole area — and I'm worried that whatever move kills (4) kills that too.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine before you rejected it? If there's an equivocation, spell out both readings and say exactly where the argument switches between them. If you think it's actually valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to give up and price it — specifically whether there's any way to keep (1) without buying (6), and whether one of those asymmetry intuitions (reason to prevent misery, no reason to create happiness) gives me an exit or just relocates the problem. And how much of this rests on (2) as an empirical claim? If it turns out most wild lives are net-positive, does the argument simply evaporate, or is there a version of it that survives that finding?
