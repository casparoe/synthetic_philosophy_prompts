so Ruth and I spent basically all of saturday arguing about whether the cross-cultural intuition studies actually settle anything — the Gettier-style probes where east asian and western participants supposedly split, the proper-name one with the mathematician whose theorem turns out to have been proved by an uncredited grad student, the free will ones — and we got nowhere. Partly because we kept sliding between two claims that are not the same: (a) that verdicts about these little stories are unreliable evidence for anything, and (b) that there's no shared thing being judged in the first place, so the two groups were never actually disagreeing. Ruth's whole position is that the variation is elicitation noise — translation drift, story framing, order effects, and a chunk of the original stuff hasn't replicated cleanly. My position is that even a flawless replication doesn't get you from "verdicts differ" to (a) without an argument about concept-sharing, which she keeps skipping.

I've been sketching my own vocabulary for the argument and I want to stress-test it. I sort apparent divergence into four kinds: *elicitation variance* (same competence, the probe artifact moves the verdict); *calibration drift* (same concept, different cutoff — both groups are tracking something like "enough evidence to rule out the salient alternatives," one just demands more); *conceptual homonymy* (same word, different concept, so neither group is wrong and the philosophical debate was never joined); and *theoretical load* (the verdict is downstream of a background theory the participant brings, which I think is the only one of the four where variation is genuinely informative about the concept). Plus a worry I keep calling the same-syllabus asymmetry: within-culture variation — SES, gender, framing, question order — gets read as showing the intuition is unreliable, but cross-cultural variation gets read as showing there's no fact of the matter. Why the different treatment?

What I actually want is the spec, before I read another paper on either side of this. Write the desiderata for an account of the evidential significance of cross-cultural variation in philosophical intuitions, where "adequate" is fixed like this: good enough that a working philosopher could use it as a decision procedure — a new study reports variation in some intuition, and the account tells me what I'm licensed to conclude about the intuition and about the theory leaning on it.

For each requirement give the case or consideration that forces it (be concrete: a probe type, a scenario, a specific worry — not just "must handle translation"). The list needs to cover, at minimum: keeping a challenge to intuitions-as-evidence separate from a challenge to one particular intuition; explaining stability in the data, not just variation, since an account that predicts divergence everywhere owes me something when a pattern shows up across a dozen languages; being statable without treating "the folk concept" as a black box; and saying what would count as evidence *for the account itself*, or it's just unfalsifiable dressing. I especially want the homonymy problem in there — how would you ever distinguish homonymy from genuine disagreement, and doesn't that criterion need evidence that can't itself be an intuition? And something about whether philosophical training changes the picture, since both sides claim the experts and something about how much they diverge.

Flag any pair of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied. My guess is that "mustn't smuggle intuitions in as evidence" and "must separate homonymy from disagreement" are in direct tension, but I want your version of the problem, not mine.
