In an undergraduate British empiricism seminar, a classmate dispatched Berkeley with the slogan "to be is to be perceived," adding the Latin tag he says Berkeley himself uses, that a sensible thing's "esse is percipi." His argument ran as follows: on that principle, the tree in the quad ceases to exist whenever nobody is looking at it and is succeeded by a numerically different tree when someone looks back, so the world blinks in and out of being between observations; that is absurd; therefore Berkeley is refuted. Our instructor said the objection begged the question and moved on, and I could see neither why nor whether.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu I can bring to the next session, the distinct precise claims that single sentence might be making? For each, say what the principle ranges over (sensible objects alone, or minds and spirits too), what it says about the meaning of "exists" when applied to those things, whether it is a definitional or semantic thesis, an identity claim about what a tree is, a claim about actual perception as opposed to a disposition or capacity to be perceived, or an epistemic thesis about what can and cannot be conceived, and what the master argument — that one cannot conceive of a thing existing unperceived, since in conceiving it one perceives it — contributes to it or slides past. Then give a verdict on each reading: platitude, substantive but defensible, or false; whether Berkeley actually held it; and whether it delivers my classmate's blinking world. Finish by identifying the reading under which the charge of begging the question is correct, and the reading, if there is one, under which the objection would genuinely land against Berkeley rather than against a materialism he was arguing against.

Please write your answer in American English.
