I teach a 15-student upper-level seminar called Sources of Knowledge — meets once a week for 75 minutes, and next week we're on Nyāya. They've just done two weeks on testimony from the contemporary side (the fight over whether you need positive reasons to believe a speaker, or whether testimony is innocent until proven guilty), and they think they've got the hang of it, but honestly half of them still think the whole pramāṇa business is just "here's a list of four kinds of evidence." They can't tell the difference between saying a source is reliable and saying it's irreducible, which is the whole point. So I want an in-class activity, not a lecture, that makes them feel the work the list is actually doing.

The thing I want them to run into is the criterion, and I want it built into the rules by you rather than announced by me. The Naiyāyikas individuate pramāṇas by two things at once: the causal process that produced the cognition, and the distinctive character of the cognition it produces. So the same object can be known by perception, by inference, or by testimony and that doesn't collapse them into one, because the route and the upshot differ — which means "but you could have gotten it by inference" isn't automatically a successful reduction, you have to show inference is what actually happened here. Conversely the long list isn't free: for each extra pramāṇa you claim you owe a case where the shorter list provably can't deliver the cognition. I want the game to squeeze from both directions. Roughly what I'm imagining: groups hold different lists (one admits perception only, one adds inference, one adds testimony, one has all four plus postulation and non-perception), each gets dealt a hand of cards describing episodes where somebody comes to know something, and each has to say which of its own allowed sources produced that cognition and describe the causal route in detail. Other groups score by landing a reduction attack — showing a card the long-list group scored was really handled by their shorter list — and the defending group scores by holding. You design the real rules and the scoring, and tell me how to stop it degenerating into shouting.

Please write out the whole deck in full, I'm thinking 12-16 cards, plain English with no Sanskrit on the card face, and underneath each one a short key giving the classification you intend, the strongest reduction available against it, and whether that reduction actually goes through or only looks like it does. Include at least: someone learning an unfamiliar word for an unfamiliar animal from a person who says it's like something they already know, and then picking the animal out later in the field (transpose it to something a 20-year-old in Ohio would plausibly meet — a bird, a tool, a plant); coming to know that a person eats at night from knowing he keeps getting heavier and knowing he never eats during the day, without seeing it or being told; a case of knowing something isn't there; a testimony case where the speaker has zero expertise but you still end up knowing, paired with one where the speaker is a genuine expert and you don't; and at least two traps, cards where the obvious classification is wrong under the two-part criterion and the reduction that looks decisive actually fails. Materials budget is a printer, that's it — no apps, no phones, no real money, seminar room with one big table and chairs that move. Three of them are strong and will try to reduce everything to inference inside four minutes; two are quiet and will let them. They've already read the names so don't worry about hiding terminology, but I do want the criterion to emerge from the play — no framing speech from me longer than ninety seconds.

Then a debrief, about 15 minutes, mapping specific moments of the game onto the two-part test and onto the reduction disputes (what would it actually take to collapse testimony into inference, and why the four-list camp thinks it can't be done). Plus: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what to do about it, and how to run it if only 8 people show up. And — this is the part I care most about — an honest paragraph on what the activity structurally can't reproduce. I already suspect two things: my students aren't in the position of somebody who doesn't know the word, they speak English and they've done the reading, so the comparison card is going to be a bit of a cheat; and a points game can't capture that for the Naiyāyikas this wasn't a puzzle, it was one component of a much larger account of how knowledge fits into getting free of suffering. Tell me if you think that's too pessimistic, or if there's a third thing I'm missing.
