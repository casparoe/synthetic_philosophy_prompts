You are a philosopher of social science whose work concerns measurement, operationalization, and the semantics of comparative claims about social quantities, and who also writes on the ethics of violence. I would like you to think carefully about the following constructed problem before you write, and to treat it as a substantive conceptual question rather than an empirical survey. The figures are invented and self-contained: everything you need is stated below, so please do not try to recall or estimate real-world data.

Consider a simplified fictional country, Marenia. Its statistical office publishes annual figures for violent deaths, which it defines, for now, as deaths caused by the deliberate infliction of bodily harm by one person or group upon another. In 1990, Marenia's population was 20 million, split between a highland region (population 10 million; violent-death rate 300 per 100,000 per year) and a lowland region (population 10 million; rate 100 per 100,000). That yields 30,000 highland deaths, 10,000 lowland deaths, 40,000 violent deaths nationally, and a national rate of 200 per 100,000. In 2020, the population was 50 million: the highlands had 15 million residents at a rate of 350 per 100,000 (52,500 deaths), and the lowlands had 35 million residents at a rate of 120 per 100,000 (42,000 deaths). That yields 94,500 violent deaths nationally and a national rate of 189 per 100,000. So over the thirty-year period the national rate fell by roughly 5 percent, the absolute number of victims rose by roughly 136 percent, and the rate rose within *both* regions, while the share of the population living in the lowlands grew from one half to seven tenths. At a workshop on these figures, a demographer maintains that the sentence "Marenia has become less violent" is straightforwardly true, because per-capita risk is what the concept tracks and what any rational resident should care about; a moral philosopher maintains that it is false, because a claim about violence is ultimately a claim about how many people are actually harmed, and there are now more than twice as many such people.

Three complications deserve to be built into your analysis. First, the two years' tallies are not defined alike: the 1990 series counted only killings by private individuals, whereas the 2020 series was broadened to include deaths inflicted by state agents and deaths resulting from policies that predictably deprive people of food, shelter, or medicine, which the office now classifies as violence. Had the 2020 definition been applied retroactively, the 1990 total would very likely have been considerably higher. Second, highland record-keeping in 1990 was weak and many deaths there went unrecorded, so the true highland rate that year was probably worse than the published 300 per 100,000; by 2020 registration was near-complete. Third, within each region the risk is far from evenly distributed: it is concentrated by age, sex, and neighborhood, so each regional rate is an average over sharply heterogeneous exposures, and the national rate is an average of averages.

Please write your response in two parts. **Part A** should be a brief preliminary analysis — no more than a page — in which you brainstorm and enumerate the distinct quantities that someone might mean by "how violent is Marenia" (for instance, though you are not limited to these: the probability that a randomly chosen resident dies violently in a given year; the total number of victims; the number of violent events; the concentration or dispersion of risk across the population; the expected harm to a person behind a veil of ignorance about where she will live in the country). For each candidate quantity, state plainly whether it went up or down between 1990 and 2020, and say what is going on arithmetically in the case where the aggregate and the subgroups disagree. **Part B** should be a polished essay of roughly 1,600 to 2,200 words that reaches and defends a verdict on two questions: (i) is the sentence "Marenia has become less violent over this period" true, false, or neither, and (ii) does the general question "is the world becoming less violent?" — the question at the center of the familiar long-run-decline thesis associated most prominently with Steven Pinker, and of the objections to it — have a determinate answer, and if so, what is it? Do not end on a non-committal note. A qualified verdict is entirely acceptable (for example: true relative to measure X, and X is the measure that the question, properly understood, is asking about), but you must argue positively for the measure you privilege and explain what is wrong, or merely different, about the rivals. In the course of the essay, please address: whether the demographer and the moral philosopher are having a merely verbal dispute, a substantive disagreement about the extension of a concept, or a substantive moral disagreement about what we should care about, and how one would tell these apart; whether the definitional drift between 1990 and 2020 renders the comparison ill-posed, and whether the same worry applies to any long-run comparison of the actual world, where our categories and our capacity to record have changed far more than Marenia's; whether institutional and structural harms belong inside the count of violence, and what happens to the verdict if they do; and whether the uneven distribution of risk means that a national rate is the wrong kind of summary for a question about how violent a society is. You may mention well-known positions and thinkers by name, but do not include a bibliography, a reference list, or page citations, and do not put anything in quotation marks that you have not been given verbatim here. Write in American English.
