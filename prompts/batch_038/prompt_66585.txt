Popper died in 1994, so he never had to reckon with cliodynamics, and I think that's a shame because it's the closest thing we've got to a direct test of his thesis.

Quick sketch of the thing I mean, since it's the crux and it's not exactly household knowledge. Peter Turchin and his collaborators built a structural-demographic model of political instability: they take slow-moving variables like the trend in ordinary workers' real wages, the degree to which there are more elite aspirants than elite positions to fill (they call this elite overproduction), and the fiscal strain on the state, code them up over long stretches of time, and fit a model that spits out a political-stress index. Around 2010 they went public with a forecast that the decade running to 2020 would be a period of growing instability in the US and Western Europe, which more or less occured. They also run a big cross-cultural history databank, Seshat, to test general claims about how societies scale up and centralize, and Turchin has argued separately that agrarian states show recurring multi-century secular cycles of growth, elite proliferation, breakdown, and collapse.

So: what would Popper have made of all this? I want a real two-sided working-through, not a Popper hagiography and not a cheap gotcha. Things I'd like you to actually argue about rather than just list:

Would he classify it as historicism, or as exactly the kind of conditional, law-based, initial-conditions-specified social prediction that he always insisted was legitimate? Where does he draw that line, and does cliodynamics land on the right side of it or does it sneak across?

His argument that we can't predict the future growth of knowledge, and that the growth of knowledge is itself a driver of history, so the course of history is in principle unpredictable — does that argument even get a grip on this? Cliodynamics isn't forecasting ideas or inventions, it's forecasting unrest from demography and elite structure. Is that a genuine escape hatch, or a change of subject that dodges his point?

The Oedipus effect. Turchin's forecast was public, widely reported, and used politically. Does the possibility that people read about coming instability and act on it — to avert it, or perversely to position themselves for it — wreck the enterprise on Popper's own terms? Or does it cut the other way, since using forecasts to guide intervention is basically piecemeal social engineering wearing a quantitative costume?

And the one I'm most interested in: is there a reading under which cliodynamics shows Popper's own position was too strong? If you really can get decent medium-range forecasts of aggregate instability out of structural variables, maybe what's objectionable about historicism isn't prediction as such but the claim to know the direction and meaning of history as a whole. Can Popper make that narrower distinction with the resources he's got, or does his logical argument overrule and thereby discredit a lot of ordinary social science he'd want to keep?

Finish with an actual verdict: what do you think his considered judgment would have been — dismissal, cautious interest, something more complicated? You can absolutely conclude that he'd have had to revise parts of his own case, but you have to earn that. If you want to channel his voice while doing it, paraphrase; don't invent quotes or put words in his mouth in quotation marks.
