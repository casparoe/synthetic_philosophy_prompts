I cover probability and physics for a general-interest science magazine, and next month I'm giving a talk at a small institute seminar. The room will be maybe a dozen working statisticians and physicists who use probabilities every day but never touch the philosophy, plus two or three philosophers who know the Lewis literature cold. My thesis is that the Principal Principle isn't really a principle about chance at all — it's a special case of a general norm about deferring to a law-like informant — and that once you see it that way, the admissability problem stops looking like a bug. Inadmissible evidence is just evidence your informant doesn't share with you, and the principle goes quiet precisely when you know more than they do.

Here's the sketch of my argument, which is what they'll be aiming at:

1. Any norm of the form "match your credence to what a source tells you about how the world will distribute itself" needs an account of when you're allowed to override that source with private information.
2. Chance claims are just the claims of an idealized informant whose evidence is the whole prior history plus the laws, and nothing indexical about you in particular.
3. So deference holds whenever your extra evidence is stuff the informant already has, and lapses otherwise — and thats not the principle failing, it's the principle working as intended.
4. Which means the interesting question isn't what makes evidence admissible, it's what makes an authority law-like. That's where I want to end.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense. In this order:

- How the room will hear this — about 100 words: what a statistician takes my claim to be, what a philosopher takes it to be, and where those two readings come apart in a way that will bite me mid-talk.
- Objections, worst first — six to eight, numbered. For each: how that specific person in that specific room would actually phrase it (their vocabulary, not textbook vocabulary), a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, and a one-line tag on where the reply is soft.
- Concede these up front — about 150 words, the things I should give away in the first five minutes to buy credibility with the philosophers without losing the physicists.
- The one that has to land — about 100 words: the single reply without which the whole talk collapses.
- The question I'm hoping nobody asks — about 75 words.

Two notes on the epistemics. Where an objection touches something that's genuinely settled among people who work on this, say so flatly; but where it's a live fight — the undermining futures business, whether chance needs the Principle in order to deserve the name at all, whether Hall-style conditionalizing actually repairs anything — mark it as contested and don't smuggle one side in as the consensus. And please flag anywhere my sketch above misstates a standard position or overstates how novel the move is. I'd much rather hear it from you than from the third row.
