**Draft: "Recourse, Not Reasons: A Diachronic Account of Testimonial Entitlement"**

*§1. The shared premise.* Reductionists in the Humean tradition hold that a hearer's testimonial belief is justified only if she has positive reasons for taking the speaker to be reliable on this occasion, reasons that ultimately bottom out in her own perception, memory, and induction. Anti-reductionists in the Reidian tradition hold that testimony carries a presumptive warrant: the hearer is entitled unless she has a defeater, and she need not marshal anything. I want to argue that both sides accept a premise that generates the stalemate — namely, that the epistemic work is done synchronically, at the moment of uptake, by properties of the hearer's evidence. Once that premise is dropped, the disagreement largely dissolves.

*§2. The proposal.* What marks testimony off from perception, memory, and inference is not its reliability profile but its liability structure. In telling you that p, a speaker does not merely cause a belief in you; she undertakes an obligation to stand behind it — to correct it if she learns it was false, to retract it if she withdraws, to answer for it if you relied on it and were misled. Call this the repair obligation. The hearer's entitlement is then not a matter of possessing reasons for thinking the speaker reliable, nor a bare default; it consists in her having standing access to that repair obligation. She is entitled because she can go back. Entitlement is diachronic and relational rather than evidential.

*§3. Why this is not a compromise view.* It sides with the anti-reductionist in denying that positive reasons are required at uptake, but it denies that entitlement is a default: it is conferred by a structure that must actually obtain. It sides with the reductionist in insisting that something beyond the saying is needed, but that something is the availability of recourse, not evidence about reliability. In ordinary face-to-face exchange, recourse is so cheap and so readily available — you can ask again, object, be told she was joking, be corrected next week — that it is invisible, and this invisibility is what makes the entitlement look like a default. On my account the anti-reductionist intuition is real but misdescribed: entitlement is earned, just inexpensively.

*§4. Consequences.* (a) Testimony that severs recourse — anonymous postings, machine-generated text, one-shot encounters with strangers — should lose its presumptive warrant and require something closer to reductionist reasons. This explains why it sounds odd to demand that you have reasons for believing a friend and quite natural to demand reasons for believing a pseudonymous forum post. (b) The child case: children are entitled not because entitlement is innate but because a child's testimonial environment is saturated with recourse; caregivers are perpetually correctable and corrective. (c) Epistemic institutions are good insofar as they preserve recourse — bylines, errata, retraction policies, comment threads, appeals procedures — which yields both a principled preference for them and a diagnosis of what is wrong with unattributed and uncorrectable communication.

*§5. The hard case: the dead.* Authors cannot retract. I suggest that repair obligations are transferable, and that editors, translators, archivists, publishers, and interpretive communities act as proxy custodians who inherit them. Reading a text whose author died two centuries ago is therefore not epistemically anomalous; the recourse is real but institutional and slow. Where no custodian survives — an oral tradition whose community has dispersed, an orphaned archive with no maintainer — testimony on my account has degraded into something that must be assessed evidentially, and the reductionist is simply right about that case.

*§6. Two worries I have not resolved.* First, collapse: if "I have access to repair" is itself something the hearer possesses, is it not just a positive reason, so that I have restated reductionism in better vocabulary? My answer is that what does the work is a normative standing rather than a piece of evidence, and that it need not be represented by the hearer at all — but I am not confident that this is a difference in kind rather than of description. Second, the liability analogy may be load-bearing in a way I cannot support: warranty and tort liability have enforcement behind them, and if enforcement is what generates the obligation, my account may only get off the ground where something like a legal or institutional system already exists, which would make it far less general than advertised.

**What I would like comments on**

I suspect an anti-reductionist could reply that presumptive warrant is already a standing, time-extending entitlement rather than a moment-of-uptake property. How damaging would that reply be?
- Is the thesis genuinely distinct from the family of views on which accepting testimony is accepting an assurance, or a second-personal transaction between speaker and hearer? If it is a variant of those, tell me what the recourse framing adds and what it costs.
