I am a novelist at work on my fourth book, and I have written myself into a difficulty I cannot resolve without help. Chapter Eleven consists almost entirely of a lecture delivered by a physicist character — a serious, meticulous, possibly self-deceiving man — who believes he has refuted the second law of thermodynamics by way of Maxwell's demon. I drafted his lecture from popular treatments, and my problem is that I cannot tell whether he is right. If he is right, this is a novel about a discovery; if he is wrong, it is a novel about a mind quietly coming apart, and the two books are not remotely the same book. I would prefer not to learn which one I have written from a physicist reader after publication.

Please take the role of an expert in the thermodynamics of information: someone fluent in the demon literature from Szilard onward, comfortable with Landauer's bound and the reversible-computing response to the demon, and equally at home in the philosophical questions about how the second law is to be stated. Think carefully rather than quickly. I would like you to begin with a short brainstorming pass — two or three paragraphs canvassing every place in the argument where a slide might plausibly be suspected, without yet committing to one. Only then give me your diagnosis.

Here is the lecture as drafted.

(1) Every step of a computation except erasure can in principle be carried out reversibly, and a reversible step, performed slowly enough, costs an arbitrarily small amount of energy.
(2) The single operation with an unavoidable thermodynamic price is the erasure of a bit: at least kT ln 2 must be dissipated as heat, whatever the physical implementation.
(3) Therefore a computing being that never erases — that keeps an append-only record of everything it has ever learned — pays nothing for its thinking, however long it thinks.
(4) Such a being can run a Szilard engine: determine which half of the box contains the molecule, insert the piston, allow the molecule to expand isothermally against it, and thereby extract kT ln 2 of work from a single heat bath.
(5) Its measurement record is written into fresh memory each cycle and never overwritten, so no erasure ever occurs and no cost is ever incurred.
(6) Nothing limits the number of cycles: memory can always be extended, writing into fresh memory is of the cheap reversible kind described in (1), and the being is by stipulation patient and long-lived.
(7) Hence it extracts an unlimited quantity of work from a single heat bath at uniform temperature.
(8) The obvious objection is that the process is not cyclic, since the being never returns to its initial state. But the second law is not a bookkeeping convention about where an agent happens to end up. Bath, engine, being, and memory together form a closed system, and the only change in that system over the whole run is a longer record. A record is a physical state, yet holding one costs nothing, and extending one is simply more of the same cheap writing described in (1).
(9) So the extraction stands, and the second law, stated without qualification, is false.
(10) The rescue of the demon by erasure-cost accounting therefore rescues nothing: it applies only to beings that forget.

My character is most attached to (2) and to (10). I have written him as a man who would sooner lose the second law than lose Landauer's principle, and I like that about him. But I am not committed to his preferences, and I will happily rebuild the chapter around a different casualty.

What I need from you:

- Which step do you reject — or, if you judge the argument valid, which premise is least costly to surrender? I do not want a survey of the general possibilities; I want your verdict on this argument.
- Explain why that step looked innocent to me when I drafted it. The reader of the novel will feel exactly the pull I felt, and I need to know precisely what intuition the step exploits and how it misleads.
- Price the alternatives. If he gives up (2), what has he lost? If he gives up (6) or (8), what has he conceded, and is there a weaker conclusion he may still hold — something an honest physicist could keep while admitting this argument fails?
- Tell me whether there is any defensible formulation of the second law under which he is simply right, and if so, how that formulation must be qualified. This matters more than it may appear: the entire book turns on whether my character is a crank or a martyr to a truth nobody can use.

Work through the accounting explicitly — energy and entropy, cycle by cycle, for the composite of bath, engine, and memory — rather than appealing to the fact that the consensus is against him. Be quantitative where it clarifies. Define your terms as you go, since the eventual audience is a diligent lay reader, but do not simplify the physics until the difficulty vanishes; the difficulty is the scene. I do not want references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want the reasoning, and I want it argued rather than asserted.
