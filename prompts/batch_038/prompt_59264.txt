I'm a third-year doing a course on societal collapse (lots of Tainter, some Diamond, plus the archaeologists who think both of them are wrong), and there's a line that comes up in every single seminar: societies don't collapse, they transform. Different tutors say it in different tones and I genuinely can't tell if they're making the same claim. Sometimes it means something deflationary about the word — nobody died, the population carried on, they just reorganised. Sometimes it sounds like a claim that states and dynasties ended but the people and their language and gods carried on, which is not obviously the same thing. And sometimes it's clearly doing something else entirely, like saying that calling it a collapse is a value judgement dressed up as description, or a way of writing off living descendendant communities. I keep sliding between these and getting destroyed in tutorials.

Can you act as someone who's properly steeped in the collapse-debate literature — archaeology and historical sociology — but who's also careful about the conceptual distinctions? Don't just reach for the standard "it depends what you mean by collapse" paragraph. Actually brainstorm first: sketch six to eight candidate readings of that sentence, from the near-trivial to the radical, then prune them down and present only the ones worth taking seriously, with a verdict on each — platitudinous, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or actually false.

The reading I most need is the one that would license the use I keep hearing: my tutor shut down a question about whether the end of Classic Maya lowland cities counts as a collapse by saying it, and someone else used the same sentence about Easter Island / Rapa Nui to insist that nothing collapsed there at all because the society carried on. For each of those two cases, say which reading the sentence would have to carry to do that work, and whether it does carry it — i.e. whether the slogan is settling an empirical question, a semantic one, or a political one, and whether it's entitled to.

Where the readings turn on real cases (Maya, Rapa Nui, the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, whatever you think is cleanest), use the cases to test the readings rather than narrating them — I've read the case studies, I don't need a summary of them.

Hard limit: 800 words for the whole thing.
