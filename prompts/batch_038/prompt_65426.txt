I'm running a methods workshop for graduate students in sociology and economics, most of whom arrive assuming they must choose between methodological individualism and holism, and I want a decision tree that shows the choice is not binary. Please build one with seven to nine nodes and ten to twelve leaves, laid out as indented text.

Every node must be a question about a concrete explanatory situation rather than about terminology: no node may use the words individualism, holism, reduction, emergence, or supervenience, and a student should be able to answer it without knowing which leaf any answer leads toward. Candidate nodes include whether an explanation citing only aggregate phenomena must be traceable to the actions of identifiable people; whether a social kind can be picked out without knowing which individuals instantiate it; whether a group can hold a belief or intention that no member holds; and whether what makes an explanation correct is the causal history of the explanandum or its predictive and unifying performance.

The leaves must include at least: rational-choice reductionism; mechanism-based analytical sociology; the view that group agents are real but analyzable into individual commitments and decision procedures; Durkheimian social facts as irreducible; a systems theory on which communication rather than persons is the basic element; critical-realist emergence; an interpretivist position on which the explananda are meaning-laden actions; and a deflationary view on which the dispute is merely verbal. Then mark the unoccupied combinations your nodes generate, and say for each whether nobody is there because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or simply unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot in fact separate.

One case must serve as a node or leaf-tester: an explanation whose central relatum is an algorithmic ranking system — a credit-scoring model, say — that has persisted unchanged through several complete turnovers of the engineers who built it and of the people it scores.

No references or bibliography; labels for the positions are enough. Close with a note on how confident you are that the tree discriminates the leaves it claims to, and single out the node or leaf placement you are least sure of.
