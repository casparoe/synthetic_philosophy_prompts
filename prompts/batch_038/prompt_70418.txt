I teach an intermediate social choice module — 24 students, one 75-minute session — and I want them to run into Sen's liberal paradox through play rather than through my explanation of it. Design a game in which students in pairs each receive a private written preference ordering that covers their partner's two personal choices as well as one of their own, constructed so that the meddling component feels like something a real person might genuinely feel rather than a caricature, and in which the class must then produce a single ranking of the outcomes. Give me the materials, a token-based payoff scheme that requires no money, and a two-phase structure: in phase one the protected personal spheres are assigned by fiat, and in phase two those spheres become tradeable and waivable, so the group discovers on its own whether allowing trades dissolves the conflict or merely relocates it. The words Pareto, impossibility, and Sen must not surface until the debrief.

Then supply a debrief that maps particular moments of play onto each of the two formal conditions and onto what waivability does and does not repair; the three things most likely to go wrong the first time I run it, with a fix for each; and a frank note on which feature of the theorem the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.

Format: exactly five numbered items — setup and materials; the preference-profile generator and payoff scheme; the two phases and their rules; the debrief mapping; failure modes plus the irreproducible feature — one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion.
