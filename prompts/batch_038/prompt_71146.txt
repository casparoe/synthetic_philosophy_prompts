For four years I have served on a volunteer panel at a mid-sized logistics company that decides whether an employee gets modified duties, and somewhere around the second year it became clear to me that nearly every argument we have comes down to a disagreement nobody on the panel can state cleanly. Roughly half of us behave as though whether something is a disease is settled by the body: if a part of you is doing what parts like it statistically typically do in people of your age and sex, you are healthy, and whatever else we want to say is preference dressed up as fact. The other half behave as though that formulation is a smokescreen — as though calling something a disease always carries a judgment about how a life ought to go, and as though the supposedly neutral statistics were compiled by somebody with an interest in the result.

I have no academic training in any of this. I have read what I could get hold of, including a theory that defines health as normal functioning relative to a reference class, and the two objections that stuck with me: that it quietly imports values anyway, and that the choice of reference class looks arbitrary. What I want from you is the document our panel should have produced three years ago — the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between a committed defender of the factual, statistical account of disease and a committed defender of the view that disease is irreducibly value-laden. Neither may be a strawman. Both should be people I would recognize from my own table, and both should be writing to end an argument rather than to win it.

The document should contain:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign. Substantive ones, not "both take suffering seriously." If they can agree that a condition can be a disease without being painful, or that the same bodily state can be pathological in one environment and unremarkable in another, or that a person can be diseased while feeling perfectly well, say so, and note in one line why each side accepts it for its own reasons.

2. A table of the disagreements that survive the exercise. For each row: the shared description of the case, then the two positions worded so that each side would accept the wording of its own row without flinching. For at least three rows, state whether you judge the dispute to be partly verbal rather than about the world; if it is, name the word doing the damage and say what remains once the verbal part is stripped away.

3. The cruxes. My own guess is that there are exactly three, and I want to be told if that is wrong. First: whether the statistical account can fix its reference class without already making a value judgment. My test case, which I invented and which you should treat as a hypothetical rather than as something you need to look up — an isolated community in which nearly everyone carries a variant that prevents them from metabolizing the local dietary staple, so that what is statistically typical there is precisely what makes people sick, while a newcomer with ordinary metabolism is the outlier. Which side does that case favor, and can either side absorb it without giving up its central claim? Second: whether the value-laden account can explain why we decline to call ordinary human limitations — poor pitch, short stature, the slow healing of old age — diseases, without sliding into the position that a disease is whatever a given society happens to disvalue. Third: what the two accounts actually disagree about when they hand down practical verdicts. Name a real category of case in which they come out differently, or concede in the document that they never do and explain what that would mean for the debate.

4. For each crux, the test both sides agree bears on it — a case, an argument, or a settled piece of diagnostic or epidemiological practice — with each side's prediction recorded in advance, before anyone runs the test. I want to be able to put this in front of two people and have them argue about the prediction rather than about the framing of the question.

5. A short closing clause stating what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and whether there is anything at all that both agree would move the dispute.

Write the document itself, not guidance about how to write it. Where you need a term of art or the proper name of a position, introduce it in parentheses the first time it appears, since I will not have come across it.
