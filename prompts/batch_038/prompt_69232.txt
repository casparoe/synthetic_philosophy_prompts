For thirty-eight years I worked in comparative molecular biology, and only since retiring have I taken up philosophy with any seriousness. What strikes me is that your field has inherited, almost unawares, a problem my own spent a century on: deciding when two things count as the same mechanism. Biologists came to separate sameness by descent (orthology) from sameness by function arrived at independently (analogy), and we learned the hard way that a molecule can retain its catalytic activity while its physiological role changes completely — which is precisely the case that matters.

I should like you to apply that discipline to a concrete situation. An auditor must determine whether a deployed model still contains the mechanism that was inspected during a safety evaluation, after the model has been fine-tuned, quantized, and scaled up. Rank the following criteria for mechanism identity from most to least useful to that auditor, assigning each a score out of ten together with one sentence of justification:

1. identical input–output behavior on a benchmark suite;
2. identical algorithmic description (both, say, copy and extend patterns found earlier in the context);
3. identical causal role — ablating either produces the same pattern of downstream behavioral effects;
4. identical developmental origin, arising at a comparable stage of training from comparable data;
5. proximity in weight space, allowing for permutation and scaling symmetries;
6. identical response to interventions designed to suppress or amplify it;
7. descent from a common checkpoint or training lineage.

Then tell me which of these are orthology criteria, which are analogy criteria, and which are neither; whether "descent" is even well defined for mechanisms in two independently trained models that merely share an architecture family and a data mixture; which single criterion you would retain if you could run only one test; and name at least one pair of criteria that no realistic audit could pull apart. Please write in American English.
