I have no academic training in philosophy. I am a structural engineer, and I arrived at the following views over several years of reading cosmology on my own and thinking about what it would mean for ethics if the universe turns out to have no edge. I would like a careful audit of whether these seven commitments can all be held at once. I am not looking for an introduction to the subject, for the name of the school of thought I might belong to, or for suggestions about what to read; I want to know which of these pull against one another, how badly, and what it costs to keep the remainder.

Here is what I believe, in my own words.

1. The universe may well be spatially infinite, and roughly the same sort of thing everywhere on the largest scales. If it is, then there are infinitely many people, and every joy and every horror that occurs here occurs endlessly many times elsewhere.

2. What makes one outcome better than another is the total wellbeing it contains. You add up how much good there is in the lives of everyone who ever exists, and the larger sum wins.

3. If two outcomes each contain an infinite amount of wellbeing, neither is better than the other. I cannot make sense of one endless quantity being larger than another, and when people say that one is, I suspect a trick being played with words.

4. A change that leaves every individual's life exactly as good as it was cannot make an outcome better or worse. Picture a street with no end to it, a house at every address, and one contented person in each house. If everyone moves one house along so that the first house stands empty, nothing of moral importance has occurred: nobody's life improved or worsened, and the empty house is no loss to anyone.

5. I have a test case of my own, and I am confident of the verdict. Take two universes like the one in (1), each containing infinitely many people. In the first, everyone's life is worth ten units. In the second, everyone's life is worth ten units, except that every billionth person's is worth eleven. The second universe is better than the first, and I do not see how anyone could say differently.

6. When I got out of bed at four in the morning last winter and drove a woman I had never met to the emergency room, the outcome was better than it would have been had I stayed where I was. That is not an illusion produced by the size of the cosmos.

7. I will not accept a moral theory whose conclusion is that nothing anyone does matters. If a line of reasoning arrives there, I take it to have refuted the reasoning rather than discovered something about morality.

These have always felt to me as though they support each other, and I have been assuming that they do. Please work through the set pair by pair and tell me: which pairs genuinely conflict, and which merely look as though they conflict and dissolve once you state them precisely. I expect that at least one pair I would have flagged myself is of the second kind, and I would like to be told where my instinct went wrong. Rank the real conflicts by severity rather than presenting them side by side as equally serious. For each of them, say exactly what I would have to give up or water down to save the rest, what that costs, and whether the resulting position is still one an ordinary person could live inside. Then tell me which single revision to the set would repair the most damage at once. If holding onto some of these commitments requires me to add a premise I have not listed, name it and say whether it is worth the price. And where your conclusions rest on assumptions about the cosmos that I have stated loosely or left out entirely, say so explicitly instead of filling the gap for me.
