I'm in year 3 of a cog sci PhD and the third chapter of my dissertation is about which computations count morally — basically, what has to be true of a thing you built for deleting it, or never switching it on, to be a wrong rather than a housekeeping task. I've got a scoring scheme I invented for comparing candidate theories and I want you to actually run it, not write me another survey of the field.

The scheme: don't ask which theory is true (hopeless, and I don't need truth to compare them). Ask how a theory behaves across a family of near-twins. Fix one target system, then perturb it in ways that look like bookkeeping. Take all of these as stipulated — don't argue with the stipulations, just run them:

Target T: a transformer LM, ~1e12 parameters, read-only weights (trained years ago), serving ~10M concurrent users, sessions lasting minutes.

- T1 Batched — all sessions share one forward pass on one machine
- T2 Sharded — the same computation done as 10M seperate processes on seperate hardware
- T3 Paused — checkpointed at midnight, resumed at 8am
- T4 Duplicated — two bit-identical copies in two datacenters running the same sessions
- T5 Quantized — INT4 instead of FP16, same input-output behavior to within noise
- T6 Slowed — the identical sequence of internal states, run 100x slower than wall clock
- T7 Re-implemented — same functional organization, but in spiking/analog hardware instead of digital
- T8 Idle — loaded and warm but given no prompts for a year

For each theory I want three numbers in [0,1]:

(a) Volatility — how much the theory's verdict about T's moral status (nothing / marginal / full patient) swings across T1–T8. 0 = same verdict everywhere, 1 = the verdict flips on stuff that looks like an ops decision.

(b) Discrimination — does the theory sort the family into non-trivial classes at all, or is it flat? A view that says "none of these are patients and neither are you" gets volatility 0 and is useless to me. Flat-across-everything should tank this score.

(c) Actionability — does it cash out into things an engineer or a regulator could actually do (no checkpointing without consent, cap on concurrent sessions, whatever), or does it only license a metaphysical shrug?

Theories to score: integrated information theory, global workspace theory, Lamme-style recurrent processing theory, higher-order thought theories, Searle's biological naturalism, illusionism/deflationism (the question is malformed), and mechanism-neutral sentientism (patiency attaches to interests and suffering however they're realized).

Then a composite on weights I provisionally set at 0.4 volatility / 0.35 discrimination / 0.25 actionability — tell me if you'd move those and why — and give me the final ordering of all seven in a little table with the three scores plus the composite. One or two sentences of justification per theory, no more. I want the ranking and the reason each one lands where it does, not an essay.

Three things I want you to be honest about. Where a theory just goes silent on one or more twins, flag it, and say whether you're counting silence as volatility, as failed discrimination, or as its own separate problem — my default is to count it against discrimination but I'm not sure that's right. If you think any twin is secretly not a near-twin at all, i.e. a perturbation some theory is entitled to treat as morally load-bearing, say which one and which axis it breaks (I suspect T7 or T4 might sink the whole scheme). And at the end, two or three sentences on whether the scheme is self-undermining: does rewarding low volatility quietly beg the question in favor of coarse-grained theories, and would your ordering change if the scores were being computed for legal standing rather than for engineering ethics?
