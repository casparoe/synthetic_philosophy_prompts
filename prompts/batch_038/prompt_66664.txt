I'm taking a social ontology seminar this semester — the whole course is "what kind of thing is this category that social scientists use every day," so we've done race, money, borders, unemployment, and now we're on generations. Honestly it's the week I'm least ready for, because I've absorbed so much pop sociology about boomers and gen z from news feeds and twitter threads that I can't tell anymore which parts are actual theory and which parts are just newspaper columns wearing a theory costume. My prof assigned a short paper and I want to use you to think out loud with, basically.

Here's what I want: an essay answering the question "are generations real kinds?" I don't want a survey or a both-sides tour, I want you to pick a side and defend it. The live options as I understand them are (a) generations are genuine social kinds, somewhere in the neighborhood of money or race; (b) they're real but only as arbitrary demographic bookkeeping — birth-year bins that happen to have nicknames; (c) they're a journalistic fiction that researchers tolerate because it's convenient. A qualified verdict is fine the way "one-box if the predictor is reliable" is a fine verdict in Newcomb — but "it's complicated and there are good arguments on both sides" is not allowed to be your conclusion, you have to actually commit to something.

Some specific things I'd want you to get into rather than just mention in passing:

- The identification problem. In any dataset cohort = period − age, so the three are perfectly collinear and you can't estimate a cohort effect separately from age and period effects without importing an assumption from outside the data. Does that make generational effects unmeasurable in principle, or just really hard? And if it's in-principle, is that an ontological finding about generations or just a finding about our methods?
- Mannheim's layered picture. He didn't think everyone born in the same window automatically belonged to a generation — there's the bare fact of being born into the same historical location, then the smaller set who actually get activated by it and respond in a shared way, and then rival sub-groups inside even that who react to the same events in opposed directions. If the real action is only at that last level, then the thing that's real isn't the cohort at all, it's something much smaller and messier than "Millennials." Does shrinking the category that way save generational realism or kill it?
- Cusps. Someone born in December 1980 and someone born in January 1981 land in different generations with supposedly different psychologies and nobody can say what the cutoff is tracking. Are there other social categories with boundaries this arbitrary that we'd still insist are real, or is this the disanalogy that does the damage?
- The naming loop. Once a label is out there people read the description of themselves and start conforming to it or defining themselves against it, so the category feeds back into the thing it names. I can see this cutting both ways — it could make generations more real, because they become self-stabilizing, or less real, because they turn out to be artifacts of the classification rather than of the world — and I genuinely don't know which direction you'll argue.
- Strauss and Howe. The version I know of has history running on a repeating cycle of four roughly twenty-year generational types, each slot getting a fixed character. I know it's treated as fringe, but I want you to say *why* in a way that's more precise than "it's astrology for business consultants" — is the problem the fixed cycle length, the archetypes, the unfalsifiability, the way it handles the identification issue above, or all four? And does throwing it out commit you to throwing out generational kinds entirely?
- Portability. I'm pretty sure the anglophone scheme doesn't travel cleanly — my vague memory is that Chinese usage is basically birth-decade bins, and other countries have thrown up one-off labels tied to particular events instead of to birth windows at all. If the granularity and the boundaries shift from country to country, is there any fact of the matter about where a generation starts?

Two requests on top of that. First, please be explicit about which parts of this debate are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on it professionally — don't dress up a minority position as the consensus, but also don't hedge the stuff where there's real agreement; if everyone sensible thinks the pop-psychology trait lists are junk, just say so flatly. Second, no bibliography and no further-reading list at the end, my prof counts that as padding and I couldn't check it anyway. Around 1,800 to 2,200 words, American English please.
