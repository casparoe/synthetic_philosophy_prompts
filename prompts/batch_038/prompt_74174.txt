Give a worked example of what I'll call a self-undermining lost cause: a national myth built on a military defeat whose meaning for the community that carries it depends on the defeat's staying a defeat, so that the community's ritual wish that the cause had triumphed pulls against what the myth actually supplies. Choose a case where the tension surfaces in practice rather than only in principle — in a slogan, a ceremony, a monument, a school textbook convention, or a public argument in which victory for the cause would have cheapened or dissolved the meaning now drawn from the loss. Set out the mechanism in your chosen case, saying what exactly the defeat provides that a victory would not; say how far the mechanism generalizes across the familiar defeat-myths; and then name one defeat-myth that your account should predict to be self-undermining but which visibly is not, a case in which the community plainly wants the counterfactual victory and loses nothing by wanting it.

State your example and your diagnosis of it in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question. Distinguish throughout between what is settled among historians of national memory, what is genuinely contested among them, and what is your own reconstruction, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
