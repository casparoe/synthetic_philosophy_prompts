I'm a second-year philosophy MA student and my department has a small internal summer research fellowship — ten weeks, basically covers my rent, and they want a four-page proposal plus a one-page timeline by the end of the month. Here's the half-baked idea I keep circling back to: everyone frames the reference class problem as a problem about entitlement, i.e. which class am I allowed to use when I assign a probability to this one event, and the answers are either a rule (use the narrowest class for which you have reliable statistics, demand maximal specificity, insist on homogeneity in something like von Mises's sense) or a shrug (single-case probability was never a frequency anyway, it's a propensity or a credence, so the problem dissolves or relocates). Neither feels like progress to me. But I've been reading enough statistics on the side to notice that the tension people are gesturing at is pretty much the bias-variance tradeoff: a narrower class is more homogeneous and less biased but has fewer observations and hence a noisier estimate, and statistics has spent decades building tools for exactly that situation — shrinkage, partial pooling, hierarchical models, empirical Bayes — where you don't pick a class at all, you interpolate between a broad one and a narrow one with a weight set by how much variance each contributes. My hunch is that the reference class problem has been stuck partly because it's been treated as a rule-following problem when it's really a loss-minimizing problem, and that once you ask which class minimizes expected error relative to a stated loss function, some of the old disputes start to look like disagreements about the loss function rather than about what probability is.

Turn that into the proposal. I want the research question stated precisely enough that someone could tell whether I'd answered it; a diagnosis of why the existing positions leave it open, including an honest word on whether the propensity/subjectivist move really does dissolve the problem, since I suspect it partly does for some cases and not others; the argument — this is the bit my advisor keeps pushing on — for why this is philosophy rather than importing a chapter of a statistics textbook, i.e. what conceptual work is left over once the machinery is in place; a week-by-week plan for ten weeks at roughly twenty hours a week; what a successful result looks like, meaning what specific claim I'd be defending at the end; and what would count as the project failing. On case selection, which I think decides whether the proposal is worth anything: I want at least one case where there's real data to pool, the courtroom sort where the denominator is what's contested (a plaintiff shows that four of the five buses on a route belong to one company, or a DNA match probability shifts depending on which population you take frequencies from), and one where there's no data whatsoever and the class choice is doing purely philosophical work, like the Doomsday argument or Sleeping Beauty, where moving the reference class around moves the answer. My worry, and the proposal should confront it rather than hide it, is that the shrinkage move needs sample sizes and frequencies that simply don't exist in the second kind of case, so the framework might only bite where the problem was already tractable; if that's the honest verdict I'd much rather the proposal narrow its scope and say so than oversell. Also tell me whether the Goodman angle is worth including — the thought that both grue and the reference class problem turn on which classes are projectible — or whether its a distraction that'll blow up the scope of a ten-week project.

One last thing, and it matters a lot to me: keep a visible line between what's actually settled here and what people who know this literature still argue about. I've only been reading around the edges, so I cant tell whether the single-case objection to frequentism is still live or whether everyone's moved past it, and I especially can't tell whether the bias-variance connection I think I've stumbled onto has already been made by someone — if it has, say so plainly and tell me what would be left for me to do, because that changes the whole project. And if you think the framing itself is wrong — if the tradeoff isn't really bias-variance, or if you think the loss function can't be stated without begging the question against one side of the original debate — tell me that up front instead of writing a polished proposal around a bad idea.
