For two terms I have convened a graduate reading group on pragmatics, and the same argument surfaces whatever we read. On one side are those working in the neo-Gricean line: a small set of general heuristics — one keyed to what the speaker did not say, one to what would normally be the case, one to the markedness of a form — which generate generalized implicatures by default, defeasible but computed unless context blocks them. On the other, those in the relevance-theoretic line: no maxims and no defaults, only the single presumption that an utterance is worth the hearer's processing effort, with enrichment, loosening, and strengthening all determined ad hoc by the particular context, so that what looks like a default scalar inference is simply the fastest contextual route available in a typical case. Both sides appeal to the same data: some, empty, warm, the extra inference carried by a prolix or unusual form, and the fact that these inferences seem to arrive without effort yet can be cancelled.

The dispute has produced a great many experiments and no convergence, and I doubt that is an accident. My working hypothesis — which I want tested rather than adopted — is that the two camps are not answering the same question: one is asking what a theory of linguistic meaning must posit to explain what speakers communicate, the other what a theory of cognition must posit to explain how hearers actually recover it in real time, and the appearance of head-to-head contradiction arises because both use the word implicature and both present themselves as completing Grice's project. On that reading the disagreement is largely a division of labor misread as a dispute, and progress would consist in stating which questions each framework is competent to answer. The alternative I can see is that there is a genuine single crux — whether any pragmatic inference is triggered by form alone, prior to and independently of contextual assumptions — on which the theories make incompatible predictions, and that what has blocked settlement is measurement rather than confusion.

Diagnose this: substantive disagreement, verbal disagreement, two questions under one heading, or a substantive core wrapped in verbal disagreement at the edges.
