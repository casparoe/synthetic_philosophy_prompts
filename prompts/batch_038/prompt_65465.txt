I cover science and ideas for a magazine — mostly biology, some tech — and I've been going around in circles on a piece about what a genome actually is. Not the "book of life" stuff. The practice side.

Here's what's bugging me. The companies that print custom DNA to order — a lab emails them a few thousand As, Cs, Gs and Ts and gets back a vial — screen orders before they make them. The string gets run against databases of sequences from known pathogens and known toxins, and if it hits above some threshold the order gets flagged, held, or refused, and there's paperwork and customer verification behind it. This is now a pretty standard industry arrangement, with government guidance pushing it along. Meanwhile the same field tells you, constantly and correctly, that a sequence on its own doesn't do anything: what a gene does depends on where it sits, what's upstream and downstream, the cell type, the organism, the environment, the epigenetic marks, the whole developmental context. And everyone agrees you can't build a virus out of a text file — labs have gotten infectious virus out of DNA assembled from ordered synthetic pieces with no natural template, but that took a real laboratory, cell machinery, and years of tacit skill, and the string was maybe a third of the work.

So what picture of DNA does the screening practice actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not an opinion on whether the policy is too strict or too loose — I've got no interest in the policy debate and my editor has even less. Specifically:

- What has to be true about sequences for "does this string match a known bad string" to be a meaningful test of how dangerous an order is? What notion of information is doing the work — is the sequence being treated as a blueprint, a recipe, a code, an identifier, a causal trigger, or some inconsistent blend? Pick apart which of those the machinery actually needs versus which it just borrows language from.
- Which rival pictures of what a genome is does this quietly rule out? And which rival pictures is it compatible with even though nobody operating the system would admit it out loud?
- Is the practice internally consistent, given that the people who build the pathogen databases and the people who write the screening rules broadly accept that context determines what a sequence does? If there's a real tension, say which belief has to give, and whether it's a tension inside the practice or just between the practice and the field's official self-description.
- Where is the thing underdetermined — where would two people with genuinely opposed theories of genetic information end up endorsing the exact same screening protocol, for different reasons? I want to know which parts of the setup carry philosophical weight and which parts are just cheap insurance that anyone would buy.
- And if the field swapped the string-picture for the context-picture, what would screening have to look like instead? Concretely: what would you screen for, if not the sequence? Don't just say "capability" — tell me what the artifact under scrutiny would be.

Two caveats. First, I know there's a long-running argument in philosophy of biology about whether "genetic information" is a real technical notion, a useful metaphor, or a category mistake, and I'm deliberately not asking for a survey of it or a reading list — I can't check sources anyway and I don't want guessed-at titles. Bring in that argument only at the points where it changes the answer about the practice. Second, if I've got any of the mechanics wrong above — the thresholds, who screens what, how far sequence-only construction actually gets you — say so plainly at the top rather than quietly working around it.

Format: work through the five bullets in order, keep it tight, and don't spend a paragraph on background I've already given you. Then end with a single last line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <label>

where the label is one of three, with nothing after it — NECESSARY (screening only makes sense if the sequence is close to self-sufficient), FITS-BUT-NOT-REQUIRED (the sequence picture is the natural story but weaker views license the same protocol), or INCOHERENT (the practice's presuppositions contradict each other and something has to give).
