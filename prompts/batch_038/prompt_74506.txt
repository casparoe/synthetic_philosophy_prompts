I write a long-form column on science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about why safety work is said to cost something even when no one can point to the capability that went missing. I have absorbed the vocabulary — the alignment tax, the competitiveness constraint — but when I try to state precisely what is being taxed, in what units, and by whom, my draft turns to fog. Could you explain the conceptual issue for me at a level an informed general reader could follow, without flattening the structure of the problem?

Here is the tension I cannot resolve. The alignment tax is nearly always described as a shortfall in capability: a system built to be safe is said to be somewhat less capable than the otherwise-equivalent system that was not. But the competitiveness constraint is a claim about races — a developer that absorbs costs its rivals decline to absorb risks being overtaken, and therefore cannot afford the costs, regardless of whether it wants to. Those are not obviously the same currency, and I want to understand where and why they come apart.

Four specific points I would like sorted out:

1. Is a tax that manifests as delay the same kind of thing as a tax that manifests as a permanently lower ceiling? My instinct is that they matter very differently when what is at stake is who crosses some threshold first — a one-time lag can be made up, and might even be worth paying, whereas a ceiling cannot. I would like to know whether that instinct survives scrutiny, and what would falsify it.

2. The tax is normally presented as a property of a technique, something a lab pays for choosing to do safety work. But its size appears to depend entirely on what rivals are doing at the same time. Does that mean the alignment tax is not a property of a technique at all but a property of a strategic situation — and if so, what would it even mean to estimate one in isolation, as people routinely try to do?

3. Certain kinds of safety work seem to make a developer better informed or a system more tractable rather than less capable. Is that a negative tax, a genuine good of a different kind, or a category confusion on my part?

4. The evidence most often cited that the tax is small is that models given safety training score roughly the same on benchmarks as models that were not. What does that license, and what does it not? I especially want the distinction between a capability gap measurable at present-day scale and the gap that would matter at the frontier, if there is one.

Please make the points with concrete hypothetical cases rather than abstractions, and tell me plainly which of my four framings is simply wrong. I would much rather be corrected than reassured, since this is going to print.
