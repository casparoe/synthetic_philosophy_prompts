So a little background, because it'll help you calibrate what I'm after: I'm 69, retired three years ago after spending my whole career in renal physiology and a good chunk of it measuring kidney function in clinical trials. Since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy of medicine as a hobby, mostly the health-and-disease stuff, and I've hit a wall I can't get past. I want you to play the role of a philosopher of medicine who has actually worked through both sides of the naturalism/normativism debate closely enough to know where each one's argument genuinely bites and where it's just rhetorical momentum. Please think hard before answering and don't give me the polite survey version.

Here's the puzzle. The naturalists say disease is a value-free biological notion — a statistically typical part-function of the species, relativized to a reference class by age and sex, doing its job below some threshold of efficiency. The normativists say that can't work, that you can't get from "atypical function" to "disease" without a judgment about which deviations are bad, and that the whole reason we have the word is to license treatment, sick leave, insurance, and so on. Then the hybrids come along and say you need a dysfunction component that's genuinely natural plus a separate judgment that the condition is harmful. And nobody has moved anybody in forty years, as far as I can tell.

My hunch — and I want you to stress-test it, not flatter it — is that there's no factual disagreement in there at all. Every case they trade back and forth, both sides would agree about the underlying physiology if you put them in a room with the labs. What they're actually fighting over is what job the word is supposed to do: track a natural kind that explains, or gatekeep who gets treated and who gets compensated. Possibly it's a fight about which concept to adopt rather than about what "disease" already means. And possibly the debate is just ill-posed because the single word is doing three jobs at once — explanation, legitimation of intervention, allocation of benefits — and each side picks one job and calls it the essence.

The concrete case that got me going: by the standard cutoff, an eGFR persistently under 60 is stage 3 chronic kidney disease. A large fraction of people over eighty sit under that line with no progression, no proteinuria, nothing going to happen to them. The age-relativized reference class makes the naturalist say that's normal-for-age and so not a disease — but then the naturalist owes us an account of why we'd still be right to monitor or even treat it, which feels like it concedes the normativist's point. Meanwhile some nephrologists argue the threshold itself should be age-adapted, and I never quite understood whether that argument was doing philosophy or medicine.
