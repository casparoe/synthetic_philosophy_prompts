I convene a graduate reading group in a history department that has spent the term on the historiography of British abolition, and two of my students — one trained in economic history, one in moral philosophy — have been talking past each other since February. The first holds that norms of this kind end because the material position of the elites who uphold them changes: Eric Williams's account in Capitalism and Slavery, on which the West Indian system had lost its economic weight, is his paradigm case, and he treats appeals to honor or humanity as downstream legitimation. He concedes that Drescher's Econocide badly damaged the crude version of that story — the British slave system was still expanding and profitable when the trade was abolished in 1807 — but he relocates the interest rather than abandoning it, pointing to the shifting political weight of the planter class against metropolitan commercial and industrial interests and to what abolition did for British naval power. The second holds that neither argument nor interest explains the timing, and that what collapses a practice is its revaluation inside an honor world: once the practice becomes a mark of disgrace before the peers whose esteem one actually cares about, it goes quickly and irreversibly. Her cases are Appiah's — dueling among British gentlemen, footbinding in China, British abolition — and she makes much of the 1829 encounter between Wellington and Winchilsea, in which a sitting prime minister fired a pistol at a peer and emerged with his standing enhanced even as the press denounced the practice as a superstition.

Please write the joint statement these two should produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want, first, a numbered section of claims both can sign about how such norms actually end, including the reciprocal concessions: the economic historian's admission that Drescher's profitability data stands, and the honor theorist's admission that esteem is itself something people have interests in. Second, a table of the residual disagreements with three columns — the materialist's own wording, the honor theorist's own wording, and a neutral restatement that each would accept as fair to the other side. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them. I suspect there are at least three — whether the timing of abolition across the Atlantic polities tracks an inflection in the relevant elite's material position or in its status configuration; why dueling withered in Britain while persisting among comparably status-competitive elites in France, Germany, and the American South under broadly similar economic conditions; and whether, in the contemporary West African programs for abandoning female cutting practices that pair public community declarations with alternative marriage arrangements, the declaration machinery is doing the causal work or merely riding along with improvements in women's economic prospects. For each crux, state what evidence or argument both accept as bearing on it and record each side's prediction in advance, so that the disagreement is falsifiable rather than rhetorical.

If you judge that some portion of the dispute is verbal — that interest and honor are two descriptions of a single selection process rather than competing causes — say which portion, and identify what substantive residue survives the deflation. Keep the document under 1,000 words, make the table its longest component, and include no bibliography or reference list.
