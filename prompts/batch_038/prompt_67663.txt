The claim I want your credence in: take a citizens assembly of about 100 randomly selected people who spend two weekends hearing evidence and arguing with each other, then take a straight majority vote on a factual question that has a knowable answer — something like "will this proposed waste plant exceed its emissions limits within ten years, yes or no." The claim is that the assembly's majority verdict is more likely to be correct than the median individual member was before they started deliberating. So, roughly: does the Condorcet jury theorem survive the trip from idealised coin-flipping jurors to actual humans who talk to one another and read the same newspapers?

Give me your number, then the decomposition — the sub-questions the number depends on, a rough probability for each with a line of justification, and how you combine them into the final figure. Candidate terms, though add or drop as you see fit: that individual competence is genuinely above 0.5 on questions of this kind rather than 0.5-ish with a fat tail; that deliberation leaves enough residual independence for the majority to still beat the individual; that "more likely right than the median pre-deliberation member" is even a well posed target once you notice that deliberating changes both what people believe and what they believe it about; that there's a determinate fact of the matter about who the median member was, given they're a different epistemic agent by the end of the weekend.
