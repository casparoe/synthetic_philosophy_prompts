The dispute between moral generalists and moral particularists concerns whether morality can be captured in principles that hold invariantly across cases. Generalists maintain that at least some considerations are reasons of fixed valence: a consideration that counts in favour of an act in one case cannot, on this view, count against it in another. Particularists deny this, holding that the valence of a consideration may shift with context, so that the most we can manage is a loose picture of how matters tend to go. A familiar rejoinder is that the particularist need not be hostile to principles as such, only to invariant ones, and that a range of intermediate formulations is available. It is those intermediates I want to evaluate.

Take the case of promising. Let φ stand for the act I have promised to perform, and consider six candidate principles concerning the reason-giving force of the fact that I promised to φ:

1. **Unqualified invariance.** Whenever I have promised to φ, the fact that I promised is a reason for me to φ, and the strength of that reason is the same in every case.

2. **Invariance with fixed weight.** Whenever I have promised to φ, the fact that I promised is a reason for me to φ of constant weight w; whether I ought to φ, all things considered, depends upon whether the reasons on the other side together outweigh w.

3. **Invariance with enumerated defeaters.** Whenever I have promised to φ, the fact that I promised is a reason for me to φ, except where φ-ing would bring about disaster, or where the promisee has released me, or where the promise was extracted by coercion or deceit, or where — and the list is to be continued until the exceptions have all been stated.

4. **Default with a shifting burden of proof.** Whenever I have promised to φ, the fact that I promised is a reason for me to φ unless the contrary is shown; whoever denies that the reason applies here must make the case, whilst no requirement is imposed that the defeating conditions be capable of complete enumeration in advance.

5. **Loose generalisation.** In the ordinary run of cases, the fact that I promised to φ is a reason for me to φ. No claim is made about any individual case, and the principle carries no more confidence than a generalisation of the same shape elsewhere in practical life.

6. **Deliberative rule without metaphysical commitment.** In deliberating, I am to treat the fact that I promised to φ as a reason for me to φ; nothing is thereby asserted about whether it genuinely is one, and the practice of so treating it is itself justified by what it does for trust and for the reliability of one's dealings with others.

Please rate each of the six against two scales, each out of 10:

- **Robustness**: how well the formulation remains true, without amendment, in cases where the reason-giving force of a promise is in fact defeated, absent, or reversed.
- **Bite**: how much of the work we want principles to do the formulation retains — specifically, whether it can guide an agent in advance of deliberation, whether it grounds criticism of someone who simply ignores the consideration, and whether it allows others to predict how the agent will behave.

For each formulation, give both scores with a sentence or two of justification, then assign it to a tier (A, B or C) according to how well it fares on the two desiderata taken together. In addition, state which trade-off you take to be doing the real work in your tiering, and identify the formulation you regard as least stable — that is, the one most liable to collapse into another on the list under sustained pressure.

Finally, two further questions. Would your ranking change if the chosen formulation had to be written into the code of conduct of a professional body, whose members are required to accept the text whilst disagreeing about the theory underneath it? And would it change again if the text had instead to be applied by a decision-support system capable only of evaluating conditions it can actually check?
