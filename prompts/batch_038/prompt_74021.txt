I am closing a graduate seminar on personal identity with a session on Hofstadter's suggestion that a self, being a pattern rather than a substance, can be spread across more than one skull — that some part of a person's "I" is literally located in those who know them well. For that session I would like a dialogue of about a thousand words between a defender of this claim and a genuinely formidable critic of it.

Let the case be a living one rather than a bereavement: two philosophers who have co-authored everything for thirty years and now speak of themselves, half-jokingly, as a single thinking thing with two bodies. The critic's best argument should not be about numerical identity in the abstract but about arbitration: within one brain, competing candidate states are resolved by a single attentional stream, one autobiographical narrative, and one mouth making commitments on the whole system's behalf, whereas across two brains there is nothing to play that role — so what the pair has achieved is stable coupling, not a distributed subject. The defender should be allowed to reply that the same drift-and-repair happens inside a single skull across decades, and should then press the critic to say why the intracranial arbiter is anything more than a further pattern that could itself in principle be shared.

Neither character may reach for the teleporter, the ship of Theseus, or split-brain patients; each must at least once take up the other's own argument and turn it around. The exchange should end not in a conversion but with the disagreement relocated onto some sharper question, and both positions must be stated well enough that a specialist in the field would feel obliged to answer them.
