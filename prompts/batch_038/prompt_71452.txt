Two people in my lab's reading group got into a pretty heated argument after a talk on interpretability, and I sort of volunteered to referee, which I now regret. Let me try to lay out the back and forth as accurately as I can. The setup they were fighting over is the kind of result everyone seems to be producing right now: take a small transformer trained on a grid-navigation task, find a direction in activation space that a linear probe decodes as "wall to the north," show that the direction is present across lots of different prompts, and show that patching it in or out shifts the policy the way you'd expect. Then the paper says the model represents wall-relative position.

M said: grant all of that for the sake of argument, but none of it gets you to a representation rather than a causally efficacious internal state that happens to be decodable under a description we like. Her complaint was that "decodable" is doing sneaky work — given a rich enough activation space a probe can find a direction matching nearly any sensible description you hand it, so the result tells you as much about the probe and the description as about the model. And patching only upgrades the claim to "this state is used," which is also true of the bimetallic strip in a thermostat. She said representation has to require something extra — a norm of correctness, or counterfactual tracking, or systematic compositional structure — and that interpretability work never tests for any of those.

R said that's moving the goalposts to a standard no empirical finding could ever clear, and that M's "extra ingredient" is precisely what the generalizes-across-prompts result delivers: the same direction gets recruited for the same content in new contexts, which is what you'd expect if the content is real and not what you'd expect if the decoding were an artifact of how flexible probes are. He also pointed to a follow-up experiment where a differently-architected model, trained on a different task that also happened to have walls, turned up a closely-corresponding direction. He said independent convergence like that is about the best evidence you could ask for.

M's answer was that convergence says nothing about content, because two models trained on similar data by similar optimizers will land on similar geometry, and shared geometry isn't shared semantics. Then she made a move I couldn't answer at the time: in the ablation condition, killing the wall direction changed behavior only when the agent was adjacent to a wall, and left it untouched everywhere else. She said if the model genuinely represented wall-relative position, patching ought to have had graded effects across all positions, and that the locality of the effect is exactly what you'd predict if "the wall feature" is a convenient fiction for summarizing a context-dependent computation instead of something the model has.

R said the locality result cuts the other way, and honestly this is where I loose the thread: a representation is supposed to be used when it's relevant, so a feature that only matters near walls is behaving exactly the way a real, task-appropriate representation should, and demanding global causal leverage is demanding that the model deploy information it has no reason to deploy. He then asked M what experiment would convince her the model represents the wall at all, and she said she didn't think any decode-plus-patch result could, in principle, which he said was an admission that her side is unfalsifiable — and she said his is unfalsifiable in the opposite direction, since he's already decided in advance what any result would have to mean.

So can you go through this move by move? For each thing M and R actually said, tell me whether it's right, wrong, or right only with qualifications, and then say who comes out ahead overall. I'm especially curious whether R's convergence argument and M's locality argument really do point in opposite directions like they claimed, since those two feel like they should be settleable independently of the big question. And finally tell me what neither of them brought up — is there a third position that dissolves the fight, or are they just using "representation" to mean two things the other wouldn't recognize?
