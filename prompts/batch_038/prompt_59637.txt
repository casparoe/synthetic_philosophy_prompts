I'm prepping a two-page handout for a grad seminar session on rationality over time, and the anchor case is Parfit's hitchhiker: you're stranded in the desert, a driver will take you to town only if he's confident you'd hand over a decent chunk of money once you get there, and once you get there paying is pointless because he'll never find out. I want a decision tree that sorts my students into named positions about what's rational here, but I specifically don't want the usual single-case tree, because with only the standard version on the table the whole debate collapses into two camps and people end up wherever they were leaning before they walked in.

So please build the tree out of a family of variants, and let every node be a question about one of them. The variants I want in play:

- V1, the standard case, with the driver near-perfectly accurate at reading whether you'd pay.
- V2, same but the driver is only about 80 percent right.
- V3, no mind-reading at all, but a third party will hold a bond that makes paying genuinely your best option later, at a price.
- V4, the sum is small enough that paying still looks fine on the later calculus, once reputation and self-respect are counted in.
- V5, you'll be stranded on this road again and the drivers compare notes.
- V6, the toxin-style twist: the payoff is delivered just for having the intention tonight, before any ride is on offer, and nobody ever checks whether you follow through.
- V7, you can't pick your dispositions directly, but you can take steps that will change them over the next few months.

Six to eight nodes, each phrased as something a student can answer honestly without knowing which label it leads to — no node should read like a definition of a view. The axes I care about: whether rationality gets assessed at the level of the act, the intention, the disposition, or the whole decision procedure; whether you can rationally intend something you already know you'll have no reason to do when the time comes; whether the cost of keeping the promise is already sunk and priced in by the time you reach town; whether transparency matters or mere reliability is enough; and whether you can choose to become a different kind of agent only by taking detours that cause it.

For leaves I need at least: the plain act-by-act causal answer, where you die in the desert and that's just what rationality costs; resolute choice; sophisticated choice plus self-binding devices; the planning-agency answer that leans on non-reconsideration rather than resolve; constrained maximization; Parfit's own move, that it can be rational to cause yourself to be irrational; the self-effacing or indirect consequentialist version of that; the evidential answer where paying is at least good news; the newer updateless/functional-decision-theory style answer; and a leaf for people who think the case is under-described rather than hard. Nine to eleven leaves total, each carrying the name of the position plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and the name of whoever's most associated with it if that's obvious — but no reading list, I know the literature and the handout has no room.

Two extra things. First, tell me for each pair of neighbouring leaves which variant actually separates them and which ones don't, and flag any pair the tree genuinely can't tell apart — I'd rather see that admitted than have two leaves that secretly mean the same thing. Second, after the tree, sketch the combinations of node-answers that no one occupies, and say for each whether it's empty because the view looks incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered. My hunch is there's an unoccupied cell involving V2 and V7 that's actually quite live, and I'd like to know if that's right or if I've misread the space.
