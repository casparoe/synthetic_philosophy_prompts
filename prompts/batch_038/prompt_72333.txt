I am drafting a chapter for an edited collection on what the empirical study of cultural variation ought to mean for political philosophy, and I would like a self-contained essay that settles a question I have been circling without progress. No reference list, bibliography, or citation apparatus is wanted; I will add those myself. What I need is the argument worked out.

Background, stated as neutrally as I can. The tightness–looseness research program in cultural psychology treats societies as differing in how strongly they enforce social norms and how much deviance they tolerate. Its broadest generalizations are these: groups under sustained threat — scarcity, disaster, epidemic, chronic conflict, population pressure — tend to tighten; tightness purchases coordination, order, and low defection at the price of innovation and openness to outsiders; looseness does the reverse. The normative discussion that has grown up around these findings is almost always posed as a single question: should a society be tight or loose, and how much rule-following should it demand of its members?

My own proposal, which the essay should test rather than assume, is that the question is malformed, because tightness bundles at least three independently variable features. First, determinacy: how precisely the content of a norm is fixed, so that people can tell what compliance requires. Second, breadth of enforcement: how many domains of conduct are open to scrutiny and sanction by third parties who are not themselves wronged by the deviation. Third, severity and graduatedness of sanction: how costly deviation is, and whether cost tracks the magnitude of the deviation. A society can be crisp about few norms and vague about many; permissive yet intensely monitored; or strictly monitored but only by those directly affected. I also suspect that much of what presents as looseness in a liberal society is not weak norms but restricted standing: the norms are held firmly, yet only a narrow class of people is permitted to police any given one.

On that framework, write an essay of roughly 1,800 words that reaches and defends a determinate verdict on the following question. Is there an all-things-considered reason for a political community to aim at looseness — and if so, looseness along which of the three dimensions, in which domains of conduct, and at whose initiative?

The essay must do at least the following. State the strongest available case for tightness, including its character as a collective self-binding device and the fact that its benefits are diffuse while its costs fall on identifiable individuals; do not caricature it as mere authoritarianism. State the strongest case for looseness, including error-correction over time, the position of the non-conformist, the moral cost of surveillance and of conferring standing on uninvolved enforcers, and the way tight norms entrench whatever happened to be normal. Confront the agency objection: whether a society can decide to be tight or loose at all, given that norms mostly change from below, and whether the normative question therefore dissolves into questions about what individual citizens owe one another when they witness deviation. Distinguish tightness achieved through law from tightness achieved through informal sanction, and say whether the verdict differs for each lever. Distinguish the moral stringency of a requirement from the social strength of its enforcement, and say what follows when the norms being enforced are weighty but contested rather than arbitrary conventions. Finally, apply the analysis to one concrete setting — a public health emergency, the conduct rules of a very large employer, or a large online platform — and report honestly whether the general verdict survives contact with it.

Do not end on a both-sides note, and do not retreat to the claim that it depends on the society without saying which feature of a society decides the matter. A structured verdict is entirely welcome: determinacy high, breadth low, severity graduated, and so on. Close by identifying what would have to be true for that verdict to reverse.
