The talk is forty minutes with a twenty-five-minute discussion period, in a joint philosophy and cognitive science colloquium series, and what I want from you is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense of the view. Nobody in the room is a Davidson specialist, which is part of my problem.

The thesis, briefly. I argue that Davidson's principle of charity is not a defeasible heuristic — something an interpreter adopts because it tends to work and abandons where the evidence points elsewhere — but is constitutive of there being an interpretation at all. A scheme that attributed mostly false beliefs to a speaker would not be a rival, uncharitable reading of that speaker; it would fail to identify a speaker, because belief is individuated only against a largely true and largely coherent background. The refinement I take to be the substantive part of the paper is this: the constraint should not be stated as "maximize the number of true beliefs attributed," but as "maximize the intelligibility of the pattern of agreement and disagreement," so that charity fixes the shape of the space of attributed attitudes rather than pushing each attribution toward truth. Two corollaries I intend to press. First, massive systematic error is not an empirical discovery that radical interpretation could in principle make about a community. Second, radical interpretation is not a model of any process a real interpreter performs, so the fact that actual comprehension is fast, incremental, and driven by prediction error does not bear on the constitutive claim.

The room, as precisely as I can characterize it. About forty-five people.

Two are metaphysicians working in the Lewisian tradition. They hold that fit to behavioral evidence plus charitable fit leaves reference badly underdetermined, and that an eligibility or naturalness constraint on properties is needed to break the ties; one of them thinks a truth-conditional account pitched at whole sentences cannot deliver compositional structure or determinate lexical content. They are polite and they will not let go once they start.

Roughly half the room are computational psycholinguists and cognitive scientists who work on pragmatic inference: rational speech act style models, Bayesian word learning, recursive belief modeling. They are friendly but impatient with anything they would describe as unfalsifiable in principle, and several of them already treat a charity-like bias as a prior they have operationalized and measured. They will want to know what my account rules out that a rival account does not, and what my refinement predicts.

One field linguist does endangered-language documentation and has run elicitation with speakers whose conceptual inventory she had to build from scratch. She knows the mid-century debate over whether apparently exotic belief systems — the canonical case being a divinatory oracle whose verdicts are structured so that they can never conflict — are evidence of irrationality or of a different evidential setup. She will press on whether "maximize the intelligibility of disagreement" is a license for declaring the unfamiliar unintelligible, and whether it is simply ethnocentrism with better manners.

Two or three people work on machine translation and large language models and will not resist asking whether a system that maps utterances to appropriate behavior while attributing no beliefs at all is a counterexample to my constitutive claim.

The rest are graduate students with no stake, who will ask for clarifications I should not mistake for objections.

What I want back:

The seven to nine objections I should actually expect from this room, ordered by expected damage — likelihood weighted by how much of the thesis each would take down — rather than by the order in which they will arrive. Phrase each one in the register of the person raising it: the metaphysician's version should sound like a metaphysician, the psycholinguist's like someone who wants an operationalization, not like a textbook gloss of either.

For each, give me what to concede in the first sentence, since I would rather concede fast than defend badly; a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; and a blunt note on where that reply is soft, meaning which step would not survive a determined second follow-up.

Mark which of these are at bottom the same objection in different vocabularies, so that I can answer it once and reuse the answer.

Tell me which stock objections this particular room will not raise — the ones a general philosophy of language seminar reliably produces — and why not. I want to know what I do not need to rehearse.

Name the question I am hoping nobody asks: the one that goes directly at the joint where I have least to say. Give me a fallback answer anyway, and tell me honestly whether the fallback is true or merely survivable.

Finally, list the two or three concessions I should make explicitly during the talk, before discussion opens, in order to defuse the most damaging lines — including your judgment on whether my refinement about the intelligibility of disagreement is a real departure from Davidson or a redescription of him in nicer words. I would rather hear the unflattering answer now than in the room.

No citations, bibliography, or reading list; I need something I can read through twice in the forty-eight hours before the talk.
