I've been reading the acausal-trade and multiverse threads since they were new, and I have finally met a reductio I cannot answer — from a correspondent who grants the whole setup and then turns it around.

Setup, conceded for the sake of argument. The world is large: N agents, N astronomically beyond my causal reach, each sealed in a causally isolated region, each running one of finitely many decision procedures from a library L. L partitions into similarity classes; agents in the same class facing the same problem choose alike with probability one, and agents in different classes choose independently. Every agent plays a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with every other agent (3/3, 1/1, 5/0), and each agent's utility is the average of its payoffs across all of its games. The population is well mixed and its composition is common knowledge: a known fraction sits in each class. L contains ALWAYS-C, ALWAYS-D, and a conditional cooperator that cooperates with agents it predicts are running a procedure sufficiently similar to its own and defects on the rest.

The argument.
(1) Rational choice takes the act that is best news about the utilities you care about; here your act is news about the acts of the many agents in your similarity class, and your utility is set by those acts.
(2) Which class you are in is settled by which procedure you run, so your average utility is a function of your procedure and the population mix, and nothing an agent decides inside a decision changes any of that.
(3) So from inside any procedure, every act on the menu is equally good news about your class-mates' behaviour, that behaviour being already fixed by the procedure you are running.
(4) And a principle that cannot rank acts cannot rank procedures either, since every procedure is self-confirming in exactly the same way, and self-confirmation is all (1) and (2) ever delivered.
(5) With evidential reasoning thus idle, the procedure has to be chosen on other grounds, and on those grounds ALWAYS-D wins: given anyone else's act, defecting is never worse for you than cooperating, and choosing a procedure cannot change anyone else's procedure, hence cannot change what they do to you.
(6) Therefore large-world evidential cooperation, argued through honestly, recommends becoming a causal decision theorist — and a theory whose own premises point at its rival is refuted by them.

Two things make this more than a curiosity. First, none of the steps mention the multiverse, the size of N, or acausality: the same six lines run verbatim on a single perfect copy of you in a locked room, on Hofstadter-style superrationality, and on "act as you would want every agent like you to act," so if your diagnosis needs the world to be big, say so plainly, because I don't think it does. Second, I cannot simply reject the stipulation: if the correlation in (2) is incoherent or trivial, I have been reading the wrong threads for fifteen years. Given that, I would rather lose (1) than (2), though I would like to keep both.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no preamble and no closing summary: the single step you would cut; why it looked innocent to someone as steeped in this material as my correspondent; what cutting it costs, stated as a specific claim the program can no longer make; the runner-up step and the price of cutting it instead; and what, if anything, the argument still gets to conclude once your cut is in place.
