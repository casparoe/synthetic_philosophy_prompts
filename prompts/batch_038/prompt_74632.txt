I do localization for a mid-size B2B software company, so I spend most of my week post-editing machine output into German and Japanese and occasionally arguing about it. For something like two years now I've been having the same argument, in slightly different costumes, with our ML lead (who I genuinely like and who is much better at the technical side than I am). He thinks the Hofstadter-style complaint — that translation systems don't actually understand anything, they're just shuffling surface patterns — is a moving goalpost dressed up as philosophy: every specific failure the critics point at gets patched by the next generation of models, the critics immediately relocate to a harder example, and so the claim is unfalsifiable in practice and useless as a research programme. My side of it is that the failure pattern doesn't look accidental to me at all, and that "we keep finding new bugs" is not the same thing as "the bugs are converging to zero." Worth saying that this dispute started life about statistical phrase-based systems and has been replayed almost verbatim with neural and now LLM-based translation, which I find suspicious — the critics say nothing structural changed, the engineers say the outputs obviously got much better, and both think they're talking about the same object.

The three flashpoints we keep circling, concretely: (a) coreference across a long document, where the referent is only recoverable from world knowledge and not from the sentence, and the system picks a grammatically fine, factually wrong reading with total confidence; (b) register and audience — one source sentence has several adequate targets depending on who is talking to whom and in what medium, and the system reliably lands on the statistically central one, which in a manual for field technicians is often just wrong in a way no aggregate score ever registers; (c) the system translates nonsense fluently — give it something grammatical but impossible and you get a fluent impossible rendering back, never a hesitation. On his side: aggregate human-eval and post-editing-effort numbers that have genuinely moved, plus the point that the critics never say in advance what a system would have to do differently for them to concede. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as something I've argued for: the impasse isn't about machines or minds at all, it's a mismatch of unit of analysis. He scores a distribution over a corpus; the Hofstadter-types score individual adversarial tokens; each treats the other's test set as unrepresentative by construction, so they can't share a data point even in principle. On that reading "understands" is doing indexical work — for one side it means something like "would survive a hostile, out-of-distribution, high-stakes reading," for the other "would produce output a rater prefers on average" — and there's no fact about which threshold the word picks out. But I suspect that's only part of the story, because there does seem to be a live empirical question buried underneath (is there some scale or architecture point past which those pragmatic failure classes stop recurring?), and if that question ever got a clean yes, the critic would have to either concede or admit the objection was never empirical to begin with.

Please treat this as a diagnostic exercise rather than a verdict, and take on the role of someone who has done real work in both philosophy of mind/language and machine translation evaluation — someone who has actually sat through the argument from both chairs. Start by brainstorming the space of possible diagnoses before you commit to one: purely verbal dispute about "understand," two sides answering different questions under a single heading, disagreement about what counts as data or about standards of evidence, a hidden empirical or formal question doing all the work, an ill-posed question (asking whether a translation system understands might be like asking whether a submarine swims), or a genuinely substantive metaphysical disagreement that is simply hard and not stuck. Then argue for one, or for a mixture, and tell me roughly how much weight you'd put on each component. Given whatever you land on, say what would count as progress: a distinction, an experiment design, a formal result, a change of question, a shift in which cases are admitted as evidence — or make the case that no progress should be expected and explain why the structure of the disagreement guarantees it. And give me a straight answer on whether my colleague's unfalsifiability charge actually sticks, or whether it only looks like it does because he's assuming his own scoring rule is the neutral one. Think carefully and show your reasoning before the conclusion. No bibliography and no reference list, I'll add those myself — but engage with what each side actually says rather than surveying the history of the debate, and don't hedge into "both sides have a point" without telling me which points are doing the work.
