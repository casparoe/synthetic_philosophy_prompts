I am in the fourth year of my third novel, and I have run into a structural problem that feels less literary than philosophical, though I cannot tell where one stops and the other begins. The book is set in the 1870s and follows two people who arrive at the same result without ever meeting: a woman who keeps the copy books for a provincial patent agent and works out, mostly at night, how speech might be carried along a wire, and a well-connected professor in the capital who gets there at roughly the same moment with better instruments and worse arithmetic. The engine of the plot is the priority dispute that follows, the letters, the lawyers, the name that ends up on the thing. Last spring I audited a history-of-science seminar to get the texture right and found myself reading instead about the two filings that reached the patent office in Washington on the same February day in 1876, about the joint reading of Darwin's and Wallace's papers at the Linnean Society in 1858, and about Robert Merton's distinction between singletons and multiples in science. My agent thinks the middle of the book sags. I think the middle of the book rests on an assumption I am no longer sure I believe.

Here is the assumption. The drama of a priority dispute presumes that the winner did something that mattered, that but for her the discovery would not have existed when it did. Everything I have been reading suggests that this counterfactual is usually false, that the idea was already loose in the world and would have been caught by someone within the year. But I keep finding that the shape of this problem is not historical so much as a familiar puzzle about preempted causation: a backup assassin waits in the bushes, and the first one shoots, and it is true that the victim would have died anyway, and yet we do not want to say the first shot made no difference to what actually happened. My second discoverer is precisely that backup, and the question I cannot settle is which picture of credit the novel should endorse. If desert tracks difference-making, then nobody in my book deserves anything, because the result was coming regardless, and the whole quarrel collapses into a fact about who reached a clerk's desk first. If desert tracks having actually brought the thing about, then my professor, who did the work and published it and made it real for everyone else, is the one who deserves the name, and my heroine's private notebooks count for nothing even though she was, so far as anyone can tell, weeks ahead. There is also the reverse embarrassment, which is that on the second picture a person who plainly had it in her to make the discovery but never did gets no credit at all, which seems to make desert depend on luck in a way my characters would find intolerable.

So what I would like is an example of a conceptual issue that arises exactly at this juncture, developed out of a documented historical case of near-simultaneous discovery rather than out of a thought experiment, though I will take a constructed case if the real ones refuse to cooperate. Please describe the case concretely enough that I can see what turned on what: who knew what, when, who wrote to whom, what the record actually supports about the timing. Then state the conceptual issue sharply, as something I could hand to a philosopher and have her recognize it, rather than as a mood: what the case shows about whether individual credit survives the discovery that the discovery was inevitable. If you have a view on it, tell me which of my two pictures a novelist ought to build into her characters, since the choice will decide which of them the reader ends up sympathizing with by the last chapter, but I would rather have one example worked through carefully than a survey of the field. Please write your reply as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
