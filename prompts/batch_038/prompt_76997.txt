I'm running a two-day workshop where roughly half the room are philosophy grad students and half are people building collaborative robots, and it turns out nobody agrees on what counts as acting together. I want a sorting instrument: a decision tree people can walk down and then discover which account of joint action they've been silently presupposing. It should be self-scoreable, and the point is to sort them by what they say about cases, not by what they call themselves.

Things I care about:

- Every branch node is a question about a concrete case, with two or three answer options, each option pointing at the next node or at a leaf. Nothing like "do you think there are irreducible we-intentions?" — the nodes have to be answerable by someone who has never read any of this literature.
- Cap it at ten nodes. I'd rather have seven or eight well-separated leaves than fifteen mushy ones.
- Leaves get the usual names where names exist: Bratman's planning/shared-intention account, Searle's primitive we-intention, Gilbert's joint commitment and plural subject stuff, Tuomela's we-mode versus I-mode, List and Pettit on group agents, a minimal or deflationary leaf for the thin accounts that don't need shared intentions at all, and an eliminativist leaf if you think the space genuinely allows one.
- Build it out of the logical space first rather than out of a list of names: give me the branching questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then place the positions in that space, then tell me which combinations are empty and whether each is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's got round to it. I want at least three empty cells diagnosed.
- Say plainly which pairs of leaves your tree can't actually distinguish, and why not.
- If somebody's answers route them into an empty cell or contradict each other, the key should say which leaf to nudge them towards and what follow-up question settles it.

Four of the nodes have to be these, since I think they're the ones doing the real work: whether the we-attitude reduces to interlocking individual attitudes or is primitive; whether joint action needs common knowledge or mutual belief, or whether something weaker will do; whether entering into it generates obligations and standing to complain when the other party bails; and whether the agent here is the group or the individuals. Two more I made up for the robotics half of the room, so work them in wherever they fit: whether a participant can be flat-out wrong about whether they are acting jointly with someone (they think we're lifting the sofa together, I think I'm just lifting a sofa next to a stranger), and whether one party can dissolve the joint action unilaterally or whether dissolving it is itself a joint act.

Route these six cases through the tree and show me where each one sends a typical answerer: (a) two strangers who wordlessly heave a fallen cyclist and his bike upright and then walk off in different directions; (b) a neighbourhood WhatsApp thread that ends up sending a letter to the council which nobody drafted on their own; (c) a couple who say "we decided to move cities" when only one of them ever put it into words and the other says she just went along with it; (d) a co-authored paper where one of the two authors only did the literature search and the introduction; (e) a jury returning a unanimous verdict, one of whom says afterwards in private that he only caved; (f) a person playing catch with a robot arm that predicts and adjusts mid-throw. Then, for each leaf, tell me which of the six it handles worst.

Start straight in with question one — no preface, no restating the brief, no summary at the end of what you did. British English, please, and if you think any of my six branching claims is a bad question or is really two questions welded together, say so where it comes up rather than quietly dropping it.
