I'm an ML engineer putting together a handout for an internal reading group. I have no philosophy training, so please treat anything beyond the mathematics I spell out below as fair game to introduce, but don't assume I already have the vocabulary. Nearly everyone in the group has used the no-free-lunch results in some form — averaged across all possible objective functions, no search or learning algorithm does better than random guessing — and I keep seeing the claim that this is a formal version of Hume's problem of induction. I want an instrument that sorts my colleagues by which position on that claim they actually hold, rather than by which slogan they repeat back.

Please build a decision tree. Entry condition: the reader accepts the theorem as proved. Exit: a named position on what, if anything, no-free-lunch says about whether inductive inference is warranted. Five to seven branch nodes, six to nine leaves.

Every node must be phrased as a question an engineer can answer from the mathematics alone. No node may mention Hume, skepticism, or any position label. These are the splits I want the tree to turn on, though you decide the order and may add or drop one:

1. Whether the collection of all possible objective functions is a genuine space of possibilities with a canonical uniform measure over it, or a bookkeeping device whose size depends on how finely we slice the search space.
2. The sharpened result that average performance comes out equal across algorithms exactly when the set of target functions is closed under permutations of the search space and each function in the set is equally likely, together with the observation that practically relevant problem classes are unlikely to be closed under permutation: does a world like ours fall inside the theorem's scope at all?
3. Whether the conclusion is about expected performance across problems or about warrant for a particular prediction — can a claim about performance averages contradict a claim about justification?
4. Whether the object being averaged over includes the prior or only the hypothesis-selection rule run on a fixed prior, i.e. where the inductive content is being loaded in.
5. Whether meta-induction — averaging over predictors and weighting them by past success — escapes the theorem, or is just another entry in the set being averaged over.

At minimum the leaves should cover: no-free-lunch as a proof of inductive skepticism; as a mathematical triviality with no epistemic content; as resting on an arbitrary way of counting possibilities; as answering a question nobody was asking about justification; as leaving rationality relative to the choice of prior; and as compatible with a meta-inductive vindication.

For each leaf, give one sentence on what it commits the holder to say about a concrete case — a vision model that generalizes correctly to held-out examples of a category it never saw in training. Then list the node-answer combinations your tree can reach but that correspond to no position anyone actually defends, and diagnose each as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Finally flag any two leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say what further question would split them. No bibliography, citations, or reading list — I want the instrument, not the literature.
