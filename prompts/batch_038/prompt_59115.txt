My editor wants the piece to have a real historical spine rather than a string of contemporary analogies, so I have gone back to the founding text of the genre: Book VI of Polybius' Histories, and specifically the opening of his discussion of the Roman constitution.

Below is an excerpt in the Loeb Classical Library translation (vol. III, 1923), with the standard section numbering. I have deliberately stopped mid-sentence at 4.9; the chapters that follow, which I have not reproduced, are where I expect the causal machinery to be spelled out.

2.8 What chiefly attracts and chiefly benefits students of history is just this — the study of causes and the consequent power of choosing what is best in each case. 2.9 Now the chief cause of success or the reverse in all matters is the form of a state's constitution; 2.10 for springing from this, as from a fountain-head, all designs and plans of action not only originate, but reach their consummation.

3.1 In the case of those Greek states which have often risen to greatness and have often experienced a complete change of fortune, it is an easy matter both to describe their past and to pronounce as to their future. 3.2 For there is no difficulty in reporting the known facts, and it is not hard to foretell the future by inference from the past. 3.3 But about the Roman state it is neither at all easy to explain the present situation owing to the complicated character of the constitution, nor to foretell the future owing to our ignorance of the peculiar features of public and private life at Rome in the past.

3.5 Most of those whose object it has been to instruct us methodically concerning such matters, distinguish three kinds of constitutions, which they call kingship, aristocracy, and democracy. 3.6 Now we should, I think, be quite justified in asking them to enlighten us as to whether they represent these three to be the sole varieties or rather to be the best; 3.7 for in either case my opinion is that they are wrong. For it is evident that we must regard as the best constitution a combination of all these three varieties, since we have had proof of this not only theoretically but by actual experience, Lycurgus having been the first to draw up a constitution — that of Sparta — on this principle. 3.9 Nor on the other hand can we admit that these are the only three varieties; for we have witnessed monarchical and tyrannical governments, which while they differ very widely from kingship, yet bear a certain resemblance to it, 3.10 this being the reason why monarchs in general falsely assume and use, as far as they can, the regal title. 3.11 There have also been several oligarchical constitutions which seem to bear some likeness to aristocratic ones, though the divergence is, generally, as wide as possible. 3.12 The same holds good about democracies.

4.1 The truth of what I say is evident from the following considerations. 4.2 It is by no means every monarchy which we can call straight off a kingship, but only that which is voluntarily accepted by the subjects and where they are governed rather by an appeal to their reason than by fear and force. 4.3 Nor again can we style every oligarchy an aristocracy, but only that where the government is in the hands of a selected body of the justest and wisest men. 4.4 Similarly that is no true democracy in which the whole crowd of citizens is free to do whatever they wish or purpose, 4.5 but when, in a community where it is traditional and customary to reverence the gods, to honour our parents, to respect our elders, and to obey the laws, the will of the greater number prevails, this is to be called a democracy. 4.6 We should therefore assert that there are six kinds of governments, the three above mentioned which are in everyone's mouth and the three which are naturally allied to them, I mean monarchy, oligarchy, and mob-rule. 4.7 Now the first of these to come into being is monarchy, its growth being natural and unaided; and next arises kingship derived from monarchy by the aid of art and by the correction of defects. 4.8 Monarchy first changes into its vicious allied form, tyranny; and next, the abolishment of both gives birth to aristocracy. 4.9 Aristocracy by its very nature degenerates into oligarchy; […]

I have a working hypothesis about this passage that I would like you to test rather than flatter, because if it survives I will build a section of the article on it.

My hypothesis is that the definitions in 4.2–4.5 are doing far more work than they appear to. Polybius does not define kingship, aristocracy, and democracy structurally — by who holds office — but morally: consent plus rule by reason rather than fear and force; rule by the justest and wisest; a community marked by reverence for the gods, honour to parents, respect for elders, and obedience to the laws. On that reading, a regime that fails these tests is not a corrupted instance of the good form; it ceases to be that form at all and becomes something else that merely resembles it, which is exactly what 3.9–3.12 says about the "likeness" of monarchy to kingship and oligarchy to aristocracy. If that is right, then the sequence in 4.7–4.9 is not a causal transformation of a single regime through stages of decay but a series of replacements among types that are "naturally allied" to one another — a taxonomy with transition rules, not an organism with a life cycle. And the uncomfortable consequence is that Polybius' true democracy, containing obedience to law and piety by definition, cannot decay internally at all: any decay would be a reclassification, not a corruption. That would make the famous cycle partly an artifact of how the terms were defined.
