My mother died in April. Since May I have been attending, on Wednesday evenings, a reading group on Madhyamaka at the centre she used to go to; I came for the company and stayed for the arguments. Last week the facilitator wrote out the following on the board as a reductio of the position we had been reading, and I have not been able to think about anything else since. I have tried to reconstruct it as carefully as I can, adding premises he left implicit. I would be grateful for a diagnosis rather than a survey: I have read enough introductions to know what the two truths are supposed to be, and what I lack is an account of where this goes wrong, if it does.

(1) Nothing has intrinsic nature: for any thing, what it is, is not had in its own right but is borrowed from its causes, its conditions, its parts, and the designations laid over it.

(2) Whatever mark distinguishes a thing from other things — the feature in virtue of which it is this and not that — either belongs to it in its own right or is borrowed from its relations.

(3) If a mark is borrowed from the relations, it is not the thing's own mark; and a mark that is not the thing's own cannot serve to pick the thing out.

(4) By (1), no mark belongs to anything in its own right.

(5) So nothing has a distinguishing mark.

(6) Things with no distinguishing marks cannot be told apart from one another; and what cannot be told apart from anything is not a distinct thing.

(7) So there are no distinct things — no effects distinct from their conditions, no wholes distinct from their parts, no persons distinct from the aggregates.

(8) But (1) is meant as a claim about things: emptiness is the emptiness of something, and dependent arising is the arising of relata which must, for the relation to obtain, stand on both sides of it.

(9) So if (1) is true, (7) is true, and (7) removes the subject matter of (1). Emptiness would be a property with no bearers, and the conventional truth would be a truth about nothing — which is not a second truth but the absence of one.

(10) Therefore either the conventional world is genuinely populated, in which case (1) is false, or it is not, in which case the account of how things can arise, cease, and function — the account emptiness was offered to make possible — fails with it. There is no third position.

What made (3) feel undeniable to me in the room was an example he gave. Two fields adjoin, and between them runs a boundary. The boundary has no features of its own; it is entirely a function of the fields and of a surveyor's decision, and indeed, he said, we all agree that there is no line in the ground. Then he asked us to notice that the same holds of the fields: each is what it is only as the other's neighbour, so their distinguishing marks are borrowed in exactly the manner (3) describes. I found this compelling at the time and have found it alarming since, because I cannot see where it is meant to stop. On the same reasoning, my mother is distinguishable from me only by marks borrowed all the way down, and I want to say that this is false, but I cannot see how to say it without simply asserting the denial of (1).

Two things followed that I also want you to weigh. One member objected that the same steps would show there are no causes either, which nobody in the group wants, since even the opponents of the first chapter of the root verses take causes to be real. The facilitator said that this is not a reductio but the argument working as intended, and that this is precisely what the first chapter concludes. I cannot tell which of them is right, and it matters, because if he is right I do not know what separates (7) from a conclusion Nāgārjuna himself would sign. He added that to escape, Nāgārjuna must fall back on the fourth corner of the tetralemma and hold that things neither exist nor do not exist, which is a way of declining to say what one means; and that if the argument goes through, the difference between the middle way and outright nihilism is one of tone rather than content. He then said, to me and quite kindly, that this is why grief is a philosophical error about persons rather than a fact about the world. I remember enough to think the fourth corner is refused along with the other three, but I could not have said why refusing all four is anything other than an evasion.

So, if you would be so kind: which step or steps do you reject? For each, say why it looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down, and price the rejection — in particular, say whether rejecting it leaves any account of how a thing can be distinguished from what it depends on, and whether that account is one the objecting member could accept or one only a Mādhyamika could live with. Then take up the exchange about causes: is the fact that the same steps yield the denial of causation evidence that the argument overshoots, or is it the argument arriving where it was heading, and if the latter, what distinguishes that destination from (7)? Finally, if the argument can be repaired so as to be valid with a conclusion Nāgārjuna would accept, state the repaired conclusion and tell me whether it still supports the closing remark about grief. If I had to choose, I would rather give up (1) than (8): (8) seems to me the least that a doctrine about the world must retain in order to be about the world at all. If you think (8) is the step that has to go, I want the reason rather than the reassurance.
