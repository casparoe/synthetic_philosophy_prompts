I recently finished a chapter in a well-reviewed popular book on rational thinking, written by a cognitive scientist who writes for general audiences, and one passage in it has stayed with me. It goes something like this:

"Ramsey had the idea in the 1920s, and de Finetti turned it into a proof in the 1930s: if your degrees of belief are not probabilities, then a bookie who knows them can offer you a package of bets, each of which looks perfectly reasonable to you considered on its own, that guarantees you a net loss whatever happens. If your degrees of belief do obey the axioms of probability, no such package exists at all — you are simply immune. Incoherent people can be caught out this way because they hold contradictory beliefs. Savage then reversed the direction in the 1950s, deriving both a probability function and a utility function out of nothing but a person's choices, provided those choices satisfy a few structural conditions. And Joyce showed in the 1990s that you do not even need money for the argument to go through: an incoherent set of beliefs is dominated in accuracy, meaning that some coherent set is closer to the truth than it in every possible situation, so incoherence is a way of being wrong no matter how the world turns out. The upshot is that coherence and rationality do not come apart. Because these are theorems rather than opinions, there is nothing left to argue about: an agent is rational exactly to the extent that its beliefs are probabilities and its choices maximize expected utility, and anyone who says otherwise is not disagreeing with a philosophy but denying mathematics."

I find the first half persuasive and the last two sentences overbearing, but I am not equipped to say where the line falls. Two things in particular bother me and I cannot tell whether they are genuine problems or just my own confusion. First, my own degrees of belief are surely not probabilities in any strict sense, yet nobody has ever sold me a package of bets I regretted — does the result really have anything to say about me? Second, I cannot tell whether the accuracy version is doing something genuinely different from the betting version, or is the same worry about money dressed up in epistemic language.

Please give me an accuracy audit of this passage, working claim by claim. For each substantive claim, say whether someone who works on coherence arguments would endorse it as stated, accept it as a tolerable simplification of something true, or regard it as a misrepresentation — and in the last case, give me the accurate statement in its place. I would especially like you to address the following points: whether violating the probability axioms really amounts to holding contradictory beliefs, and what incoherence actually looks like in a concrete case; what the betting argument has to assume beyond the agent's degrees of belief in order to generate a guaranteed loss, and whether that loss is a loss of money or a loss of utility, and why that matters; whether the accuracy argument shares the premises and the conclusion of the betting argument or differs in some important respect, including whether the dominating alternative is always one an agent could actually adopt; what Savage's theorem licenses and what it conspicuously does not; and how much of the normative force in the passage is carried by the mathematics as against premises that could themselves be contested. Then judge the conclusion on its own terms: once every error you have identified is repaired, does the claim that an agent is rational exactly to the extent that it is coherent survive, or must it be weakened — and if weakened, to what?
