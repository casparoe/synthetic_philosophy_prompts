I am in the fourth year of a PhD in history. My dissertation concerns the administration of records in a nineteenth-century colonial bureaucracy, so it is less a history of events than a history of what was written down, by whom, on whose instruction, and what fell between the registers. This autumn I am convening a small methods seminar for first-year graduate students, and for the opening session I want to screen Citizen Kane — not as an illustration of the theory, but as a provocation: a film whose entire plot is a research project, complete with a funding body, an archivist, a set of oral-history informants, and a failed finding.

What I would like from you is a real piece of criticism that reads the film through the literature on archives and power, and that is faithful in both directions: it should not reduce the film to a diagram, and it should not bend the theory to fit.

Set the film first against Trouillot's account of silencing, which holds that silence enters the production of history at four separable points: the making of sources, the making of archives, the making of narratives, and the making of retrospective significance. Map the film onto those moments with some care. The newsreel is plainly the first; Thompson's rounds — the Thatcher archive, Bernstein, Leland, Susan's initial refusal to see him, the butler's account at Xanadu — belong to the second and third. But I wonder whether the film's real subject is the fourth, the retroactive conferral of significance, which it assigns to the audience rather than to any historian. And what is Rosebud in this scheme: a source that exists and cannot be retrieved, or one that was never a source at all? We are shown the sled in the furnace; Thompson is not. Does that asymmetry support the silencing thesis, or does the film cheat it by handing us exactly what the archive withholds from its own investigator?

Second, Derrida: the archive as the house of the archons, where commencement and commandment coincide, and where consignation — the gathering of signs into a coordinated corpus — is the operation that makes an archive rather than a heap. Xanadu is full of unopened crates and galleries no one enters. Is that an image of archival failure or of archival power, and does Kane function as archon over his own record, authorising what may be said about him even after his death? Third, and as a corrective to that, Steedman's insistence that the archive is a material place with a door, a cloakroom, dust, and a body inside it, together with her complaint that the Derridean archive is a metaphor bought at the price of never having been in one. Which account does the Thatcher library sequence vindicate? A librarian tells Thompson which portions of a dead man's papers he may read and which he may not; that seems to me to be Steedman's archive rather than Derrida's, but I want to know whether you agree, and whether the scene is doing epistemological work or merely supplying a plot device.

Fourth, and this is where I most want to be argued with. Bloch distinguished deliberate testimony from the involuntary witness, the source that was never intended as one and therefore says more. Bernstein's memory of a girl glimpsed from a ferry decades earlier, which he claims to have recalled once a month ever since, is precisely that: the only record in the film of something no document recorded. Does the film therefore privilege memory and testimony over documents, and is that a critique of the archive or a sentimental substitute for one? Then the harder objection, which a student will certainly raise: Trouillot's framework was built for the silenced — the enslaved, the colonised, those who enter the record only through the accounts of their masters. Kane's silences are the silences of very powerful men. The people genuinely absent from his file (Susan at the moment she refuses to speak, the child in the Colorado boarding house, the chorus girls and the household staff) are excluded for reasons that have little to do with state or imperial power. Does applying the framework here stretch it past its use, or does it reveal something about the blind spots in the record of the powerful as well?

Finally, tell me what the film does that the theory cannot. My own suspicion is that it has to do with the experience of being the person who cannot be retrieved, and with the awkward fact that a narrative about retrieval failure is itself a successful narrative: Thompson's defeat is our entertainment. If you think the enterprise is a category error — that a fiction film cannot bear evidential weight in a debate about archives, and that I should screen something else — say so and say why. I would much rather hear that now than in the seminar.

Three practical constraints. Please do not give me a bibliography or a list of further reading; I know the literature and I have no use for citations I have not checked myself. Write as a critic, not a summariser, and assume I have seen the film many times over. And because you cannot consult the film or the texts, if you are unsure of the content of a particular scene or of the exact form of an argument, flag the uncertainty and reason from what you are confident of rather than filling the gap by invention. I intend to circulate what you write as a short reading of a few pages, so please pace and structure it accordingly.
