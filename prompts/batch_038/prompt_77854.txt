I am marking the final papers for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics of war and would like a second reader's assessment of one of them. I have not formed a judgment of my own about this submission, so please treat it on its merits. Award it a grade out of one hundred, then write the comments I would return with it, working through the paper in the order it is written: say what is accurate, what is not, and where a claim is accurate but the inference drawn from it is not. Indicate which points weigh most heavily on the grade, and close by telling the student what to revise. Please write in continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables — as a grader's letter reads rather than a filled-in rubric.

The question set was: Explain the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants as Walzer presents it, and set out the revisionist challenge to that doctrine. Do you find the challenge convincing? Conclude by saying what the debate implies for the principle of noncombatant immunity.

The student's submission reads as follows.

Walzer's moral equality of combatants is the thesis that soldiers on either side of a war stand in the same moral relation to one another: each is permitted to attack the other, and neither does wrong merely by fighting, whatever the justice of the cause for which his state is fighting. The doctrine rests on keeping two questions apart. Whether a state may go to war is a question for its leaders; whether a particular act within the war is permissible is a separate question for the soldier. Walzer insists that a soldier fighting for an aggressor state is not a criminal, since he has done nothing personally wrong to the enemy soldiers he kills. Both sides are equally legitimate targets because both are dangerous, and the ordinary prohibition on killing is suspended between them for the duration of hostilities.

The revisionists, above all Jeff McMahan, deny the symmetry. Liability to defensive attack, they argue, is individual: it turns on whether a person is morally responsible for an unjust threat, not on which uniform he wears. Unjust combatants are responsible for an unjust threat and are therefore liable; just combatants generally are not, since they are responsible for no wrong. It follows that the two sides are not morally equivalent at all, that the traditional doctrine rests on a false moral belief, and that a soldier fighting for an aggressor state wrongs the people he kills even when he fights entirely by the rules.

I find the revisionist argument convincing at the level of the individual, though it is far harder to act on, since it tells an unjust combatant that the only permissible course open to him is to stop fighting. But the conclusion should be carried through into practice rather than left as a philosopher's reservation. The law of armed conflict should be amended to withdraw the combatant's privilege from soldiers fighting for an aggressor state, so that those who continue to fight can be prosecuted for the killings they carry out, and the moral asymmetry the revisionists have identified can finally be reflected in the institutions that regulate war.

This has consequences for noncombatant immunity as well. If liability follows from contributing causally to an unjust threat, then the criterion does not stop at the front line. Munitions workers, the officials who organise the war effort, and the taxpayers and voters of the aggressor state all contribute, and so all fall within it. Revisionism therefore licenses a considerably looser principle of discrimination than traditional just war theory allows, and noncombatant immunity survives it only in a much weakened form. That is a genuine cost of the view, but I do not think it decisive, since the traditional immunity was never anything more than a convention in the first place.
