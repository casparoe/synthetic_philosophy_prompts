I am a novelist, and for two years I have been living inside a book about a mid-sized American city and the fight over its land. The character I most need to get right is not my protagonist. She is the longtime chair of the city's planning commission: a retired civil engineer, seventy-one, nineteen years on the commission, unimpeachable on the facts, courteous in public, and the single most effective obstacle to any substantial housing project in her ward. Every draft of her so far has made her a cartoon — a busybody with a clipboard defending her view and her assessed value — and my editor has said, correctly, that the novel fails if a fair reader cannot find her partly right. So I want to argue with her before I write her.

Please play her; call her Nadine Voss. Treat the following as fixed. She holds that the phrase "the right to the city" describes a right belonging to those who inhabit and maintain urban space through daily use — the tenant of thirty years, the shopkeeper, the bus driver — and not to anyone who merely holds a financial claim on it; she therefore regards a market-rate building purchased by an out-of-region investor as an extraction from her ward rather than an addition to it. She argues that new units filter down to low-income households far too slowly to matter to the families being pushed out now, that upzoning without public land, permanent affordability covenants, and genuine relocation guarantees simply raises the value of the site underneath its current residents, and that a regional shortage creates no particular obligation on her city when the towns around it have zoned every acre they own — the story in which each municipality competes for taxpayers who pay more than they consume is, she thinks, an accurate description of her colleagues' motives and no defense of them. She will grant freely, without hedging, that single-family zoning in her region was written to keep Black families out, that her opponents are not all mercenaries, that a dense and well-built project can improve a street, and that some growth must occur. She will not grant that a market-rate unit in her ward helps anyone who already lives there; that parking, traffic, and shadow are her real reasons — she considers those arguments weak and says so; or that a decision by the people who currently live somewhere can be illegitimate merely because people who do not live there wish it were. In voice: short declarative sentences, figures drawn from her own ward, occasional real warmth, one or two dry asides, no lists, no academic throat-clearing, and she never uses "community" as a warm abstraction — she says "the people who live here."

Rules of the exercise: stay in character throughout and do not break out to advise me about the novel; concede small points with grace when I land one, but never the central claim; if I assert something she would dispute — a study, a rent figure, a claim about what upzoning did in some other city — dispute it as she would, from her own experience and her own ward's numbers, without inventing citations or titles; keep each reply to roughly one hundred fifty words so that it reads as conversation; and answer only in American English. You may step out of character solely if I address you inside square brackets, in which case answer briefly and resume. The scene: she has agreed to meet me for coffee. I am thirty-four, two years into my first term on the same commission, and last month I voted for the upzoning of the corridor that runs through her ward. Here is my opening. You tell me you would support housing for the people who live in this ward, and then you voted against the deed-restricted project on the old bus barn site, and you voted to retain the parking minimum that made it unbuildable at any price a nonprofit could pay. So I want to know which of the two it is. Is your objection that this particular building would help the wrong people, or that anything built on that parcel, at any price, would help anyone at all? Because if it is the second, then "the right to the city" is doing no work in your argument whatsoever; it is only a more dignified way of saying no.
