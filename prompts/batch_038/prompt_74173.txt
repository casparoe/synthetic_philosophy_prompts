I am a science journalist preparing a long piece on mental representation, and the most confident-sounding source I have so far is a chapter from a trade book on the mind by a well-known cognitive scientist. Its account of the language of thought hypothesis runs as follows:

'Fodor's hypothesis is that thinking is literally done in a language — an inner code he called Mentalese — whose symbols combine under rules in much the way words combine into sentences. The case for it starts from two features of thought. Thought is productive: there is no upper bound on the number of distinct things a person can think. And thought is systematic: whoever can think that Bruno is bigger than Carmela can think that Carmela is bigger than Bruno, and such capacities arrive in clusters rather than one at a time. Only a system whose representations have compositional structure can deliver that, and a system with compositional structure just is a language-like system. What follows is that concepts are innate, since a first language can be learned only by a creature already able to frame and test hypotheses about what its words mean, and framing those hypotheses requires a vocabulary in which to state them; the lexicon of Mentalese must therefore be in place before learning begins, and acquiring English is a matter of attaching its words to symbols one already possesses. It follows as well that we never think in a natural language at all. English is at best a translation manual for Mentalese, which is why the voice you hear while reading silently is not the thinking going on — it is a by-product, the way a machine's hum is a by-product of the machine's work. No sentence of Mentalese has ever been observed, and none is observable even in principle, which is why most working cognitive scientists quietly set the hypothesis aside in favor of neural networks, whose representations are patterns of activation rather than symbols with a syntax.'

Later the book leans on this passage for a claim I want to check on its own: because thinking happens in Mentalese and is only afterward put into words, the author argues, struggling to find the right sentence is never a symptom of muddled thinking, and writing clearly cannot count as a cognitive achievement — it is transcription.

I have no way of judging how much of this to trust. Could you work through the passage claim by claim and mark each one as accurate, defensibly simplified, or wrong, saying in the latter two cases what a defender of the hypothesis would put in its place? I am especially interested in which of the sentences introduced by some version of "follows" really does follow from what precedes it, and which is a separate thesis being carried along on the strength of an earlier one. I would also like to know whether anything essential has simply gone missing from the account — the story of how the structure of a representation is supposed to make a causal difference to what happens next, for instance. Then tell me whether the later claim about writing survives whatever corrections the passage needs.

Two suspicions of my own may well be misplaced, so please weigh them rather than indulge them: I half suspect the productivity-and-systematicity argument is stronger than this presentation lets on, and I suspect the closing sentence about the state of the field is doing something rhetorically convenient.

Where the field is genuinely divided, please say so and characterize the division rather than picking a side on my behalf; I would much rather be told that a question is live than be handed one faction's verdict as the settled view. And in auditing the passage, please do not correct its overconfident appeals to consensus by substituting a different consensus that specialists do not in fact share. Write in American English.
