I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am about to file a long feature on whether machine learning has quietly reopened the socialist calculation debate — that argument, running from Mises's original impossibility claim through Lange's trial-and-error model to Hayek's later essay on dispersed knowledge, about whether an economy can allocate capital goods without market prices. The spine of the piece is a sidebar in which I state my own position plainly, so that readers can see exactly what I am and am not committed to. My editor read the sidebar and says it does not hang together; a friendly economic historian who reviewed the draft said something similar but would not be specific about where. Before I revise, I want a rigorous audit of the package. I am not asking for a history of the debate or for references — I know the literature well enough to have written the piece. I want to know what my commitments commit me to.

Here is what the sidebar asserts:

1. Mises was right that rational economic calculation requires money prices for capital goods, and that such prices exist only where those goods are actually exchanged; hence an economy that socializes the means of production cannot calculate in the strict sense of comparing alternative uses of the same resources on a common scale.

2. Nevertheless, the enduring lesson of the debate is epistemic rather than institutional. Ownership is a proxy; the real obstacle is that the relevant knowledge is dispersed, local, indexed to particular times and places, and much of it inarticulate.

3. Lange's model never was the strong reply its partisans take it for. A central board that adjusts administered prices in response to observed excess demand might converge on a tolerable static allocation, but it cannot replicate the entrepreneurial discovery of profit opportunities that no existing price discloses and that nobody has yet formulated.

4. At the same time, I take the internal organization of very large modern firms — a global retailer allocating inventory across thousands of locations with no internal markets — as evidence that non-market coordination at scale is not merely possible but routine, and that this shows Mises overreached.

5. I hold that prices, in the ideal case, function as sufficient statistics: the content of what a decision-maker needs from the rest of the economy is compacted into them, which is precisely why agents who understand nothing about tin can respond correctly to a change in its relative price.

6. I also hold that the economically decisive knowledge is tacit — known in practice, exercised in judgment, not fully expressible in propositional form or in a report to a superior. This, more than any incentive story, is why markets outperform plans.

7. Machine learning changes the calculus, in my view, because systems trained on transaction-level behavioral data can extract and act on exactly those tacit patterns without anyone having to articulate them. Tacitness is therefore a barrier to bureaucratic centralization, not to centralization as such.

8. Finally, I regard the incentive objections — soft budget constraints, the absence of residual claimants who bear losses, principal-agent decay inside planning hierarchies — as real but secondary: a distinct and smaller problem than the calculation problem, and one that better institutional design could in principle mitigate.

What I need is an audit, not a summary. Please:

- Identify which pairs or clusters of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and rank the tensions by severity, from the one that would most damage the argument to the one that is merely untidy. Do not list them as equally bad.
- Distinguish the real tensions from the ones that only look real. I suspect at least one of my commitments is in fact compatible with something a critic would say it flatly contradicts, and I would rather be told which, and why, than have everything flagged.
- For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the revision costs: would abandoning that commitment weaken the feature's thesis, or leave the thesis intact while removing a liability?
- Tell me which single revision would clear the largest share of the difficulty, and separately, which commitment a competent and unsympathetic economist would attack first if the sidebar ran as written.

I have no attachment to any particular item and I am not going to tell you which one I would sacrifice, because at present I do not know. I would rather find out.
