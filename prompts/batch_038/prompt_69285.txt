So I need a favor and you're the only thing available at 1am.

I'm a novelist. Twenty-some years ago I was in a country that still had elections, mostly real ones, while doing research for a book, and I got introduced to a comparative politics professor who'd come out for the same reason — she was there counting things. She became, weirdly, the person I've run everything past ever since, the way other novelists have a lawyer friend. She retires this spring and her daughter has asked me to give a short toast at the dinner. Five minutes, six-fifty words tops, delivered aloud by someone who is not an academic and will be slightly drunk.

Her whole career was one long refusal to say the thing everyone wanted her to say. She had a method instead — not a published framework, just her own habit of mind, three rules she'd enforce on you at lunch with a fork:

1. No single act ever adds up to the diagnosis. What looks like one bad appointment, one redistricting map, one regulator replaced with a loyalist — each one has a legal argument attached to it and a precedent and a plausible ordinary explanation. Her line was that you can't see the thing until you've laid twenty years of clippings end to end on the floor, and by then nobody wants to look at the floor.
2. Never name it, describe it. She thought the label was the partisan move, not the analysis: say the word and you've picked a team, and half the room stops listening and the other half starts quoting you. So she'd describe — who appoints whom, who can be fired and by whom, which court gets to hear which case, where the money comes from — and then stop, dead stop, and let the description sit there doing the work.
3. No standing, no verdict. If you don't live there, pay taxes there, and could be put in a cell there, you don't get to say what's happening to that country. Outsiders are always recruiting the diagnosis for something else, and they never pay for getting it wrong.

The room is going to be exactly the mixed thing that makes these impossible. Half political scientists and lawyers who'll catch every allusion and are themselves split — some think her refusal to name it was integrity, some think it was a career-long dodge that kept her quiet about things that should have been shouted, and there'll be at least two people whose home countries are currently being argued about in the press using precisely the vocabulary she banned. The other half is her kids, her grandchildren, a widower, some very old friends from a choir.

Here's what I need. Write the toast. Constraints, and they're real, not garnish:

- The word itself never appears. Not once. Nor any of its usual synonyms — erosion, decay, illiberal turn, the backslide, whatever the op-ed columnists are using. She'd have hated it and the room will know I'm obeying her, which is part of the affection.
- No country gets named, and nothing in it can be checked against the news the next morning. Rule 3 means I have no standing over anyone's situation and the speech has to be built out of that fact rather than around it.
- It has to do what rule 2 does: describe, don't diagnose. If there's a moment where the room understands perfectly clearly what she spent forty years refusing to say, it should arrive by description, and it should be the emotional peak rather than a punchline.
- One good joke that lands for both halves — the experts laugh at the technical thing, the family laughs at grandma being impossible at dinner parties. Ideally the same sentence.
- Affection throughout, no lecture. It's a retirement, not a seminar.

And one more wrinkle, because I don't want a hagiography. I think rule 3 is wrong, or at least half wrong — I think it kept her quiet about things that should have been named loudly, and I've told her so, twice, and she's never conceded an inch. I don't want to argue with her on her night, but I don't want the toast to pretend the disagreement isn't there, either, because it was one of the best things about our friendship and she'd spot the fakeness immediately. So: let it register in a way she can hear and be amused by and still not have to surrender to.

Give me the toast, then afterwards a short paragraph — separate from the speech, clearly marked — telling me where each of the three rules actually bit into the writing, which lines exist only because of them, and how you handled the disagreement without stating it. I want to be able to check you against her rules like she would.
