I am the instructor for a final-year undergraduate seminar on the metaphysics of modality, and I am writing the two-hour closed-book final. I need one essay question, worth 20 marks, on the necessity of origin — the thesis that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg than the ones she in fact came from, together with the general strategy of arguing from the actual provenance of a thing to de re necessity claims about it.

My problem is that every question I draft can be answered by a student who has merely memorised the shape of the argument: restate the thesis, describe the familiar example of an artefact necessarily originating from the particular matter it was in fact made from, gesture at the possibility that the matter could have been arranged differently, list two or three standard objections, conclude that the issue is contested. I want a question that forecloses that performance. So I want it built around three stipulated cases that I will supply to students as a short case file, and that you should incorporate into the question as you draft it:

Case One. A mother produces two eggs in one cycle, and each is fertilised by a different sperm from the same father. Within a few days, before implantation, the two early embryos aggregate and continue developing as a single organism, which is born as one person whose tissues contain two intermingled and genetically distinct cell lines. Does this person originate from one gamete pair or from two, and what does the essentialist owe us by way of an answer?

Case Two. Nuclear material from the mother's egg is transferred into a second woman's egg from which the nucleus has been removed; the resulting egg is fertilised by the father's sperm. The child's nuclear genome is the mother's and the father's, while the mitochondrial genome is the donor's, and a trace of the mother's original cytoplasm carries over. Does this child come from the mother's egg, and is the identity of an egg fixed by its nucleus, its cytoplasm, or the whole? If the essentialist answers with a tolerance principle — small alterations to an egg's constituents leave it the same egg — does repeated application of that principle yield a child who came from a wholly different egg?

Case Three. At the two-cell stage, one cell of an embryo is removed and replaced by a cell from a second embryo created from an entirely different gamete pair. The substitution is repeated at each subsequent division, so that the developmental process is continuous throughout while its material provenance is progressively exchanged. Is there a point at which the resulting person's origin changes, and how does the essentialist avoid both an arbitrary cutoff and the conclusion that the final person shares nothing of the original origin?

The question you write should require students to state the most defensible precisification of the relation of coming-from that survives all three cases, or to argue that no such precisification exists, and then to say what philosophical work, if any, remains for the thesis — in particular whether a retreat to identifying the origin with the zygote, or with the continuous developmental process rather than the gamete pair, still supports the de re necessity claims and the account of cross-world identity that motivated the thesis in the first place.

Please deliver: (a) the question exactly as it will appear on the paper, including the case material written in plain language, the word limit, and the mark allocation; (b) a model answer of roughly 750 words that takes a definite position on at least one of the three cases rather than surveying options; (c) a marking scheme of five must-hit points with marks summing to 20, each accompanied by a single line identifying the characteristic way in which a fluent but unremarkable answer mishandles it; and (d) two brief descriptions of answers that sound sophisticated but should be capped at a low pass, with the reason.

The exam is closed-book, so the question must contain every factual detail a student needs and must not presuppose acquaintance with any particular article or monograph. For the same reason, do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list anywhere in the materials.
