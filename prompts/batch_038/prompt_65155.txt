I am three-quarters through a third novel, and one scene in it refuses to resolve. My narrator, a woman in her late twenties, insists to two friends — both professional musicians — that a pianist she has heard twice in a hotel bar is extraordinary. They come. He plays exactly as she described: unhurried, clean, indifferent to effect. They hear nothing of what she heard, and afterwards none of the three can say what was missed or where it went. My editor's marginal note was that the scene "proves either too much or too little," and I have come to think she is right, though I cannot yet articulate why. I have written a scene in which the report is accurate in every particular and the verdict nonetheless fails to transfer, and I am no longer certain that this constitutes a failure at all.

To find out, I want a reading of Oscar Wilde's *The Picture of Dorian Gray* through the epistemology of aesthetic testimony — the question of whether the value of a work can be known on someone else's say-so. Please work from a map I can recognise: the demand, associated with Kant and with the eighteenth-century writing on taste, that a judgment of taste issue from one's own encounter with the object and cannot be compelled by proofs; Hume's sketch of the true critic — practised, comparative, delicate, unprejudiced — together with the anecdote he borrows from Cervantes, in which two of Sancho's kinsmen report iron and leather in a hogshead of wine and are vindicated when an old key on a leather thong is found at the bottom; and the standing disagreement over whether the deficiency of second-hand aesthetic belief is epistemic (it cannot amount to knowledge, because aesthetic properties concern how an object strikes a perceiver) or merely practical (one may know perfectly well that a painting is beautiful on a friend's word, but cannot thereby be moved by it or cite the fact in criticism).

Wilde's novel is unusually hospitable to this lens, and I want you to concentrate on a specific run of episodes: Dorian's long account of Sibyl Vane to Basil and Henry before they have met her, cataloguing the Shakespearean parts she plays; the night the two of them attend the shabby theatre on his word and she performs flatly; Sibyl's own explanation afterwards, that the stage has gone hollow since she found something real; Henry's arrival at breakfast with the morning paper and his reframing of her suicide as an artistic event; and, later, the chapter in which Dorian pursues perfumes, music, jewels and vestments under the direction of a book Henry gives him, a book that becomes the script of his life.

Three further things I would ask you to take seriously, two of them mine. In my working notes I distinguish what a witness can hand over: a verdict, a description, and a standard — a claim that this is beautiful, an account of how it goes, and a rule for what counts as beautiful in this kind of thing. The distinction is my own invention, not a philosopher's, and I want it tested rather than flattered. I also suspect the literature underweights a difference of object: testimony about a novel, a painting or a score can be checked by the recipient at leisure, whereas testimony about a performance or a particular night cannot, because the thing described will never be there again. Sibyl is a performer; so is my pianist. Finally, the priming problem: Basil and Henry arrive already told what they are to see, and if prior report shapes perception rather than merely supplying belief about it, I am not sure the acquaintance principle survives contact with the scene — or that Dorian's report did not partly manufacture the Sibyl the two men came to miss.

With that in hand, please answer: (1) Does Dorian qualify as a Humean critic before the collapse, and does being in love damage his testimony as testimony, or change what his testimony is about? (2) Is the night at the theatre a failure of testimony at all, given Sibyl's own account that she has changed — and what does it mean for a theory built on fixed works that this testimony has simply gone stale? (3) Whose testimony does the novel honour and whose does it overrule: the theatre's own audience had been delighted, and the manager had praised her. Is there a Humean answer, or is the true critic's "freedom from prejudice" doing social work here? (4) Can aesthetic testimony ever be corroborated by a discoverable particular, as Hume's kinsmen's was by the key, or is the absence of any such key the real difference between his wine and my pianist? (5) Does Henry's doctrine — echoed in the preface's claim that the artist proves nothing and that art is useless — dissolve the epistemology altogether, so that there is no fact for testimony to track, only new objects made out of reports? (6) Where does the lens overreach? An honest answer may have to concede that the Sibyl episode is not about knowing-on-say-so but about a category mistake — a man unable to separate a woman from the parts she plays — and that my framing is the weaker of the two.

Please write between 1,600 and 2,000 words, as criticism rather than summary. Do not quote the novel: I have the text open, and I would rather you risk nothing in that direction. No bibliography, no citations, no page numbers — I cannot check them and will not use them; naming philosophers is welcome, referring me to sources is not. Faithfulness runs both ways: do not reduce Wilde to an illustration of a position he never held, and do not soften the epistemology to make the novel fit. End with what the novel gives this debate that argument alone cannot supply, and then, briefly and concretely, with which of the two failure modes is the more interesting thing to write — mine, where the description holds and the verdict will not travel; Wilde's, where the verdict once held and the object changed — and what each demands of a reader.
