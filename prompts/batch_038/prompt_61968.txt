A colleague of mine — I'll call her H. — is writing a review chapter on top-down influence in visual experience. As part of it she has been classifying individual reported effects as genuine cases of cognitive penetration of perception, or as something else (post-perceptual judgment, memory, response bias, ordinary perceptual learning, an artifact). I have her classification for seven effects, each with her one-line note. My summaries are stipulative: treat them as complete descriptions of the studies as she read them, do not attempt to match them to published work, and do not supply references or a literature survey.

1. Trained colour names. Volunteers spent two days learning two arbitrary names for adjacent regions of the yellow–orange range, with feedback. At test the names were never mentioned again. Two dependent measures agreed in magnitude and both shifted at the trained boundary while untrained controls did not: continuous hue adjustment (set a patch to look like a remembered exemplar) and a two-alternative choice (which of these two patches is closer to the exemplar). Fixation enforced, 150 ms displays, eye position monitored. The chromatic adaptation aftereffect was unchanged, and a same–different discrimination task on physically identical pairs showed no group difference. The effect was fully intact at a one-week retest. Her note: what they came to know about that part of the colour range is what they now see there — penetration.

2. Trained artifact category and apparent size. Over several hours participants learned a novel category of tool defined partly by a characteristic size. At test, physically identical objects — one shown in a scene that made the trained category salient, one not — differed in apparent size. Two measures agreed: adjustment of a comparison object, and a two-alternative comparison. Free viewing throughout, no eye tracking. The cover story was thin, and several participants volunteered afterwards that they had guessed the point. At a one-week retest the effect was gone. Her note: penetration, messy as it is — the category they acquired is a category of things of that size, and size is what changed.

3. Position knowledge and seen location. Expert and novice players of a territorial board game saw the same mid-game position for 600 ms, after which a probe required them to indicate where a particular stone had been, immediately and with no retention interval. Experts placed it closer to the territory the position assigned it; novices did not. Two measures agreed: a drag-to-place localization task and a two-alternative choice between reconstructed positions. Eye position was monitored during the probe and neither first saccades nor scan paths differed between groups; a same–different discrimination task on the identical displays showed no group difference. Her note: what experts know about a position is knowledge about where the stones in it belong, and that is where the stone looked to be — penetration.

4. Mood and slope. Participants watched a sad or a neutral film clip and then estimated the steepness of a photographed hill by adjusting a line to match the incline. That was the only dependent measure; there was no forced-choice counterpart. The sad group's estimates were higher. The clip was never mentioned again, no participant guessed the hypothesis, and the display was a single static image. Her note: not penetration — one estimate, and a mood about a film is not about the hill.

5. Hunger and saturation. Overnight-fasted and normally fed participants matched the saturation of photographs of food; the fasted group set matches higher. Only the adjustment measure showed it: when the same images were run through a two-alternative task (which of these two looks more saturated?), the groups did not differ. Eye movements were recorded and did not differ between groups. The fast was never mentioned and the stated topic was memory for pictures. Her note: the content lines up, hunger and food, but the effect lived in one response mode and died in the other — not penetration.

6. Arithmetic and line length. After a demanding mental-arithmetic block participants judged the lengths of lines. The effect appeared in adjustment and in a two-alternative comparison, with matched magnitudes. A follow-up with fixation enforced and eye movements recorded found the effect gone; the arithmetic group had simply scanned differently. Her note: two measures, and both wrong for the same reason — the eyes did it; not penetration.

7. Suggested weight. Participants told that a box contained lead judged it heavier than participants told it contained feathers, on a 0–100 verbal rating, which was the only dependent measure. A later group running the same materials with force-matching and grip-force measures found nothing and suggested the original result was an artifact of the rating scale. The belief was plainly about the box's weight, and weight was what was rated. Her note: a rating scale and nothing else — not penetration.

Two of her programmatic remarks bear on how to read this record. In her introduction she says that what she cares about is whether an effect survives a change in how the observer is asked to report it. In a footnote she says that a perceptual system which has merely been told what to expect has not been penetrated but instructed.

The manuscript now on her desk reports the following. Participants were told, as part of a cover story about display calibration, that the monitor was being viewed through a filter that shifted colours slightly toward the red. They then set neutral grays to look neither warm nor cool: the filter group's neutral point was distinctly greenish, the controls' near physical neutral. Three dependent measures agreed in size — continuous hue adjustment, a two-alternative choice (which of these two patches looks more neutral?), and a hue-nulling procedure using 120 ms presentations at enforced fixation with eye position monitored. The chromatic adaptation aftereffect was identical across groups, and a same–different discrimination task on physically identical pairs showed no group difference. Scan paths did not differ. Post-experimental inquiry found no participant who thought the study concerned colour appearance. The effect was present in participants who endorsed the filter claim on a manipulation check and absent in those who did not. The belief was installed fifteen minutes before testing, and when participants were told at the end of the hour that no filter had ever been in place the effect decayed within minutes.

Please give me the following.

(a) Every rule you can construct that is consistent with all seven verdicts. I am confident there are at least three, and I do not think the obvious ones exhaust them. State each as explicit conditions over features of a study — features such as whether the state driving the effect is about the property that is experienced differently, how many qualitatively distinct response measures show the effect, whether the state is standing or transiently installed, whether an alternative explanation has been demonstrated as opposed to merely left untested, whether the effect reaches implicit or early measures, whether attention and demand were controlled, whether it persists — and be clear about which features each rule treats as doing no work at all. I care less about the labels you attach than about whether each rule is precise enough that the seven verdicts fall out of it mechanically.

(b) Which of the criteria commentators standardly reach for are already refuted by her record, and by which case or cases. Where a refutation depends on coding one of my summaries in a particular way, say so.

(c) Apply each surviving rule to the new case. If an application turns on how some feature of the new case should be coded rather than on the rule itself, flag it, argue for the coding you think she would use, and state what happens to the prediction under the rival coding.

(d) A single best prediction of her verdict on the new case, with your reasons for preferring the rule that generates it. Tell me explicitly what evidential weight you put on her two programmatic remarks, and whether they can settle anything given that every case she called penetration satisfies both descriptions.

(e) The further study or studies that would discriminate the surviving rules. For each, state what every surviving rule predicts, and if no single study suffices, give me the minimal set. Design them so that the coding difficulties you identified in (c) cannot arise.

If you conclude that no rule fits all seven verdicts, say which verdict you would have to give up and why; but I would rather you first tried harder to find the rules that do fit.
