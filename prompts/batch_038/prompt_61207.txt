**The question (25 marks):**

Frege held that any adequate account of identity statements must explain how a = b can differ in cognitive value from a = a even when both are true. Explain the puzzle, and explain why, on Frege's diagnosis, an account on which a name contributes nothing but its bearer to the thought expressed cannot solve it. Set out his solution in terms of sense and reference, including what he says about indirect contexts. Finally, consider the objection that sense is an ad hoc posit: since any two expressions can be said to differ in sense just in case they differ in cognitive significance, the notion has no content independent of the phenomena it was introduced to explain. Does the objection succeed?

**The candidate answer:**

Frege's puzzle begins from the observation that a = a and a = b are not on a par, even when both are true. Hesperus is Hesperus is trivial: anyone who understands it can see that it is true, and it tells us nothing. Hesperus is Phosphorus was a genuine astronomical discovery, knowable only empirically. If the semantic contribution of a name were nothing but its bearer, then, since Hesperus and Phosphorus have the same bearer, the two sentences would express exactly the same thing, and would have to stand or fall alike in every respect, including how much they tell us. They plainly do not. So a purely referential semantics cannot explain the difference in cognitive value. Frege also considers the metalinguistic move of taking a = b to assert merely that the two names have the same bearer, and rejects it, because what we assert in such a statement is about the objects rather than about our stipulations; on that reading, the truth of Hesperus is Phosphorus would be a fact about language rather than about the heavens.

The solution is to distinguish, for each name, its reference, the object it stands for, from its sense. Sense determines reference, and it is the way in which the object is given. Hesperus and Phosphorus have the same reference but different senses, roughly the bright heavenly body seen in the evening and the bright heavenly body seen in the morning; so the two sentences express different thoughts, and there is no contradiction in one being trivial and the other informative. The thought expressed, Frege insists, is not to be identified with a sense impression, nor with a mere string of words. What a sense is, on his account, is the mode of presentation a speaker has in mind: the idea (Vorstellung) connected with the word, which belongs to the speaker's inner world. That is precisely why the identity can be a discovery for the Babylonians and unremarkable for us. The sentence expresses a different thought for a speaker who has not worked out the identity than for one who has, and for the latter it is trivial.

The distinction also handles the cases that defeat substitutivity. In Lois believes that Superman can fly, the embedded names are used indirectly: a word then refers to its customary sense, and the embedded clause refers to the thought it customarily expresses. The report is true just in case Lois stands in the belief relation to that thought. Since Superman and Clark Kent have different customary senses, they have different indirect references, and substituting one for the other changes what is said; that is why Lois can believe the one and not the other.

The charge that sense is posited ad hoc, as a placeholder manufactured whenever two expressions differ in cognitive significance and with no further work to do, does not succeed. Sense is needed independently of the identity puzzle. It is what makes composition possible: we understand sentences we have never encountered before because we grasp the senses of their parts and the way those combine, and reference alone could not deliver that. It is also what makes disagreement possible, since two speakers contradict one another only if they can grasp the same content. And there is nothing mysterious about the grain of senses. A sense is what contemporary semantics calls an intension, a function from possible worlds to a reference; on that reading Hesperus is Hesperus and Hesperus is Phosphorus express different intensions, because the first is knowable a priori and the second is not, and the puzzle is solved with a perfectly respectable formal apparatus rather than by stipulation.
