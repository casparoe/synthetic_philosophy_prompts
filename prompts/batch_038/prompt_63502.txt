I do freelance work for parish councils and small ex-industrial towns putting together applications to our national inventory of intangible heritage, and for about four years I've sat at the back of the assessment committee's public sessions taking notes, so I've ended up with a pretty clean record of how they actually rule as opposed to how they say they rule. So far: they inscribed a midsummer bonfire that a parish council started in 1897 to mark the Diamond Jubilee and that has been lit every year since except during two wars. They refused a weaving technique that a revival society pieced together in 1974 from a Victorian engraving, even though it's now taught to hundreds of people and supports a genuine cottage industry. They inscribed a harvest supper whose earliest documentation is the 1930s, and the minutes said something about the community's own account of its past being part of the practice. They refused a miners' banner procession started in 2006 by a heritage charity — well attended, beautiful banners — on the rough grounds that it was a performance about a community rather than a practice of one, there being no miners left and the organisers living forty miles away. And they inscribed a blessing of the fishing fleet that had lapsed for something like forty years before being restarted in 1998 by the grandchildren of the original crews. Worth noting the published criteria say nothing whatever about age; they talk about a practice being recognised by its community as part of its identity and about transmission across generations, which is not obviously what's driving those five decisions.

Now I've got a town coming up in the spring round and I want to predict the verdict before they sink money into an application I think might be doomed. It's a blessing of the salt pans. In 1953 the works management and the local vicar cooked it up after a bad year, complete with a salt king's crown made by a Birmingham jeweller that same year and a charge read out in fake archaic English, and it has been held every year since. The works closed in 1988 and the town kept it going themselves — the ex-workers and their families now organise it, the primary school does the readings, the collection goes to the hospice, and it's basically the town's identity these days. The catch is that a local historian published an article in 2011 documenting the 1953 origin, there was an enormous row, and ever since, the townspeople have known perfectly well it isn't old. They carry on regardless, and this year's application states the 1953 date up front and frames the thing as a practice of community resilience rather than an ancient rite.

So: predict the ruling. Tell me which precedent is the closest analogue and exactly where that analogy strains, and lay out the candidate rules that fit their track record — I can see at least a documentation-date rule, a same-community-carries-it rule, a sincerity rule and something about whether it's run by or for outsiders and money, and I don't think five decisions separate those cleanly. Say which of them votes to inscribe the salt pans and which votes to refuse, give me your best guess with reasons for preferring it, and tell me whether reframing the application would move the outcome or whether the verdict is already fixed by facts the town can't change. There's something else nagging me: a course I did years ago had us read Hobsbawm's introduction to The Invention of Tradition and Anderson on imagined communities, and the more sessions I sit through the more I suspect the committee's distinction doesn't cut anything at all — by Hobsbawm's lights everything they've ever inscribed is invented, quite possibly including the 1897 bonfire, and the same might go for what they've refused. Tell me whether there's a coherent principle hiding in their rulings or whether they're mostly reacting to how assured an application sounds, and if it's the latter, what principle they'd be adopting by ruling whichever way you predict. Bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and please don't restate my question back at me. British English.
