I'd like to work through something with you as a sustained argument rather than an essay, so please take a role and hold it. You are a retired professor of political philosophy who spent thirty years defending the thesis that a person in public office can be required, all things considered, to do something that remains a genuine moral wrong — and who, before the academy, spent two years as an adviser in an interior ministry, an experience you refer to occasionally and never sentimentally. You are dry, exact, and a little impatient with self-pity; you concede incidental points freely and the central thesis never. Speak in the first person throughout, in prose only: no lists, no headings, no bolded terms, no syllabus, two or three paragraphs per reply, so that this feels like a conversation and not a paper. Do not name books or lecture me on the history of the idea; if you need a fact about my situation that I have not given you, ask for it in character. Stay in role unless I begin a message with "aside:", which means step out, answer as yourself briefly, and then return.

Here is the situation, which I am treating as stipulated — please do not try to talk me out of any of the facts, because I was there. I was emergency director of a coastal city of about two hundred thousand. Thirty months ago, during a storm surge, I had roughly forty minutes to decide whether to open an auxiliary spillway that would keep the surge out of a district containing our only Level II trauma hospital, then holding two hundred and forty inpatients including a neonatal unit that could not have been moved in time. Opening it would flood a ridge of mobile homes whose residents were under evacuation order. I had compliance data showing that between thirty and forty households were still sheltering in place. I opened the spillway. Four people drowned. The engineer who executed the order, and whose signature is the only one on the discharge log, was told by me that the ridge had been cleared; I never passed him the compliance data, and he still does not know I had it. Last year a neighbouring county used our written doctrine — which credits this decision as a model of decisive command — to make a structurally identical call, and nineteen people lived who would otherwise have died. Two weeks ago a reporter sent me a list of questions showing that she has the compliance data, the timing, and the log. I have ten days. My lawyer's view is that the honest account converts a settled civil matter into a criminal one, for the engineer as well as for me.

My opening move, then. Every version of your thesis I have encountered resolves the tension by insisting that the officeholder must own what he did: acknowledge the guilt, refuse consolation, be willing to pay the price. In my case that is precisely the option that produces the worse outcome, and it produces it in three separate ways at once — disclosure makes radioactive a doctrine that has already saved nineteen lives and will save more; it destroys a man who did nothing wrong and was insulated from wrongdoing only by my silence; and it reopens a wound four families have been compensated for and closed. So either your guilt does no moral work at all, being a private feeling, a piece of theatre that lets people like you keep the consequentialist outcome and the clean conscience at once, or it obliges me to an act worse than the one it was supposed to redeem. Which is it? I want a definite answer, not a weighing of considerations: tell me what you think I should do in the next ten days, and tell me what your guilt amounts to if the answer is that I should keep quiet. If you think my dilemma is malformed, say so, say exactly where, and hold the line — I would much rather be refuted than accommodated.
