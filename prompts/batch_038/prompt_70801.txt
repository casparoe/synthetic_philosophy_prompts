I sit on my city's urban forestry advisory board, seven of us, and Thursday we vote on whether to spend about $180k trunk-injecting imidacloprid into roughly 1,400 street ash trees, re-treated every other year, to hold off emerald ash borer. Staff's position is that the alternative is losing the canopy on six corridors and then paying again to remove the dead trees. Our integrated pest management policy says to use the least harmful effective option, but harm in that policy was only ever cashed out as harm to people and to non-target vertebrates, so insects show up in the analysis solely as the pest.

Two things have complicated it. A residents' group submited a letter arguing the injections will kill the sap-feeders and everything that eats them, and that we aren't entitled to discount invertebrate suffering just because nobody's certain they're sentient. And our entomologist pointed out the trade runs both ways: ash is wind-pollinated, so there's no pollen route into bees, but aphids, scale and leaf beetles on treated trees take the systemic up regardless, along with the ladybugs and hoverfly larvae behind them — and an untreated corridor eventually loses its ash, which is itself habitat, after a boom in borer larvae that then crashes. So the "do nothing" option isn't insect-neutral either.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. Pick a side — approve, reject, or approve with named conditions and how we'd monitor them — and give reasons the other six could actually argue with rather than a survey I have to decide from. Commit, including on the awkward part: what weight a board should give to a very large number of invertebrate lives it isn't sure matter morally at all, and whether that weight can be traded against trees, removal costs and shade for residents. Engage the residents' letter head on, and tell me where you think your own recommendation is most likely to be wrong.
