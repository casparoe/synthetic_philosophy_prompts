My flatmate and his friend from the math department got into it last night over a coin-flipping game and I couldnt follow half of it, but I'd like to know who was actually right at which point. The game is the St Petersburg lottery with two changes: you flip a fair coin until heads comes up for the first time, say on toss n, and you get $2^n/n if n is odd and *lose* $2^n/n if n is even. So +$2, then −$2, then +$8/3, then −$4, then +$6.40, and so on. Each branch contributes ±1/n to the expectation, so the series is 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 …

Here's how it went:

(1) The math friend said the game has no expected value at all. The terms ±1/n only sum to ln 2 because of the order you add them in, and the sum of the absolute values is the harmonic series, which diverges — so there's no number there to compare against anything else, not even ln 2.

(2) My flatmate said that's overreading the math. Any finite version of the game — cap it at N tosses, with the last branch paying out whatever it would have — has a perfectly well-defined expected value, and those values converge to ln 2 as N grows. So the infinite game is worth 69 cents as a limit, and that's what you should be indifferent to.

(3) The math friend said the limit procedure already assumes what's in dispute: you're taking partial sums in the toss-number order, and since the positive part and the negative part each have infinite expectation, other listings of the very same outcomes give you any value you like. If the worth of a gamble depends on how you happen to list its outcomes, it isn't a property of the gamble.

(4) My flatmate said the listing here isn't a convention — it's the order the coin is actually flipped in. The game unfolds in that order in time. You can't rearrange the sequence of tosses.

(5) The math friend said okay, grant that. But then look at what you're indifferent to: a bet that can lose you $2 with probability 1/4, $4 with probability 1/16, and arbitrarily more than that, against a bet with no ceiling on the downside. Any real person has a finite bankroll, so the dollar expectation isn't the object that matters even when it exists, and in this case it doesn't.

(6) My flatmate said that's an argument that dollars are the wrong unit, not that the game lacks a value — and that the math friend's position has a bad consequence: it turns an infinite class of perfectly well-posed choices into no-verdict cases. He finished with: you'll still take a sure 69 cents over the game if I actually make you choose, so you do have a ranking, you just refuse to call it an expectation.

Can you go through these six moves and say who's right at each one, and then what each of them is missing? And please be explicit about which parts are settled mathematics and which are live disputes among people who work on this stuff — I don't want to be handed a contested position as if it were the consensus.
