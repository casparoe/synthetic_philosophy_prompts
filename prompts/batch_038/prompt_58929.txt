Our reading group has spent the term on constitutional self-reference, and to keep the discussion from floating away we have been playing a Nomic-style game of the kind Peter Suber devised, one in which changing the rules is itself a move. We drafted our own Standing Orders, which are short enough to state in full. Article 1 (Ranks): every rule of the club bears a rank of 1, 2, or 3, a lower number meaning higher precedence, and Article 1 itself has rank 2. Article 2 (Amendment): any rule of the club, whatever its rank, may be amended, repealed, or supplemented by a two-thirds vote of the members present, provided that the motion was tabled in writing one meeting in advance and provided that the ruleset as it will stand immediately after the vote contains no two conflicting rules of the same rank; Article 2 has rank 1. Article 3 (Conflicts): where two rules conflict, the higher-ranked prevails, and where two rules of equal rank conflict, neither prevails and the question is simply undecided under the rules; Article 3 has rank 2. Article 4 (Entrenchment): Article 4 is immutable, and may not be amended, repealed, suspended, or overridden, nor may any rule be enacted authorizing its amendment; Article 4 has rank 2. Article 5 (Binding force): the rules bind every member until amended in accordance with Article 2, and a member who acts contrary to a rule in force may be excluded by simple majority; Article 5 has rank 3.

One of us then produced the following argument, which I cannot resist step by step and cannot accept at the end. (1) Article 2 authorizes the amendment of any rule of the club, whatever its rank, and Articles 1, 3, and 4 are rules of the club, so Article 2 authorizes amending each of them. (2) Article 4 says that Article 4 may not be amended, so Article 2 and Article 4 conflict over whether Article 4 may be amended. (3) Article 3 resolves the conflict by precedence, and Article 2 at rank 1 outranks Article 4 at rank 2, so Article 4 is amendable and our entrenchment clause was inert from the moment we wrote it. (4) That looks like a drafting slip rather than a deep result, so suppose we mend it from inside: by a vote under Article 2 the club amends Article 3, and Article 1's gloss on ranks if need be, so that a rule declared immutable prevails over any rule with which it conflicts, whatever their ranks. (5) Article 4 then genuinely stands above the amendment rule; but the club can take that away from itself just as legally, since Article 2 remains the amendment rule and Article 4 nowhere forbids using it on Articles 1 and 3, so at the next meeting the club repeals the immunity clause from Article 3, whereupon Article 2 again outranks Article 4 and Article 4 can be deleted the meeting after that. (6) Every vote in that sequence was authorized by the rules in force at the moment it was taken, so the entrenchment won in (4) is lost in (5) with nothing illegal anywhere along the way. (7) Generalizing: whenever a rule R is shielded by some further provision P — a precedence clause, a ranking clause, a clause fixing who may amend what — P is itself a rule and therefore reachable by the amendment rule, so R's shield can always be lifted by amending P first; immunity never bottoms out, it only relocates one level up, and every level is reachable. (8) Therefore in any ruleset that permits self-amendment no rule is immutable, and every rule is revocable by the very body it purports to govern. (9) But a rule its subjects can always revoke does not bind them; it advises them. So Article 5's formula, binding until amended, collapses into recommendation, the Standing Orders do not bind this club, and entrenchment in a self-amending system is a fiction. (10) Nor is the reasoning specific to rulebooks: a promise, a contract with a mutual-rescission clause, and a treaty with a withdrawal clause are all norms their holders can jointly rescind, so on (9) none of them binds either.

(10) is false, and so is the last clause of (9), since our rules plainly constrain what members may do this week whatever might be voted next term; yet I cannot locate the joint that gives. What I want is a diagnosis, not references. Tell me which step or steps fail and why each of them looked unobjectionable when read on its own; tell me plainly whether you take this to be an invalid argument with a concealed slide, or a valid argument in which I must simply choose the least costly premise to abandon; and price the repairs, because I expect each one forfeits something I wanted to keep. Three things especially. First, does (7) really go through, given that Article 2's second proviso makes the legality of each vote depend on the shape of the ruleset it produces, so that the chain in (4) through (6) has to be checked link by link rather than in the abstract — and does Article 3's equal-rank clause, which leaves some questions undecided rather than resolved, block the sequence somewhere? Second, what licenses the move from (8) to (9)? I have a vague sense that the subject of the sentence changes between them, from what the club can do collectively and in conformity with Article 2 to whether the rules constrain the members meanwhile, but I cannot tell whether that sense tracks a real defect or is only my reluctance to accept the conclusion; and if there is a coherent notion of a rule that binds procedurally without binding permanently, say what distinguishes it from mere advice. Third, (10) extends the reasoning to promises and treaties, and I suspect the extension is where the argument shows its hand, though I cannot say why, since in each case the holders of the norm can indeed jointly rescind it. For what it is worth, I am most attached to (5) and (6): I think those votes really are legal, and I would rather not be told that the whole difficulty evaporates once our charter is redrafted, because then it was never about self-amendment at all. But if you think the fate of the argument does turn on features of Articles 1 through 5, say which feature, and describe what a charter built to resist (7) would have to look like.
