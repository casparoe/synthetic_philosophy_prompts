So — odd favor, and please don't just explain the essay to me, I need the plan. I write historical novels (two out, the third one four years late because I kept disappearing into archives), and the place where I did a residency last spring has asked me to run the closing Friday session for a mixed room of about fifteen novelists, poets and one memoirist, ninety minutes, wine afterwards, nobody's read philosophy since college. The director billed it as "how much research is too much," which is fine, but what I actually want to do is hang the whole thing on Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation — the history-for-life one, with the monumental, antiquarian and critical modes — and let them arrive on their own at the idea that their research habits are a philosophical problem and not a time-management problem. None of them will have read it. Two will have strong opinions about Zarathustra. The memoirist, I'm fairly sure, will hear "critical history" as permission to settle scores with her family in print.

Give me the shape of the ninety minutes as a teaching plan rather than a talk: what has to land first and what genuinely depends on it. I specifically want to know whether I can get the three modes across without first doing the unhistorical/plastic-power business — the animal that forgets, the horizon, the thought that the only measure is whether a living thing can still grow out of its past — because my instinct is that if I open with the three modes they'll just file them as three genres of nonfiction and never recover. And I want one carrying example, not three: ideally something out of our shared working life (a novel's research folder, a town's local history society, the fact that three of us in that room are writing about the same decade) that can be re-read sucesively in all three modes, so the modes show up as stances toward one and the same material instead of three separate piles of material. Tell me what that example has to have and what it has to lack, and where the seams will start showing under pressure.

Then the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where I head each one off: that the three modes are three methods (or worse, three personality types — monumental is the plotter, antiquarian is the pantser); that Nietzsche is ranking critical as the good one and the other two as training wheels; that the essay is anti-research and that forgetting is advice to skip the archive; that "life" here just means my career or my feelings; plus whatever else you think they'll invent that I haven't. I also want the two or three things I should openly refuse at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing — I'm guessing the suprahistorical turn and the whole Schopenhauer-as-narcotic framing, but you tell me — and one closing question or small exercise, doable in ten minutes with a glass in hand, that would show me whether they've actually got the inversion (history judged by what a living thing needs from it) rather than just enjoyed being told that their reading list is a moral failing.
