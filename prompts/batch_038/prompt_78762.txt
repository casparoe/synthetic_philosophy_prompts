I am preparing a short written statement of my own position on death for a workshop at which it will be read closely and then attacked. The other participants include several people who work in the analytic literature on the badness of death and at least one specialist in early Chinese philosophy, so I would like a genuine consistency audit of the statement before I circulate it — not a restatement of my views, not a survey of the relevant debates, and not advice about how to present it.

Here is the position, as I would state it. The passages I allude to are all from the Zhuangzi; I am describing them rather than quoting them, so please do not spend time correcting my paraphrases unless a paraphrase is itself what generates one of the problems.

1. Death is not bad for the person who dies. The ground is not that anything continues afterward but that there is then no one for whom it could be bad — and this is not a special feature of death, since the same is true of the child I once was, who no longer exists in any sense that would let me say a harm has befallen him.

2. The image I find most convincing is the one Zhuangzi reaches for after his wife dies: the sequence that produced a living being — undifferentiated stuff, then breath, then form, then life — is the very sequence now producing death, and to grieve at that point is like protesting the arrival of winter. I take the force of the image to lie in the continuity: there is no principled line at which the process that constitutes me stops being my process and starts being an assault on me.

3. Nothing survives. I do not treat the transformation story as a survival story and I would refuse it if it were one: any consolation that requires a continuer is a consolation I should decline.

4. Grief at the death of someone I love is fitting, and a life from which it were absent would be defective. What the text means by being without feelings, as Zhuangzi glosses it to Hui Shi, is not the absence of feeling but a refusal to let liking and disliking do inward damage; my model is the mourner whom others praise for having mourned perfectly — one who weeps when others weep and whose inner life is nonetheless not diminished by it.

5. Fear of one's own death is irrational in a strong sense: not merely disproportionate but resting on a confusion about what there is to fear. I am prepared to say that someone who has thought the matter through can properly claim to know that death is no harm.

6. The repeated how-do-I-know moves in the text — the doubt about whether loving life is a mistake, and the story of the girl who wept at being taken away to a ruler's household and was later glad of it — are therapeutic: they are meant to loosen a confidence, not to install suspension of judgment. I do not read Zhuangzi as a skeptic who thinks we can have no views.

7. Some deaths are much worse than others. A death at twenty is worse than a death at ninety for the one who dies, not merely for the mourners, and my account has to be able to say so.

8. The practical upshot, which is what I most want from all of this, is a regimen: attention practice, deliberately unproductive time, repeated exercises in letting projects go, undertaken because they demonstrably reduce death anxiety. I think of this as the same thing the text points at with the cook whose knife does not dull in nineteen years, because he moves along the gaps rather than forcing his way through.

Please work through this set and tell me the following.

- Which pairs of commitments cannot be held together as I have stated them, and what exactly the incompatibility consists in — I want the reasoning traced, not just the verdict.
- Which apparent tensions dissolve once the commitments are stated carefully, and why I was wrong to worry about them. Discriminating here matters to me as much as the positive findings; an audit that flags everything as equally troubling would be useless.
- For each real tension, the cheapest available revision and what it would cost me elsewhere on the list. Several of these commitments support each other, and I want to know which fixes have hidden consequences.
- A ranking of the tensions by severity, with the criteria you are ranking by made explicit.
- Which single change to the set would clean up the most, and which one of the eight I should be most afraid of defending in front of people who know the text and the literature better than I do.

Two further requests. First, tell me where your audit turns on contested interpretive questions rather than on logic. In particular, say whether any of your findings presupposes that a single coherent author stands behind the episodes I have drawn on, and whether the reading that makes one of my commitments defensible is a reading that specialists would dispute — if the skull's praise of death, or the seasonal explanation given beside the basin, is plausibly something the text is staging rather than endorsing, I want that flagged, since it would change what my commitments are committed to. Second, and throughout: separate sharply the claims about what my own position entails, where I expect a good deal of agreement, from claims about what the Zhuangzi says or about what scholars take it to say, where I expect real disagreement. Do not present a contested interpretation as though it were settled, and do not soften a verdict for my benefit — if one of these eight is not merely in tension with the others but incoherent as I have put it, say so plainly.
