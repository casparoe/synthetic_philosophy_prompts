ok so I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, and this started as a hobby project that a friend of mine who does data science got me properly stuck on.

I built a dataset of ~180 polities since 1800 and I score each one with a hazard model: given the features, what's the chance it stops being the same state (collapse, partition, regime discontinuity, whatever) within the next 50 years. I only trust the outside view -- the base rate of the reference class -- and never my own read of the individual case, because my read of individual cases is garbage. Problem: I've talked myself into an argument that seems to prove the tool doesn't mean anything, which is annoying becuase it backtests fine.

(1) The best estimate of the chance that my case ends within 50 years is the base rate of its reference class K -- the share of past Ks that ended within 50 years.
(2) For that base rate to be evidence about my case rather than just about Ks in general, my case has to be an ordinary member of K and not a special one.
(3) But there's no way to check that without stepping outside K and asking which class my case really belongs to, and there are always dozens: for one state in my set I can slice it as "post-colonial states whose borders were drawn by a departing power," "states where more than 40% of revenue comes from one commodity," "states that had a mass mobilization event in the last 15 years," "states founded in the same decade," even "the 61st member of my dataset ordered by founding date."
(4) Every one of those slicings was equally available to me before I looked at the case, and they give 50-year ending rates ranging from like 8% to 70%.
(5) So which class my case is in isn't fixed by the world, its fixed by my choice.
(6) If the class depends on my choice, then the base rate isn't a fact about the world either, it's a fact about me.
(7) So the outside view is just my gut wearing a frequency as a costume -- I pick the class whose number matches what I already believed.
(8) But (7) has to be false, because my hazard estimates beat my own gut calls and beat a coin flip on held-out data, and a method that only encoded my prior couldn't outperform my prior.
(9) Contradiction, so something in (1)-(8) goes, and I can't tell what.

Where I'd like to end up: I'd rather keep (1) and (8), and I'm happy to burn (5) or (6) if you can tell me what's actually wrong with them, because they read as obvious when I go fast. So -- which step do you reject, why did it look fine at first pass, and what does rejecting it cost you? And please be concrete about the engineering side too: I currently have a hack where I only accept a class if at least 3 out of 5 plausible slicings land within 10 points of each other, and I don't know if that's a real fix or a superstition. Is there a rule I can put in the pipeline that stops this argument from working, or is the argument right and the whole approach is theater?
