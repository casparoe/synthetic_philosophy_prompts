Some background, then the ask. I moved into a 70-unit housing co-op in the spring and in October I got talked onto the board, where apparently my job is drafting rule changes before the monthly membership meeting. Turns out we touch the house rules almost every single month — guest limits, quiet hours, subletting, the laundry sign-up, whether the steward can let herself into your unit, how many chore hours you owe, who gets the spare parking spot. The argument is the same every time: somebody says the rule is controlling and treats grown adults like children, somebody else says that without it the loudest four households make the place unlivable for everybody, and then we take a vote at 9:40pm with six tired volunteers and thirty residents in a basement room.

I've been reading a little around the three-way split between freedom as non-interference, freedom as actually having the capacity and the resources to do the thing, and freedom as non-domination — that last one being about living under someone's arbitrary will even when they never once actually interfere, the kind master who's perfectly nice. I think that vocabulary would sort a lot of our arguments, or at least tell us which kind of fight we're having. But I don't have the theory in my head well enough to run it live in a meeting, and nobody else on the board has read any of it, so a procedure that requires me to explain republicanism to a plumber at 9pm is a non-starter.

So: write me a decision procedure my board can run on any proposed rule change. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, and for each one, what counts as a sufficient answer versus a hand-wave — "ask whether the power is arbitrary" is not usable, I need something a person can actually say out loud and get a yes or no from. I want the terminal outcomes marked clearly: rule is fine as written, rule is fine if you change this feature of it, rule has to die, and here-is-a-values-question-so-stop-and-put-it-to-a-vote. Our bylaws need a two-thirds membership vote for any rule change, and I have maybe fifteen minutes per rule, so budget for that.

Then tell me, honestly:

- which of the three conceptions of freedom is doing the real work in your checklist, and whether one of them earns its place at all or is just decoration once you translate it into questions you can answer about a guest log
- which distinctions from the theory you deliberately dropped because they can't survive at board-meeting speed, and what we lose by dropping them
- where you expect the procedure to misfire. My guess: it flags every rule that gives a named human being discretion, and a co-op physically cannot run without discretion, so I need to know how to write discretion into a rule so it passes rather than just getting a wall of failures
- what the procedure cannot do, including whether it can ever tell us how much interference a rule is worth

Then run it on three live proposals, showing the answers to each question, not just the verdict:

1. Overnight guests capped at six nights per month per unit, guests registered by name with the steward, steward keeps the log, and any resident can petition the board for an exception.
2. If a resident hasn't been seen or heard from in 48 hours and doesn't answer the door, the steward may enter with the spare key — no notice, no second person present, no report filed afterward. This one is mine. I wrote it after an elderly member fell and lay there overnight, and I like it, so don't soften the result; if the procedure says the thing I'm worried it says, say it and then tell me what a version of the rule that survives would look like.
3. Four hours of community work per month per household, assigned by the work coordinator, who can also excuse households for any reason she thinks adequate.

Finally — one thing I care about a lot: don't let the procedure quietly import the values of one side of our argument and present them as steps. If a step assumes that being left alone is the default and any rule has to justify itself, or the opposite, I want that flagged as a judgment call dressed up as procedure, because that's the exact move people use to win votes here. And keep it to something I could hand to a new board member in the fall without me standing next to them explaining it, not a essay.
