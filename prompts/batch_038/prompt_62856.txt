I'm doing a part-time cognitive science conversion course and we've just spent three weeks on Hofstadter and Mitchell's Copycat — the slipnet, the coderack, the workspace, temperature climbing when too many possibilities are competing, the whole apparatus. The worked examples are always the same sort of letter-string puzzle (abc becomes abd, so what does ij become?), which is fine for teaching the machinery but does leave the whole thing feeling like a very small toy in a very small room. I want to run it on something real and see whether it survives the contact. My candidate is the Pluto business, and I'll put down the facts I think matter so you don't have to guess at them: Pluto was found in 1930 by a search for a hypothetical Planet X and was assumed to be substantial — larger than Mercury at first, then smaller and smaller as the measurements improved; Charon turned up in 1978 and let people calculate Pluto's mass properly, which came out absurdly small; through the 90s more and more Kuiper belt objects were found, a lot of them sharing Pluto's odd eccentric, tilted orbit; the Hayden Planetarium in New York quietly left Pluto out of its planets exhibit when it reopened in 2000; then in 2005 Eris showed up, roughly Pluto's size and actually more massive, and the choice went stark — either Eris is the tenth planet or Pluto isn't the ninth. A committee set up in 2005 offered three candidate definitions: a cultural one (roughly, a planet is a planet if enough people say it is), a structural one (round enough to be in hydrostatic equilibrium), and a dynamical one (massive enough to have cleared its orbit of other bodies). The 2006 General Assembly in Prague ended up adopting a three-part definition — orbits the Sun, in hydrostatic equilibrium, has cleared its neighbourhood — plus a brand-new dwarf planet category for things satisfying the first two but not the third, and the rival proposal to treat dwarf planets as a subclass of planets was voted down. It was settled by a show of hands among the members who were still in the hall at the end of a fortnight-long meeting. And it hasn't settled: a large chunk of the planetary science community, including people who work on New Horizons, still use a geophysical definition on which anything round is a planet, so there are now two live communities with two different extensions for one word, plus a public that never accepted the change at all. My brother's eight-year-old has a glow-in-the-dark mobile of the nine planets on his bedroom ceiling and he refuses to update it, which I think is data of a sort.

So first do the mapping, properly, before you evaluate anything — I'm not after a general essay on Pluto. Treat the episode as one long analogy-making run and tell me what the microdomain actually is here, what the source and target are, and what the slipnet neighbourhood of planet looks like: which nodes are genuinely adjacent (world, body, satellite, moon, minor planet, asteroid, comet, Kuiper belt object, dwarf planet, round thing, thing that clears its orbit, "the nine") and how the connection strengths between them would have to have been arranged in, say, 1995 versus 2005 for the eventual outcome to be the cool, low-temperature solution. Then say which slips happened and which were refused. Eris is the interesting case: the structural parallel between Eris and Pluto is about as tight as an analogy can get, and it's what heated everything up, but the community refused the obvious slip and instead moved the target concept into a node that didn't exist beforehand. Is inventing a node something the model can do at all, or does it only ever slide between things already sitting in the net? And what's the temperature story — did the vote cool the system down or just freeze it mid-slip?

Then run it the other way, which is the bit I care about most. Everyone at the time said the fight was merely semantics, an argument about a word, no fact of the matter either way — but it definately didn't feel like that. People experienced it as the solar system having got smaller; there was real, if half-joking, mourning. Does the fluid concepts picture have the resources to explain why a dispute about a category boundary feels substantive rather than verbal, something about the concept being entangled with a load of others so that a slip propagates and drags them along — and can it say anything about the cost of a slip, or about why that cost landed so unevenly (a child's mnemonic versus a dynamical astronomer's taxonomy)? Be honest about where the model goes quiet. It has no notion of authority, so it can't represent the difference between a slip forced by structural pressure and one imposed by a vote; it has no notion of stakes; and it doesn't seem able to represent the current situation, where the slippage simply never resolved and two communities carry on in parallel with different nets. Also tell me whether I'm making a category error by pointing a model built for three-letter strings at seventy-odd years of astronomical taxonomy — if the analogy between Copycat and this case is itself a bad one, show me where it slips and what would have to be added to the model for it to earn its keep. One more thing: there's a control case. In the 1840s and 50s Ceres and the other objects between Mars and Jupiter were counted as planets, the list reached twenty-odd by 1851, and then astronomers quietly started calling them asteroids instead and nobody minded in the slightest. Same structural pressure, none of the fuss. What does the contrast between those two episodes say about when slippage is cheap and when it isn't — and is that something the model could have predicted, or only something we can see with hindsight?
