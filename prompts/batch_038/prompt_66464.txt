I run the methods function for a regional health authority's priority-setting committee, which meets fortnightly to rank competing service configurations. I am drafting an internal note for the clinicians and lay members on the ethical foundations of a rule the committee adopted last month. The rule reads, in substance: whenever every patient affected by two candidate allocations is indifferent between them on their own reckoning — indifference measured in each patient's own expected quality-adjusted life-years — the committee is likewise required to be indifferent. A colleague assures me that this provision, together with the assumption that both individual and social preferences over risky allocations satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, yields Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and therefore commits us to maximising the unweighted sum of patients' expected QALYs. A second colleague, who describes herself as a prioritarian, says she is perfectly content to accept the provision and nonetheless maintains that we ought to give greater weight to the worse off. I should like a careful conceptual explanation of who is right, and more generally of what the theorem does and does not establish.

The prioritarian's case is the part I most need untangled, because I suspect she is equivocating. Her argument is this: a social ordering that sums the square roots of individuals' utilities obviously satisfies the committee's provision — if each patient is indifferent between two allocations, then each patient's utility is the same under both, so the sum of square roots must be the same too — and it obviously respects anonymity; therefore, she concludes, the theorem cannot be telling us what people say it tells us. Please diagnose precisely where this reasoning goes wrong. I also have a hunch that there are two distinct things one might mean by prioritarian social evaluation here, one that takes the expectation of a concavely transformed utility profile and one that concavely transforms a profile of expected utilities, and that each of them runs into trouble with a *different* premise of the theorem. Please tell me whether that hunch is right, or correct me if it is not. Either way, a small worked example would help enormously: two patients, two or three prospects with explicit probabilities and explicit utility figures, carried through far enough that I can see for myself which hypothesis is violated in each case and by how much.

Beyond that, the note should address three further points. First, the derivation itself: what is genuinely doing the work, why the indifference form of the Pareto condition — rather than a strong Pareto condition — is the crucial hypothesis, how the linearity of social utility in the probabilities combines with it to force an affine aggregation, and what, if anything, in the standard statement stops the resulting weights from coming out negative. My reading is that our rule as drafted supplies only the indifference half, so please say explicitly whether that is enough for the conclusion my first colleague wants. Second, the scale-fixing problem: individual von Neumann–Morgenstern utility functions are unique only up to positive affine transformation, so adding them looks meaningless until the zero points and units have been pinned down; explain what role the impartial-observer or extended-sympathy device plays in pinning them down, and give me your judgement on whether that move derives the interpersonal comparisons or merely restates the assumption that they are available. Third, the apparent collision with Arrow's impossibility theorem, which seems to rule out exactly what Harsanyi's theorem delivers; identify the informational assumption on which the two results diverge, and say something about whether the divergence is a technicality or a philosophical fault line.

Finally, I want the note to take a position on the charge — associated with the Sen–Weymark line of criticism, though please do not treat that as an invitation to survey the debate — that Harsanyi established a linear-sum representation of social preference but not utilitarianism in any substantive sense, because nothing in the theorem connects von Neumann–Morgenstern utility to wellbeing. Say whether you find that charge devastating, partly right, or a quarrel over labels, and explain what would have to be added to the theorem's premises to close the gap.

Please write in British English throughout, in about 1,400 to 1,700 words, with light notation where it earns its keep but pitched so that a clinician without formal training in decision theory can follow the argument. I am after the reasoning rather than the literature trail, so please give no bibliography, no footnotes, no citations, and no attributions of particular claims to particular papers; write it in your own words rather than reproducing quotations you may be recalling imperfectly. Use short headed sections for the four parts above, and end with a two- or three-sentence bottom line that I could read out at the start of the meeting.
