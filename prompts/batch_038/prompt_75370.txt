> (a) Explain the distinction between the inside view and the outside view of a prediction problem, and give an example of each. (b) Some authors hold that the outside view should come first and that inside-view information should only be used to adjust away from it. And here is the student's answer, copied out in full (her own wording, I only fixed the paragraph breaks):

> Kahneman and Tversky noticed that people making forecasts are usually inside their own case, so they distinguish two approaches. The inside view starts from the particulars of the project at hand: our plan, our team's track record, the story we can tell ourselves about how it will go. The outside view starts from a reference class of comparable past cases, works out the distribution of their actual outcomes, and uses that distribution as the forecast. If a city is budgeting a new tram line, the inside view asks how detailed the schedule is and how experienced the project director is; the outside view asks what comparable tram lines in other cities actually cost and how late they actually ran. Flyvbjerg turned the second approach into a method called reference class forecasting, and he argues it should be compulsory for big public projects, because planners are systematically optimistic about cost and duration — that's the planning fallacy.
>
> The case for putting the outside view first is that base rates are just facts about the world, so a forecast anchored on them isn't exposed to the biases the anchor is supposed to correct. Outcomes also regress to the mean, so anyone currently running unusually well — or anyone with an unusually detailed plan — will tend to do worse next time, and the outside view folds that in automatically. Hence the standard advice: take the reference-class distribution as your starting point and move off it only where you have specific grounds for thinking your case really is different. This is also why the outside view just is epistemic modesty: modesty means declining to treat your own case as special, and that refusal is precisely what the outside view consists in.
>
> The policy can be pushed too far. Gigerenzer's work on fast-and-frugal heuristics shows that simple rules using very little information often beat elaborate statistical models, so a good forecaster should mostly ignore reference classes and rely on a well-designed heuristic instead. Tetlock's superforecasters are the proof: they beat analysts with access to classified material in the IARPA tournaments by holding to the outside view and refusing to let case-specific stories move their numbers.
