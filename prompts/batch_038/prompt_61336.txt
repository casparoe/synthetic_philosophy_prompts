So I teach a senior seminar with the grandiose title "Nations and Natures," and this week is Henry V, and the discussion has stalled in the most predictable way imaginable: one half of the room reads it as a pageant about what the English essentially are, the other half reads it as Shakespeare winking at us about how ugly that idea is, and everyone reaches for the word "stereotype" without being able to say what the assertion they're objecting to actually claims. I want to hand them something with more purchase, and the thing I want to hand them is the work on generics. The point I care about is that generics don't behave like covert statistics — "ticks carry Lyme disease" gets assented to even though only a tiny fraction of ticks carry it, because the property is striking, whereas "books are paperbacks" gets rejected even though it's true of most books, because there's nothing striking about being a paperback. So a generic looks licensed by a handful of vivid instances rather than by a majority, its cognitively sticky once installed, it licenses inferences about new members of the kind, and — the part that matters for class — you basically can't falsify it with distributional data, because it was never tracking a distribution in the first place. My working hypothesis, which I'd like you to attack rather than flatter, is that nearly every national-character claim in the play is a striking generic wearing a statistical costume, and that the interesting move Shakespeare makes isn't asserting them but staging what they're *for*: mobilization, not description. The Crispin speech doesn't measure a pre-existing English character against five-to-one odds; it manufactures one on the spot and then retroactively credits the men standing there with having always had it — anyone who sheds blood with the king becomes his brother "be he ne'er so vile," and the day itself will gentle his condition — so the speech is in the business of creating a kind and then claiming it merely described it. Even Henry's swagger at the herald is a generic: he tells Montjoy to mark the "abounding valour in our English," as though that were a fact about a population rather than an order to it.

Here's what I want you to actually work through. Act 3 scene 5 is the one I think gets criminaly underused, because the French court runs an entire philosophy-of-national-character seminar and nobody notices. The Constable asks where on earth the English get their mettle, given that their climate is "foggy, raw and dull" and all they drink is "sodden water" and "barley-broth" — he is holding an environmental theory of character and has just been handed a counterexample to it. The Dauphin answers with a grafting image, about wild stock shooting up suddenly past the men who grafted it; Bourbon answers with genealogy, "Normans, but bastard Normans, Norman bastards!"; and the Dauphin reports that the French ladies say "our mettle is bred out" and threaten to give themselves to Englishmen "to new-store France with bastard warriors." Climate, blood, culture: three rival explanations of why peoples differ, offered inside twenty lines, and all three of them wrong about the people offering them. Then Fluellen, after the battle, explains to Gower that Harry Monmouth's life follows Alexander the Great's "indifferent well," on the grounds that Macedon and Monmouth both have a river and "there is salmons in both" — that is what the form of a national-character comparison looks like when nothing at all constrains the content, and when Gower blocks one inference by pointing out that Henry never killed a friend, Fluellen doesn't abandon the comparison, he just reroutes it to the dismissal of Falstaff. Fluellen is also the play's most literal essentialist, assuring the king that all the water in the Wye couldn't wash his Welsh blood out of his body, and Henry takes it as a compliment. And then there's Macmorris: Fluellen starts to say there aren't many of his nation who understand the disciplines of war, and Macmorris cuts him off with "Of my nation! What ish my nation? Ish a villain, and a bastard, and a knave, and a rascal." I want your view on whether that's the play's one genuine refusal of the whole enterprise or just another striking generic with the polarity flipped — Macmorris substituting a worse characterization for Fluellen's, which would make the moment a joke about the form rather than a protest against it.

Then take a position on the two things my students actually want to fight about. The night before Agincourt, Henry walks among his own soldiers in disguise and Williams and Bates press him on whether the king's cause can carry the weight of every death it orders — is that scene a philosophical objection to national-character talk, on the grounds that obedience has been converted into a collective trait so that "the English follow their king" stops being an observation and becomes an instrument, or is it just Henry winning an argument against two men who have no standing to win it? And does the play anywhere offer an argument for when characterizing a people is legitimate rather than only showing the bill? Second: the Epilogue's admission that Henry the Sixth lost France and made his father's England bleed — does that falsify the character the Crispin speech asserted, or does the generic simply absorb the loss the way striking generics absorb everything, so that no subsequent history could ever have dislodged it? Finally, tell me what the play gives me that a paper on genericity wouldn't — where it shows something about the function or the feel of these claims that the semantics misses — and also where I'm wrong and the reading flattens the play into an illustration of my thesis; I'd genuinely rather you scrap the hypothesis then stretch the text to save it. Start with your bottom line — one sentence saying whether you'd keep, revise, or scrap my hypothesis — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
