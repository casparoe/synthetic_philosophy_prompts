I'm the intake lead at a community mental health clinic and I've now been on two committees that revise our forms, so I've been trying to figure out when a diagnostic label actually changes the people it gets applied to versus when it's just a change in what the paperwork says. I read some Hacking on looping years ago and it felt right, but I can't tell whether it's tracking a real distinction in my own case list rather than a nice story. Here are my verdicts, going by what I've actually watched happen.

When Asperger's got folded into autism spectrum disorder, a lot of adults who'd built an identity on the old word lost something real, and the term went underground and came back as a badge. That looped — the classification changed the people. When "mental retardation" became "intellectual disability," I don't think anybody's inner life moved an inch; it only changed what other people are allowed to say out loud. Those two are both euphemism cycles and they came out opposite, and that bothers me.

Multiple personality in the 80s: the caseload exploded the moment clinicians started asking about alters, and I'll say flat out that the category made the condition. Depression in primary care once the pills and the screening questionnaires arrived: the numbers exploded just as hard, but I won't say the category made the condition — those people were miserable beforehand, we just started counting them. Same epidemiological shape, opposite verdict from me, and I can't defend the difference.

PTSD: pushed in largely by veterans' advocacy, so politically made in an obvious way, and still I think it fit what was already there. So a loop can be a discovery rather than a distortion.

The one I keep circling is this. When a label unlocks resources — ADHD and extra time in school, a disability determination — I watch reported symptom profiles drift toward the criteria and I call that contamination, the measure stops measuring. When a label unlocks a community and a vocabulary for yourself — autism — I watch exactly the same drift and I call it people finally saying what was true all along. I'm definately not just doing this for political reasons, but I can't say what I'm keying on.

Also: our EHR quietly flags certain patients as high-utilization-risk by algorithm and nobody is told. I think that loops, because the flag changes what we offer them and that changes what they turn into. But I also think that if we ran the flag and never acted on it and never told anyone, nothing at all would happen. So self-consciousness doesn't look like the trigger, which surprises me.

My tentative rule is that a classification loops if and only if it gets into the classified person's own practical reasoning — if it changes what they count as a reason for doing things. That handles the intellectual disability case nicely, but it says the risk flag can't loop, and it gives me nothing on ADHD versus autism, since both labels are absolutely in people's practical reasoning. Maybe the real variable is who authored the criteria, insiders or outsiders, rather than anything about self-awareness, but I don't see how that handles the grief or depression cases either.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that gets most of this? State it precisely and tell me which of my cases motivates each clause, and what it costs. Then tell me what it predicts about cases I haven't listed — I specifically want to hear about prolonged grief disorder now that it's an official diagnosis, and about a "burnout" flag a colleague wants to add to our own intake form that no external manual recognizes. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up, because I'd rather know.
