A friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether the result of a referendum can properly be described as expressing what the majority wants, and we have reached an impasse that I believe turns on a conceptual distinction neither of us could state cleanly. I would like you to supply it.

His view, roughly: a bundled measure is an instrument for smuggling provisions past the electorate. A single yes-or-no question covering a package lets a provision that would lose on its own ride in behind one that would win. Hence the reform proposal he favors — split measures up and vote provision by provision — on the theory that disaggregated voting recovers the genuine popular verdict that the drafting process distorted. Underneath this he holds something stronger, which I want to isolate: that a referendum is legitimate insofar as its outcome *tracks* an antecedent fact about what most voters want, with the ballot functioning as a measuring instrument rather than as the thing that settles the question.

My view: unbundling does not recover anything, and the tracking picture is itself the confusion. But when I tried to make that concrete I could only gesture, and he reasonably accused me of obscurantism. So I built the following example, and I want to know whether it does the work I think it does.

A ballot measure contains two provisions: T, a regional sales tax funding a rail line, and H, upzoning of land near the new stations. Voters accept or reject the pair together. The electorate divides into three blocs. Bloc A (40 percent) prefers T without H, then neither, then both, then H without T. Bloc B (40 percent) prefers H without T, then neither, then both, then T without H. Bloc C (20 percent) prefers both, then neither, then the two half-measures, equally last.

Under his scheme, T passes 60–40 (A plus C) and H passes 60–40 (B plus C), so both provisions are enacted. Under the package vote, "neither" beats both 80–20 and beats each half-measure 60–40. Separate provision-by-provision voting enacts precisely the bundle that four-fifths of the electorate ranks below doing nothing.

I would like the following explained, with attention to the assumptions rather than the arithmetic:

1. Why do the two procedures diverge? State the property that preferences over bundles must have in order for item-by-item majority rule to be a faithful decomposition of the question what does the majority want taken as a whole, and say why my three blocs violate it. I believe this property has a standard name; I care about the property, not the label.

2. There is a wrinkle I noticed but cannot evaluate. Bloc C's votes are expectation-dependent: if C expects H to pass, its ranking tells it to vote yes on T, and if C expects H to fail, the same ranking tells it to vote no. Working through the possibilities, I count exactly two self-consistent profiles — one in which both provisions pass 60–40, and one in which both fail 60–40 and the Condorcet-winning bundle is enacted. Check that. Does the existence of two such profiles help my side or his? Is it evidence that unbundling reintroduces indeterminacy rather than removing it, or an artifact of my assuming C voters reason about the other question at all?

3. What, precisely, is the difference between "60 percent voted yes" and "60 percent want this"? If the first is supposed to be evidence for the second, what inference connects them, and what would have to be true of the electorate for that inference to be sound?

4. Is any of this specific to referendums? Logrolling in a legislature is the deliberate manufacture of bundles; division-of-the-question rules and amendment procedures are deliberate unbundling. If the pathology travels intact, what survives of the claim that direct democracy has a distinctive difficulty in expressing a popular will, as against the claim that majority rule over multi-dimensional questions has a difficulty that direct democracy merely displays without the filters a representative assembly supplies?

5. Two design choices to read against your answer. Switzerland appears to require that a popular initiative preserve a single subject matter, on pain of being declared invalid — a constraint on what citizens may propose, not on how votes are counted. California measures routinely combine a bond, a tax and a regulatory change in one question. Is the first rule best understood as an attempt to enforce the property identified in (1), and if so, why would enforcing it by limiting the initiative power be thought preferable to enforcing it by changing the counting rule?

I want an explanation, not a survey. Use my example, and tell me where the construction is doing more work than I realized — including whether it is rigged in some way that makes the divergence a curiosity rather than a general feature of bundled ballots.
