Consider the methodological thesis Milton Friedman advanced in the opening essay of his 1953 volume *Essays in Positive Economics* — the thesis Paul Samuelson later labelled the F-twist: that a positive theory is to be judged solely by the accuracy of its predictions about the phenomena it is meant to explain, and that the realism of its assumptions is not merely irrelevant but, up to a point, inversely related to the theory's significance, on the grounds that a genuinely important hypothesis works by abstracting away a welter of predictively irrelevant detail.

Take the career of the Phillips curve, from 1958 to the early 1980s, as a test case for that thesis. The sequence, as it is usually told, runs as follows. A. W. Phillips fitted a stable inverse relation between unemployment and the rate of change of money wages using British data from 1861 to 1957. Samuelson and Solow recast the relation in terms of price inflation in 1960, gave it its name, and it entered American macroeconomic discussion as something like a menu from which a government might choose its preferred combination of inflation and unemployment — a reading of their intent that some historians of the period dispute. Through the 1960s the curve appeared to hold. Then Friedman, in his presidential address to the American Economic Association in December 1967, and Edmund Phelps independently, argued that the relation could not be exploited: it held only while workers were systematically mistaken about the rate of inflation, so once expectations adjusted, the long-run curve would become vertical at a natural rate of unemployment that policy could not permanently push below, and attempts to do so would yield not low unemployment but accelerating inflation. The 1970s, combining high inflation with high unemployment, were widely read as a vindication of that argument. In the early 1980s the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker brought inflation down from roughly 13 percent to roughly 4 percent at the cost of the deepest American recession since the 1930s — and economists immediately disagreed about whether the sacrifice had been larger or smaller than the expectations-augmented curve implied, a disagreement that was never fully resolved.

Please analyze this episode through the lens of the F-twist, and then turn the lens back on itself. Specifically:

1. Do the mapping carefully before evaluating it. Identify the principal idealizations in each successive version of the curve, and sort them into three classes: assumptions that are false but inert with respect to the predictions at issue; assumptions that are false and load-bearing for those predictions; and assumptions whose falsehood is a function of whether the model itself is being used. On that basis, introduce and defend a distinction I will call that between a *benign* idealization and a *self-consuming* idealization — the latter being one that is safe so long as the model is merely descriptive and that becomes causally false the moment the model is adopted as a target of policy. Is the collapse of the Phillips curve a paradigm case of the second kind? If so, can the F-twist even be coherently stated for theories of that kind, given that it makes predictive success the only criterion and the predictions here are unstable under the theory's own adoption?

2. Say what counts as "the predictions" in this case. Should the curve be scored on its in-sample fit to 1861–1957, its out-of-sample performance through the 1960s, or the natural-rate hypothesis's forecast of 1970s stagflation? Note that Friedman's alternative rested on its own idealization — slowly adapting expectations — which Lucas attacked in 1976 on grounds that generalise the point in (1). Is the Lucas critique a refinement of the F-twist, a restriction on its domain, or a refutation of it?

3. Deliver a verdict that runs in both directions. Does the episode confirm the thesis — an instrumentally successful model with implausible assumptions displaced by another such model, with complaints about realism correctly ignored — or does it expose a failure mode the thesis never anticipated, namely a theory undone not by the messiness of reality but by the target system's learning the theory? And does the Volcker credibility dispute illustrate a case where the F-twist is silent because the parties cannot agree on which prediction was the relevant one?

Where the lens does not reach, say so plainly rather than forcing an answer, and flag any step where the historical record is genuinely contested rather than presenting it as settled. No bibliography or reference list is needed; I am interested in the analysis, not the sourcing.
