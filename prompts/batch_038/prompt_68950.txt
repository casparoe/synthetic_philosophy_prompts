I sit on the scientific advisory panel of a seafood certification scheme, and this year we have been asked to extend our welfare standards beyond finfish to crustaceans. The panel is badly split, and our chair has asked each of us to submit not a position paper but a single probability together with the reasoning that produced it, so that we can see where we actually disagree instead of trading adjectives. I have been resisting this on the grounds that my view is not the sort of thing that can be stated as a number, but I have been told — probably rightly — that a hedge is just a number in disguise. So I should like to try the exercise on you before I try it on my colleagues. The proposition is this, and I have tried to state it so that it has a determinate truth value. Call it P: a typical farmed whiteleg shrimp (Litopenaeus vannamei) raised to market size in a commercial semi-intensive pond operation — stocked densely, handled at grading and at harvest, exposed to the water-quality swings and disease pressures that go with that system, and killed at the end of a grow-out period of three or four months either by immersion in ice slurry or by being left in air as the pond is drained — has a life that is on balance not worth living, in the sense that it would have been better for that animal never to have been hatched. I am not asking you to survey the literature or to tell me where the consensus sits; I have read the consensus summaries and they turn evasive at precisely the point where I need help. I want your number and the derivation.

Here is the decomposition I have been trying to build. Please use it as a starting point, amend it where you think a term is missing or misplaced, and justify each term with a rough probability and a sentence or two. The first is the probability that phenomenal consciousness is present in such an animal at all, as distinct from nociception, from adaptive modulation of behaviour, or from any of the other things the usual evidence might equally well be tracking. The second is the probability that, if there is consciousness, its states have hedonic sign — that they are felt as good or bad for the animal rather than merely as discriminative — since I take it these two things can come apart. The third is the term I most want you to engage with, because I suspect it is generally left out. The decapod nervous system is a chain of ganglia with a great deal of local autonomy: isolated ganglia can generate coordinated motor patterns, and an animal can go on behaving in an apparently purposive way when parts of the chain are disconnected. So I want a probability for the claim that, if there is a subject of experience here, it is exactly one subject rather than several partial ones whose states are not co-conscious; and, more importantly, I want you to tell me which way that uncertainty pushes P. I can see it both ways. Several subjects in a single body might mean several loci of suffering where we had been counting one; but a subject with so little integration might also have so little in the way of unified affect that its states scarcely count as good or bad for it at all. The fourth term is the probability that, given a valenced subject, the negative states actually dominate across a whole commercial life — partly an empirical question about husbandry, partly a question about how much weight a short life with few prospects should carry. The fifth is the probability that the comparison P makes is even well-formed for such a creature: that "worse than never having existed" can be true of an animal with no plans, no enduring projects and no evident anticipation of its own future, rather than being a judgement that gets a grip only where there is someone for whom a future can matter.

Give me your number for P, then your numbers for the five terms, then how you combine them, saying explicitly whether you are multiplying and, if so, which terms you think are not independent and how that dependence changes the arithmetic. Then tell me which single term your total is most sensitive to — which one, if it shifted by twenty points, would drag your answer furthest — and which term you would be least willing to defend in front of a sceptical colleague. For calibration: my own credence in P is about 0.55, and that of the colleague who will be reading your reply alongside mine is about 0.1. She is not a sentience sceptic, and I should like your view on which of the five terms she and I most likely disagree about, given the size of that gap. Please do not offer me references, a bibliography or a reading list — I want reasoning, not sourcing — and please write the whole answer as continuous prose in the manner of a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold and no tables, in British English throughout.
