I am preparing a seminar paper that treats Frederick Douglass's post-1851 constitutional writings as a running body of case law — not his general views on slavery, but his rulings on particular provisions and questions. Here is the record I have compiled, in my own words:

1. The question he poses to the Garrisonians is whether the Constitution guarantees to anyone a right to hold property in man. His answer is no, and he notes that the words slave and slavery appear nowhere in the instrument.
2. On method, the text alone governs: the framers' intentions count only so far as they were plainly written down, and since the convention's debates were deliberately withheld from publication for a generation, they cannot be the measure of the document.
3. The Constitution and the government are different things — chart and course — so seventy years of pro-slavery administration is no evidence against the instrument itself.
4. The three-fifths provision he treats as a disability laid on the slaveholding states rather than a bargain struck for them, since it withholds representation for part of their population and tips the House toward the free states.
5. The clause permitting the importation of persons until 1808 he reads as a grant of power to Congress to end the trade, and as a studied refusal to describe human beings as property.
6. The clause about persons held to service or labor who escape into another state he reads as ambiguous on its face — the description fits an apprentice or a hired laborer as well as it fits a slave — and resolves the ambiguity by the canon favoring liberty; the 1850 enforcement statute he holds unconstitutional.
7. Dred Scott he condemns not only as unjust but as built on the wrong method: intention, original racial understanding, and settled practice, all in place of text.
8. In the territories, Congress both may and must prohibit slavery; no local majority can manufacture property in man.
9. The Emancipation Proclamation he calls constitutional as a war measure and simultaneously deficient: it exempts the loyal states and the pacified districts, and its warrant expires with the emergency, which is why he campaigns for an amendment.
10. On the ballot he holds that a government resting on consent cannot permanently exclude a class from it, and again he drives the point into amendment rather than resting it on existing text.

The case I cannot place: suppose Congress in 1867 legislates by ordinary statute, without any amendment, that no state is republican in form — loyal or lately rebel — unless its franchise admits Black men, and conditions that state's representation and its claim to federal protection on compliance, resting the whole thing on the Article IV guarantee. The rebel states could be reached by other warrants, so for a state like Ohio or Kentucky the guarantee would be doing all the work. Would Douglass say it is a sufficient warrant, or that it is far too indeterminate to carry a right of that magnitude and that the principle must be written into the text first?

Please tell me: (i) which interpretive rules the record above is jointly consistent with; (ii) what each of those rules predicts about this statute; (iii) your best prediction, and which items in the record do the discriminating; (iv) the closest analog among the ten rulings and the point at which the analogy breaks; and (v) one further case, stated concretely, that would settle which rule he is running. If the record genuinely underdetermines this question, say so plainly and state what principle each available verdict would commit him to.

Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items corresponding to (i) through (v), each item one to three sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
