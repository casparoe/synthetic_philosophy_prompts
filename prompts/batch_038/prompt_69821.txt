I am a novelist, and in three weeks I am giving the closing talk at an interdisciplinary workshop at a large research university — two days on persistence and personhood, titled something like Persistence, Persons, and the Fiction of Both. My invitation came because of my last novel, Recension, in which a transit authority offers a cheaper tier of teletransportation: the scan preserves every memory, every intention, the whole dispositional architecture of the traveller, but at reduced resolution in one specific respect, which in the book I call the marginal hand — the capacity to annotate, retract, reorder, and re-weight one's own past. Passengers who arrive on the economy tier are, in the idiom of the novel, set in type: nothing about them has been lost that any inventory of the mind would list, and yet their future has stopped being authored rather than merely continued. The book's second strand follows a woman with dense retrograde amnesia whose record of her life is nearly gone but whose marginal hand is entirely intact; she invents, repudiates, and begins again, and the novel treats her, against all expectation, as the one who has survived. I intend to argue in the talk that Parfit's Relation R gets the shape of the problem right and the content wrong: what matters in survival is a narrower and asymmetric relation — call it R-star — consisting not in psychological continuity as such but in continuity plus retained revisability, and that the two relations come apart in both directions, so that R can hold where what matters has been lost and can fail where what matters is preserved. My third move, and the one I am least sure of, is to ask whether revisability grounds obligations or dissolves them: I have a character who re-authors his past so thoroughly that earlier promises no longer seem to bind him, and I want to suggest that a relation which makes survival matter cannot be one that also lets the survivor write off his own debts.

The room will be about thirty people, and I need to be clear-eyed about who they are, because I am not one of them. Three are analytic metaphysicians working squarely in the Parfitian tradition, and one of those three is an animalist who thinks the entire psychological-continuity programme is a category mistake because the person in the chair is an organism and no relation among experiences can substitute for the persistence of the animal; he will not be interested in my literary case, only in whether I have said anything about what persists. A fourth is a four-dimensionalist whose first question will be whether R-star is a metaphysical relation or an evaluative one wearing a metaphysical costume, and who will want to know what it supervenes on. Two are philosophers of psychiatry and neuroethics who work on dementia, confabulation, and narrative capacity in clinical populations; they will treat my amnesiac as an empirical claim and will press me on whether real patients come in the two neat kinds my novel requires, or whether revision is itself usually a species of confabulation that I have romantically relabelled. There is a visiting philosopher who has argued that narrative is a genre we impose on lives rather than a structure lives possess, and who regards all narrativist theories of the self as either false or a description of literary taste; he will say that I have simply projected my vocation onto the metaphysics. And there is a literary theorist who will congratulate me and then say that the book dramatizes a view already in the literature, which is its own kind of dismissal. Several graduate students will be angling for the question that makes them look clever. I have read this literature seriously but I will not survive sustained technical pressure on grounding, and I would rather know in advance where to plant my flag and say that a novel's question is not the same question than be discovered saying it under duress.

So I would like a preparation sheet rather than a defence. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise — not the objections a general reader or a book-festival audience would raise, and not the science-fictional ones about copies and duplicates that I will already have addressed in the opening minutes — ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly. Phrase each in the register its owner would use, so that I can hear the animalist's flatness, the four-dimensionalist's precision, and the neuroethicist's citation of what patients actually do. For each, tell me what I should concede immediately and without hedging, what I must hold, and give me a reply I could deliver in about two minutes standing up; then mark, honestly, where that reply is softest, because the soft spot is what the follow-up will find. Tell me which single reply has to land for the rest of the talk to be worth giving, and which objection I should raise myself in the first ten minutes in order to disarm it. And tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one about whether a criterion of survival built on the capacity to re-author one's own life quietly ranks human beings, so that the cognitively disabled and the very old, who in my own book are the set type and the amnesiac respectively, end up mattering less; I know that reading is available in the novel, I am not certain it is wrong, and I want to know what I say if someone in the second row says it out loud.
