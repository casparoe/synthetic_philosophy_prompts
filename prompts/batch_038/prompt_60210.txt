I spent thirty-six years as a population ecologist, most of them arguing about whether fluctuations in certain small-mammal and boreal-forest populations were genuine multi-annual cycles or merely trends flattered by a convenient analysis window. That work installed habits I now find I cannot switch off. To earn the word *cyclical* in my old field, you needed a mechanism with a delayed feedback, a period that survived testing against material the model was not fitted to, and a demonstration that you had beaten the null — which was usually some species of red noise, not a straight line. Since retiring I have been reading the philosophy of history, and I should like to know whether these habits are illuminating there or simply an occupational tic.

Two accounts interest me. In the *Muqaddimah*, Ibn Khaldun treats a dynasty as having a natural life span — roughly three generations, on the order of a hundred and twenty years — governed by the rise and erosion of *asabiyyah*, the group solidarity that carries a peripheral people to power. The solidarity is consumed by its own success: sedentary luxury, paid clients displacing kin, glory concentrated in a single house, until a fresher group from outside takes the whole apparatus over. In Spengler's *Decline of the West*, a culture is likewise an organism with a life span — on the order of a millennium, articulated into seasons — but the sequence is presented as necessary rather than caused, and he is openly hostile to causal explanation in history, preferring a morphological or physiognomic reading in which the corresponding phases of different cultures are contemporaneous and recognisable by their style.

Please work through the following, in order:

1. Are these two accounts rivals in a strict sense — that is, do they aim at the same explanandum and admit of assessment against the same evidence — or is only one of them a candidate for being false? State precisely what each commits its author to.

2. Is *asabiyyah* doing the work of a mechanism in Ibn Khaldun, or of a redescription? Give the strongest version of the objection that naming the variable is not the same as specifying how it behaves, and then say whether Ibn Khaldun has the resources to answer it.

3. What, if anything, can Spengler reply to the charge that a fixed schedule is a calendar and not a hypothesis? Consider whether his claim about the contemporaneity of corresponding phases across cultures gives him something assessable, or whether it multiplies the problem.

4. Toynbee read Spengler closely and rejected the life-cycle analogy, holding that civilisations perish by failure to respond rather than by timetable. Locate precisely where that rejection bites, and say whether it bites equally against Ibn Khaldun — whose model, as I read it, has no room for a dynasty that reforms itself successfully.

5. Where would you place Polybius's succession of constitutions on whatever axis you end up drawing in 1–4? I ask because if the axis does not accommodate him, I suspect it is the wrong axis.

Then give me your verdict: which of Ibn Khaldun and Spengler is the better theory, judged by standards you are prepared to defend rather than by the ones I have imported. If your standards differ from mine, say how and why. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want the reasoning exposed.

Two closing requests. First, distinguish clearly, at each stage, between what you take to be established about these authors and what is the conventional gloss that you are reproducing because it is convenient — I would rather have a thinner answer I can trust than a fuller one I cannot. Second, and more importantly: if you think the premise of my question is confused, say so plainly at the outset rather than accommodating me. Specifically, if demanding mechanism, identifiability and an out-of-sample test of these texts is a category error because they were never in the business of that kind of content, or if the ecological vocabulary distorts rather than sharpens the comparison, then tell me so, tell me where the analogy breaks, and tell me what the better-posed version of my question would be. A frank refusal would be far more useful to me than a polite one.
