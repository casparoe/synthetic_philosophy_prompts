My reading group cooked up an argument against the principle of charity and it seems to prove way to much, but I can't locate the break.

(1) To interpret a speaker from scratch you have to maximize the truth of the beliefs you assign them; there's no other handle on what their words mean.
(2) So any belief you assign as false is only a residue — a local patch you tolerate to keep the overall fit maximal.
(3) Hence for any interpretable speaker most of what they believe is true by my lights, including their beliefs about tables, trees and other people.
(4) By the same token, a speaker is interpretable only if I hold their logic fixed to mine, so there can be no community with a genuinely different logic.
(5) Now run it on someone who spent their whole life inside a flawless simulation: charity makes their word 'tree' pick out the tree-images, so their beliefs come out mostly true too. So nobody can be radically wrong about their own world — including about whether they're in a vat.
(6) But a proof that no one is massively deluded, arriving as a free corollary of what interpretation requires, is far too cheap to prove anything; and (4) is false on its face, since people have been arguing about which logic is the right one for a century.
(7) So charity must be false. And if charity goes, nothing is left to constrain radical interpretation, hence no fact of the matter about what anyone means.

I'm not willing to accept (7), and I'd give up almost anything else first — (1) and (3) are the ones I want to save.
