I hand-paint signs for a living — shop fronts, menu boards, the occasional wedding board — and I've got stuck on a question I can't get out of my head. What actually makes a mark an *a*? Not a good one, a *legitimate* one.

It started because my daughter was learning to write and she asked why the a in her reading book and the a I write in cursive are supposed to be the same letter. They're not remotely alike. Hers is the two-storey job — little hood on top, closed bowl underneath, that flick at the bottom right. Mine is one storey: a circle with a straight stick down the right-hand side. There's a word for two different shapes counting as one letter, I'm fairly sure, but I can't dig it up.

So I started keeping a list of my own verdicts, and the list doesn't behave.

- The two-storey a and the one-storey a: both a's, no hesitation, and yet they share hardly anything. A roundish body with a straight bit on the right — which is also what you get if you take a **d** and cut the tall stem down to normal height. And that trimmed d, I'd say instantly: that's an a now, different letter entirely.
- But here's the one that wrecks me. If I draw an a and then show it in a mirror, I still call it an a — a backwards one, but an a. Yet if I mirror a **d** I don't see a weird d, I see a **b**. Same operation, totally different result, and I'm completely unbothered by that until I try to explain it.
- A stencil a, with the little gaps cut into the strokes so the paint has somewhere to sit: still an a, obviously. In pieces, still an a.
- A fast italic a where I never close the bowl: still an a. Take away the top arc as well so it's just an o with a tail, and I start to lose it, and I cannot tell you where in that slide it stopped being one.
- A graffiti tag where the a is basically a triangle with a bar through it: read it in the middle of a word on a roller shutter and I get it instantly, no effort. Paint that identical mark on its own in the middle of a white page and I'd say it's a capital A, or a triangle. Same mark. Two answers from me.
- An old blackletter a I was copying off a pub sign. Could not read it at all until the landlord told me the word, and then I saw it, and now I can't unsee it. The shape didn't change once across that week. My verdict did.
- Wedding-invitation lettering where the designer has turned the bowl of the a into a leaf. My gut says that's a *picture* of an a, not an a — but a plain open-bowled a that's merely ugly is still an a, just a bad one. So "counts as an a" and "is a decent a" come apart, and I don't know if the leaf case fails on the first question or only the second. This is the one I go round and round on.
- A Greek alpha in a maths book: same letter as an a in some historical sense, obviously descended from it, but I would never accept it standing in for an a in an English word. So "same letter" is doing two seperate jobs there too.

The theory I keep sliding back into, despite all of the above, is the skeleton one: strip a letterform down to its stick figure, and any shape with the a-stick-figure is an a. Which can't be right, because the one-storey and two-storey a have different stick figures and the blackletter one has a stick figure no other a has, and they're all a's.

So: is there a principle — or a small handful of competing ones — that would reproduce as many of these verdicts as possible, stated sharply enough that I could apply it to a brand new shape without secretly just consulting my gut? For each part of it, tell me which of my cases forced that part in. Then run it on some cases I haven't thought of (the two-storey a rotated ninety degrees; an a with the bowl filled in solid so there's no hole left; a child who writes every a with the bowl on the wrong side, consistently) and give me one further case of your own that would decide between the candidates if they disagree. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it has to throw out — I'd rather lose two verdicts and have a rule than keep them all and end up with eleven exceptions. One last thing I can't articulate: I suspect it matters whether you're allowed to look at the rest of the alphabet when you make the call, or only at the shape in isolation. If that's the crux, say so and tell me which side you're on.
