I'm in a second-year rational choice course and we did the Nash bargaining solution this week, but the thing I actually want to ask about is something I keep running into outside class that I don't have a word for.

Three cases. (1) Splitting rent with a roommate: our disagreement point was basically "we both move out," and that never changed, but the more my roommate talked up a gorgeous studio she flat-out could not afford, the bigger my share got. (2) A negotiation exercise in another class: one team put an option on the table that nobody could realistically end up with, and the final compromise moved toward them anyway. (3) A friend negotiating a starting salary who mentioned a dream job at a place that almost certainly wasn't going to hire her, and the number went up.

So the pattern is: the outcome shifts because one side's best *conceivable* payoff went up, even though that payoff isn't reachable, the walkaway situation is unchanged, and nobody is actually being fooled about the odds. It doesn't feel like anchoring to me, because an anchor is just a number with no relation to what's on offer — here the fantasy has to be a real option in the space, just a better-for-me one. And it isn't a BATNA or outside option either, because those change what you get if you leave, and leaving didn't get better.

Is there an established concept for this, in bargaining theory or in the empirical negotiation literature? I half-suspect it's the ideal point in the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, but that's a normative axiom about how you *should* split, and there the ideal point is read off the feasible set rather than off someone's imagination, so I don't think it can be the same thing. Lay out the nearest candidates, how each differs from what I described, and which one you'd actually bet on — don't hedge evenly across all of them. If nothing fits, coin a term with boundary cases. And please push back if the whole framing is muddled: if I'm just describing anchoring with extra steps, or sliding between a solution concept and a psychological mechanism, say that plainly instead of playing along.
