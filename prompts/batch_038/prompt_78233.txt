**DRAFT — "The Dye, the Valve, and the Pump: Why the Institutions–Geography Debate Is Not a Causal Question"**

**1. The shape of the dispute.** For three decades the question of why some nations are rich has been run as a horse race among three entrants: institutions, geography, and culture. Since the late 1990s the race has been run mainly on the terrain of identification. The complaint against ordinary cross-country regressions was that they cannot separate cause from consequence, because rich countries can afford good institutions; the proposed remedy was to find something that moves institutions but is otherwise unrelated to income. The most influential candidate was the mortality rate suffered by European settlers in the colonies, which is said to have determined whether colonists arrived in large numbers and built institutions that protected property, or ran extractive enterprises designed to be looted before the looters died of fever. The subsequent history of that instrument is itself part of my argument. A critic who went back to the archival sources reported that for a majority of the countries in the sample the mortality figure had been assigned from another country's records, frequently on citations that were mistaken or misleading, and that under corrected data the results weakened or disappeared; the original authors replied that the proposed recodings conflated soldiers on campaign with civilian settlers and that the estimates survived. Whatever one makes of the exchange, it shows that the load-bearing measurement is a patchwork of archival fragments, and that the dispute over it is unresolvable by further regression.

**2. Five steps.**

*Step 1.* The regressor is not a substance. In these designs "institutions" is typically an index of expropriation risk compiled for investors, reflecting the perceived likelihood that the state will take assets without adequate compensation. Such a rating moves with income, with political stability, and with expectations of growth. The variable whose causal power is being estimated is therefore partly an effect of the outcome it is invoked to explain.

*Step 2.* The instrument belongs to the other camp. Settler mortality is, in the end, a measure of a disease environment. The exclusion restriction requires that variation in that environment affect present-day income only through institutions; but the same variation plausibly affects income through malaria prevalence, agricultural yields, and adult health. Adding controls for these often leaves the estimate standing, but it concedes the deeper point: the design is the horse race again, and the winner is chosen by the control set.

*Step 3.* The complier problem. Instrumental variables identify a local effect — the effect for those units whose treatment was in fact moved by the instrument. Here the compliers are the colonies where European settlement was feasible enough to respond to mortality but not so easy as to be automatic: a band defined entirely by ecological conditions. Because the complier set is selected on geography, the estimated institutional effect is biased upward in magnitude; and in any event the design cannot be neutral between the two camps, since it asks what institutions do inside a subsample carved out by geography.

*Step 4.* The ordering problem. The three channels were not simultaneous. Geography acted first, on the pattern of settlement; institutions next, through the colonial state; culture, if it is a distinct channel at all, last and slowly, through the habits of populations formed by those institutions. Entering all three in one cross-sectional regression and reading the coefficients as causal shares silently treats them as co-present causes competing to explain a common variance. They are stages of a single sequence. And when three channels operate in sequence, entering them together attributes to the earliest the whole of the later channels' effects, because the earliest is the only one that is predetermined.

*Step 5.* The decomposition is order-relative. Where channels interact, the share assigned to each depends on the order in which they are introduced — just as splitting a between-group gap into an "explained" and an "unexplained" part yields different numbers depending on which group is taken as the reference. A decomposition whose parts shift with the analyst's ordering is not a measurement of anything in the world.

**3. Three roles, not three rivals.** A hydraulic system has a pump that generates the flow, a valve that governs it, and a dye that makes it visible. I want to suggest that the literature has systematically confused these roles. Geography is the pump: originative, and now out of reach. Institutions are the valve: not where the divergence came from, but the only thing whose movement could close it. The measured index of expropriation risk is the dye: it registers the flow, and it is what the regressions have actually been estimating. Each camp is right about the role it is describing and wrong to claim the other roles for it. The originative question ("what made the difference?") and the load-bearing question ("what would have to change now?") have different answers by construction, and no regression can rank them, because a coefficient on a mediator in a chain is a direct effect conditional on the pump — a quantity of interest only if one believes the pump can be held still.

**4. What could settle it, and what cannot.** Cross-country regression supplies counterfactuals about populations; history asks counterfactuals about events. The two never meet. The strongest available evidence is not cross-national but internal to a single ecology: jurisdictions divided by a line drawn through continuous terrain, with the same climate, soils, disease burden, and often the same language and kin networks on both sides, and different rules. There the pump is held fixed by construction and only the valve differs. It is also where the samples are far too small for the standard errors anyone would accept.

**5. Conclusion.** "Which of institutions, geography, and culture causes wealth?" is malformed. What produces wealth is a sequence, and what can be estimated is the effect of intervening on particular links in it, for those units that respond to the interventions we happen to be able to instrument. The honest summary of this literature is not a ranking of channels but an inventory of levers and their reach.
