Context. My friend is a legal historian and we have just spent five hours on a reconstruction he built out of Pascal, and we reached an impasse that neither of us can dissolve. He calls the argument the ceremonial regress and its conclusion the settlement thesis. His reading of Pascal, which I do not contest for present purposes, is roughly this: justice without force is inert and force without justice is tyrannical, so the two must be joined; and since we cannot make what is just strong, we have instead made what is strong just. Custom is the entire foundation of equity, for no other reason than that it is accepted, and anyone who tries to trace law back to first principles destroys it. And what secures that acceptance is not the content of the norms but the apparatus around them — the robes, the guards, the magistracy's accumulated record of application — because opinion is what governs the world, but force makes use of opinion.

Here is his argument, as I have tried to set it out faithfully:

1. A norm imposes obligations on those subject to it only if those subjects take it to impose obligations on them.
2. For any subject who takes a norm to impose obligations, what produces that attitude is not the norm's content or its merits but the apparatus attached to the norm: the enforcement practices, the standing displays of office, the record of prior application.
3. If A produces S's taking N to impose obligations, and S's taking N to impose obligations is required for N to impose obligations on S, then it is A that makes N obligatory for S.
4. Therefore every norm is obligatory only in virtue of some apparatus. (From 1–3.)
5. No apparatus is itself a norm. An enforcement practice and its ceremonial surround are not the sort of thing that can be taken to impose obligations; they operate, they do not bind.
6. So nothing is obligatory in virtue of a norm at any level; the only thing doing work throughout is the operation of the apparatus, that is, force.
7. Therefore legal obligation is force that has settled into a stable pattern, and justice is the name we give to such a pattern.
8. Stability and generality are matters of degree, not of kind: a settled pattern of force differs from a standing threat only in duration and reach.
9. Therefore no established law is unjust and no unenforced norm is law. The settlement thesis is true.

I cannot accept (9). I am confident that some enacted laws are unjust and that saying so is not merely reporting a clash between my preferences and the local pattern of force. He agrees that (9) is what I must reject, and then presses me with the following: every step above is either something Pascal argues for or an inference from what precedes it, so if I reject (9) I must reject something earlier; and the earlier candidates are exhausted. Denying (2) is just empirical obstinacy. Denying (8) begs the question, since the point of (8) is that I have no principled place to draw a line of kind between a settled pattern of coercion and a threat. Denying (3) or (4) is denying logic. That leaves (1) and (5), and he insists that dropping (5) is not available to me, because (5) is just the observation that apparatuses are not the sort of thing that can be believed in. So, he says, I must give up the acceptance condition, and then I owe him an account of what makes a norm bind that does the explanatory work (1) was doing.

Two cases we argued over and did not resolve, and I would like your diagnosis to tell me what the argument is actually committed to in them.

Second, what he calls the hollow state: total surveillance-driven compliance in which no one, including the enforcers, takes the norms to impose obligations. He says his argument delivers that such a state is fully lawful. I say it instead delivers that nothing binds there, since (1) is unsatisfied and (1) is supposed to be a necessary condition. We never settled which reading his own chain supports, and he said that whichever way it falls, the case is a vindication rather than a reductio.
