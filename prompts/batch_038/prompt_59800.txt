A friend of mine — an old poker player who spent some years at it for a living, and who reads more carefully than his job title suggested — recently wrote up a long post on the Kelly criterion and sent it to me before publishing. I have no formal training in any of this. I have been reading about probability and betting on my own for a couple of years, mainly to work out how large a stake to put down in situations where I believe I have an edge. Here is the core of his post, condensed but I hope not distorted:

"Kelly betting is what you get when you take expected value seriously and apply it to a whole sequence of decisions instead of a single one. The rule — stake the fraction (bp − q)/b of your bankroll, where p is your chance of winning, q your chance of losing, and b the odds offered — is the choice that makes the logarithm of your wealth grow as fast as possible. Because wealth compounds multiplicatively rather than additively, maximizing that growth rate is the same thing as maximizing expected wealth; people who insist the two come apart are doing arithmetic on a problem that is geometric. The rule also settles the question of ruin. Stake the Kelly fraction and you are always left holding some positive portion of your bankroll, so no streak of losses, however improbable, can ever wipe you out. Nor is the rule merely the best performer on average: across a long enough run of bets, the Kelly bettor's wealth exceeds that of every other bettor with probability one, whatever fractions of their bankrolls the others choose. That is why the criterion does not depend on your being able to repeat the gamble — the growth-rate argument constrains a single wager just as firmly as it constrains a thousand. The only objection worth taking seriously is that you have to know the true odds and nobody ever does; but that is an objection to decision theory in general rather than anything peculiar to Kelly, and in practice you simply plug in your best estimate."

I suspect the post is more right than wrong, and I am inclined to think its critics are squeamish about large swings rather than actually confused. Two things snag for me anyway, and I cannot tell whether they are real problems or my own ignorance. First, I have been staking something like half of what the formula tells me for years, and I have never been able to articulate a reason for it beyond a vague fear that I have overestimated my edge; if my friend is right, I have been giving up growth for no principled reason at all. Second, "with probability one" is doing a great deal of work in that paragraph and I have no way to judge how much it can bear.

So please go through the passage claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on this subject would accept without comment, what is a simplification that costs nothing, and what is wrong. For each part that is wrong, give me the correct statement and then identify which step in my friend's own reasoning generated the error — I want the mechanism, not merely the verdict. Then tell me whether the two headline conclusions, that ruin is impossible and that the Kelly bettor finishes ahead of everyone else with probability one, survive once the faulty premises are repaired, or whether repairing the premises dissolves them. I would also like to know what the repaired version implies about my half-sized stakes: is that a superstition, or is it the right response to something the post waved away?

Finish with a committed judgment rather than a balanced one: is this, overall, a fair summary of the Kelly criterion, and if it is not, which single error does the most damage to the argument as a whole?
