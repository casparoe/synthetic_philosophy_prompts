I'm in the middle of a novel about a staking operation — the sort where a backer puts up all the money and a young poker player grinds it out for a cut — and I've been talking to people in that world to get the details right. What keeps striking me is how much of a whole moral and mathematical system is baked into the paperwork, and I can't tell which bits are load-bearing and which are just custom.

The arrangement as it's usually described to me: the backer fronts the bankroll, takes half the winnings, the player keeps half. If the player loses, the loss isn't wiped — it goes on the books as 'makeup', and the player doesn't see a penny of their share until makeup is cleared. Makeup survives the end of a session, a month, sometimes the end of the deal itself: if the player wants to walk and sign with another backer, the debt has to be bought out first. Alongside that, players are told constantly never to risk the roll — one or two per cent a game, and if you drop below a threshold you move down in stakes rather than up. But the same people will tell you to take a shot at a game you can't really afford when a rich fish sits down, and they'll say the reason its fine is that you're not risking your own money, you're risking the backer's.

What does that whole package presuppose about what it is to bet well? I keep getting told it's the Kelly criterion in practice — bet the fraction of your roll that makes it grow fastest over the long run, and never risk going bust, because a bust ends the growth story. But makeup looks like it changes the story: nobody goes bust, they just owe, indefinitely, and the person doing the owing keeps playing. So is the rule about the money or about the person? And how can never risk the roll and take the shot both be right unless the two are measuring different things, or unless somebody's long run is not somebody else's?

Can you reconstruct the theory of rational betting the staking world is actually running on, and tell me what it commits my characters to that I hadn't noticed — in particular whether it's a view about long-run growth, a view about utility, or a view about who a player is across time, and whether the long run it appeals to is something a single mortal person ever gets to stand in for. I'd like to know which parts of the practice strictly require that theory and which would sit just as happily under a rival one, plus where the practice is simply underdetermined, so I don't put a contradiction in someone's mouth that isn't really there. And if you swapped it for a straightforward expected-utility view of a person who's going to die in fourty-odd years and can't reinvest forever, what would these contracts have to look like instead — would makeup still make any sense?

British English please. Don't worry about keeping it short, this is for my notes rather than the book.
