I've been going back and forth with my editor about a sidebar for a column I'm finishing on how the free-energy principle escaped the neuroscience lab and turned into a theory of everything -- minds, cells, markets, culture, the works. The reported part is fine, but she wants one boxed thing at the end that's actually funny, so people don't bail at paragraph nine. So: can you write me a deadpan policy memo, roughly 750 words, from an invented "predictive governance" consultancy (give it the blandest name you can manage) to an invented city council, proposing that the city formally adopt expected free energy minimisation as its operating principle. It should read like a Swiftian modest proposal -- the firm never breaks character, never winks, and follows the framework's own logic to somewhere the reader recoils from.

The jokes have to come from getting the technical content right, not from making the theorists stupid. Some threads you can pull on: the memo proposes a public "blanket register" where every resident has to be assigned a Markov blanket, and the resulting administrative chaos is really the familiar objection that the blanket is up to the modeller -- a married couple get registered as a single agent so their arguments become "internal self-prediction error," a man successfully petitions to have his dog folded into his own boundary, and the consultancy turns out to be nested blankets all the way down, which conveniently means nobody at the firm is ever accountable. Have them reduce prediction error not by fixing anything but by turning down the precision on inconvenient signals -- pothole complaints, the noise hotline, the water quality sensors -- which is a legitimate move in the formalism and a genuinely horrifying one in a city. Have expected free energy's split into epistemic and pragmatic value become a budget line, with "curiosity" rationed and the arts defunded on the grounds that they generate surprise without pragmatic payoff. And the centrepiece should be the dark room problem: someone on the council asks the obvious question about why the city shouldn't just shut everyone in a windowless room, the firm gives the actual standard answer about priors over outcomes encoding an adaptive phenotype -- and then, in the next paragraph, quietly proposes a "phenotype amendment" letting the city edit those priors instead, at which point the policy can be made to justify anything at all. I'd also like a line where the firm boasts that no outcome could ever count against the programme, since a failed prediction just means the blanket was drawn wrong.

Please don't put words in any real researcher's mouth -- invent the consultants, invent the council members, no verbatim quotes from real papers and no citation-dropping, since I'll be checking and my readership includes at least two computational psychiatrists who enjoy dunking. One boxed sidebar inside the memo where the firm answers a citizen's written objection in language that is technically correct and completely chilling. And then, out of character at the end, give me five or six lines saying which bits are real features of the framework and which ones I've stretched, so I know what I can defend if this gets quoted back at me.
