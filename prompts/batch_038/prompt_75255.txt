I run a small formalization project: two graduate students, a proof assistant in which excluded middle and choice are not in scope by default and every use must be declared as an explicit axiom, and a standard undergraduate real-analysis sequence to port — completeness of the reals, the intermediate value theorem, uniform continuity, Bolzano–Weierstrass, the Riemann integral. Several times a week a student brings me a theorem that refuses to port, and my response is ad hoc: sometimes I let them assume excluded middle, sometimes I make them restate the result in an ε-approximate form, sometimes I send them off to hunt for a countermodel. The decisions are inconsistent, and nobody can tell me which downstream lemmas have quietly inherited a classical principle.

Write the triage procedure I should hand them, runnable in under half an hour on a candidate theorem before it reaches my desk. I want an ordered sequence of tests with a definite verdict at each: is the conclusion stable under double negation, does the classical argument split cases on a predicate that is undecidable for reals, must a witness be extracted from a doubly negated existential, and so on through the negative translation, Markov's principle, and the limited principles of omniscience. For each test, say what counts as a settled answer and what failure looks like. Where the procedure terminates it should land on one of four outcomes — port as stated, port with the hypothesis strengthened to decidability or locatedness, restate approximately, or add a named principle to the file's axiom list — and where it should instead escalate to me, because the question is which foundations the library is committed to rather than what the theorem says. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls in procedural drag; I know that Cauchy versus Dedekind reals is an architectural choice no checklist resolves.

Then run it on three cases: the intermediate value theorem; the claim that every real number is either nonnegative or nonpositive; and Bolzano–Weierstrass for bounded sequences. At least one outcome should be one my students will resent. Finish with what the procedure cannot do — above all, whether it can catch a principle smuggled in through a definition rather than a proof step, which is where Diaconescu's theorem makes me nervous about admitting choice anywhere. And take a position rather than surveying the trade-offs: pick the defaults, say which principles you would ban from the library outright and which you would permit behind a flag, and defend those choices. No bibliography; I want your reasoning, not your references.
