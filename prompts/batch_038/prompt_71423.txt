I am in the second year of a philosophy of economics doctorate, and for the past month I have been reading Hume's essays on money, interest and the balance of trade alongside various central bank strategy reviews, an exercise my supervisor considers mildly unhealthy. What has lodged itself in my head is not a point about Hume but a point about the practice: the near-universal institutional arrangement in which an advanced-economy central bank publishes a numerical inflation target of about two per cent, steers towards it by setting a short-term policy rate rather than by controlling any monetary aggregate, pays interest on the reserves commercial banks hold with it, communicates its likely future path in order to move expectations, and treats mild ongoing inflation not as a failure but as a buffer to be deliberately maintained. Since 2008 several of these banks have bought government bonds on a scale that multiplied the monetary base without producing a comparable movement in consumer prices for the better part of a decade, and then inflation arrived in 2021 and 2022 anyway, to general surprise; one major central bank responded by redefining its target as an average to be made up over time. My question is what theory of money this whole apparatus presupposes. Which rival accounts does it quietly exclude — a strict quantity theory on which the price level stands in a determinate proportional relation to the money stock, a real-bills doctrine, a view on which money is neutral at every horizon, or a view on which the international distribution of money equilibrates itself without anyone's management? And is the practice coherent, or does it consist of an assertion of long-run neutrality bolted onto an assertion of short-run non-neutrality, with an instrument that has migrated from a quantity to a price?

The reason Hume will not leave me alone is that his account seems to make the interval the whole point. As I read him, money is not itself one of the objects of commerce but the instrument men have agreed upon to facilitate exchange, its value being fixed by its quantity relative to the commodities and industry available to be circulated; and yet the benefit of an increase in the quantity of money lies entirely in the gap between the arrival of the new money and the completion of the adjustment of prices, because that gap is sequenced rather than simultaneous — it reaches merchants and undertakers before it reaches wages and the price of labour, and the profit thereby temporarily enlarged is what rouses industry. On this picture the wise magistrate tries to keep the stock of money increasing precisely in order to keep that transitional advantage running, and a diminishing stock is bad for the same reason in reverse. If that is right, then inflation targeting looks less like a rejection of Hume than like the institutionalisation of his interval: a permanent, engineered state of monetary disequilibrium, with the transition no longer a windfall from a gold inflow or a favourable balance of trade but a standing policy commitment, calibrated in percentage points. I want you to press on that thought rather than dismiss it. Does the practice presuppose something close to Hume's mechanism while officially denying that any such mechanism exists? Or is the analogy hollow, because in a fiat system with floating exchange rates there is no specie to flow and no automatic international adjustment to rely on, so that what Hume described as a self-regulating process has been replaced by something that only works insofar as people believe it does?

Please answer without references or a reading list; I want the argument, not the apparatus of citation, and I am perfectly happy for you to work from the characterisations of Hume I have just given, correcting me where you think I have got him wrong. Specifically, I would like: a reconstruction of the implicit theory of money that two-per-cent targeting presupposes, distinguished carefully from the theories it is merely compatible with; an account of which rival views the practice rules out and which it leaves standing, including whether a monetarist, a Keynesian and a quantity theorist could each endorse the same target for mutually inconsistent reasons, so that consensus about the practice masks the absence of consensus about its foundations; a verdict on whether Hume's interval is genuinely what the practice is built out of or only a suggestive redescription of it; and an account of what would have to change in central banking if the presupposed theory were replaced by a serious long-run neutrality, or by the bleaker possibility that the decade after 2008 revealed no stable relation at all between the central bank's operations and the price level. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the one point you are least sure of — I suspect it will be a claim about what Hume actually held rather than about what central banks actually do, but say which and why.
