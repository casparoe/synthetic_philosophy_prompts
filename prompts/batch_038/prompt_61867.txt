I work in the secretariat of a standing review body — call it the Certification Panel — that examines voluntary arrangements between public agencies and certifies some of them as safe Pareto improvements over inaction. The charter states the test as follows: an arrangement qualifies if the panel can specify a set of scenarios S, treated as exhausting the scenarios that are decision-relevant for the parties, such that in every scenario in S each party does at least as well under the arrangement as it would under inaction, and at least one party does strictly better. Arrangements that would leave the parties worse off than inaction in some scenario outside S may still be certified. The wrinkle is that the charter never says whose list of scenarios fixes S — the parties' own submissions, their overlap, or the panel's construction — and in practice the panel has been supplying it case by case.

I have to draft the secretariat's opinion on a new file, and I would rather know where the panel will land before I commit anything to paper. Our record to date is as follows, in each case an arrangement among two or more public agencies; "certified" means endorsed as a safe Pareto improvement, with or without conditions.

1. 2016, certified 9–0. Two space agencies pooled collision-avoidance telemetry. Each submitted a scenario list; the lists proved coextensive, and pooling was at least as good for both in every listed scenario. The one scenario outside S — an agency withdrawing mid-year — left both marginally worse off than inaction and affected no one else. The minutes note only that the parties' lists happened to coincide, so the question of whose list governs did not arise.

2. 2018, rejected 7–2. Two agricultural agencies proposed a joint moratorium on field trials of a self-propagating microbicide. The moratorium was at least as good for both in every scenario on the union of their lists save one, which both listed as live but both put below one percent and the panel's own assessors at roughly 0.2 percent: emergence of a resistant strain in a staple crop. The loss in that scenario was judged irreversible and to fall overwhelmingly on several hundred million people who were not parties. The two dissenters argued that a probability that low should not defeat an otherwise clean improvement.

3. 2019, certified 5–4, with conditions. Two regulators shared a deprecation schedule for legacy control software. Exactly one scenario was unsafe, and only one of the two regulators listed it as live; the other called it impossible. The harm was confined to the parties and recoverable. The majority certified on the ground that the safe set need only cover scenarios the parties hold in common, and attached an exit clause allowing either regulator to suspend the schedule on thirty days' notice. The dissent held that a scenario one party takes seriously cannot be struck from S by the other's disbelief.

4. 2021, rejected 6–3. Two firms proposed a reciprocal escrow of source code, released to the other upon detection of cheating. The union of the parties' lists was fully covered. The panel itself raised the unsafe scenario: the escrow's licensing terms would foreclose an unaffiliated third developer from independent work on the same standard. The majority rejected because the arrangement imposed costs on non-parties. The dissent answered that the charter's test speaks only of the parties' payoffs and that the panel had no business widening S on its own initiative.

5. 2022, certified 8–1. A reciprocal no-first-use understanding concerning offensive cyber tooling. On the panel's own assessment the safe set carried about 97 percent of the probability; the residual exposure was confined to the parties, bounded, and could be unwound at their own expense. The minutes state that the panel does not demand certainty, only that residual exposure be bounded and unwound at the parties' cost.

6. 2023, rejected 6–3. Three port authorities proposed to share dredging equipment. The arrangement was safe under every scenario the parties listed and under everything the panel added, but implementation required a jointly owned, physically integrated facility that could not be dismantled or re-divided once built. The majority rejected, holding that reversibility is a precondition of certification rather than a condition the panel may attach afterward, and distinguished the 2019 file on precisely that point.

The new file. Three national health agencies propose a commingled stockpile with a rotation schedule for a countermeasure with a short shelf life. In every scenario on the union of the three agencies' lists but one, each agency does at least as well as under inaction and at least one does strictly better. The exception is listed as live by Agency C alone: a supply shock originating in a fourth country that is not a party, which would strand C's contributions in the rotation. C puts this at 4 percent; the other two agencies regard it as remote; the panel's assessors put it at 3 percent. Separately, the panel's technical staff added a scenario no party listed, in which the protocol's standardization of one formulation forecloses a non-party manufacturer's competing product; staff judge that manufacturer's exit from the market permanent, and the loss falls on it rather than on any agency. On the panel's own assessment the safe set carries roughly 94 percent of the probability — below the 2022 file, above the 2018 file. Any agency may suspend the schedule on sixty days' notice, but once stock is physically pooled, individual contributions cannot be re-identified or returned: exit is available prospectively, restitution is not. No agency faces catastrophic or irreversible loss; the harm in the unsafe scenarios is concentrated on C, recoverable within one season, and on the non-party manufacturer, permanent.

Taking that record as given — I am not asking for the surrounding literature or for citations of any kind — please tell me:

(a) what candidate rules the six verdicts are jointly consistent with, given that more than one fits; in particular, whether the panel has been treating S as the intersection of the parties' scenario lists, their union, or its own construction, and whether the probability remarks, the non-party remarks, and the reversibility remarks are independent rules or glosses on a single one;

(b) what each surviving rule predicts for the new file, and where two of them pull in opposite directions on it;

(c) your best guess at the vote and at the reasoning the majority will write, with the reasons for preferring that reading over the alternatives;

(d) which of the six files is the closest analog and exactly where the analogy strains;

(e) what principle the panel would be adopting for future files if it certifies, and what different principle if it rejects — stated so that a member can see the commitment she is making rather than the outcome she is reaching;

(f) one further hypothetical file, concrete enough that I could put it to a member and get a verdict, that would discriminate between the two strongest candidate rules; and

(g) if you think the record is better read as no single rule at all — a shifting coalition rather than a doctrine — say so and identify the fault line along which the panel splits.
