I sit on the design committee for the content-appeals body of a mid-sized platform. Disputed moderation calls are currently re-reviewed by a standing panel of fifteen trained reviewers. A proposal now before us would replace that panel, case by case, with a randomly drawn crowd of five thousand account-holders, on the grounds that scale beats expertise. The proposal's sponsor circulated a passage from a recent trade book on collective intelligence in support of it. The passage runs roughly as follows:

> In 1785 the Marquis de Condorcet proved something that has never been satisfactorily answered: that a majority vote of ordinary, uninformed people is a better truth-tracker than any expert you might put in their place, so long as each of those people is likelier to be right than wrong. The conditions are almost embarrassingly weak. Voters need not be knowledgeable, need not be well intentioned, and need not agree about anything except the question in front of them; they need only be more accurate than a coin flip, and arrive at their answers separately. Given that, the arithmetic is inexorable. Each additional voter can only improve the group, the majority's accuracy rises with every head you add, and as the group grows the probability that the majority is right approaches certainty. At fifty-two percent individual accuracy, a panel of a few hundred is right almost every time. What does the work is that error is random, and randomness cancels: bias, inattention, and self-interest, provided they pull different people in different directions, are washed out by the aggregation itself. This is why the theorem is usually taken to have ended a very old argument. Democracy's claim on us does not rest on consent, or fairness, or the dignity of participation; it rests on the fact that counting heads gets the answer right more often than any rival procedure, and that this advantage compounds with size. Condorcet's other famous result points the same way. He showed that majority rule can cycle, a group preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A; the lesson is that no ranking of the alternatives is safe from a large enough electorate, which is precisely why the only procedure that can claim reliability is the one that simply counts.

Two pieces of local evidence are in play. Our internal audit scored the trained panel at seventy-one percent agreement with a gold standard; a pilot crowd of three hundred scored fifty-four percent. The gold standard is the settled judgment of five senior policy staff, who reach it by majority vote among themselves. The sponsor reads the fifty-four percent as license to scale: above a coin flip, so the theorem does the rest.

The committee is split and I lack the background to adjudicate. I would like a claim-by-claim accuracy audit of the passage: separate what a social choice theorist would sign off on, what is a defensible simplification of a true result, and what is wrong, overstated, or simply a non sequitur. Flag anywhere the passage slides from a technical notion to its everyday lookalike, and anywhere a conditional result is reported as an unconditional one. Then tell me which of the errors actually bear on our decision and which are only the book being loose for effect. In particular: does the fifty-four percent pilot number mean what the sponsor says it means, given how our gold standard is produced and given what else the pilot tells us about how those three hundred people formed their judgments? And is my own instinct sound? I am sympathetic to the proposal, because the fifteen insiders all completed the same training program and work from the same guidelines, which strikes me as a textbook case of correlated error, whereas five thousand strangers drawn from the whole user base seem likelier to be genuinely diverse. I want to know whether that reasoning survives scrutiny or whether I have merely inverted the book's mistake.

Finish with a recommendation on the design that you would be willing to defend in front of the committee: panel size, how members should be selected, how their verdicts should be aggregated, and what we should do about the fact that our only measure of correctness is itself a small committee's vote. If the honest answer is that it turns on quantities we have not measured, name those quantities and say what pattern of results would settle the matter. I want a verdict, not a menu: where the passage is right, say so plainly; where it is wrong, say what is wrong and why; and commit to the design recommendation rather than laying out the options and leaving us to choose. Tell me also which sentences of the passage you would strike if you were editing it. I am not looking for references or a reading list — work from the argument and the numbers.
