So my town is redrafting its charter and for the first time we're going to have a citizen initiative process — people can get something on the ballot if they collect enough signatures. I'm on the design committee and a consultant handed us a menu of six possible features. We have to actually choose, and honestly half the committee doesn't have strong intuitions and thier patience for long meetings is gone.

Here's the menu, roughly as the deck described it:

A. Counterproposal pairing — if an initiative qualifies, the council drafts an alternative addressing the same problem, and the ballot asks three things: yes to the initiative, yes to the council's version, and if both pass, which one takes effect. (Apparently Switzerland does something like this.)

B. Citizen jury — 40 residents drawn at random from the voter roll, paid for three weekends of hearings and expert testimony, who publish a one-page recommendation that goes out with the official ballot pamphlet.

C. Single-subject rule — the clerk can reject or split any initiative that bundles more than one subject or tries to do two seperate things, with appeal to the courts.

D. Fiscal supermajority — if the official cost estimate puts the price above 2% of the annual budget, the measure needs 60% to pass instead of a simple majority.

E. Sunset re-ratification — anything passed by initiative expires after 8 years unless voters re-approve it, which requires a fresh signature drive.

F. Signature sampling — instead of checking every signature by hand, the clerk audits a random 400 and extrapolates; if the projected valid count clears the threshold with reasonable confidence, it qualifies.

The thing I actually care about is one specific failure mode: measures passing because nobody read them. A bunch of people vote yes on something with a nice-sounding title and a good slogan, and if those same voters had sat with the actual text for an hour they'd have rejected it. Call it low-information passage.

Rank A through F by how much you'd expect each to cut low-information passage in a town of about 60,000 that sees maybe three or four initiatives a year. Give each a score from -5 to +10 — negatives are allowed and encouraged, I genuinely suspect at least one of these backfires and makes the problem worse — plus a sentence or two on why it lands there. Then tell me which two you'd fight hardest for if we can only adopt two, and which one you'd bet against in front of the whole committee.
