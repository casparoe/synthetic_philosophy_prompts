Write a dialogue between two metaphysicians. One maintains that what makes a de re modal claim true is the existence, in some other possible world, of a distinct individual who bears an appropriate similarity relation to the individual in question — a counterpart — rather than the individual herself. The other maintains that numerically the same individual can exist in other possible worlds, and that a de re modal claim about her is made true by her own properties at those worlds.

The setting is a graduate seminar that has just been handed the following case and the following stipulation, and has asked the two of them what their respective views make of it.

The case. Vera is a cellist in the actual world. In world w1 there is a cellist who has all of Vera's memories, her bowing technique, the scar on her left hand, and her temperament, but whose body is composed of entirely different matter and who was born to different parents on a different continent. In world w2 there is a person built from the very same matter, molecule for molecule and in the same arrangement, and with the same parentage as Vera, but whose psychological life is nothing like Vera's: she is a reclusive apiarist who has never touched an instrument.

The stipulation. For the sake of argument the seminar adopts a version of the counterpart-theoretic view on which the counterpart relation is functional: for any individual at any world and any world whatever, there is at most one counterpart of that individual at that world.

The dialogue should work through three questions. First, what must each view say about whether the cellist at w1, the apiarist at w2, both, or neither, bears the relevant relation to Vera, and what does each theorist owe by way of a principled account of that answer rather than an appeal to intuition about the case? Second, does the functional constraint actually rescue the counterpart theorist from the familiar trouble that arises when a single world contains two competing candidates — the trouble of being unable to say that Vera could not have been two people, or that she must have some property she has in every world where she is represented — or does the constraint quietly turn the theory into a notational variant of transworld identity supplemented by a rule for selecting which individual at each world represents Vera? Third, can the transworld identity theorist meet the demand for an account of what makes it the case, if it is the case, that the apiarist rather than the cellist is Vera herself, given that identity is supposed to be all-or-nothing while the resources being appealed to are matters of degree?

Both characters should be fully competent advocates: no strawmen, no conceding points they have a good answer to, and no alternating monologues. Each should respond to the actual content of the other's previous remark, and at least once each should press an objection the other has not anticipated.

Do not end in a draw. Commit: by the close, one of them should have gained real ground on a specific question, and it should be evident which exchange secured it. After the dialogue, add a short note in your own voice identifying the move you take to have been decisive and explaining why, and flagging any place where you have taken liberties with either doctrine or leaned on a contested reading of it.
