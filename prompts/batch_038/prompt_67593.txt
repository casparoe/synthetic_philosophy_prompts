I'm a staff writer covering science and ideas, and for the past year I've been sitting in, with permission, on the collecting committee of a mid-sized state historical society for a piece about who gets preserved and who gets left out of the record. Here is their track record so far. They accepted the papers of a Depression-era state highway engineer, including his survey photographs of the neighborhoods later razed for the interstate, on the grounds that the demolition was documented even though the displaced residents went unnamed. They declined a complete set of corporate records from a regional utility because the donor required personnel files be sealed for seventy-five years. They accepted, with conditions, a tenant union's digitized phone trees and rent-strike flyers, on condition that the union rather than the society control any future removal of names. They rejected a well-funded offer of a governor's wife's correspondence because her family insisted on reviewing quotations before publication. They accepted an oral-history project with widows of a mine disaster even though only a partial transcript index existed. And they turned down a set of plantation ledger books that arrived with no attempt to identify the people listed only by first name and age — a refusal the chair described as declining a finding aid in which the abstraction was itself the harm.

Next month they consider a cache of roughly forty thousand photographs taken by an anonymous staff photographer at a state psychiatric hospital between 1948 and 1971 and donated by the estate of a former administrator: many patients are identifiable by face, no consent is documented, and the surviving captions describe the subjects in the institution's own clinical vocabulary. Predict how the committee will vote and identify the closest analog among their earlier cases. Lay out the candidate rules that fit all six precedents jointly — I suspect both a "no donor control" rule and a "whose names survive" rule are compatible with everything they've done, and there may be others — say what each predicts for this cache, then commit to a single best guess with reasons. If the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what principle the committee would be adopting under each option, and name one further hypothetical acquisition that would discriminate between the rival rules.

Approach this as an expert in archival theory and the history of collecting practice. Think carefully: start by brainstorming which rules are consistent with every vote and which are quietly falsified by one of them, testing each candidate against all six before you settle on anything. Then separate explicitly what is settled professional practice from what is genuinely contested among people who work in the field — consent, privacy, and the revision of existing description are live arguments, and I don't want a contested position presented as consensus. Please write in American English.
