I chair the Classification and Reporting Subcommittee of a state department of public health's behavioral health data board. Beginning next fiscal year, the department's public dashboard will report prevalence for prolonged grief disorder, and county clinics, school-based health centers, and a new grief-care reimbursement pathway will all key eligibility off that same label. Two years ago our data governance charter was amended to require that any newly added reporting category be accompanied by a "classification feedback assessment": an analysis of how publishing the label, and wiring services to it, is likely to change the very population the label counts — in either direction. No one has ever written one, and I have to circulate the first before the board votes in three weeks.

The positions on the table are these. Our surveillance methodologist argues that once the category is public and reimbursable, the counted population shifts, so a prevalence series across years is not tracking a stable target; she wants publication postponed two reporting cycles, with service-utilization counts published in the meantime. A coalition of bereavement advocates and county clinicians argues the reverse: naming the condition is precisely what makes it visible, gets people entitlements, and gives people words for suffering that is currently waved off as ordinary grief — and postponing publication is itself an intervention that shapes who gets recognized. A third faction, including me, worries about the other loop: in a state that has just absorbed a mine collapse, a flood, and years of overdose deaths, publishing the category alongside a screening tool in primary care may convert culturally shaped, ordinarily resolving grief into a treated condition. Compounding this, the ICD-11 allows a shorter duration threshold for adults than the DSM-5-TR does, and we must pick one; our own back-coding suggests the choice moves the counted population by a wide margin.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the full board, whose members include two epidemiologists, a county behavioral health director, a hospice nurse, and a bereaved-parent advocate. In American English, in a formal memo register, roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words. It must:

1. Recommend a concrete decision — publish now, publish in modified form, delay, or substitute a different measure — with a time frame and the implementation steps attached to it.
2. Perform the classification feedback assessment seriously rather than gesturing at it: identify the specific channels through which the loop would run in our setting (screening in primary care, reimbursement incentives, advocacy, local media, self-identification at intake) and say which of them we can actually influence.
3. Judge whether prolonged grief disorder shows the marks of a diagnosis that comes and goes historically — a taxonomy ready to name it, a pool of symptoms already present in the population, a cultural setting that gives the condition meaning and standing, and something the diagnosis offers the person who receives it — and explain whether, if it does show those marks, that is a reason to withhold the count.
4. Confront the objection that the philosophical point here is not that looping classifications are false or pernicious: a classification whose objects respond to it can be entirely real, and creating it can enlarge what people are able to say and do about themselves. Weigh that against the surveillance-integrity worry instead of assuming that medicalizing grief is automatically a harm.
5. Resolve the duration-threshold choice and the comparability problem, and say whether a discontinuity note in the dashboard suffices or whether something structural — a fixed cohort, a criteria-anchored series, utilization-only reporting — is required.
6. Include a short monitoring plan stating what we would examine in year two to learn whether the loop ran as predicted, given that our own measurement is part of the loop.
7. Extract from the case a set of criteria a future subcommittee could apply to the next contested category, so this is not a one-off judgment.

Treat the strongest consideration on the losing side at length and honestly, and say why it does or does not carry the day. No reference list, no citations, no attributed quotations: argue from the structure of the problem and from the concepts themselves. You may invoke the distinction between classifications that leave their objects undisturbed and classifications whose objects respond to being classified, but do not spend words on exposition for its own sake — the board wants a decision it can vote on.
