I'm putting together the final for a third-year seminar on early modern political economy — about 30 students, mix of philosophy and econ majors — and I want one essay question on Hume's monetary essays that they can't answer just by reciting the price-specie-flow mechanism. They've read "Of Commerce," "Of Money," "Of Interest," and "Of the Balance of Trade," and half of them have clearly already got the story in their heads as a diagram: gold flows in, prices rise, exports fall, done. I want to break that.

Here's the case I've made up, and I want the question built around it — tighten the wording if you like but keep the details, because the details are doing all the work:

> A small kingdom on a silver standard. Overnight its money stock doubles: a single windfall shipment arrives and goes straight into the royal treasury. Over the next year the king spends all of it, on the wages of foreign mercenaries and on imported wine, plate, and silk for the court. None of the new silver passes through the hands of domestic farmers, weavers, or laborers. Domestic money wages and the prices of domestically produced goods do not move during that year. At the end of the year the kingdom's money stock is back where it started.

What I want students to have to do with this: (1) separate Hume's two distinct claims — that an increase in money enlivens industry in the short run, and that the stock of money is in the long run indifferent and self-correcting — and say what each of them needs in order to be true; (2) explain why in this case the silver leaves without the usual channel ever engaging, and say whether the kingdom's return to its original proportion counts as the mechanism working or as the mechanism being bypassed; (3) say what Hume would have to add — or which of his own considerations about why a country that has lost its money can recover it would have to be doing the work — for the mechanism to be automatic in anything like the diagram version. Then an optional bonus part on what changes if the windfall is convertible paper credit rather than silver.

The crux I'm after is that the mechanism quietly assumes something about how prices and foreign demand respond, and Hume never states it, and the case is designed so that the money flows out while that assumption is switched off.

Deliverables:

- The question exactly as it will appear on the paper: the case set out in no more than ~150 words, parts labeled, marks shown against each part.
- A model answer of about 750–800 words, written the way a strong student would write it in 50 closed-book minutes — exam-essay shape, not journal-article shape.
- A marking scheme out of 25 that a TA who didn't teach the course could apply mechanically: each must-hit move, what it's worth, and one line on how students typically blow it. Include at least one entry for an anachronism move (reaching for modern monetary vocabulary without connecting it back to anything Hume says).
- A closing paragraph on what seperates a first-class answer from a solid pass here, plus the single sentence that, if it turns up in a script, tells you the student hasn't understood the issue at all.

Two constraints. No citations, no bibliography, no further-reading list — I'll handle attributions myself. And don't put anything in quotation marks as if it came from Hume; paraphrase him throughout, since I won't be able to check quotes against the essays at speed. Please write the whole thing in American English, including the model answer.
