I'm one of seven trustees at a small foundation that gives out about £400k a year, and we're deadlocked on something I'd like a proper answer to before Thursdays meeting.

The money can go to one of two places. Option 1 spreads it across three towns, so every town gets a middling but certain grant. Option 2 puts nearly all of it into one town picked by lottery, and the other two get a token amount — so with option 2 each town has a one-in-three shot at something genuinely transformative and a two-in-three shot at almost nothing.

Our most forceful trustee has a procedure he thinks settles it. Don't ask the towns what they want, he says, because they'll each just say "pick us". Instead, have the affected people choose between the two options while pretending they don't yet know which town they'd turn out to be in. If from that position they'd all go for option 2, then option 2 is right — and he adds that this is exactly the same thing as just totting up everyone's expected benefit and taking the bigger sum. Two other trustees object in different directions and I can't tell whether either objection lands.

Objection A, from our accountant: you can't add people up. Benefit isn't a number, my satisfaction and yours aren't on the same scale, so "the bigger total" is meaningless, and the pretend-you're-not-you business is a conjuring trick that smuggles the conclusion into the setup.

Objection B, from a philosopher who's also on the board: fine, suppose the procedure works, but notice it only ever compares options before the draw is run. Its perfectly possible, she says, for everyone to prefer option 2 in advance and yet for option 1 to be what we should want in each of the ways things could turn out, once you look at how the money actually lands rather than at the odds. She thinks when those two come apart the second one is what matters. She wouldn't elaborate, which was infuriating.

So, three questions, and I want direct answers rather than a survey of the options.

1. Is the pretending-you-don't-know-which-town-you're-in step doing any real work? Does choosing from behind that sort of uncertainty generate anything that summing expected benefits wouldn't have handed you anyway — and if it doesn't, why does anyone bother telling the story?
2. Does objection A sink the whole thing, or is the accountant attacking something weaker than what we actually rely on? I'd like to know what we're minimally committed to when we say one option is better for these towns, in whatever thin sense has to hold for the argument to run at all.
3. Explain objection B properly, because I genuinely don't see how the two can come apart — surely "everyone prefers it in advance" just is a sum over the ways things could turn out, so how can the after-the-fact view disagree? If you think she's right, say what makes the disagreement possible and what we should write in the minutes. If you think she's wrong, say so and tell me why she'd think otherwise.

Please no reading list and no roll call of who argued what — I can't look anything up between now and Thursday and names won't help me in the room, just the reasoning. Write it in British English, since I'll be lifting bits of it straight into a document going to a UK funder.
