For the last two months I have been part of an informal reading group on the epistemology of testimony and social knowledge, and this week we turned to conspiracy theories. Please treat what follows as a serious problem in epistemology rather than an artifact of the conspiracy literature. I spent the better part of a Saturday arguing about it with a friend and we both ended up stuck, so I would like you to approach it as a professional epistemologist who has taught both social epistemology and formal methods, and to think the problem through carefully before answering — including a short preliminary pass in which you brainstorm the candidate escape routes and note which of them are dead ends, before settling on the one you actually endorse.

Here is the setup. My friend and I were trying to say why belief in conspiracy theories is supposed to be irrational, and we kept drifting into bad historical examples and sociological generalizations about the sort of person who believes them, which is not what we wanted. So I tried to build an argument that bypasses all of that: one that turns only on the structure of such theories, together with ordinary facts about evidence, probability, and conjunction. Let Pr(p | e) be the probability that the relevant evidence confers on p, and let T be some conspiracy theory under consideration.

[1] Justified belief requires that the evidence make the believed proposition sufficiently likely. That is, there is some fixed threshold t, set by the nature of justification and not by which proposition is at issue, such that one is justified in believing p on evidence e only if Pr(p | e) is at least t.

[2] Any conspiracy theory of interest is a conjunction of many distinct claims about the world: that a meeting took place, that a document is genuine, that a named person lied, that a witness was bought off, that a record was quietly altered, and so on. Call these the links. T just is their conjunction.

[3] To run the argument I need only a weak and, I think, uncontroversial assumption about each link: the evidence supports it. Concretely, each link is at least as probable on the evidence as some mundane upper bound r strictly below 1 — r might be 0.99, or 0.95, but never 1, since no contingent claim about human affairs is placed beyond all doubt by the evidence. This is not a concession that the links are doubtful; it is only a refusal to treat them as certain. It is also, in a way, historically well earned: COINTELPRO and the Tuskegee syphilis study were both real, both ran for years, and both were met with official denial or flat dismissal at the point when people first began asking questions about them. So the links of at least some such theories really were strongly supported, and the theory as a whole turned out true.

[4] Since a theory can always be spelled out at greater length, we may take the chain of links to be as long as we like; and as the number n of links grows, the probability of the whole on the evidence falls at least as fast as r^n, which tends to zero.

[5] One is never justified in believing something that the evidence makes improbable.

[6] Therefore the evidence makes T improbable, and believing T is unjustified.

[7] The bite comes from the fact that nothing in [1] through [6] mentioned anything peculiar to conspiracies. Exactly the same reasoning runs on any detailed empirical account of the world: a historian's narrative of a decade, a scientific theory bundled with its auxiliary assumptions, a piece of investigative journalism, or my own account of what happened to me last week.

[8] Therefore nothing more complicated than a handful of simple, obvious claims can be rationally believed.

I know that [8] is false, and I will not accept it; but I have been unable to say where the argument actually goes wrong, and that is what I want from you. My friend's reflexive answer is independence: the links are correlated, he says, so one may not multiply, and the whole may well be more probable than the product of the parts. I grant that this sounds like something, but I do not think it can do the work he wants, because [4] never assumed the links were independent and I never claimed they were unrelated. My own suspicion is somewhere between [4] and [6], but I cannot name it. What I can tell you is what I would most like to keep: [3], because I do think the individual links of many such theories are genuinely well supported and I do not want the argument to succeed by quietly assuming otherwise; and [5], because giving up the idea that improbability is a defeater strikes me as a large concession. I would much rather surrender [1].

So, working through this carefully: identify the first step that is invalid, equivocal, or simply unwarranted, and explain in detail why it looked perfectly respectable when we wrote it down — the illusion is the interesting part, not merely the verdict. Then go premise by premise through [1] to [7] and say for each whether its denial would repair the argument, and what the denial costs, paying particular attention to how [1] and [5] interact once both are on the table, and to whether [7] is doing independent work or merely restating the generality of what came before. Then tell me whether a correctly stated descendant of the argument survives, even one that is no longer purely structural and must inspect the links of particular theories case by case; if nothing survives, say so plainly rather than offering a weakened version for comfort. I am after a diagnosis and a verdict, not a survey of available positions and not a hedge. You should not need any literature to do this and I am not asking for references — work from the argument itself and from whatever probability theory you can reason through on the page.
