I am in the second year of a doctoral programme in philosophy, and over the past month I have been drafting the portion of my examination portfolio that deals with individuation. Three of us meet on Thursdays to exchange drafts; the third works in philosophy of physics, and she assured me recently that the quarrel over Leibniz's principle has been settled for some decades now, which is precisely the assurance I would like to be able to resist before an examiner presses me on it. So I have tried to state my own view as cleanly as I can. It begins from Black's thought experiment: a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, exactly alike in diameter, colour, temperature and every other qualitative respect, standing some distance apart in an otherwise empty space. I take this to show that Leibniz's Law is false, since it presents us with two numerically distinct objects that nonetheless agree in every respect; and I take the consequence to be that qualitative character can never underwrite an inference to numerical identity, so that any identification of one thing with another made on the strength of its properties alone is without foundation.

I am aware of the familiar reply — that the spheres are not in fact indiscernible, because each of them stands in spatial relations the other does not: each is at a certain distance from a sphere, and each occupies a place in the surrounding emptiness that the other does not occupy. My difficulty is that this reply seems to me either question-begging or self-defeating, and I cannot see a third alternative. It begs the question insofar as it appeals to anything indexical or demonstrative, since the difference between here and there already presupposes the two locations whose diversity it is supposed to establish. And it is self-defeating otherwise: if relations of that kind genuinely suffice to tell the spheres apart, then the universe Black describes was never a live possibility, and the whole exercise tells us nothing about whether numerical difference requires a qualitative ground. Either way, my inclination is to say that the disagreement was never really about the spheres at all, but about whether we are prepared to countenance numerical plurality that is brute.

Could you tell me how much of this survives scrutiny? I would particularly like to know whether my characterisation of what Black's case is meant to refute is accurate, whether the dilemma I have posed for the relational reply is a genuine one or whether I have mis-stated what that reply actually claims, and whether my conclusion about brute plurality follows from the premises I have offered or is being smuggled in among them. Please write in British English, keep the entire response to no more than four hundred words, and begin with whichever single correction you regard as most consequential.
