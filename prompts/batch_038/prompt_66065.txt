Last term I ran a small graduate seminar on epistemic paradoxes, and the last two sessions were on the lottery paradox and the preface paradox. Afterwards two of the students — Rosa and Ben — kept the argument going over email and eventually forwarded me the thread asking me to settle it. I've decided not to, at least not in my own voice: I want to hand them an adjudication they can't accuse of being my thumb on the scale, since I was in the room for the original session and both of them have a pretty good idea where I stand. So treat me as a bystander reporting this. Below is the exchange, lightly trimmed for length; I haven't cleaned up their phrasing.

1. Rosa: The two paradoxes are the same shape. In each you have a set of individually held beliefs plus one further belief whose content is the negation of their conjunction. So any solution that appeals to that structure is automatically a solution to both, and the habit of treating them as two seperate paradoxes demanding two seperate treatments is mostly a way of doubling the page count.

2. Ben: Same shape, fine, but not the same in the way that matters. In the lottery the individual probabilities are fixed and equal, so you can block it by putting the threshhold for outright belief above (n−1)/n and just not believing anything about any particular ticket. You can't do that in the preface, because the author's individual claims can be supported as strongly as you please, so no threshold short of 1 blocks the conflict. The lottery paradox is therefore an artifact of picking a modest lottery; the preface paradox is the real result — it shows the Lockean thesis can't be made consistent at any point below certainty.

3. Rosa: Your asymmetry dies the moment you scale the lottery. A billion tickets, and each individual belief has probability 0.999999999, which clears any threshold below 1 you care to name. So the threshold move is exactly as unavailable in the lottery as in the preface, and you've simply chosen a conveniently small lottery. What this shows isn't that the preface is worse — it's that there's one argument here, and the scaling trick reproduces it identically in both dressings.

4. Ben: Grant the scaling. There's still a difference in the source of the probabilities, and that's what does the work. The lottery beliefs rest on nothing but statistics about a reference class — no evidence bearing on the particular ticket — while the preface beliefs rest on the author's evidence about that particular claim. That's the old Kyburg line: statistical evidence doesn't license outright belief about the individual case. So a norm about which kinds of evidence support flat-out belief dissolves the lottery and leaves the preface untouched, because there the evidence is of the right kind.

5. Rosa: That names a distinction and calls it a solution. Why should evidence about the particular claim license conjoining when evidence about the class doesn't? And note that your own humility belief — that the book contains at least one error — is itself nothing but a statistical generalisation from the history of book-writing. So the preface contains a lottery of its own, and if your norm really killed statistical support it would kill the very belief that generates the paradox, leaving nothing to explain. That's a reductio on the norm, not a diagnosis.

6. Ben: Drop the norm, then. The asymmetry I actually want is about opting out. In the lottery you can decline to hold any belief about any individual ticket at no cost whatsoever, and no demand of rationality is violated by that silence. In the preface you can't opt out: she wrote the book, she asserts its contents, and refusing to believe your own assertions isn't an available stance for her. So the preface forces a live conflict between two real demands — getting each thing right, and being appropriately modest about your own fallibility — whereas the lottery stages a conflict between a demand and a stipulation you were free to avoid. That's a difference in kind, and it's why the preface is the one that should keep us up at night.

7. Rosa: Which refutes itself. If they're the same shape, as I said, and the lottery's conflict is avoidable, then the preface's is avoidable too — but you say it isn't. So either the shapes differ, in which case you owe me an account of the difference in shape, or avoidability doesn't transfer, in which case you've conceded my point that there's one paradox here.

Can you go through this move by move? For each of the seven, give me a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right plus a sentence or two of justification, and flag explicitly wherever a move is formally fine but begs the question against the other side — I have a suspicion about one of them, but I don't want to lead you to it. Then tell me who came out ahead overall. And the part I actually care about, because it's what goes in the write-up I'm giving them: is there any real asymmetry left between the lottery and the preface once the scaling point in move 3 is on the table, and what did the two of them jointly fail to consider? Don't point me at the literature, I know it well enough — I want your own reconstruction of the dialectic and where it actually breaks.
