I'm the TA on a third-year philosophy of law course. The instructor set this question and the mark bands; I'm marking the scripts blind and I haven't formed a view on this one, so I'm not going to tell you what I think of it. Here's the question as it appeared on the final:

> Hart separates the question of punishment's general justifying aim from the question of its distribution. Explain that separation and the contrast between what he calls negative and positive retributivism, illustrating the two poles with one classical utilitarian (Bentham or Beccaria) and one retributivist (Kant, or a contemporary retributivist such as Moore). Then consider the following stipulated view, which is not one you will have met in the reading. Call it **Ceiling Retributivism**:
>
> (i) For each offence-type the penal schedule has a maximum, and that maximum is fixed by what a *standard offender of that type* deserves; no penalty above the schedule maximum may be imposed on anyone.
> (ii) Whether to impose a penalty on a given person at all — and on which person — is settled exclusively by the expected social benefit of doing so, subject to (i).
>
> Say precisely where Ceiling Retributivism differs from Hart's own view, and say whether it is vulnerable to the objection that a theory of punishment must not license punishing the innocent. If you think it is not vulnerable, explain what does the work of blocking the objection.

Mark bands, 25 total: 8 for accurate exposition of Hart's framework and the negative/positive contrast; 9 for locating the difference between Ceiling Retributivism and Hart's view; 8 for the argument about the innocent-persons objection, including whether the candidate actually discharges the conditional at the end. And here's the script, written in timed conditions in about 45 minutes:

> Hart's insight is that "why have the practice at all" and "who gets it, and how much" are different questions with different answers. The general justifying aim is forward looking: punishment exists because it reduces crime and protects society. That part is utilitarian, and its the part Bentham and Beccaria got right, since both ground the practice in deterrence rather than in desert. Distribution is where the retributive principles enter: only offenders may be punished, and only in proportion to their offence. Hart calls this negative retributivism and the "negative" matters — retribution on his account is a limit on punishment, never its purpose. Positive retributivists like Kant and Moore go further and hold that the guilty ought positively to be punished, that punishing them is good in itself and that leaving them unpunished leaves a moral imbalance in the world. Kant is the pure case, since desert is doing the work at both levels: the aim and the distribution come from the same source.
>
> The obvious pressure on Hart is that his two principles can come apart. If the aim is social protection, then an official who is forbidden to frame an innocent is being asked to leave very large benefits on the table, and Hart owes us a story about why the constraint survives precisely when it is expensive. I think that's the real objection to mixed theories and it's close to fatal, because the constraint has no independent grounding once the aim is consequentialist.
>
> On Ceiling Retributivism: it looks like a rival to Hart but I think it collapses into his view described from the other end. Both make desert a cap rather than a purpose and both let expected benefit decide whether to punish. Clause (i) is in fact stronger than it first appears, and this is what the people who press the objection keep missing: if the schedule maximum is set by desert, then for someone who deserves nothing the maximum is nothing, so (i) rules out punishing the innocent all by itself, with no need for (ii) to mention guilt at all. So Ceiling Retributivism isn't vulnerable to the innocent-persons objection, and neither is Hart, and the habit of using that objection as the test between retributivism and consequentialism is just confused. What decides it is that desert-caps are person relative — what the schedule permits is a function of what *this* person deserves.
>
> Bentham's side can't make that move, though, because for a straightforward utilitarian there is no proportionality limit at all: the penalty should simply be whatever level deters, so if a heavier penalty deters more, utility tells you to impose it. Proportionality is a retributivist import, which is why Hart has to smuggle it in as a distribution principle instead of deriving it.
>
> That brings me to why something in the Ceiling Retributivism family is the view to hold. Positive retributivism cannot make sense of mercy: if the guilty must be punished, then every act of clemency and every dropped charge is an injustice, which is an absurdly harsh picture of ordinary prosecutorial life. Since the mercy objection sinks the positive view, and the innocent-persons objection doesn't bite on the ceiling view for the reason given above, the ceiling view is what's left. The only cost is giving up the idea that punishing the wicked is intrinsically worthwhile, which is no great loss.
