My professional vocabulary is that of clinical trial methodology: estimands and their attributes, primary and secondary endpoints, surrogate validation, intercurrent events, analysis horizon, control arms, identifiability, power. I have spent fifteen years in it and have no formal training in ethics; my philosophical formation is entirely from the rationalist blogs and forums I have read for twenty years, and it was through them that I met Lenman's 2000 cluelessness argument. As I understand it, the claim is that the long-run effects of ordinary acts ramify so massively and so sensitively that we cannot know even the sign of the net difference between our options, which makes consequentialist deliberation unavailable for most of what we actually do, with a second wrinkle: many of our choices are identity-affecting, so the people who will exist are not fixed across options.

Restate that argument in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can, then give me the residue report. Adjudicate three candidate translations I have been turning over: cluelessness as a power problem (a well-defined estimand, insufficient information); as a surrogate-endpoint problem (we act on short-horizon proxies whose link to the long-run outcome of interest is unvalidated); and as a horizon problem (no estimand at all, because no analysis time point can be non-arbitrarily specified). My own guess is that the first concedes too much, that the third is closest, and that the identity-affecting wrinkle belongs to none of them, since with no common population across arms there is no contrast to estimate. Tell me where that guess is wrong.

Two further things. First, what has no expression in this vocabulary at all — I have in mind the absence of a control arm, since refraining is itself an intervention with ramifying effects, and Lenman's positive suggestion that we deliberate instead by rules and dispositions, which I cannot tell is a methodological move or a change of subject. Second, and this is the point of the exercise: does the residue indict my framework, which seems to have no way of treating the specification of an outcome as itself a morally loaded choice, or does it show that the original argument slides between an epistemic complaint about what we can know and a stronger claim about what can even be asked?

Keep the translation honest. If a step only goes through because you have quietly supplied a fixed endpoint, a well-population, or a scoring horizon, say so explicitly rather than letting it pass as faithful rendering.
