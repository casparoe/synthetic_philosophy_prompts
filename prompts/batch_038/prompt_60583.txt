I run a small patient-safety program at a regional health system and I review maybe six to eight incident write-ups a month before they go to our review committee. Here's the problem I keep hitting: the explanations read beautifully. Night shift, two interruptions, two bags that look alike on the shelf, a nurse who had been asked to cover a second unit. Everyone in the room nods. But the grip of a good story is not the same thing as an explanation, and I have no way of telling our reviewers which is which. On the other side, some write-ups give me something that looks like a bare law — drug A interacts with condition B, patient had condition B — and nobody can do anything with that either.

I want you to turn the old argument about whether explanations work by subsuming events under general laws or by telling a well-constructed narrative into an actual procedure my reviewers can run on the explanation section of a report in about twenty minutes. Our reviewers are nurses and a couple of engineers. Nobody is going to read philosophy, so the distinctions have to survive being translated into questions you ask the writer and things you check in the text.

Specifically, build it so it can separate the failure modes, which I think are at least three and which we currently lump together as "weak analysis": an account that is only a chronology, an account that is a story whose details are doing no explanatory work, and an account that states something general but explains nothing about this patient on this night. For each step give me the question the reviewer asks, what a passing answer looks like, what a fail looks like, and where the reviewer is being asked to make a judgment call that no procedure can make for them — I want those flagged explicitly rather than buried, because I'd rather have an honest checklist than an impressive one. Include the disposition at the end: accept, send back with specific questions, or escalate.

Then run it on three cases, and invent the details you need:

1. A well-written narrative about a wrong-route medication error where the writer genuinely cannot tell me whether the same thing would have happened on a different night with a different nurse.
2. A thin, unpopular one — an interaction between a drug and a condition the patient already had, plus a monitoring gap — that passes your procedure even though our family advisory committee will hate it and will say it misses what actually happened.
3. A report that passes the first couple of steps and then bottoms out in facts nobody can change.

For case 2, don't soften it. I want to see the procedure hand me an answer I don't want, because that's the only way I'll know whether it's doing work or just decorating the room. And close with a short section on what the procedure can't settle — including whether a satisfying narrative has value on its own even when it fails your tests — since that argument is going to come up at the committee and I'd rather walk in expecting it.

One last thing, and please actually do it rather than gesturing at it: tell me how confident you are that these tests discriminate, and single out the one step you're least sure about — the one most likely to pass a bad report or fail a good one, and why. I'd rather have your doubt located in a specific place than spread thinly over the whole thing.
