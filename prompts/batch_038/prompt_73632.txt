I teach an interdisciplinary seminar on collective judgment — juries, elections, forecasting panels — and our last class is on the jury theorem: when each voter is likelier to be right than wrong and their mistakes are uncorrelated, the majority's accuracy climbs toward certainty as the group grows, and the very same arithmetic drives it toward certainty of error the moment each voter is likelier to be wrong. I want a short poem to close the session, in pantoum form: five quatrains, each stanza's second and fourth lines becoming the next stanza's first and third, with the closing stanza folding the two lines of the opening quatrain that have not yet returned into its own second and fourth positions, so that the poem ends where it began.

Set it in a room where votes are spoken aloud, and let the carried lines be the votes. At first their return should feel like independent witnesses confirming one another; only gradually, as the same phrase keeps coming back down the row, should it read as a single voice being handed along. Nothing in the poem should let a listener tell which of the two things has happened — a group converging on the truth, or a group converging on a rumor — since that is exactly what the mechanism cannot see from inside. No probabilities, no stated numbers, no jargon, and no mention of the theorem or its author.
