I am to give the farewell address this spring at the retirement dinner for Justice Harriet Vance, who has sat for thirty-one years on my state's intermediate appellate court and retires at the end of the term. The room will hold perhaps a hundred and eighty people: sitting and retired judges, three generations of her former clerks, the usual bar dignitaries, and — the part I keep in mind when drafting — her husband, her three children and their spouses, and four grandchildren, not one of whom has ever read a judicial opinion. Two of the children teach school; the third is a veterinarian. I clerked for her twenty-six years ago, before I left practice for academic philosophy, and I now work on collective decision-making. She has known for two decades that I regard her profession's favorite argument about panel adjudication as far weaker than it looks, and she has enjoyed telling me so at dinner.

I want the address built on the Condorcet jury theorem — not mentioned in passing and then dropped, but supplying the architecture, so that what the address can honestly say is determined by what the theorem can honestly support. Please write it, five to six minutes when read aloud, roughly eight hundred words, and then append a note of no more than one hundred and fifty words identifying the two or three sentences in which the theorem constrained you, and what in each case it forbade.

The difficulties I want the draft to negotiate are these:

1. I do not accept that the independence condition holds on a deliberating three-judge panel, and I will not assert in her presence that her panels were for that reason more likely to be right than any single member of them. The address must nevertheless not abandon the theorem; the interest lies precisely in setting up the expectation and then handling it straight.

2. Nor may it lean on the competence assumption. At the top of a state court system the remaining questions are the ones on which informed and conscientious people differ, and the claim that each judge is likelier than not to get such a question right is exactly what is in dispute. The asymptotic result — majorities tending toward certainty as they grow — must not be smuggled in for a panel of three. If it can be managed in a subordinate clause, I would also like it acknowledged that her cases were almost never the single yes-or-no question the theorem is about in the first place.

3. The dissent problem is the heart of it, and I want it faced rather than avoided. On the standard Condorcetian reading, a colleague's published dissent is evidence that should lower one's confidence in the majority's verdict. Harriet dissented once in thirty-one years. There is a good reading of that record and a bad one: a panel of genuinely competent judges converging, or a judge so thoroughly absorbed into the panel that her agreement carried no information, because it was never independent. The address should let the room feel the second possibility, briefly and without cruelty, so that what follows is earned rather than assumed. What I will not accept is a passage that chooses the flattering interpretation by simply asserting it.

4. The landing. If the theorem cannot supply the reason her colleagues' decisions commanded our respect, the address must find that reason elsewhere, and I have a view about where: not in the reliability of the tally but in what sitting on a panel does to the judges themselves — it obliges each to state her reasons in a form another person must answer, and it puts the losing side's reasons into the public record where they cannot be quietly forgotten. Arrive at something like that, but it must not read as the conclusion section of an article.

In point of register: state the theorem once, in a single sentence of plain English, before any technical talk; give the family one concrete image or one short story they can hold onto; no case names, no quotations from opinions, no citations, and no philosopher's name besides Condorcet's. I would like one brief acknowledgment of what became of Condorcet himself — the man who put his faith in majorities, proscribed by one, who went into hiding and died in a village prison — used once and not milked. Avoid the vocabulary of my trade entirely; the words "epistemic" and "posterior" do not appear in a room containing a veterinarian. Sentences must be speakable on one breath, and no more than three people may be thanked by name.

Above all, it should sound like a former clerk's gratitude and a colleague's respect, not like a seminar. I would rather the address be honest and slightly uncomfortable than warm and hollow.
