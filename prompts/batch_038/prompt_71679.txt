I am presently drafting a proposal for a two-year workshop series in formal epistemology, funded for six residential meetings of about a dozen participants each, and I must commit to a spine of problems before the panel will consider it. The nominal theme is what a rational agent ought to do upon learning a conditional — the territory opened up by van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin case — but a theme is not an agenda, and I have no appetite for six meetings that amount to a tour of the available positions.

Here is the terrain as I currently map it. In the single-report case the familiar response holds the odds between the two halves of the region fixed and moves the agent's credence within the antecedent partition to three to one; whether that is the uniquely correct response, or merely a response, is already contentious. Beyond that, the candidate problems include: (i) whether the case admits of a unique correct posterior at all, or is irreducibly underdetermined by what the agent is told; (ii) whether the governing constraint falls on the odds within the antecedent partition, on the probabilities of its complement, or somewhere else entirely; (iii) what happens when conditionals arrive in sequence, and whether the resulting posterior depends on the order in which they were learned; (iv) how the treatment should go when the antecedent is one the agent already assigns very low credence; (v) whether conditional probability ought to be taken as primitive — the Popper-function or Rényi-style strategy — and whether that manoeuvre genuinely resolves the problem or merely relocates it; (vi) how an agent should accommodate uncertainty about the reliability, or the epistemic standpoint, of the informant who supplies the conditional; (vii) the suppositional or Adams-conditioning line, on which learning a conditional is not learning a proposition at all, and what survives of the problem if that is right; (viii) whether diachronic coherence considerations — the usual betting arguments — constrain the choice among rules at all, given that the agent is nowhere being asked to bet on the report itself; and (ix) whether any of this alters what the agent should subsequently do, as opposed to what she should believe.

Let me now introduce a case of my own construction, since I suspect it may reorder that list. Call it Benjamin Twice-Told. The setting is the familiar one: a region divided into a Blue half and a Red half, each subdivided into a northern and a southern sector, with the agent initially uniform over the four sectors. But rather than one report, she is handed a single sealed message containing two sentences, from two informants whom she takes to be equally and independently reliable and whom she has no reason to think have been comparing notes. The first writes that if she is in the Blue half, the probability that she is in Blue-North is three-quarters. The second writes that if she is in the southern sector of whichever half she happens to occupy, the probability that she is in the Blue half is one-quarter. The two sentences arrive together, and neither is addressed to the prior as it stood before the other.

My question is not primarily how to solve that case, though I should like you to say whether you take it to be jointly consistent with a single probability function, and whether the familiar rules handle it, mishandle it, or simply fail to apply to it. My question is what to do with the six meetings. Which of the problems above — with Benjamin Twice-Told itself offered as a candidate in its own right — should the series take as its spine, and in what order? Defend the ordering rather than merely presenting it. For each item you rank highly, explain what makes it upstream of the others; say whether it is tractable with the conceptual and mathematical tools now in hand, or blocked on something not yet available; and identify plainly which items I should cut, with reasons. Be explicit about the criteria you are trading off — centrality, tractability, how much else depends on the answer, and how likely a mixed company of philosophers and probabilists is to make progress in two days rather than talk past one another — and mark the places where you are weighting one against another rather than satisfying both.

Two further requests. For each of your top three, tell me what would count as genuine progress within a single meeting: the sort of thing a dozen people could produce in two days that would be worth having afterwards. And tell me what I should watch for over the next two years that would lead you to demote one of those priorities, or to restore one of the items you have cut. Please write throughout in British English, and confine yourself to analysis and argument: I have no need of a bibliography, a reading list, or any references whatsoever.
