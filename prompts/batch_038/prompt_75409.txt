I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working on theories of well-being, and before I commit a chapter of my dissertation to the heterogeneity problem for hedonism I want to know how much damage it really does. I'd like an essay that argues to a conclusion rather than surveying the terrain.

The problem as I understand it: sensory pleasures (a warm bath, scratching an itch, cold water when you are parched), aesthetic ones (a chord change in a piece of music you know extremely well), and attitudinal ones (taking pleasure in the fact that a friend's paper was accepted, which seems to have no distinctive feel and no obvious location in experience at all) appear to share nothing intrinsic. If they share nothing, then "pleasure" does not name a single property, and the hedonist thesis that pleasure is the sole intrinsic good threatens to become either false or a mere list of unrelated claims bundled under one word.

My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and one I expect to be corrected — is that the disagreement is less about the cases than about which model of kindhood each party is implicitly assuming. Four models seem to matter:

1. **Water-model.** Pleasure has a shared underlying essence that explains the surface variety. The essence may be phenomenal, representational, or neurofunctional; the task is to name it.
2. **Game-model.** Family resemblance: overlapping similarities, no single shared feature, and "pleasure" is nonetheless a legitimate if non-natural kind.
3. **Jade-model.** Two or more genuinely distinct phenomena have been lumped together for historical reasons, the way nephrite and jadeite were long sold under one name. The correct response is disjunctivist or eliminativist, and hedonism must be restated as a conjunction of separate theses.
4. **Role-model.** Pleasure is unified not by what it is like but by the work it does: by a common cause, by a common effect (that all and only pleasures are states the subject wants to continue, or states that make the experience they occur in better), or by its place in a theory of motivation.

Say which of these is right. Then answer the question I actually care about: does the heterogeneity problem, correctly understood, kill hedonism, wound it badly but leave it recoverable, or dissolve into a pseudo-problem?

Two things should be handled carefully rather than waved at. First, the role-model looks like the most promising escape, but I suspect it pays a price I cannot articulate: if the common effect is being wanted to continue, hedonism seems to turn into a desire-satisfaction theory wearing a different hat; if the common effect is making experience better, the account may be circular, since making experience better is precisely what the hedonist is trying to explain. Tell me whether that suspicion is right, and if it is not, why not. Second, please distinguish the demand for a shared property (metaphysical) from the demand for a reason to treat all pleasures alike in our axiology (normative), and say whether a hedonist who satisfies only the second has genuinely answered the objection. I would also like your view on what an empirical result — a common neural substrate, or a shared affective architecture across all these cases — could and could not establish here.

Engage at least two of the standard hedonist replies at their strongest rather than at their weakest, and do not end in a standoff: I want a verdict and the reasoning that gets you there.
