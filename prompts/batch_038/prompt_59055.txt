I am a professor of political philosophy, and in three weeks I must deliver the opening address at the first session of a citizens' assembly in a city of about 180,000 where I have lived for twenty years. I designed the assembly's method and recruited its members, so I am not a neutral convener, and the speech cannot pretend otherwise. I would like you to write it for me. The background you need is as follows.

The city is under a federal compliance order requiring it to eliminate its combined sewer overflows within twelve years. There are three realistic options: a large underground storage tunnel financed by a thirty-year bond, which would raise sewer rates by something in the neighborhood of ninety percent over the decade, with the increase falling hardest on households already spending the largest share of their income on water; a distributed green-infrastructure program across the watershed, cheaper on paper, slower, and of genuinely uncertain performance; or minimal compliance plus fines. The council commissioned an assembly of forty-eight members, selected by stratified lottery from the residential file, to meet over five weekends and recommend one of the three. In November, meanwhile, a neighborhood coalition will put a measure on the ballot capping any increase in municipal utility rates at three percent per year. If it passes, the tunnel is unfundable and the city is in contempt of the order. Several of my forty-eight members almost certainly signed that petition.

The room will contain the forty-eight members; the mayor and seven councilors seated behind me on the dais; three local reporters, one of whom writes headlines; and perhaps a dozen rate-cap campaigners who have come to observe.

The speech must satisfy the following, and I want each treated as a constraint rather than a suggestion.

First, within the opening minutes I must tell the members that their recommendations are advisory. The council resolution says only that the report will be received, that the council will vote on it within ninety days, and that the vote will be public and recorded. I may say exactly that and no more: no "the council will listen carefully," no "your work will shape the decision," nothing implying a commitment that was never made. And I must say it before they have invested anything, without letting anyone in the room conclude that they have agreed to be a decoy.

Second, I must tell them that the November vote may overtake their work entirely. I may not treat the rate cap as obviously foolish; I must give them something true about the difference between the two instruments that they can act on rather than a rebuke.

Third — and this is the spine, so please build on it rather than substituting a familiar one — my own view of the matter is that a referendum converts reasons into a number and then discards the reasons, while an assembly converts numbers into reasons and then discards the number. The ballot yields an authoritative outcome carrying no inspectable justification: fifty-one percent, and nothing on the record about what the majority was responding to, which considerations moved it, or what it was willing to trade. The assembly yields an inspectable justification carrying no authority: a report anyone can read and no one is bound by. Each instrument lacks precisely what the other has and cannot supply. The members' work matters not in spite of being advisory but because the city will have a number in November whether or not they succeed, and will have reasons only if they produce them. This must be argued, not asserted, in language a plumber and a retired schoolteacher can follow.

Fourth, meet the objection I know is coming — that a member is no expert on hydraulics, represents nobody, and does not live near an outfall. Do not flatter her. Draw the distinction between being a miniature of the city and being representative in the electoral sense, and say what the members were in fact selected for. You choose what that is, but it must be something a person can be good at and can be seen doing over five weekends. If Athens appears at all, do not use it in the usual way: the point is that the Athenians placed the body chosen by lot upstream of the body that voted, and gave it control of the agenda — which is why a sortitioned body is not decorative even where the voters decide otherwise.

Fifth, address one or two sentences over the members' heads to the dais, saying what the council will not enjoy: that convening this assembly also shifts part of the cost of a bad decision onto people who did not ask for it. Say it so the mayor cannot resent it and so the reporters print it accurately.

Sixth, quotability. No sentence may be worse detached from its context than it is in it. Wherever you would write a concession followed by a correction, restructure so the concession can stand alone.

Form: 900 to 1,200 words, about nine minutes aloud; no headings, bullet points, or stage directions; no verbatim quotation of any author, ancient or modern, and at most one invocation of a name, paraphrased in my own words; at most one moment of humor, at my expense; avoid "stakeholders," "journey," "deliberative democracy," and "unprecedented." Close not with an inspiration but with the first thing that will actually happen after I sit down, so the room knows what to do in the next five minutes.

Afterward, in no more than 150 words, tell me which sentence you judged most vulnerable to being quoted badly and why you kept it; what you settled on as the thing the members were selected for; and any place you found yourself reaching for a promise the council resolution will not support.
