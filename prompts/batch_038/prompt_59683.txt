I have been asked to run a seventy-five-minute session for the journal club of a cognitive neuroscience department. The attendees are second- and third-year PhD students plus a few postdocs; all are physicalists of a confident and largely unreflective kind, several routinely use computational and intentional vocabulary in their own work without embarrassment — encoding, gating, representing a prediction error, computing a value signal — and one or two are temperamentally eliminativist about folk psychology. Their default stance going in, which I confirmed with two of them over drinks, is that mental causation is a pseudo-problem: the mental just is the neural, so there is nothing left over, and the whole debate is philosophy spinning its wheels. My topic is the exclusion argument as Kim formulated it, and my difficulty is not that they will fail to follow the argument but that they will find it trivially answered and then stop thinking. What I want from you is therefore not an explanation of the argument, which I know, but a plan for sequencing it so that by around minute forty they feel the squeeze and can say precisely what it would take to get out of it.

Three constraints on the design. First, I want to open on their own explanatory practice rather than on agency, and I am considering carrying the whole session on a single non-agential case of the form "why did the network get this input wrong" or "why did the animal press the lever," where the candidate causes are a functional-level property and a physical-level property and the room's instinct is that obviously both are true and obviously they are not competing. Tell me whether a case of that shape can actually bear the weight, and what it must have and must lack for the argument to bite; my worry is that it invites the reply that functional properties are multiply realized and so were never rivals, which is near the point but needs careful handling. Second, I want the reflexive moves defused in a fixed order rather than allowed to turn the session into a list of them: overdetermination offered as a solution, causal powers inherited from the base property, an invocation of downward causation, and the assumption that the argument is neutral about what causation is. Tell me which of these four is genuinely the most dangerous one for this audience, and name the one or two I have missed. Third, I want them to leave able to state what the nonreductive physicalist in particular owes, and to see that their own "the mental just is the neural" remark is not an escape from the argument but the selection of one exit with a price attached to it.

Please give me the plan as exactly five numbered items — the entry point, the carrying case and its specification, the misconception map with the point in the sequence at which each is headed off, one question I can put to the room at the end that would reveal whether the squeeze has actually landed, and what I should openly refuse to cover at this level — with each item two or three sentences and no introduction or conclusion.
