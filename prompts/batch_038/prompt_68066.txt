so, background: I work in a county environmental health division (small metro, ~400k people) and I'm the one who writes the risk memos that the board actually votes on. Last month we had a genuinely ugly fight about a permit — a manufacturer wants to swap a fluorosurfactant we already know has contaminated a well field for a newer replacement that's much less studied. Approve it and you trade a documented bad for an undocumented one; deny it and the documented bad keeps leaching while the plant runs on the old process, which also has known occupational harms. Afterwards two board members asked me to put on one page what decision rule I'm actually using, so they can check whether I'm just deciding case by case and dressing it up in principle-language. I did it, and now I want someone to attack it before it goes in front of them.

Here's the package I landed on: (1) for ordinary, reversible, well-measured risks — traffic design, routine immunization scheduling, that kind of thing — I do straight expected value; if the data is good we multiply and compare, no heroics. (2) For risks that are plausibly catastrophic and irreversible I go maximin: pick the alternative whose worst case is least bad, and don't demand a probability for it, because in these cases we usually don't have one worth the name. (3) I reject the standard objection that this makes the precautionary principle self-undermining, the one that says it condemns everything including doing nothing — I don't think the status quo gets any presumption in its favor, inaction is a choice like any other, and when both directions have a catastrophic tail my rule still tells you to take the side whose tail is less catastrophic. (4) I also refuse Pascalian moves: I dont think you get to move a decision just because someone can't rule a harm out and says it's infinitely bad — no probability and no evidential support for the mechanism, no standing in the analysis. (5) The threshold between "ordinary" in (1) and "catastrophic" in (2) has to be written down in advance and not gerrymandered to produce the verdict I happened to want; the board sets it before it hears the case. (6) Where the two sides are unequally informed — the applicant knows its own chemistry, we don't — I think it's legitimate to make the proponent carry the burden of showing safety, even though I'm explicitly not claiming they're probably unsafe.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and how hard? Rank the conflicts by severity rather than giving me a flat list where everything sounds equally bad. For each real one I want the cheapest available fix and what that fix costs me in practice — including whether fixing it wrecks my answer to the board member who said I was being inconsistent, since that's the whole reason the page exists. Then tell me which pairs only look like a contradiction because they're operating at different levels (decision rule vs. evidential standard vs. procedural default), because I suspect at least one of them is fine and I'd rather not spend the meeting defending something I don't have to. Two more things: does the permit case I described above actually land inside my rule, or in a gap between two of the clauses — I have a bad feeling about that one. And is any item on the list just a restatement of another one, i.e. am I padding the page and should I cut it?
