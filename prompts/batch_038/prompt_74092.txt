I am midway through a novel whose protagonist is a home secretary who authorises the torture of a single detainee in order to prevent an atrocity, and who is afterwards neither prosecuted nor pardoned, but simply unable to look at his own hands. My difficulty is not the plot but the philosophy beneath it, and I want an essay that settles the matter rather than surveying it. The claim to be tested is that an act may be, all things considered, the right thing to do and yet leave the agent genuinely culpable: not merely grieved by a terrible outcome, not merely obliged to compensate someone, but guilty in a sense that would make blame appropriate. Defend that claim or destroy it. You should confront the obvious objection that it is incoherent, since a thing cannot be both right on balance and wrong, and the rival reading on which my minister's anguish is a species of admirable sentiment attaching to the cost of his choice rather than to any wrongdoing in it.

Two puzzles I cannot resolve ought to govern your verdict. First, an asymmetry: had he pulled two children from a burning house and failed to reach a third, he would be anguished too, yet we should not think he owed that third child's mother an apology; after the torture he is anguished and does owe the detainee one. On both occasions the outcome was the best available to him. Second, a substitution: suppose he feels nothing whatsoever, and signs the order in complete equanimity. Has the act then lost its dirt, or merely lost its witness?

Tell me, finally, whether dirty hands names a real moral category or is only a theatrical description of hard choices; and if it is real, state precisely what conditions an act must satisfy to fall inside it, in terms that decide the case of a minister who is horrified but not guilty.
