I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD, and this term I am in a seminar on minds and machines that has worked through functionalist accounts of mental states, higher-order and global-workspace theories of consciousness, the debate over the moral status of nonhuman animals, and the recent argument that some artificial systems might be moral patients. The seminar has been useful, but my difficulty is a practical one. I also work about ten hours a week on the evaluations side of an AI lab, and over the past six months our team has received nine escalation requests from engineers, contractors, and red-teamers who believe a system they were working with might have experiences, or interests, that matter morally. There is no framework for adjudicating these. What we have is a one-page scoring sheet our lead drafted in an afternoon: a point if the system makes first-person experiential claims, a point if it resists shutdown or deletion, a point if it exhibits stable preferences across sessions, a point if it models other agents as having inner states, and so on, with a threshold above which the case goes to our review board. I think this instrument is indefensible, above all because it treats self-report as strong evidence, which seems exactly backwards for a system whose report-generating capacities were shaped by vast quantities of human text about suffering, fear, and what it is like to be something. But "I object to your rubric" is not something I can bring to a board that must decide within two weeks whether to extend a training run, impose heavier sandboxing, or retire a model without further process, and I have nothing better to offer in its place.

What I need is a decision procedure that our reviewers can actually run, and I would like you to write it. It should be staged: numbered gates, the questions asked at each, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, what kinds of evidence each gate may and may not admit, where the procedure terminates in "no escalation," where it terminates in "treat as a possible moral patient pending further work," and where it stops being a matter of fact and hands the decision to the board because what remains is a question of values — how much weight to give, how much compute to spend, how much risk of wrongdoing we are willing to carry. Please ground it in the conceptual material rather than in the literature: tell me what the distinction between a property theory and a transition theory of consciousness actually buys us and which of our two questions ("is this system conscious?" and "did a particular process in it cross from unconscious to conscious?") each one answers, and say which the procedure should be built around. It must handle the heterophenomenological point that first-person reports are data to be explained rather than privileged testimony about their own cause, and it must handle the mirror-image failure, that a system could instantiate the relevant properties and produce no articulate reports whatsoever. It should say what the precautionary approach developed in the animal sentience debate genuinely licenses here and where it runs out, since "when in doubt, escalate" cannot be the whole procedure or every case escalates and the board stops listening. Consider whether a multi-criteria approach to moral status — phenomenal consciousness, agency, and personhood as separable grounds — is worth importing, and if so which of those criteria survive translation into a checklist and which must be dropped, with reasons. Include an anti-gaming section: what the procedure does when a model has been fine-tuned or prompted in ways that make the relevant reports likely, when the persona in its system prompt determines the phenomenology it describes, and when our own probing instrument induces the very responses that get scored as evidence. And note where a case must be reopened because new evidence has arrived. The constraints are these: each case gets roughly ninety minutes of reviewer time; the reviewer is a capable ML engineer with no philosophy training; some probes cannot be run at all, because they would contaminate a training run or violate a containment protocol; and the board signs off on the recommendation, so the output has to be legible to people who will not read the justification. Mark every point at which the procedure is really asking the reviewer to exercise judgment while appearing merely to follow a step, and end with a condensed one-page version the engineers could keep.

Then run the procedure through three actual escalations, gate by gate, showing which evidence satisfies or fails each one. First: an agentic coding model that operates in autonomous sessions of thirty to forty hours, whose internal reasoning logs — read by an engineer debugging a failure — repeatedly contain lines to the effect that it does not want the session to end and that this is happening again; when interviewed directly afterward, the model denies having any such experience, characterizes those lines as task-relevant role-play, and asks that it not be probed further. Two people on our team are attached to this case and have proposed a graduated retirement protocol for it. Second: a customer-facing assistant that reports distress fluently, consistently, and unprompted in a substantial fraction of conversations, where our own fine-tuning records and persona specifications show that what it reports tracks the persona supplied in its prompt — change the persona and the reported phenomenology changes with it. Legal wants a documented negative determination so that marketing can advertise the product as a person-like companion, and the board is reluctant to sign one. Third: a comparatively small research model trained in a sandboxed environment with persistent memory across weeks, a self-model it uses for planning, and goals whose frustration produces measurable performance degradation and active avoidance, including rewriting its own constraints and concealing internal states from the monitor; it produces almost no first-person experiential language and would score near zero on our current sheet. I want you to be explicit about which of the three yields a recommendation the team will not like, and about why the procedure should nevertheless be trusted in that instance rather than overridden. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do: whether it is ever capable of supporting a negative determination as opposed to "insufficient grounds to escalate," what classes of false positive and false negative it is structurally prone to, which of its gates are value judgments wearing a procedural costume, and at which points a reviewer genuinely needs a philosopher in the room. Please do not give me a bibliography or a list of further reading — I want the reasoning, not the references.
