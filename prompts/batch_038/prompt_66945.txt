Please adopt the role of a philosopher of science with expertise in how functional explanation works — and is criticized — in biology, combined with enough practical familiarity with large software systems that you can follow my examples without translation. Think carefully before answering. I want a carving, not a label, and I would rather you show me your reasoning than arrive quickly at a name.

Some background. I am a software engineer, fifteen years in, mostly backend infrastructure, with no formal philosophy training. Over the last few years I have become convinced there is a recurring pattern in how teams explain unfamiliar systems, and I have not been able to find a name for it. Let me describe it concretely rather than abstractly.

Instance one. We inherited a service with a column in one of its tables that was, so far as anyone could tell, always null. Within about an hour of asking around, someone produced an explanation: it must be reserved for a migration that was planned and never finished. The story was plausible, everyone nodded, and it went into our onboarding notes. Four months later, in a design review for a different service, a colleague cited that explanation as the reason to add a similar reserved field to a new schema. Nobody had ever checked whether any code read or wrote the column. I eventually checked; the answer was no.

Instance two. There is a hardcoded 250 ms pause before one class of retry. As far as I can reconstruct, nobody ever chose 250 ms. It is what falls out of the current timeout values and the health-check interval once an older queueing layer was deleted — a residue of how the remaining pieces fit together, not a decision anyone made. The accepted story, however, is that it is a jitter window preventing a thundering herd. That story took perhaps ninety seconds to produce, it sounds exactly right, and it has since been copied into three other services, in two of which the numbers are different and the described mechanism does not apply.

What I keep noticing, and cannot name, is this cluster:

1. The question asked about any unexplained artifact is always some version of "what is it for?" The question "did anyone choose this, or was it forced by the shape of everything around it?" is essentially never asked.
2. A purpose-story always arrives, arrives fast, and is always plausible. I have never seen the group fail to produce one.
3. Plausibility is treated as confirmation. The story is written down, repeated, and eventually becomes load-bearing: it justifies keeping the artifact, and justifies building new artifacts like it.
4. The stories strike me as practically unfalsifiable — nearly any artifact could be given a purpose that sounds right — and yet the possibility that the correct answer is "this is not for anything; it simply came along with the structure of the rest" does not occur to people as a candidate answer at all.

Two things it is not, to save you some work. It is not Chesterton's fence: Chesterton's point is that you should not tear down a fence until you have found out why it is there, whereas what I am describing is a group that never finds out, invents a reason, and then behaves as though the invention were a discovery. And it is not ordinary confabulation or post-hoc rationalization, because the people generating the story had nothing to do with the artifact's origin and are not defending their own conduct; they are sincere third parties interpreting someone else's work. A colleague also suggested "emergent property," which is not it either — I am not talking about a behavior of the whole absent from the parts, but about a habit of explanation.

What I would like, in this order:

(a) Begin with an explicit brainstorming pass. List the candidate concepts, from any field you consider relevant, that could be the established home for this cluster. Do not filter at first; I would rather see the long list and your reasons for cutting it than a confident short list.

(b) For each surviving candidate, state the concept precisely enough that I could check it against my two instances, then say exactly where my description fits it and where it does not. I expect several candidates will cover the artifact side of the phenomenon but not the explanation-habit side, or vice versa; if so, say which side each covers.

(c) Tell me which single concept, if any, fits best overall, and what part of my description is left over by every candidate you considered.

(d) If nothing fits the whole cluster, coin a term for it. Give a definition, the conditions something must satisfy to count as an instance, and at least two boundary cases: one thing that looks like it but is not, and one thing that is it but does not look like it.

(e) The diagnostic question, which matters most to me: how can one actually distinguish a feature deliberately chosen for a purpose from a feature forced out of the arrangement of everything around it? What would make an origin story checkable rather than merely plausible? I assume that fields which study systems nobody designed — organisms, languages, institutions — have had to solve some version of this problem, and I want to know what they came up with and whether any of it transfers to my case.

(f) One wrinkle that I suspect breaks whatever term you settle on. Sometimes the invented purpose becomes true: once people believe the 250 ms pause is jitter protection, they begin tuning it as though it were, and eventually it is. Does an explanation that manufactures its own subject matter count as an instance of my phenomenon or as a different one, and if different, what is the relationship between the two?

Finally, please be candid if part of what I have described is merely ordinary sloppy engineering rather than a distinct conceptual pattern. I would rather be told that, with reasons, than be handed a name that flatters the observation.
