I spent thirty-four years as a hydrogeologist with a state survey — contaminant transport mostly, a lot of time in the field and a lot of arguments about borehole logs — and since I retired I've gone at philosophy head first. I sit in on an epistemology reading group at the university here, and for about a year now I've been chewing on the peer disagreement business, the conciliate-versus-hold-your-ground fight. Funny thing is it turns out I have a day job for it. I volunteer as handling editor for a small open-access journal in my old field, and I'm on the editorial board, so I see maybe forty manuscripts a year and essentially every one comes back with two referee reports that don't agree, sometimes wildly. We also have this slightly odd house practice that I inherited and have grown fond of tho: the decision letter carries a short "disagreement note," a sentence or two saying where the referees split and how much weight the editor gave each side, and we print an editor-confidence figure on a 1-to-10 scale, where 7 or above means we're willing to defend the call in public if someone writes in. Right now the notes get written off the top of my head, the number is basically vibes, and two of my three board colleagues are retired scientists like me with zero patience for philosophy. One of them says "just average the two scores and get on with it," which I'm pretty sure is wrong, but I cant argue it in a way that lands in a meeting.

So here's what I'd like: build me a decision procedure my board could actually run on a manuscript, straight out of whatever the epistemology of disagreement has that's usable, but in the form of ordered steps rather than a position paper. I want the questions we ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in "one referee is simply mistaken" versus "these two are genuine equals and the split is real," and how much of the gap we're supposed to close when they are equals. Two practical snags you'll have to solve for me rather than wave at. First, our confidence figure is a coarse ordinal scale in whole numbers, so "split the difference" is often literally impossible — I need a rule for which way to round and an honest statement of whether that rounding is epistemology or editorial politics. Second, we're always tempted to call in a third referee as a tiebreak, and I can't figure out whether doing that is a legitimate way of responding to disagreement or a dodge that quietly changes who counts as my peer; we can barely find two referees as it is, so the procedure should say when a third is warranted and when it's procrastination. Bake in the independence issue in referee terms — same lab lineage, both read the same three papers, one reviewed the grant that funded the work, one is famous and the other is a postdoc — and tell me which of the finer distinctions from the literature you're keeping, which you're dropping to make it runnable, and why. Mark clearly every step that's a judgment call wearing a procedure costume. Constraints: forty minutes per manuscript, all volunteers, no philosophers present, and our board chair is a retired judge who signs the letter and wants one line he could read aloud to justify the confidence number.

Then run it on three cases. (A) Referee one is a statistician who says the analysis is unsound — model choice and multiplicity, he's blunt about it; referee two is a field hydrogeologist who says the sampling campaign was logistically impossible to improve on and the conclusions are conservative anyway. Neither has seen the other's report. I suspect part of this split is about one shared question and part is two different questions, and I want the procedure to expose that rather than produce a compromise verdict on nothing in particular. (B) Two referees who really are matched — same training, same care, no conflicts worth mentioning — who flatly disagree about whether a mechanistic interpretation is supported. The data are fine, the reading differs. Does the procedure say average, say publish-with-flag, or say something else, and what number goes in the letter? (C) The one where I don't get what I want. I've thought for fifteen years that a widely used tracer-method assumption in our field is unsound; I published on it, got shouted down at two conferences, and both my referees accept the assumption as background and don't even discuss it. Walk the procedure through on me and tell me honestly whether it says I should fold, and if it does, whether there's any principled way for me to keep pushing on it without being the crank the procedure says I am.

Finish by telling me what the procedure cannot do, which kinds of manuscript it will get wrong (I'd guess the heavily mathematical ones, the replications, and the null results, but you tell me), and where a call has to leave the procedure and go to a human because it's about editorial values rather than facts. I don't need references or a reading list — I've got library access and a reading group for that — I need the thing I can hand to three impatient retired scientists on a Tuesday.
