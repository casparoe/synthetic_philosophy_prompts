I'm drafting a paper for a seminar on the historiography of scientific method, and I've built a reconstruction that I now find embarrassing, because it appears to show that Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes collapses into exactly the permissiveness he accused his rivals of tolerating. Two classmates tell me I've simply recycled Feyerabend's complaint that Lakatosian methodology is anarchism dressed up as rules, and I'd like to know whether they're right or whether I've made a specific, locatable mistake. Here is the chain as it currently stands:

(1) On the methodology of scientific research programmes, what one ought to do with a degenerating programme is abandon it and transfer one's allegiance to a progressive rival; what makes a series of problemshifts degenerating rather than progressive is that the auxiliaries added to the protective belt lack excess empirical content. (2) An auxiliary lacks excess empirical content, and is therefore ad hoc, when it does no work beyond absorbing the anomaly it was invented to absorb: when it implies nothing that has not already been tested. (3) Whether something has been tested is a question the appraiser asks from where she stands, so the relevant evidential situation is the one obtaining at the time of appraisal rather than at the time of proposal; an appraiser working in 1845 and an appraiser working in 1855 confront the same auxiliary hypothesis together with different evidence. (4) It follows that ad hocness is not a property a hypothesis acquires once and for all. Take an auxiliary postulating an unseen perturbing body to account for a planet whose orbit keeps missing its predicted positions: appraised before the telescopes have been pointed at the calculated spot, it has no tested consequences and looks like a placeholder minted to swallow an anomaly; appraised after the body is found there, it is the textbook case of a progressive move. Same hypothesis, same content, opposite verdicts, and nothing in the hypothesis changed. (5) Since no appraisal conducted at a time can rule out that the decisive test is still to come, no programme is degenerating simpliciter; the strongest verdict any appraisal can deliver is of the form "degenerating so far." (6) But a verdict of that form cannot trigger the rule in (1): if a later appraisal can always reverse it, then the property the rule quantifies over is never instantiated, and the rule's condition is never met. (7) A methodology whose only directive is never triggered directs nothing. Its author already conceded that a programme written off as degenerating may yet make a comeback and that appraisals are inherently revisable, which is to concede that nothing is ever foreclosed; and the whole selling point of the rules was supposed to be that, unlike a free-for-all, they tell us when to walk away. (8) Therefore the methodology of scientific research programmes fails by its own standard: it generates no novel prescriptions, absorbs every objection by re-indexing the verdict to the moment of appraisal, and is itself a degenerating programme in the philosophy of science.

The awkward part is that I find (1) through (4) very hard to give up and (8) impossible to accept, and I can't tell whether the trouble is a bad inference or a bad premise. One complication: a classmate insists that (3) misstates the criterion, because on the refinement she has in mind a fact counts as novel not when it happens to be unobserved at the time of appraisal but when it fell outside the body of evidence the hypothesis was constructed to accommodate, and she thinks that blocks the slide at (5). I don't see it, since the set of facts a hypothesis was constructed to accommodate also grows as the programme swallows further anomalies, which appears to reintroduce the indexation one level down.

So: which step do you reject? Tell me first whether the chain is invalid, and if so identify the exact move where it fails and explain why that move looked legitimate when I wrote it down; or, if you think it is valid, tell me which premise is cheapest to surrender and what then has to be said about the case in (4). I also want the price spelled out, not just the diagnosis: what does rejecting that step cost me about the appraisal of programmes that are presently on a bad run, meaning the ordinary situation in which everyone is waiting for a predicted effect that has not appeared and the honest description seems to be that the programme is losing ground? And say explicitly whether my classmate's refinement is by itself sufficient to repair (3), or whether it needs further machinery to do that work.
