so, I'm a novelist and I've been going back and fourth with a philosopher friend about what my own book is actually doing, and I'd like a check on whether my working assumptions hang together before I keep defending them.

The setup: the novel has one page of front matter — an author's note — where I, the real me, tell the reader that everything after it is invented and that nobody should go looking for themselves in it. Then the narrator, who is a character, tells her own story for four hundred pages and is, I think, unreliable about the central events.

My commitments, roughly:

(1) when my characters talk they're genuinely doing things — promising, warning, insulting — and I want a promise made in chapter four to be a real obligation inside the story, with real consequences when its broken;
(2) the narrator is a character too, but her say-so doesn't automatically make things true in the story; readers are supposed to work out what actually happened underneath what she reports;
(3) there's nobody behind her. No implied author, no hidden narrating consciousness sorting true from false. The story's facts are whatever the text supports, and the text is just her report plus the dialogue;
(4) the front matter is different in kind: that's a real act I perform on a real reader, not make-believe;
(5) fictional names don't refer to anything. There is no such person as my protagonist, full stop.

Which of these actually conflict, and which just look like they do? Rank them by how badly they hurt rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each real one say what I'd have to give up and what the cheapest fix is. And — this is the novelist part — if two fixes are philosophically equal, tell me which one I can actually still write with.
