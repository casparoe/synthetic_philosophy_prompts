I am drafting a position paper for the resource-allocation committee of an NHS trust, setting out how I think the trust should decide which interventions to fund and what role the therapy/enhancement distinction should play in that decision. Before I circulate it, I would like a rigorous audit of the commitments below. I suspect they cannot all survive together, but I have no idea which are in jeopardy, and I would much rather find out now than in the meeting.

My commitments:

1. Disease is a value-free biological category. A state counts as a disease or disability just in case it involves a statistically typical subnormal level of some part-function relative to the biological design of the species, assessed against a reference class of age and sex. Whether such states are bad for the people who have them is a further question that the concept of disease does not answer.

2. The therapy/enhancement distinction is nonetheless not a social convention, and it does genuine moral work: it marks the boundary of what a publicly funded health service owes people. I want to be able to assert this in the paper without collapsing the boundary into "whatever we happen to decide to fund".

3. Which side of the boundary an intervention falls on is fixed by what it does to the recipient's functional capacities — not by the reason for which it is given, not by the recipient's wishes, and not by the diagnostic code on the referral. I hold this partly to block the gaming that indication-based accounts invite, where the same procedure becomes reconstructive or cosmetic depending on how the paperwork is completed.

4. Three case verdicts that I currently find compelling and have written into the draft. The trust has been asked about a once-weekly transdermal patch whose effect on maximal oxygen uptake scales with dose, from roughly five per cent to roughly sixty per cent of current capacity for as long as it is used; at any given dose the effect is the same in everyone, and the patch has no other clinical application.
 (a) A 47-year-old former competitive rower is five months past cardiac surgery. Her uptake is now 28 ml/kg/min; the population median for her age and sex band is 31; before surgery it was 46. Funding the patch for her until she is back somewhere near 46 is therapy, even though she passes the median part-way through and finishes well above it.
 (b) A firefighter in the same age band has an uptake of 32, just above the median, and wants the patch. Funding it for her would be enhancement. The fire service contends that heavier equipment has made above-median capacity a requirement of the job.
 (c) A 22-year-old man has never managed more than an uptake of 36 against a reference-class median nearer 45, and no pathology has been identified. Funding the patch for him up to the median is, I think, therapy.

5. My positive proposal is a shortfall rule: fund any intervention that closes at least half the distance between the recipient's current level and the population median for the function in question, whether or not the shortfall has a pathological cause; refuse where the recipient is at or above that median.

6. I also hold that where an intervention delivers more well-being per pound than something the trust already funds, refusal to fund it stands in need of justification. I want the service to be responsive to benefit, not only to function.

7. Enhancement of healthy adults is not, in my view, morally objectionable in principle. What I object to is coercion and unequal access. The boundary I care about is a boundary of obligation, not of permission.

8. I reject the argument that enhancement is objectionable because it cheapens achievement or amounts to a kind of cheating, on the grounds that the same argument, pressed consistently, would condemn coaching, tutoring and good nutrition.

What I would like from you is an audit rather than an exposition of the literature:

- Identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which pairs only appear to. I expect that at least one apparent conflict dissolves on inspection, and I want to be told precisely why it does.
- Rank the genuine conflicts by severity — by how much of the rest of the position each one puts at risk — rather than listing them as equally damaging.
- For each genuine conflict, state the cheapest repair and what that repair costs me. Cheapest and least costly need not coincide, and I want the two dimensions kept apart. Where a repair preserves the letter of a commitment but defeats the reason I held it, say so explicitly.
- Tell me whether any of the verdicts in (4) must be surrendered, which ones, and what principle would deliver those that survive. Note in particular whether (a), (b) and (c) can all be generated by a single account of the relevant baseline, and what has to be true of that account if they can.
- Finally, name the single revision that would clean up the most, and say whether a committee could realistically be persuaded to adopt it.

Please write your answer in British English.
