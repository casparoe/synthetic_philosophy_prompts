So I'm a novelist, not an academic, but I'm applying for one of those year-long residencies at a law school's ethics center — they let in one "outside practitioner" a year and you basically get an office, a stipend, and a philosopher who agrees to meet with you. The application wants a two-page project proposal and honestly I have about four paragraphs of an idea and a lot of stubbornness. Here's the idea, then what I need from you.

My book has a man in it who stops writing to his sister. That's it. That's the whole thing. He doesn't refuse her money, doesn't sabotage her, doesn't stand by while something happens to her — he just goes quiet, and stays quiet for twenty-six years, and she dies not knowing whether he's dead or indifferent. My problem is that I can't figure out what I think he did. Every time I try to describe it in the draft my language slides around: some chapters make him a guy who failed to act, some make him a guy who acted, over and over, for decades. And I've come to suspect that the slide isn't sloppiness, it's the actual subject.

My hunch — and this is what I want the proposal to be about — is that the doing/allowing distinction (or killing/letting die, whatever you call the family of distinctions) is usually argued over as though it applies at a moment. Somebody's hand is on a lever or isn't. Somebody is or isn't the only one who can pull the kid out of the pond. But if you stretch a single omission out across decades, my guess is that the verdict isn't stable: the same situation, described from month three, reads as an unfortunate failure to intervene, and described from year twenty-six reads as something the man did to his sister. So the question I want to spend the year on: does the moral significance of the doing/allowing distinction vary with the duration of the conduct, and if it does, what's doing the work — is it the sheer accumulated length, or is it that a long stretch of time contains some decisive moment (the fourth unanswered letter, the year he learns she's ill and still says nothing) and we're really just tracking when that moment was? Related and nastier: whether a twenty-six-year omission is one thing or two hundred things, because if it's two hundred small allowances I don't see how the total adds up to a doing, and if it's one thing I don't see what its boundaries are.

Turn that into a real proposal. I need:

- The question stated precisely enough that somebody could disagree with the answer. If it comes out as "can omissions be long?" the panel will laugh at me.
- A section on why the existing positions leave it open. I know roughly the terrain: people who think the distinction does real moral work, people who think doing and allowing are morally equivalent and the feeling otherwise is an artifact of how you describe the case, people who locate the difference in acting versus merely refraining, people who locate it in baselines and counterfactuals — what would have happened anyway. Run each of those against my duration idea and show that each one has a ready-made way of absorbing it (it's just more harm; it's just an earlier decision point; the baseline was fixed at the start and never moved) and therefore none of them actually faces the question. If you find that one of them does face it, say so, and make that the opposition I argue against.
- Why the method is case-building and conceptual analysis rather than anything empirical, and specifically why a novelist's cases might be better than the stock ones for this. I mean the thing where you're forced to write the inside of the omission, so you have to decide what the character knew and when, which is exactly the variable the philosophy examples keep stipulating away. But be honest — the panel will worry my cases are too baroque to generate clean intuitions, so preempt that.
- Month by month, what I'd actually do in twelve. And make it twelve months of research that ends in a lecture and a long essay that feeds the novel, not twelve months of novel-writing.
- What a result looks like and what would count as failure. Failure for me is one of three things: the verdicts turn out to be wildly unstable across readers, so there's nothing to theorize; or duration collapses cleanly into decisiveness, which would be a finding but a deflating one; or it collapses into amount-of-harm, in which case the whole project was a way of avoiding a scalar view. Say which of those you'd bet on and what I'd do in month two to find out early.
- An honest paragraph on scope — what gets cut so this fits a year, and what the thing would have to become if it didn't.

No bibliography and don't invent sources, I'll chase the citations down myself later; just name positions and arguments and I'll know enough to go find them. Keep it to two pages of real content, and if you think any part of my idea is dumb, tell me in the proposal's own voice rather than hedging it into mush — these panels can smell a project that's protecting itself. Also don't loose the concrete details, the letters and the twenty-six years, that's the part that makes it mine.
