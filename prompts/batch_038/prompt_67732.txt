I cover science and statistics for a magazine and I've been going back and forth with a source — a statistician who does methods review work — about the whole replication mess, and he handed me an argument I can't get out of. He thinks it shows the decade-long fight over whether findings are "real" was a fight about nothing. Its laid out like this:

(1) In psychology and biomedicine no true effect is ever exactly zero — Meehl's crud factor. The null of "no effect at all" is false in essentially every study anybody has ever run.

(2) If the null is always false, then rejecting it isn't a discovery. It's a prediction that was guaranteed to come out right.

(3) So a significant p-value carries no information about whether there's an effect, and a bigger sample just gets you to the same verdict faster.

(4) The Bayesian doesn't escape. Her posterior for the effect size is a continuous density, so the posterior probability that the effect is exactly zero is 0 whatever the data turned out to be. She can't accept a point null either.

(5) And the Bayesian who rigs it — puts a point mass at zero next to a spread-out alternative — gets an answer that swings with how wide she made the spread. Same data, same p-value, verdict flips, depending on a scale paramter she picked off a shelf. So that version isn't measuring the evidence either.

(6) Therefore neither framework can ever say an effect is real or unreal, and all the replication arguing was about a question with no answer.

I want to say (6) is flatly wrong, because replications do come back smaller than the originals and that difference obviously means something — but I can't locate the step doing the damage. Which one do I reject, why did it look fine, and what does rejecting it cost me? I'd rather keep (1) and (5) if I can; (5) seems just true and several people I trust say the prior width really does move the answer.
