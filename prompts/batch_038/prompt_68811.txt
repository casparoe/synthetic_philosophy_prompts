My dissertation seminar this term is on free will, and I come to it from a computational neuroscience background: I read evidence-accumulation models natively — drift rates, bounds, decision variables, race models of response inhibition — and I am used to the distinction between a signal that predicts a choice and one that constitutes it. Please restate Libet's readiness-potential argument, as faithfully as you can, in that vocabulary: the finding that preparatory brain activity builds up well before subjects report any urge to move, together with the influential counter-reading on which that build-up is not a decision already taken but a stochastic drift towards a threshold which the subject becomes aware of only at or after the crossing. Include Libet's fallback — the veto exercised in the final moments before movement — which I suspect can be rendered only as a second accumulator racing the first, and tell me whether that rendering grants the veto everything he wanted from it. Then give me the residue report: what translates without loss, what survives only if I bend my terminology, and what cannot be said in it at all, above all the thought that the subject authors the act rather than merely hosting the threshold crossing. I want your considered view on whether that residue marks a genuine limit of my framework or an unclarity in the original argument.
