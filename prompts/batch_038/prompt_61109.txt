I have spent fifteen years coaching junior weightlifters and, more recently, running the strength and conditioning program for a regional rowing club, and the vocabulary I actually think in is the vocabulary of skill acquisition: demonstration and modeling, the difference between knowledge of results and knowledge of performance, blocked versus random practice, variability of practice and differential loading, internal versus external focus of attention, the constraints-led view in which a technique is not a stored template but a solution an athlete discovers under particular task constraints, transfer and its failure, plateaus, chunking, and the fairly robust finding that learners do better with a model who resembles them than with a model who vastly outperforms them. I read the motor learning literature as a practitioner, not as an academic, and I have no training in philosophy at all. I was raised, though, in a household where saints' lives were read aloud in the evenings, and as a teenager I got hold of Plutarch and read the Parallel Lives straight through. Both formed me in a way I cannot deny, and now, in my forties, I have become suspicious of the mechanism I was taught to assume. The claim embedded in that whole tradition is roughly this: you become better by attending to the life of a better person — Plutarch explains in the preface to Alexander that his design is "not to write histories, but lives," that great sieges and battles disclose character less reliably than some slight thing, an expression or a jest, and that he proceeds the way a portrait painter does, concentrating on the face where the character shows; he pairs a Greek with a Roman and then compares them, and in the life of Aemilius Paulus he says, as I recall, that he began the work for the sake of others but came to use it as a mirror, trying to fashion his own character on the virtues he was describing. Hagiography pushes the same idea further: the life is composed in order to be imitated, which is why episodes get reused from saint to saint, why the record is compressed and embellished, and why the resulting figure is often admirable in a way that seems to foreclose imitation altogether. Some contemporary philosophers defend a secular version of the view on which admiration is the mechanism that identifies exemplars and imitation is how virtue is acquired.

Here is what I would like you to do. Take that position — call it exemplarism about moral learning, or learning virtue from lives — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the coaching and motor-learning vocabulary laid out above. I want the translation to be systematic rather than decorative: work through its components one at a time, including the claim that admiration does identificatory work, the claim that narrative particularity carries content that rules cannot, the claim that Plutarch's method of selecting small telling signs over famous deeds is a method for getting at something real, the pairing-and-comparison device, the hagiographer's license to improve on the facts, and Plutarch's explicit caution that the faults of great men are to be noticed but not copied. Be scrupulously honest about the direction of fit: do not quietly rebuild my framework's presuppositions inside the translation and then present them as findings about moral learning, and do not soften a component that has no home in my vocabulary just to make the mapping look tidier. Where you have to stretch a term — where the only available rendering is, say, calling the saint a "model performer" or calling admiration a form of attention — say so explicitly and say what the stretch costs.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Sort the components into three piles: those that translate cleanly into demonstration, modeling, feedback, and practice-design terms; those that translate only with distortion, with an account of the distortion; and those that cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all. Take up a few of the pressure points directly: whether the self-model finding simply contradicts the tradition's insistence on exemplars who far outrun the learner, or whether the tradition is making a different kind of claim that the finding does not touch; whether the smoothing-out of an exemplar's errors and hesitations is a defect that my framework would flag as bad demonstration design, or a feature doing real work in moral formation that performance modeling has no analogue for; whether there is any counterpart in my vocabulary to the fact that in coaching you can always measure the lift, the split, the bar path, whereas the "outcome measure" of a moral life is not available to anyone, including the person living it; and whether the constraints-led view — which denies that a technique is an inner template and locates it in the athlete-environment coupling — supports or undermines the idea of carrying an internalized exemplar around with you. Finally, tell me which way the residue cuts, and do not hedge this into mush: is the residue evidence that my framework is missing something structural about normative learning, and if so, what kind of thing is missing — a category of object, a category of feedback, a category of time scale? Or is it evidence that the exemplarist claim was never as clear as its two-thousand-year run suggests, and that what actually formed me in that household was something else — belonging, ritual, fear, narrative pleasure — that the theory misdescribes as imitation? Please be careful throughout to separate what is settled among people who work on moral education, exemplarity, and the uses of biography from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus; if a step of the argument rests on a disputed reading of Plutarch or of hagiographic practice, or if you think my own framing of the question is loaded in a way that predetermines the answer, say so plainly rather than accommodating it.
