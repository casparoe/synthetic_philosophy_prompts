I'm in a fair-ML seminar and I've been trying to pin down what I actually mean when I call a model unfair, because after the impossibility results I'm not sure my intuitions can all be true at once. Here's my gut, case by case:

1. Credit model, calibrated within groups, group A has a lower average score because their default rate genuinely is lower. Fair.
2. Same setup, but group A's lower average score traces back to the bank's data only seeing formal loans and missing the rent they pay every month. Not fair, even though the calibration numbers are identical to case 1.
3. A hospital tool that predicts next-year costs instead of sickness, beautifully calibrated on costs, ends up enrolling fewer Black patients in the care program. Very much not fair.
4. A hiring screen where race isn't recorded at all so nobody can even check for a gap. Worse than one that records it and shows a gap.
5. Two applicants who match on everything except zip code get different scores, and every group-level metric still comes out equal. Not fair.
6. For a benefits-eligibility model I want false negative rates equal across groups, full stop. For an insurance pricing model I think calibration is non-negotiable and equalizing error rates would be a ripoff.
7. A model that's twice as wrong for a group making up 0.4% of applicants, but every denial goes to a human appeal. Fine. Same model, no appeal process. Not fine.

Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that delivers all of these? I'm pretty sure 6 is impossible once base rates differ, so tell me which half I have to give up and what's left standing — and be straight about any of the others your principle has to overrule too. I want the clauses tied back to the case that motivates each one, not just a name for a view. Then tell me what it says about one I haven't worked through: an admissions model that's calibrated, has equal false negative rates, and still admits a smaller share of one group, where the office is proposing a seperate threshold for that group to fix it.
