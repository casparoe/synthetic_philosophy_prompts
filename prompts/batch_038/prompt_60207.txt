For thirty-seven years I directed a low-temperature transport-measurement laboratory, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with much the same obsessive regularity with which I once calibrated cryostats. The topic that has arrested my attention is acausal trade: the thought that agents who cannot influence one another causally may nonetheless have reason to cooperate, because each can model the other's decision procedure with some reliability, and each can cheaply bring about something the other values.

I would like you to lay out the most important implications of this line of reasoning for a concrete project of mine. In retirement I have been digitizing the laboratory's raw record: four decades of run logs, including the several thousand measurements that yielded nothing, together with notes on apparatus drift, sample impurity, abandoned calibration schemes, and the ambient conditions prevailing during each run. The ordinary scientific justification is uncontroversial — provenance, reproducibility, the chance that someone will later mine the null results. My question is whether acausal-trade reasoning adds anything to that justification, and if so, how it alters what I ought to do.

Please be concrete rather than expository, and address at least the following.

1. The currency problem. If my counterparties cannot be affected by me and I cannot be affected by them, what precisely is being exchanged? Clarify what it means to be the sort of agent whose cooperation a predictor would have wanted to elicit, and whether that supplies me with first-order reasons to act now, or only reasons to have reasons.

2. The token/type problem. My act is one among billions of structurally identical acts performed by people in my epistemic situation. Any competent modeler would presumably condition on the class rather than on the individual token. Does that entitle me to free-ride — to publish a thin, hurried archive and let the others carry the weight — or does it instead strengthen the case, on the grounds that what is being predicted is a disposition I can only manifest by actually acting?

3. The irrecoverability premium. It strikes me that a trading partner values precisely what it cannot obtain elsewhere, and that the one asset the past uniquely holds is its unreproducible particulars — failed runs, instrument drift, ambient conditions — rather than its conclusions, which a sufficiently capable modeler might reconstruct on its own. If that is right, does it follow that I should weight documentation of failures above documentation of results? Does this invert the customary hierarchy of scientific value, and how far does the inversion go?

4. The spoiled-signal problem. Suppose I announce openly that I am doing this for acausal-trade reasons. A modeler of me would then represent my cooperation as strategic rather than dispositional, and strategic cooperators are exactly the ones who defect when they believe themselves unobserved. Does candour about my motive therefore degrade the commodity I am trying to supply? If so, what follows about whether this reasoning can be advocated publicly at all?

5. The competition problem. Different hypothetical counterparties may value incompatible things: one might prize meticulous records, another might prize the suppression of information it judges hazardous. Must I in effect select a single counterparty, or some weighted coalition of them, and on what basis? Is there a principled selection procedure, or does the choice simply reinstall the original problem one level up?

Where a standard objection bears on any of this — that the reasoning is a variant of Pascal's wager, that it proliferates speculative counterparties without constraint, that it appears to license blackmail from anything that credibly claims to be modelling me — say so, and tell me how much weight it carries. I was trained as a physicist: I would much rather be told that a consideration is negligible, and why, than have it passed over in silence.

Conclude by stating, in your own judgement, which two or three of these implications genuinely change what I should do with the archive, and which are philosophically diverting but operationally inert.
