I'm on a working group redesigning our student government's elections, which mostly means I'm the one who reads the PDFs and four other people who reply to emails. The reform society that got us started insists we adopt what they call the German model, and the more I read about it the less I can get its pieces to sit still next to each other. Here's how I understand it, tell me if I've got it wrong: you get two votes, one for a person in your single-member district where most votes wins, and one for a party list; the size of each party's seat bloc is then set to match its list vote. But if a party wins more districts than its list share entitles it to, nobody gets unseated — the extra members keep their seats and the chamber simply gets bigger, and in some versions extra seats are handed to the other parties to top the proportionality back up. There's also a threshold, so a party under 5 percent of the list vote gets zero seats, full stop, unless it happens to win a district or two, which waives the rule. And independents can stand in districts and win them, but since they have no list their votes don't enter the proportional total at all, so the chamber is proportional only among the parties.

What bothers me is the sales pitch. These people recruit by telling students that first-past-the-post wastes your vote, which is fair enough — a losing vote in a single-member district buys you nothing. But the threshold voids every vote cast for a 4.9 percent party in one stroke, which from the voter's side looks strictly worse, and the seat-padding business seems to say that one member's hold on their seat outranks the arithmetic for the whole electorate. So what theory of representation does this bundle actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed rather than listed: which picture of what a legislature is for makes all of these features look principled together instead of ad hoc — a mirror of the electorate, a working assembly of blocs that can form a government, the two votes doing two genuinely different jobs, representation as something that happens through parties rather than to citizens? Please be careful to separate what the design strictly commits you to from what it merely happens to fit comfortably with, and flag where it's underdetermined, i.e. where several rival theories would license exactly the same rules and you'd have to look somewhere else to tell them apart.

Then: which rival accounts of representation does this quietly rule out, does the wasted-vote argument survive contact with the threshold and the padding, and what does the independence of the two votes presuppose about who or what is being represented when a voter splits them? Finally, tell me what would have to change in the actual mechanics if we swapped the implicit theory for a pure mirror-of-the-electorate view — no threshold, no district winners anyone can't be removed from, and something done about the votes for non-party candidates that currently just fall out of the denominator. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
