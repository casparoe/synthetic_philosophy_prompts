I'm prepping the last session of a reading group on free will — the session is about which *variety* of free will is actually worth wanting, rather than the usual is-it-compatible-with-determinism stalemate — and I want a dialogue I can hand out at the end of it. Roughly 1,000 words, no scene-setting, no weather, just the exchange.

Two people, and both have to be good at this. One is a Frankfurt-style compatibilist: free will worth wanting is the capacity for your will to be your own, for you to act from desires you endorse and identify with, and the ability to do otherwise drops out as something nobody should really be chasing, because if a choice is genuinely undetermined then nothing about the agent explains why it happened rather than the other thing — which is randomness in a nicer hat. The other is a source-incompatibilist of the Kane variety: what makes praise and blame appropriate is that the agent is the ultimate originator, self-forming wills, the buck genuinely stopping; attributability cant be enough, because you could program a machine to endorse its own desires all day and it still wouldn't be praiseworthy for anything. Each should also get to press their best shot at the other — the compatibilist with the regress (ultimate origination requires being responsible for the character that produced the choice, and then for whatever produced that, forever), the libertarian with the point that a freedom available to a sufficiently well-tuned automaton isn't the freedom we're after.

Now the bit I actually care about, which is a case I made up, and I want the whole dialogue to bend around it:

Nora took an apprenticeship years ago. Nothing settled that choice — it was genuinely open, undetermined, the sort of thing the source-incompatibilist wants. The training it involved has since shaped her character so completely that every action she now performs is fully determined by it, and in every case she does exactly what her own endorsed second-order volitions dictate. By the compatibilist's lights she is perfectly free. By the libertarian's, she was free once, at eighteen, and has not been since. But there is no fork left at the moment of action.

So: does the libertarian have to concede that origination transfers downstream, that Nora's ancient undetermined choice is a gift the compatibilist gets to claim, so that she has both varieties at once? Or does the transfer fail — and if it fails, say exactly why, since what she does now is the fruit of the one thing she was ultimately responsible for. And on the other side: does the compatibilist have to admit that they'd rather have Nora-with-the-old-fork than Nora-without-it, which looks like wanting origination after all, just at arm's length. I want them to fight about whether the fork has to sit at the moment of action or whether it's enough that it sits somewhere in the agent's own history — someone will want to reach for tracing conditions here, and the other should say why that move either rescues or begs the question.

Rules for the writing: no strawmen, they have to answer each other's actual moves rather than reload, and each of them must be made to concede one real point — not a fake one. Don't let them discover halfway through that the disagreement is merely verbal; I want it to stay substantive, and if you honestly think it can't, say so in the note rather than fudging it in the dialogue.

And I dont want a polite draw at the end. Take a side. Decide who comes out ahead and make that show in the last few exchanges — not by anyone capitulating, but by which objection is left standing unanswered and who has to change the subject. Then add a short paragraph afterwards telling me who you backed and why, plus anywhere you bent the arguments, stacked the case, or leaned on a contested reading of one of these positions to get the shape you wanted.

British English throughout, spellings included.
