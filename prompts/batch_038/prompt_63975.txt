Retired last spring after thirty-eight years in sensory physiology — mostly pain scaling, magnitude estimation, that world. Now I've got time to read philosophy and I've fallen down a rabbit hole about whether you can compare one person's utility with another's. My Tuesday reading group made me present next week, so I built the following argument for the negative side, and it went over badly, because it seems to prove a lot more than I want and nobody in the room could tell me where the leak is. Maybe you can.

(1) A number is a measurement only if there's a scale behind it: a zero and a unit that can at least in principle be calibrated against something.
(2) For a claim comparing A's utility and B's utility to be true or false rather than merely expressive, there has to be a single scale on which both sit. Otherwise "A's gain outweighs B's loss" isn't a claim, its a noise.
(3) Calibrating a shared unit would require something that pins both intensities down at once — a common reference whose experienced magnitude you can hold fixed while you read off each person's scale.
(4) No such reference exists. Two nervous systems, two histories, no way to be inside both simultaneously. The only things you can correlate across people are behaviors: reports, wincing, choice, willingness to pay. And behavior is evidence about dispositions, not about intensity. I know this one from the inside — I watched "seven out of ten" fail to track anything stable in the same subject between Tuesday and Thursday, and that was with a trained subject and a controlled stimulus.
(5) So interpersonal utility comparisons aren't merely unreliable, they're meaningless: there's no fact there for them to get right.
(6) Now run the same machine inside one person across time. Me at twenty-five and me at seventy-one are, physically and psychologically, pretty nearly different organisms, and the anchor between them is exactly as absent as the anchor between me and my wife. My memories of old pains are reports — reports to myself, but reports all the same. If (4) disqualifies them there, it disqualifies them here.
(7) So intrapersonal comparisons across time are meaningless too.
(8) That can't stand. (7) would sink not just utilitarianism but every decision anybody makes about their own future — the brutal week of side effects taken for the remission, saving instead of spending, even the plain statement that this headache is worse than the one an hour ago. It would also retroactively invalidate my whole career: we compared numbers within subjects across sessions and thought we were measuring something.
(9) Therefore (5) is false, and interpersonal comparisons are meaningful after all.

So: where does it break? I'm not sure whether there's a specific slide hiding between two of these steps — an equivocation, or a standard smuggled in from somewhere it doesn't belong — or whether the thing is valid and I just have to swallow one of the premises I find attractive. Walk me through the candidates and price them. Two things I especially want pressed on:

First, (6). Is there anything about a single persisting organism — one body, one memory chain, first-person access to both ends — that genuinely supplies the anchor two organisms can't have? If yes, does that same feature do any residual work in the interpersonal case, given that my wife and I share a species, a pain circuit layout, and roughly one environment? Or is (6) just a bad analogy that I dressed up in lab clothes?

Second, (2). I built my career on the assumption that a number without a calibration procedure is decoration. Am I importing a metrology standard from the physical sciences that utility was never supposed to satisfy — is there a route to comparability that gets you something usable without ever producing a shared zero and unit? If so, sketch what such a route looks like, and tell me plainly what it commits me to and what it gives up, because "it's ordinal but we can still add it up" is the kind of move I want to see the receipts on.

For what it's worth, if you make me choose I'd rather keep (4) than (9). Forty years of looking at data has made me allergic to uncalibrated scales. But I'd rather hear that my argument is bad and why than that it's fine.
