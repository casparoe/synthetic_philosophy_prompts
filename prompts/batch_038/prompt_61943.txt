I sit on a nine-member charter commission, appointed by a county judge and confirmed by the state senate, that must deliver the governing charter for a newly authorized Regional Flood Resilience District to the legislature by 1 March. The engineering and finance provisions are settled. The franchise provisions are not, and they are the ones I have to circulate an opinion on.

The district will own and operate levees, pump stations and a gated channel across parts of three municipalities. It will fund itself by an assessment on the assessed value of every parcel inside its boundary, and it will hold two powers that make composition explosive. First, it can designate upstream retention basins that will be deliberately flooded in order to spare the downstream city, with compensation set by a formula the affected landowners regard as far below market. Second, it can impose land-use restrictions inside an "influence area" extending two miles beyond its boundary, where its works will divert runoff onto other people's property.

The constituencies, with the figures staff has given me:

- The downstream city: about 48,000 residents, roughly 60 percent of them renters, who receive essentially all of the protection and pay the assessment indirectly, through rent.
- The upstream township: 310 residents and 1,200 separately assessed parcels, 180 of which sit in the designated retention zone. The owner-operators are mostly fourth-generation farmers, and the township would be a rounding error in any district-wide popular vote.
- Four corporate owners — a logistics campus, a data center, a grain terminal, a rail yard — holding 38 percent of the district's assessed value and thus owing 38 percent of every assessment, while owning no residences and employing almost no one who lives inside the boundary.
- The adjoining town of Halloran, 9,000 residents, outside the boundary but inside the influence area, whose council has offered to petition for annexation. It has made the offer contingent on seeing our franchise rule first: under a residents-only rule, annexation would swamp the upstream township; under a property-weighted rule, the town would be diluted by the corporate parcels. Its decision to join therefore depends on the decision we are trying to make.
- Roughly 11 percent of the district's residents are not citizens and vote in no American election. The enabling statute ties the district franchise to qualified state electors, so including them means asking the legislature for an exception, which counsel warns could sink the charter outright.
- Nobody, yet: the works are rated for a 100-year event, and the levee height chosen this spring fixes the residual risk carried by residents who will be alive in 2090 and who can neither vote now nor be represented now.

Three proposals are live. (A) Residents only, one person one vote, seven at-large seats; counsel calls this the only option that is clearly constitutional. (B) A dual roll: the resident franchise plus a landowner roll weighted by assessed value, electing two of seven seats, relying on the Supreme Court's tolerance of property-weighted voting in limited-purpose water districts. (C) An affected-interest franchise: anyone on whom the district can impose a burden votes on the decisions that impose it, with the board empowered to add electors by resolution — which would sweep in the corporate owners and Halloran's residents. If we deadlock, the statute's fallback charter applies, which is (A) with no protections of any kind for the upstream township.

One colleague's objection has to be answered rather than waved off: because we are appointed and not elected, we lack standing to define a demos more generously than the statute requires, and any attempt to do so is self-undermining, since the body that decides who may vote is the one body whose own authority nobody voted for.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the commission, about 1,200 words. It should state a single recommendation specific enough to be put to a vote — who votes, how votes are weighted, how the seven seats are constituted, what we tell Halloran about its annexation offer, whether we go to the legislature about the noncitizen residents, and what, if anything, we do for people not yet born. It should give the reasons for that recommendation by arguing from the principles themselves — whose interests count, who is subject to which power, whether an all-affected standard can be operationalized at all or collapses into a regress — rather than from who has said what; I will be reading this on a plane with no library at hand. It should set out the strongest case against the recommendation in terms its proponents would recognize. And it should close with draft language for the two or three provisions we would actually vote on, plus the fallback position I should concede if the commission will not carry the recommendation. Where you judge that the question exceeds the commission's mandate and can only be resolved by the legislature, say so explicitly, and say what we should ask the legislature for.
