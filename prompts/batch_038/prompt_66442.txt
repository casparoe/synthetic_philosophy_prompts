I am a third-year student in a philosophy of science seminar that has just moved on to the epistemology of astrobiology: how one is supposed to reason from observations that are single, unrepeatable, and confounded with instrumental error and with abiotic mechanisms that mimic the interesting ones. Our discussions have gone in circles, and I have decided that before arguing about anyone else's position I should try to systematize my own. What follows are my considered verdicts on a set of cases. I am confident about most of them; several, as I will flag, pull against each other, and I suspect that at least one will have to go.

1. The strong narrowband radio burst recorded at Ohio State in 1977 — right frequency band, right profile, exactly what one would want, and never detected again despite follow-up searches: in my view, not evidence of anything. It should not have moved a careful person at all.
2. A signal of the same strength, bandwidth, and frequency, repeating from the same coordinates twenty times with the same dispersion measure each time: this I would take extremely seriously. And I want to say the difference from (1) is epistemic rather than merely practical — not that (2) is easier to check, but that (2) carries information (1) lacked.
3. Simultaneous detection of abundant O₂ and CH₄ in the atmosphere of a temperate rocky exoplanet, at concentrations that photochemistry alone cannot sustain: strong evidence of life. Note that this, too, is a single unrepeatable observation, and I feel no temptation to downgrade it the way I downgraded (1).
4. Abundant O₂ alone, on a rocky planet orbiting an M dwarf: very weak. My understanding is that photolysis of water followed by hydrogen escape can build up an oxygen-rich atmosphere abiotically in that setting, and I think that possibility does most of the work here. The observation is of the same kind as in (3), only with one component missing.
5. Mineralogical context plus a pronounced carbon-isotope fractionation in a returned Martian sample: much stronger than a claim of microfossil morphology in the very same sample, even though I concede the morphology is far more vivid to the imagination. I cannot presently say why. "Shape is cheap" is a slogan, not a principle.
6. A mid-infrared excess around a single star, of the sort one might expect from a large warm circumstellar structure: I would file this under dust and move on. A comparable excess across an entire galaxy, with no plausible dust interpretation available: I would treat this as close to decisive. Same signature, different scale, radically different verdict.
7. Life arising on Earth within a few hundred million years of the surface becoming habitable: I think this is evidence that abiogenesis is not rare — and I also think it is no evidence at all, because any observer asking the question would find that life got started early on their planet. I hold both of these simultaneously, which I recognize is unstable.
8. This is the pair I cannot reconcile. On one hand: after decades of searching, no technosignature has been found, and I think that ought to lower my confidence that there are many technological civilizations in the galaxy — absence of evidence has to become evidence of absence at some point, or the search is pointless. On the other hand: those searches have covered a minuscule fraction of the relevant parameter space in frequency, direction, time, and signal type, and I want to say that a search that thin cannot license an inference in either direction. Separately: the fact that we have never been visited strikes me as telling, yet if the zoo hypothesis is so much as possible — civilizations that exist and deliberately refrain from contact — then the absence of visitors is exactly what we should expect whether or not they are out there, and so by my own reasoning it should be worthless as evidence. I find that I do not in fact treat it as worthless.

Here is my tentative principle, which I know is inadequate: an observation is evidence of extraterrestrial life to the degree that it is much more probable if life exists than if it does not, and it counts as a genuine detection only when every known abiotic mechanism capable of producing it has been excluded. The first clause seems to fail to separate (1) from (2), since a single pulse is not obviously much less probable under the transmitter hypothesis than a repeated one; and the failure looks to me like a failure of the second clause rather than the first — but "excluded" is doing unexplained work there, since in (3) nothing has been excluded and yet I am confident. The principle is also simply silent on (7) and (8), where what is being updated on includes our own existence as observers.

What I would like from you is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have never seen and get a determinate answer. For each clause, tell me which of the numbered verdicts motivates it. Then tell me what the resulting account says about cases I have not listed — specifically: an artificial-looking geometric structure in a single image of another system's debris disk, observed once and not re-observed; a statistically significant excess of an industrially produced compound in the integrated spectrum of an unresolved exoplanet, where the compound also has a known abiotic route at high temperature; and a claim of life resting on one unrepeatable analysis of a sample that no longer exists. Finally, and most importantly, say plainly which of my verdicts the account cannot accommodate, and which single one you would advise me to abandon rather than complicate the principle in order to save. If the honest answer is that (7) and half of (8) require separate machinery — that reasoning about life elsewhere is partly self-locating in a way that reasoning about, say, a particle physics excess is not — I would much rather be told that directly than have it smoothed over for the sake of a unified story.
