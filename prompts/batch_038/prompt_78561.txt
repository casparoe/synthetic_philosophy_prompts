Fighting with my adviser about debate as a scalable oversight method, and I wrote down an arguement against it that I now can't dismantle, and I can't tell whether it's refuting debate or refuting computability theory. Here are the steps:

(1) The core promise of debate is that if two very capable AIs argue for opposite answers and a weak judge picks whichever one argued better, then under optimal play the true answer wins — an optimal player can always assemble a defense of the truth that the other side can't undo, whereas a lie gets picked apart by the opponent eventualy.

(2) Nothing in that story caps how smart the debaters are. The only fixed constraint is the judge: polynomial time, roughly human, and the same judge for every question.

(3) So for any question a sufficiently smart reasoner could settle at all, debate hands the answer to the weak judge.

(4) A sufficiently smart reasoner can settle questions like whether this specific program halts, or whether this specific formal system is consistent. (This is the step I keep wanting to attack, but it seems plainly true — hand me one program and a smart enough reasoner tells me if it stops.)

(5) So a debate judged in polynomial time can settle whether an arbitrary program halts.

(6) That's the one thing we're certain no finite procedure can do.

(7) Therefore (1) is false: truthful play doesn't reliably win, and debate is not a general fix for the alignment problem.
