I'm TAing a cross-listed seminar this term — half environmental history, half philosophy — and the unit I'm running in week six is on "collapse" as a concept. The reading is Chinua Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1958), paired against the standard argument about whether societies actually fail or just get reorganized into something we don't recognize. My co-TA wants to use the novel as the poster child for the skeptic camp, on the grounds that Umuofia isn't destroyed so much as re-attached to a different sovereignty, and that the real collapse in the book is a documentary one: the last scene is a British official deciding how much column inches Okonkwo is worth. I think that's clever but also kind of a cop-out, and I don't want to hand the students a reading that turns a novel about conquest into a seminar gotcha. So I'd like you to actually do the reading properly and let me see whether it survives.

Here's the lens I want it held up to. Roughly three positions are in play in the collapse literature. One is the diminishing-returns view: complexity is expensive, and when the marginal payoff on maintaining it drops far enough, people quietly defect, and what looks from outside like a catastrophic fall is from inside just everyone stopping paying the bills — collapse as a rational exit, not a failure. That's the one that says the interesting question isn't why the system fell but why anyone stayed. Second is the self-inflicted-environment view, the one behind the usual teaching cases (the eastern Mediterranean palace economies at the end of the Bronze Age, the Classic Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui): a society overshoots its resource base, its elites fail to perceive the problem in time, and the whole thing goes. Third is the skeptic or transformation view, which charges that "collapse" is a term imposed by observers who quietly identify a society with its ruling stratum, its monuments, and its record-keeping, so that when the palace stops issuing tablets the world is declared over while the farmers down the road go on planting yams and paying taxes to someone new. The skeptics also press a subject-of-the-predicate worry: collapse of *what*? A polity, a way of life, a class, a population, an archive? Pick a different subject and you get a different verdict, and the standard rhetoric slides between subjects without noticing. The novel is unusually good for testing this because it stages all of it at once — there's the borrowed title from Yeats's "The Second Coming," which imports a European anxiety about civilizational disintegration onto an Igbo village; there's Obierika's bitter diagnosis near the end, to the effect that the white man came quietly with his religion, won the clan's brothers, and has put a knife on the things that held them together so that they have fallen apart; and there's the District Commissioner at the very close, filing Okonkwo's suicide away as a paragraph rather than a chapter of his planned book on the pacification of the primitive tribes of the Lower Niger.

So, concretely: which of those three frames does the novel instantiate, and where does it refuse all of them? I'd like you to deal with the fact that the environmental frame has almost nothing to grab here — nobody overfarmed anything, the yams keep coming, the soil isn't the problem — and to say whether that makes the book a counterexample to the standard case list or just a case that was misfiled as a "collapse" when it's really a conquest. Then the defection question, which is where I think the interesting argument is: the first converts are precisely the people the old order burdened or excluded, the untitled men, the outcasts handed the plot in the Evil Forest, the woman who'd lost four sets of twins to exposure, and Nwoye, who's looking for something to soothe the killing of Ikemefuna. Is that a textbook instance of the diminishing-returns story — a package that offers better terms to the people paying the most for the existing arrangement? Or does framing it that way insult the book, by making the mission look like a Pareto improvement and converting the imprisonment, head-shaving, whipping and extortion of the six leaders, and the beheaded messenger at the end into a transaction? I want the honest cost of the analogy spelled out, not waved at. And then the subject problem: is Obierika's verdict the novel's, or one grieving character's, and does Achebe's borrowing of the Yeats line commit him to a rise-and-fall plot shape that the book's own texture — the markets still running, the harvests still being planted, the new church and courts being themselves a *more* complex order rather than a simpler one — refuses? Finally, tell me whether the Commissioner's paragraph is Achebe's comment on collapse-writing as a genre, i.e. that the fall of a world is a formatting decision made by whoever holds the pen, and whether that same point rebounds on the academic skeptics I'll be teaching — is the skeptic right that collapse is always read off a surviving archive, or does the novel insist there was a real loss that transformation-talk discounts? Please be faithful in both directions: don't let the book become an illustration of a theory, and don't bend the theory to fit the book, and mark clearly the places where the reading breaks down and the critic has to concede. Around 1300–1600 words, hand-out shaped, and no citations or bibliography — I don't want students chasing references — so just point to episodes and scenes descriptively rather than quoting them. End by telling me what the novel contributes to the collapse debate that the archaeologists' arguments on their own can't deliver, or, if you think it mainly muddles the debate, say that instead.
