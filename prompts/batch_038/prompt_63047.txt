I am preparing the second session of a graduate seminar on the nineteenth-century philosophy of the public sphere, and I want to get a stretch of Kierkegaard exactly right before I put it in front of students who, without exception, arrive having already decided what it means. The text is his 1846 review of Thomasine Gyllembourg's novel *Two Ages* — nominally a book review, in substance a diagnosis of his own age. Here is the portion I intend to assign, in the standard English translation (Princeton, 1978), roughly pp. 86–96; the ellipses mark my omissions of intervening material:

> Leveling is not the action of one individual but a reflection-game in the hand of an abstract power. … For leveling really to take place, a phantom must first be raised, the spirit of leveling, a monstrous abstraction, an all-encompassing something that is nothing, a mirage–and this phantom is the public. Only in a passionless but reflective age can this phantom develop with the aid of the press, when the press itself becomes a phantom. There is no such thing as a public in spirited, passionate, tumultuous times. … The public is the actual master of leveling, for when there is approximate leveling, something is doing the leveling, but the public is a monstrous nonentity. The public is not a people, not a generation, not one's age, not a congregation … for all these are what they are only by being concretions. Together with the passionlessness and reflectiveness of the age, the abstraction "the press" (for a newspaper, a periodical, is not a political concretion and is an individual only in an abstract sense) gives rise to the abstraction's phantom, "the public," which is the real leveler. … No period, no age, and therefore not the present one, either, can halt the skepticism of leveling, for the moment it wants to halt leveling, it will once again exemplify the law. It can be halted only if the individual, in individual separateness, gains the intrepidity of religiousness.

I am not after a paraphrase, and I would rather you not tell me that Kierkegaard anticipated mass media or that "leveling" is his word for conformism; my students can produce both claims from the atmosphere. What I need is help with five specific difficulties.

First, reconstruct the argument. The passage is delivered as a sequence of declarations, and I suspect the declarations are doing at least two logically different jobs at once: some state conditions under which leveling is possible, others report who or what is responsible. Please lay out which sentences are premises, which are conclusions, which are conceptual stipulations, and where the argument moves from a claim about an age's psychology to a claim about a social process. Say explicitly whether the reasoning is best read as causal-explanatory or as something closer to a transcendental inquiry into the conditions of a phenomenon.

Second, the agency problem, which is where my own reading is least settled. The public is said to be "the actual master of leveling" and "the real leveler," and simultaneously "an all-encompassing something that is nothing" and "a monstrous nonentity." My working hypothesis — which I want you to test rather than flatter — is that the passage contains two incompatible accounts running in parallel. On one, the public is a false attribution of agency: a name people attach to anonymous processes when no determinate leveler can be found, efficacious only because it is believed in, rather like a legal fiction that generates real effects. On the other, there is no subject at all, only "a reflection-game in the hand of an abstract power," a process whose grammar invites us to posit an agent where the ontology supplies none. Note in particular the sentence about *approximate* leveling: there, "something is doing the leveling," whereas the public is precisely not a something. Does that mean the phantom is invoked only in the cases where no identifiable leveler exists, i.e. as a placeholder for completed leveling? Can the two accounts be reconciled, and if they cannot, which one does the argument about leveling actually require?

Third, the self-exemption problem. The text asserts that no age can halt leveling because any attempt to halt it "will once again exemplify the law," and then offers a single exception located in the inwardness of an individual — while the assertion itself is a published diagnosis of leveling, issued by a man who wrote for and against the daily press. Is this performatively self-defeating, self-exempting in a principled way, or merely rhetorically insulated? I want the distinction drawn sharply: what would separate a claim that genuinely cannot apply to itself from one that has simply been built so no evidence can reach it? Also tell me what work the strange phrase "the skepticism of leveling" is doing, and whether it imports an epistemological thesis that the rest of the passage does not otherwise state.

Fourth, the criterion of concretion. The list of what the public is not — a people, a generation, an age, a congregation — is justified by the remark that all of these are what they are "only by being concretions." I want to know whether concretion here is a normative condition (members severally responsible, related to one another in a way they can own) or a structural one (determinate membership, internal relations, a boundary). The choice matters pedagogically, because it decides whether the whole passage is a moral complaint dressed as an ontology or a piece of social ontology with moral consequences. If the text supports both readings, say which one makes the claim about the press follow.

Fifth, the scope of the media claim. The parenthetical remark that a newspaper "is not a political concretion and is an individual only in an abstract sense" is doing more work than my students notice. Does the passage license any general thesis about press institutions or media technology, or is the press here only the occasion on which an already passionless, already reflective age spins out its phantom? Put differently: if the press were abolished tomorrow, would leveling stop on this account? I want a defensible answer, not a hedge.

Finally, mark your confidence as you go. Where the English wording is carrying argumentative weight that the argument may not bear — for instance in "leveling" as against equalization or egalitarianism, or in "reflection" as a term of reproach rather than a neutral description of a historical condition — flag it and tell me what turns on it, without pretending to a philological authority you do not have. Please do not supply references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I will handle the apparatus myself. Work only from the passage quoted above and from what is generally known of Kierkegaard's thought, and where you are extending beyond both, label it as your own rationalization rather than his claim.
