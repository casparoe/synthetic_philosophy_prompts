I administer a municipal fund that pays for vocational training and language courses in a mid-sized European city. It is financed by a 1.5% levy on all local wages, and eligibility currently requires five years of legal residence in the city — a rule adopted on the assumption that people who have paid into the levy for some while should be the ones who draw from it. The annual budget is fixed by the levy's proceeds, so every place granted is a place taken from someone else.

Last month a group of newly arrived families, legally resident eleven months and all in full-time work paying the levy since their first paycheck, applied to be enrolled. Admitting them would mean removing places from long-resident applicants, a number of whom are older or disabled and contributed very little over their working lives. My deputy argues the newcomers' claim is the stronger one, since they are contributing now and there is more to be gained per euro spent on them; the chair of the board argues it would betray the people who built the fund and were assured it would be there for them. Each says they are defending fairness, and I find myself agreeing with whoever spoke last. My suspicion is that they are not actually disagreeing about fairness but applying different principles, neither of which has been stated out loud, and that the rule was written without deciding between them.

Which principles are in play here, which one should govern a fund of this kind, and does the five-year rule survive that answer? Please structure your response as follows:

1. **Bottom line** — three or four sentences giving your verdict on the rule.
2. **What the two sides are really appealing to** — about 200 words identifying and labeling each principle at work, rather than restating their arguments.
3. **Which principle fits this fund** — about 250 words arguing from the features of the scheme itself (how it is financed, what it provides, who bears its costs).
4. **Consequences for the five-year rule** — about 200 words, including any transitional arrangement you would recommend for the applicants already in the queue.
5. **Strongest objection** — about 150 words pressing the best case against your own recommendation and saying whether it changes your verdict.
