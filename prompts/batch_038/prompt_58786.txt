My cardiologist has told me to cut red meat sharply for cholesterol reasons, and complying has forced me to notice that a set of food verdicts I have held loosely for years do not hang together. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. I have a candidate of my own, and I suspect it is the wrong one.

Here is what I actually think, case by case:

1. Switching from beef to chicken feels like a clear moral improvement, even though I also believe it multiplies the number of animals killed per meal by a large factor: one steer feeds a great many people, one broiler feeds roughly one.
2. Venison from roadkill that a friend dresses and freezes: no qualms whatsoever. The death has already happened and my eating it changes nothing.
3. Farmed oysters and mussels: fine, on the grounds that there is no decent evidence they are sentient.
4. Field-grown vegetables rather than pasture-raised beef: I say this is fine too, while knowing that plowing, harvesting, and rodent control kill large numbers of mice, voles, and ground-nesting birds, and that a hectare of row crops may kill more animals than a hectare of permanent pasture.
5. Eggs from hens in a genuinely good pasture operation, at three times the supermarket price: not fine for me to buy, because the same money does far more good if it goes to charities that save human lives, but fine for someone else to eat if the eggs are handed to them.
6. A burger from a regenerative-grazing operation that has measurably increased bird and insect counts on its land: I feel considerably better about this than about the chicken sandwich, and I cannot articulate why.
7. Returning marginal cropland to woodland: I think this is straightforwardly good, even though it presumably supports many more wild animals who die of predation, parasitism, and starvation than the field did — and I hold that while also thinking a mouse's death by owl is a bad thing for the mouse.

Cases 1 and 6 pull directly against each other, and 2 and 5 pull against everything else; those are the knots I cannot untie. My tentative principle is: minimize the number of sentient beings killed per unit of nutrition, weighted by how much each is likely to suffer. I think it handles 1 and 3, and I think it breaks on 2, 5, and 7.

Is there a single principle, or a small ordered set of them, that captures all seven — or nearly all? State it precisely, and for each clause tell me which of my cases forced it in. Then tell me what it implies about cases I have not listed: a pescatarian diet built mainly on small wild-caught fish; a strictly vegan diet that leans heavily on soy and almond monocultures; and whether I should accept my cardiologist's advice at all, given that it moves me toward the food I believe kills more. Be blunt about which of my verdicts a good principle has to overturn, and say whether the complexity needed to save all seven is worth paying. Please argue the principle out rather than sending me a reading list.
