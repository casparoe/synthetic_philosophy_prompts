Our reading group on the ethics of collective action has spent three weeks on what the two economists in it call the harmlessness argument, and one of them — a very sharp macro student — has now formalised it on the board in a way the rest of us cannot pick apart, though nearly all of us reject the conclusion. He concedes that (1) is a consequentialist premise, and his claim is that consequentialists are therefore committed to (8):

(1) An act of yours is wrong only if it makes some identifiable victim worse off than they would otherwise have been, or raises the probability that some identifiable victim will be made worse off.
(2) In threshold-structured harms, the damage to any victim occurs only once contributions accumulate past a level the system can absorb, and each contribution either falls short of that level on its own or is one of a great many that clear it together.
(3) If a contribution falls short on its own, then whether it occurs makes no difference to whether the threshold is crossed and hence no difference to any victim; if it is one of many that clear it together, then its removal would leave the threshold cleared by the rest, so again it makes no difference to any victim.
(4) Nor can such a contribution raise the probability of harm, since raising the probability that a threshold is cleared requires being able to clear it, and by (3) no single contribution can.
(5) Therefore no individual contribution to a threshold-structured harm is wrong.
(6) If no act in a set of acts is wrong, then the set is not wrong: there is no mechanism by which a collection of acts could acquire a moral status that none of its members possesses.
(7) Therefore the totality of contributions that jointly produces the harm is not wrong.
(8) Therefore threshold-structured harms are produced without anyone acting wrongly — and since the reasoning depends only on the aggregation structure and not on the subject matter, it generalises to any harm delivered in small shares: a thousand marksmen firing at one victim, a million drivers whose combined emissions submerge an atoll, ten million voters whose candidate wins by a handful. In none of these does any individual do wrong.

My own contribution was to build him a case with the threshold made visible, in the hope of breaking (4). Ten thousand diners each post a short, accurate, mildly negative review of a small restaurant. The platform displays nothing but an average star rating rounded to the nearest half star, and essentially no diner reads the individual text. Any single review changes the displayed rating only if it happens to push the mean across a rounding boundary — which, given that the other 9,999 reviews arrive in no particular order, is a small but strictly positive chance; taken together the reviews carry the displayed rating from four and a half stars to two, and the restaurant closes. He answers that the chance in my case is precisely what (4) excludes, and that if I think a one-in-ten-thousand chance of moving a boundary counts as raising the probability of the closure, then I owe him an account of why the second clause of (1) is not doing all the work, and why (1) has not quietly ceased to be a difference-making principle at all. I work on cost-benefit analysis for a regulator, which is perhaps why (1) feels natural to me and why I keep wanting to repair the argument rather than abandon it.

That reply is the one I cannot answer, and I am not after pointers to the literature — I want the argument taken apart step by step. Which premise or inference do you reject, and is the defect an equivocation, a scope or quantifier shift, a false dichotomy, or a premise that is simply false? So that you know what I am defending: I want to keep (1), and I want to keep the conviction that the marksmen clause of (8) describes real wrongdoing, so I would like a diagnosis that lets me hold both — but tell me plainly if that pair is untenable, what the cheapest coherent package looks like, and which intuitions I surrender in buying it. Three specific things I want pressed on: whether the counterfactual test in (3) survives contact with the review case, or whether (3) is silently switching between what an act actually changes and what it makes likely; whether (6) is a principle about sets of acts or a claim about whom a harm may be attributed to, since those seem to come apart exactly where the argument needs them not to; and if I give up (4), what is left of the thought that my single review, or my single flight, is negligible rather than merely small.
