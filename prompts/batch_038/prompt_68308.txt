I came to the socialist calculation debate the way a lot of people my age did — through a long, unproductive argument about prediction markets — and I have read enough of the Mises, Hayek, and Lange side of it to be dangerous but nowhere near enough to referee anything. For about a decade I have also subscribed to a newsletter by an economist I will call V., who returns to the debate every couple of years and who describes himself, without irony, as a Misesian: his announced position is that the problem is about ownership and exchange, and that all the information-theoretic reframings of it are beside the point. His verdicts on particular arrangements, accumulated across years of posts, run as follows. Soviet material-balance planning, with producer goods allocated by physical quota and no prices at all for capital goods: not calculation, just arithmetic. Lange's proposal — a central board setting accounting prices for capital goods and revising them in response to reported surpluses and shortages: it works on the blackboard and nowhere else, the fatal detail being that the revision cycle is slower than the economy it is supposed to be revising. Yugoslavia's self-managed firms, selling output into open markets while capital remained socially owned and administratively allocated: a split verdict, with the output side doing real calculation and the capital side never doing any. Transfer prices inside a large multinational, where profit-centre heads have their bonuses and their careers riding on divisional profit: as genuine a price as you will find anywhere. A wartime agency rationing steel by administrative quota at a fixed official price, with a black market running alongside it: the quotas were noise, and the black market was doing the calculation. Rideshare surge pricing: yes, obviously, and not interesting. And a university allocating laboratory space by committee, where the deans and department chairs know precisely what they need and say so at length every spring: politics with a spreadsheet attached.

Here is the case I want to put to him, because my employer is building exactly this and I have to review it. I work at a mid-size AI lab. Our compute is purchased roughly three years ahead under fixed capacity contracts; the hardware is export-controlled, contractually non-resellable, and there is no external market anywhere for the specific GPU-hours we are allocating, which sit behind a network topology that nobody outside can replicate. Internally, a scheduling team has replaced the old practice of quarterly VP negotiation with an hourly sealed-bid mechanism that our engineers describe as VCG-flavoured: each research team bids for hours out of an internal budget, the mechanism clears the auction, and it publishes a shadow price per GPU-hour and per interconnect domain. Those published prices now feed the capital request that goes to the board, and they are being used to justify which projects get killed. Three details matter for what follows. The budgets are annual, set by the CFO from the previous year's spend and headcount, are not transferable between teams, and lapse if unspent — so a team that underbids is penalised next year and a team that hoards is rewarded, and there is no transferable money anywhere in the system that could make truthful bidding a dominant strategy. Bids that exceed remaining budget are simply dropped rather than priced. And the valuations being elicited really are private and heterogeneous, turning on run-level facts — checkpoint schedules, which failure modes a team is chasing this week, a product deadline — that no scheduler could assemble centrally even in principle. We sell no compute to outside customers, so there is not even a foregone-revenue figure to anchor the internal prices to.

So: what will V. say, and what is he actually running? I want the candidate rules laid out explicitly — the strict ownership-and-exchange criterion he advertises, some local-knowledge criterion, some criterion about whether anyone bears the consequences of being wrong, some Coasean criterion about whether the entity is an island in a wider market that supplies it with benchmark prices and an exit option, and anything else his record will bear — together with an honest account of which of them survive all seven of his verdicts and which he has foreclosed by saying yes to the multinational and no to the university committee. Then give me your best guess at his verdict on the GPU mechanism, with the reasons for preferring that rule over the others that fit equally well, and tell me how you would reconcile it with his self-description as a Misesian, since the transfer-price post looks to me like it already costs him the ownership criterion. Name the single further case — invented, specified as concretely as the one above — that would best discriminate between the surviving rules, and say what each rule predicts about it. Finally, and more practically: I sit on the design-review panel that has to decide whether these shadow prices are decision-grade, meaning whether we let them carry the weight of killing projects and setting headcount. If we want to be consistent with the reasoning that survives the audit rather than with V.'s label for himself, what should we decide, which of his precedents is the closest analogue to our mechanism, and what principle would we be adopting with each of the choices available to us?
