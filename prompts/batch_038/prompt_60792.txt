For thirty-four years I studied stress-corrosion cracking of alloys at a national standards laboratory, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with more seriousness than my job ever permitted. I attend a fortnightly reading group run by a local university's continuing-education office; this term we have been working through the early chapters of Keynes's *Treatise on Probability* (1921), chiefly because two members have been quarrelling productively over it. Margit spent thirty years as an actuary pricing catastrophe risk. Peter is a structural engineer who until recently sat on certification boards for composite pressure vessels. Last week Peter brought an actual case from his board, and the quarrel stopped being about a book. I should like to report the exchange as accurately as I can and ask you to referee it turn by turn, since I found myself agreeing with both of them at different moments and I am not persuaded that is a coherent place to end up.

The case was this. Peter's board had to decide whether to certify a composite vessel for compressed hydrogen in which the liner was a new multilayer thermoplastic film incorporating a clay-nanoparticle barrier layer. There was no service history of any kind. The supporting data consisted of forty specimens, all from one supplier's process line, tested under accelerated conditions over durations two to three orders of magnitude shorter than the intended service life. The question that divided the board was whether to state a numerical failure probability per vessel-year at all.

- **(P1)** Peter held that any figure the board wrote down — 10⁻⁵ or 10⁻⁷ — would have been manufactured rather than discovered, and that nothing in the world made one of them the correct one. On Keynes's account as Peter read it, the probability relation between this body of evidence and that hypothesis is not numerical; the demand for a figure is therefore not rigour but a category mistake dressed in rigour's clothes.
- **(M1)** Margit gave two replies. First, that "nothing makes one number correct" fails to distinguish this case from any other: an actuarial frequency estimate is likewise a number manufactured out of data plus a chosen model, and nobody supposes the model is read off the world. Second, and more damaging, the board certified. Certifying rather than declining is exactly what one does on judging the failure probability low enough relative to the costs, so Peter's own decision exhibited a preference ordering over acts, and an ordering of that kind is what a numerical assignment represents. She said this is the force of Ramsey's objection to Keynes: belief is not the apprehension of a logical relation between propositions but a disposition to act, and dispositions to act admit of numerical representation whenever they are coherent.
- **(P2)** Peter conceded the ordering but called it strictly weaker than a number: it records which of two acts is preferred, not by how much, and not what stakes one would accept. More importantly, he said, thinness was his actual complaint. A figure of 10⁻⁶ resting on forty specimens and a figure of 10⁻⁶ resting on four decades of operational records license utterly different confidence in extrapolation, and no single scalar can register that difference; Keynes's weight of argument exists precisely to capture it, since further relevant evidence can raise the weight while leaving the probability untouched. He added that he had begun advising boards to report two quantities: the estimate, and what he called the weight deficit — the shortfall of relevant evidence relative to what a comparably mature technology had enjoyed. And he insisted the two epistemic states in his example are simply not comparable with one another.
- **(M2)** Margit answered that weight is then no rival to the number but a remark about the number's stability: how far it would travel under further evidence, or under substitution of an equally defensible modeling assumption. That is a second-order fact and can be stated alongside a first-order probability — give the figure, give the range it occupies across reasonable alternatives, give the sensitivity analysis; her old profession did this daily. She offered an operationalization: fragility, meaning the proportional change in the estimate when the single most influential assumption is withdrawn. If instead Peter insists the first-order state is genuinely non-numerical, he owes a decision rule, and any such rule either smuggles a numerical comparison back in or leaves the choice indeterminate — in which case he cannot even maintain that gathering more evidence is the rational response.
- **(P3)** Peter said indeterminacy is a faithful description of the situation rather than a defect in his view. When the evidence is thin, the correct output is neither a number nor an interval; it is a recommendation to test further and defer, and that recommendation is perfectly determinate.
- **(M3)** Margit replied that deferral is one alternative among others and carries a price. If thinness always mandates deferral, thinness is functioning as a number Peter declines to write down. Name how many specimens, over what durations, would have sufficed to certify, and you have supplied a threshold; refuse, and your deferral becomes unbounded and indistinguishable from a refusal to trade off at all.

Please adjudicate the six turns individually rather than the quarrel as a whole: for each, say whether the move succeeds, fails, or succeeds only against a position the other party did not hold, and justify the verdict in a sentence or two. Then address four questions. (a) Is Peter's weight deficit anything over and above what Margit's fragility measure can express, or is the divergence bookkeeping? (b) Does the appeal in M1 to the board's own decision actually engage Keynes's claim, or does it shift the subject from what probability is to what belief does — and if it shifts the subject, does that make it a poor objection or a decisive one? (c) At which point, if any, does Peter misuse his source? I have in mind that Keynes's non-comparability appears to concern probabilities themselves, whereas Peter ends up comparing two bodies of evidence that he says carry the same probability; I cannot tell whether that is a legitimate extension or a silent substitution of weight for non-comparability, and I would rather you settled it than accepted my gloss. (d) Are Margit's two replies in M1 independent, or does granting the second concede the first? Finally, tell me who is more right overall, and identify whatever the two of them left untouched between them. I have my own suspicion about the status of the accelerated test protocol, but I would prefer that you say what is missing rather than confirm my guess; and if the exchange, taken as a whole, would leave someone actually sitting on that board with nothing actionable, say so plainly.
