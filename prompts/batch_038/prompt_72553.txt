I am the volunteer technical lead for a student-run interdisciplinary journal at my university, and I have been asked to write the incident report for an outage that cost us our annual print run. I am also in a philosophy of science seminar this term on explanation and evidence, which has given me an irritating habit of asking what people mean when they say one thing produced another. The two have collided, and I suspect the disagreement on our editorial board is not actually about the facts.

The facts, as far as I can reconstruct them:

- Our website and its DNS were both with a free-tier provider. At 2:10 a.m. on the night our final issue had to go live, that provider suffered an outage; the site was unreachable until 9:05 a.m.
- Our print deadline was 8:00 a.m. The editors had planned to upload and proof the final files overnight. Because they could not, the issue went to press three weeks late.
- We had a written emergency procedure: an on-call volunteer would repoint our domain to a mirror hosted on the university's student organization servers. Propagation from our registrar takes thirty to ninety minutes, so had the rota been filled, the site would have been reachable again by roughly 3:30 a.m.
- Nobody had been on call since October, when the two students who maintained the rota graduated. Their names were still in the document.
- The mirror turned out to be a static snapshot, last updated eleven weeks earlier. Its nightly sync had been failing silently since an access token expired, and a static snapshot cannot accept uploads in any case.

Our engineering student argues that the lapsed rota therefore cannot be the cause of the missed deadline: even if it had been filled, the mirror would have served an eleven-week-old site with no way to add the final files, and we would have missed the deadline regardless. He adds that if we are willing to call the lapsed rota a cause, we ought equally to call the decision to use a free DNS tier a cause, and the decision to found the journal, and the existence of deadlines, and there will be no end to it. The chair, who reads law, says the report must name a single cause, because the student union's emergency fund covers only losses that would not have occurred had our own documented procedures worked as written, and on the engineer's reasoning we cannot honestly say that of any one procedure in isolation. Her proposal is that we name the provider's outage and let the rest go unmentioned, which would make us eligible. I find that both attractive and slightly dishonest, and I cannot tell whether those are two feelings or one.

Separately, we have about twelve hundred dollars in next year's budget and three candidate fixes: paid hosting with a service-level agreement; rebuilding the mirror as a live, writable second site that is tested quarterly by actually switching over to it; or a stipended on-call rota with a rewritten procedure. My instinct is that the answer to this second question is not the same as the answer to the first, and that this is not a confusion on my part but something real — yet when I try to say why, I go in circles. One further thing I keep returning to: the mirror was broken for eleven weeks and nothing observable depended on it, so the only event that could ever have revealed the failure was needing it.

So, three questions. Is the engineer right that something which made no difference to the outcome on this occasion cannot be a cause of it? Is the chair's one-line report defensible, or is she exploiting an ambiguity in the fund's wording rather than resolving one? And is there a principled way to choose among the three fixes that does not simply re-litigate the first question — because it occurs to me that the report and the budget debate may not be using the same word in the same sense, and if they are not, I cannot say what fixes the sense in each case.
