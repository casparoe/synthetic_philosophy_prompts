I work in a local authority integration team, and over two years of consultations, council debates and letters pages I have watched a single analogy carry a great deal of weight. Immigration control is a queue. Those who arrive without authorisation are jumping it; those who wait abroad for years are keeping their place honestly; the welfare state is what the queue is for; and therefore the number admitted must be capped by what the country at the front can bear. A government minister used roughly this in a column I read last year, to argue against regularising long-resident undocumented people, and a colleague of mine used the very same image in the opposite direction: the wait runs to decades for some nationalities and months for others, she said, so this is not a queue at all but a filter, and the charge of jumping it cannot get off the ground. Both treated the image as though it were doing descriptive work. Neither examined it.

I would like a proper audit, and something more than a list of objections:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly; a table is welcome. What in the actual system plays the role of the queue, of the slots, of the good at the counter, of the person at the front whose place is taken, of the queue-keeper, and of the fact that a queue is an orderly arrangement rather than a scramble?

2. Identify what the image imports that the target may lack: a fixed and known supply of the good; time spent waiting as a ground of entitlement; the existence of an agent entitled to set the rate of admission and to be obeyed in doing so; the presumption that the good is consumed rather than produced by the people standing there; the presumption that those already waiting hold claims that a newcomer can wrong.

3. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusions actually being drawn from those that are merely picturesque. The conclusions I care about are two: that unauthorised arrival wrongs the people who waited, and that the scale of the welfare state sets a ceiling on admissions. I want to know which of your disanalogies genuinely undercut which of these, and which merely make the picture less tidy.

4. Press the point my colleague half-made but did not develop. In a pay-as-you-go system the people at the back of the queue are financing the people at the front, and admissions change the composition of the queue — its age profile, its contributor-to-claimant ratio — not merely its length. If that is right, the image may run backwards: there may be a version of the case in which the queue argues for admitting more, and sooner. Does it? And what does the availability of both readings tell us about how much argumentative weight the image could ever have borne?

5. Tell me whether there are in fact two distinct analogies sharing the word queue, and whether the debate slides between them: a queue at a counter with a fixed stock behind it, which runs out; and a queue to board something that departs on schedule whether or not you are on it, where being excluded is a loss to the excluded and the departure goes ahead regardless. Which of these is doing the work in the minister's version, and which in my colleague's?

6. Offer a better analogy for the same territory — or say plainly that none is available and that the substance has to be argued without pictures.

On the register I want: where you think a step in the minister's reasoning, or in my colleague's, is simply wrong, say so and say why, rather than crediting both with a partial point. Flag which of your claims rest on contested empirical premises about fiscal contribution, labour-market effects or service capacity, and mark them as contested rather than assuming the numbers suit the argument. And if you think the exercise itself is misconceived — if the image is not load-bearing in these debates at all, and the real work is done by unstated premises about desert, sovereignty or collective self-determination that the queue merely decorates — then say that, and argue it, instead of delivering the audit I have asked for. I would sooner be told my framing is off than be handed a polished answer to a question that was never the interesting one.
