I run an internal reading group at an ML lab — about ten research engineers, one whiteboard, 75 minutes, nobody has taken a philosophy class since first year except one guy who did a reading course and will definately try to steer. The reason I'm doing this session: in a capability-eval meeting last month somebody said a failed eval was "just a finked disposition," and since then the words fink and mask have been getting thrown around the team like they're technical terms with content. They aren't, mostly. So I want to teach the actual thing: dispositions, the simple conditional analysis, and the two different ways it comes apart. I don't want you to write the talk for me — I want the plan. What order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what (my instinct is: start from ordinary dispositional talk, get them to volunteer the counterfactual gloss themselves, then break it), and which single example should carry the whole session. My default pairing is the electro-fink wire on one side and something soluble sealed in waterproof wrapping on the other, but a colleague insists I should just use fragile glass the whole way through — one glass with a dead-man switch that removes its fragility, one glass packed in foam — and I think that's a mistake because it blurs exactly the line I need them to see. Tell me which pairing actually discriminates and why, and what the carrying example has to have and what it must not have.

Then the misconception map, with the point in the sequence where each one gets headed off, not at the end when it's too late. The ones I'm braced for: they'll collapse fink and mask into a single bucket of "something interfered" (they won't see that in one case the disposition is gone by the time the stimulus lands and in the other it's sitting there intact and just blocked); they'll slide into behaviourism — if it didn't shatter, it wasn't fragile — the moment the mask case appears; and an engineer in the room will almost immediately say "fine, just add a ceteris paribus clause" and I need to know how to get them from there to seeing why that clause is either empty or smuggles the disposition back in, without that turning into a twenty minute detour. Also, and this is the one that actually matters for why I'm teaching this at all: I need to stop them leaving with the takeaway that a red eval means the capability was really there and got masked. There's a difference between an eval failing because of an interfering condition and an eval failing because there was no disposition to begin with, and the fink/mask vocabulary is apparently very good at laundering the second into the first.

Last thing, and tell me honestly if this is a bad idea.
