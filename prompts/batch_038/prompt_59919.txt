Last night I spent the better part of three hours arguing with a friend — an engineer by trade, a philosopher by inclination — about epiphenomenalism, and I should like an umpire who is not exhausted. Please write in British English throughout, and give me argument rather than bibliography: no reading list, no appeals to authority by name, just the reasoning worked through. If you think I have misdescribed what his analogy is doing, say so at the outset and audit the use you think it is really being put to.

His position is that conscious experience is real and yet causally inert: the neural story of my reaching for a cup is complete and closed without mentioning any felt quality, and the felt quality is an attendant effect that never moves so much as an ion. To make this seem untroubling he leans on an analogy with a respectable pedigree, going back to Huxley's 1874 address on the hypothesis that animals are automata, where consciousness is said to stand to the machinery of the body much as the steam whistle accompanying the work of a locomotive engine stands to that engine — a companion of the process, never a mover of its parts.

The analogy carries weight in our exchange because it is what he deploys against the objection that seemed to me decisive, namely the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if experience has no causal power, then nothing about my having this experience explains why I say what I say about it, or why I find it vivid, unified, reportable, ineffable, and so forth; a physically identical world containing no experience would have produced exactly the same monographs on consciousness, and my every judgement about my own phenomenology would be a coincidence of content rather than a report. Note that I take this to be distinct from the causal-exclusion worry and from the evolutionary complaint that a useless trait would not have been retained; I want the analogy audited against the judgement problem specifically, though you should tell me if it in fact only answers one of the others.

His use of the whistle runs as follows. That the whistle does not drive the engine is no obstacle to there being an accurate treatise on whistles; the accuracy is secured because the sound reaches the author's ears and the author's ears are outside the engine. Causation, he says, runs from the by-product to the describer, never from the description back to the machinery, and my objection simply mistakes the direction. When I pressed the point that the describer here is *inside* the machine and, by hypothesis, cut off from the very thing he describes, he refined the picture rather than abandoning it: imagine a logging device bolted to the locomotive, wired only to the boiler pressure and not to the whistle at all, which nonetheless writes out a continuous trace that tracks the whistle's behaviour perfectly, because pressure causes both. He calls this common cause rather than interaction, insists it needs no outside observer, and claims it discharges my objection entirely.

What I want from you:

(i) An explicit table of correspondences for both versions — the plain whistle and the bolted-on logger — saying what in each plays the role of the brain process, the experience, the judgement that the experience has such-and-such a character, the spoken or written report, the accuracy of that report, and whatever is doing the securing. Where a slot has no occupant in the source, mark it as empty rather than filling it by generosity.

(ii) The disanalogies, sorted into those that matter for the work he is asking the analogy to do and those that are merely decorative. I have in mind at least: that a whistle is publicly detectable and a quale is not; that the fidelity of a logger is established by comparing its trace against an independent reading of the thing logged; that "faithful record" in the source is fixed extensionally, by matching outputs, whereas what is at issue in the target is whether the judgement is *about* the experience.

(iii) A verdict on his refinement in particular. Common cause may explain why a trace is emitted whenever a whistle sounds, but can it explain the trace's content — that it reads *this is what it is like to see red* rather than *pressure is rising*? Is the epiphenomenalist entitled to say his judgements refer to his experiences at all, once the referring link has been rerouted through a third thing? If the logger's symbols are caused only by pressure, what makes them about whistles rather than about pressure, and does the same question not return with full force for judgements about phenomenality?

(iv) An assessment of which of my two counters he can actually answer — the missing outside observer, and the collapse of content into mere occurrence — and whether conceding either costs him the view or only the analogy.

(v) A statement of whether the conclusion he licensed from the analogy survives: that epiphenomenalism carries no explanatory penalty. If the penalty remains, say precisely where it lands.

(vi) Finally, whether a better analogy exists for his purposes — test at least the shadow cast by a runner, the heat given off by a working processor, and the dashboard read-out that the car's own diagnostics consult — and, if none of them works, whether that failure is itself informative: is the paradox of phenomenal judgment the kind of difficulty that analogies can dissolve, or one that only a theory of mental content could?

I would prefer a decisive answer to a diplomatic one, and roughly a thousand words is ample.
