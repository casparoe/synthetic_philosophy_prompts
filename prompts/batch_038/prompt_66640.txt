I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and next Friday I have to give the eulogy for the editor who hired me — really the man who made me. He died at 84. Semi-public figure: three books, a famous column, rescued a couple of careers including mine. He was also, and this is the whole problem, cruel. Not in private, either: in margin notes, in meetings, on the record, mostly at juniors who couldnt answer back. His widow and his three grown kids want a saint's life. The service is in a church and the priest has already told me he's framing it as a commemoration "in the manner of the lives of the saints," which my friend who teaches medieval church history says is exactly the point — a vita is a copy of a type, it gets read aloud and imitated, and the flattening of the individual is the price you pay for the model.

I want the opposite. I want Plutarch. He says of his own method that "my design is not to write histories, but lives," and that "an expression or a jest" tells you more about a man than his biggest battles do. So build the thing out of small unguarded specifics, and carry the temper inside the portrait rather than around it.

Act as an expert on Plutarch's biographical method and on hagiography as a genre — a philosopher who also happens to write these speeches for a living. Before drafting, think carefully and brainstorm out loud for a few lines: how can a life that includes vice still function as something to imitate, and what exactly does a vita gain by leaving vice out that a bios loses? Then write the speech.

About 800 words, six minutes, for a room that's half grieving family who never saw the margins and half working journalists who did, plus one priest. Constraints: it has to comfort without asserting anything false; the word "complicated" is banned; no lecture about Plutarch, one explicit reference at most; and the temper has to land as the reason he's worth imitating, not as a disclaimer tacked on at the end. If Plutarch's habit of pairing two lives and comparing them is a way to get the flaw in without accusing him of it, use that.
