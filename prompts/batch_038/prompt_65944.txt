I am the lead curator at a small genome database covering three farmed fish species, and I need a written decision procedure for a dispute that now consumes more of my team's time than any other single task. We assign molecular-function terms from a widely used community ontology, and our internal guideline document says only that curators should record what the gene product does. Two members of our review panel read that sentence incompatibly, and roughly forty annotations a month come back flagged because the readings diverge. The biochemist on the panel holds that if an activity can be demonstrated in a clean assay under conditions that plausibly obtain in the animal, the term should be assigned; he regards appeals to evolutionary history as unverifiable and beside the question actually in front of us. The comparative phylogeneticist holds that an activity belongs to a gene product only if that activity is what the sequence has been retained or modified for, and that otherwise we are dignifying side effects, physical byproducts, and laboratory artifacts with the word function; her practical objection is downstream, that once such a term is in the database, genome-scale model builders and our variant-interpretation group both treat it as licensing a claim about what the animal loses when the gene is broken. I know both positions have names and that there is a literature on whether they can be reconciled, and you are welcome to use that vocabulary in your reply to me. The procedure itself, however, has to be usable by two curators who have never encountered the debate and must not require them to settle it in order to do their jobs. One related detail you should know, because I think it is where the disagreement has been hiding: our schema has a single free-text-plus-vocabulary field called justification, with three permitted values — assay, homology, or unknown — and "homology" is currently doing an enormous amount of quiet work, since it lets a curator assert a historical claim while appearing only to report a sequence comparison.

Here are three of the live disputes; I want all three run through whatever procedure you write. (1) In the second species, a duplicated gene's copy encodes a protein that cleanly cleaves a dietary ester found only in a prey item this species no longer eats and that is absent from every gut-content sample we hold; the enzyme assay is unambiguous, the only knockdown study reports no detectable phenotype, gut expression sits near our limit of detection, and comparative sequence shows the copy retained across the genus with several substitutions around the active site. Our current practice, which I introduced and like, is to propagate the term laterally from the species where the prey is still eaten. (2) An extremely abundant muscle protein binds and sequesters a commonly used antiparasitic treatment at the concentrations actually reached in that tissue, lowering effective dose; nobody has argued this binding was ever selected for, but the effect is real, dose-dependent, and the breeding program wants a term attached so the phenotype surfaces in their enrichment analyses. (3) A protein assayed in vitro against a synthetic substrate that occurs in none of the three species, with clean kinetics and no in vivo evidence whatever; the paper is otherwise excellent and its authors state plainly that the physiological substrate is unknown. I should tell you where I stand, since I expect the procedure to cost me something: I have been arguing the biochemist's side, and I would rather not be told that lateral propagation in case (1) is defensible merely to spare my feelings.

The deliverable is a reusable procedure, not an essay. Give me ordered questions, with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what evidence discharges it; say where the procedure terminates in assign, where it terminates in do not assign, and whether it should terminate in something other than the binary our schema currently supports — I suspect we need more than one field, but do not simply add fields until the disagreement evaporates. Mark at every step which of the two conceptions of function it is enforcing, and where you deliberately let one govern at the other's expense, say why the panel member who loses that step can still sign the document. Identify which questions are matters of evidence and which are matters of what the database is for, and must therefore go to a human, and say to which of the two. Name the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say what a curator's gut is actually doing at each. The constraints are hard: twenty minutes per dispute, no new wet-lab work may be required, the procedure must run on the published evidence plus our orthology table, and the output has to be a sentence a curator can paste into an audit trail. Then run the three cases, giving for each the verdict, the step that decided it, and that audit-trail sentence. Close with what the procedure cannot do and the systematic error I should expect to find in a year — which kind of term will be over-assigned and which under-assigned, and why that direction rather than the other. Please write the whole thing in American English and keep it under 900 words. Lead directly with the numbered procedure — no preamble on the philosophical background, which I already have, and no reading list, which nobody on my team will read — and put the three worked cases and the limitations after it, keeping any per-step header to five words or fewer.
