**Question 3** (20 points; suggested length around 600 words). In lecture we looked at two Moorean sentences: one where the second conjunct withholds belief (it is raining, but I do not believe that it is), and one where it asserts belief in the negation (it is raining, but I believe that it is not). Moore took the first to be the more basic case.

(a) [6] Explain precisely what is puzzling about asserting the first sentence. Say what is wrong with it and, just as importantly, what is *not* wrong with it.

(b) [8] Moore also noted that certain nearby sentences seem perfectly all right to assert. Give two such variations, and say what constraint each one puts on an acceptable account of the paradox.

(c) [6] One family of accounts explains the absurdity in terms of assertion, another explains it in terms of belief. Say which you find more promising, and state the strongest objection the other side could press against you.

---

**Student's answer:**

(a) The thing that makes this a paradox rather than a triviality is that nothing in the sentence is false or contradictory in the way an ordinary contradiction is. It can easily be raining while I don't believe it — I might have been asleep, or distracted, or just badly informed about the weather. Both conjuncts can be true together and the conjunction is perfectly consistent, so the puzzle is not that the sentence states something impossible. It's that it seems impossible to *say*. The absurdity only appears when the sentence gets used, and specifically when I use it about myself. That already tells us the explanation is going to have to be about the act rather than about the content, since the content is unobjectionable.

(b) The clearest variation is the change of person. If I tell you that it's raining but you don't believe it, there's nothing odd about that at all — I might be reporting what you've told me, or what your behaviour makes obvious. So the absurdity is not in the mere combination of a fact with someone's ignorance of that fact. It's specifically in my combining a fact with a denial of my own belief in it, and any account has to explain why the problem evaporates the moment the belief in question belongs to somebody else.

The other variation people usually mention is tense, and here I think the received line is wrong. It's normally said that it rained yesterday but I didn't believe it is fine to assert, on the grounds that I'm no longer presenting myself as out of touch with the facts. But I don't find that any better than the present-tense version — if anything it's worse, since there I'd be confessing, knowingly, that I *was* out of touch with the facts. Tense doesn't seem to me to do any real work here. And if tense doesn't matter, then the difference can't be coming from anything about the act of asserting, since I can present myself as believing a past fact just as easily as a present one. It has to be coming from the belief itself.

(c) That's why I find the belief-level accounts more promising. If a rational person simply cannot believe that it is raining while also believing that she doesn't believe it, then the absurdity of the assertion falls out for free, because assertion is a way of expressing belief — you can't rationally assert what you can't rationally believe. Shoemaker's linking principle is roughly that what can reasonably be asserted is settled by what can reasonably be believed, so an account pitched at the level of belief handles both versions at once. And the same principle run in the other direction gives you the belief-level account without having to argue for it separately: if asserting the conjunction is absurd, and absurdity just amounts to something no rational agent could be committed to, then no rational agent can hold the belief either. So the two strategies aren't really rivals — the assertion-level one is a special case of the belief-level one.

One consequence I like is that self-deception then can't be a matter of literally believing p and believing not-p at the same time, whatever else it turns out to be. So the Moorean sentence isn't merely unassertable, it describes an impossible state of mind, and that's what makes the whole thing feel paradoxical instead of just like somebody making a mistake.
