Got into a long argument at a workshop dinner last week and I want to stress-test my side before I have it again. I'm not a "machines can't be responsible" person — I don't think machine agency is a thing, and I don't need it to be for what I want to say. My view is narrower, so let me state it as precisely as I can, since the imprecision is where it gets attacked. Call it the residual-gap thesis: when a deployed learning system causes a serious harm, and (i) nobody on the development or deployment side foresaw it, (ii) no feasible pre-deployment testing regime would have caught it, and (iii) the harm wasn't the foreseeable upshot of the decision to field that kind of system in the first place, then there is no person who deserves blame for it. Blame attaches to conduct, and in these cases there's no culpable conduct — nobody skipped the red team, nobody ignored the warning email, nobody took a bet they knew was bad. What's left is entirely forward-looking: compensation, redesign, regulation. I'll happily grant that money still moves. Put the manufacturer under strict liability or a no-fault fund and I have zero objection. The gap is a gap in desert of blame, not in who pays.

Concretely, so this isn't floating: an ICU escalation model at a regional hospital network, certified, adversarially tested for two years, and it degrades badly on a small subclass of patients because of an interaction between its inputs and a rarely used legacy order-entry protocol that nobody had documented and nobody thought to test against. The internal audit trail is genuinely clean — the engineers ran everything anyone could of asked them to run, the risk register flagged nothing relevant, and the on-call physician followed the system's advice because it had earned a very strong track record over eighteen months of use. One patient dies. I say nobody is blameworthy, and the family should be compensated by the network without anyone's character or competence being impugned.

So what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked, fatal at the top, merely awkward at the bottom, and I want you to say which ones you think I'd have to concede versus which I can wave off. Go hardest at conditions (ii) and (iii), because a friend already told me that's where the view hides: his claim is that "feasible testing regime" gets quietly stretched every time somebody produces a test that would have caught the thing, until the gap is defined out of existence. Tell me honestly whether he's right, and whether it matters — this is a thesis about blame rather than an empirical hypothesis, so maybe being unfalsifiable isn't the sin here that it'd be somewhere else. I also want to know what the view predicts that its rivals don't. The prior-fault theorist, the one who thinks there's always culpable negligence upstream if you look far enough, seems committed to saying that on close inspection we'll always find someone who cut a corner; am I committed to saying there are real cases with a clean audit trail, and how would anybody tell a genuinely clean trail apart from a well-scrubbed one? And does the thesis just collapse into something tort law already has — liability without fault, no-fault compensation schemes, the old point that a loss can land on someone who did nothing wrong — in which case is calling it a gap a discovery or just a relabeling? Finally, flag anywhere you think I'm smuggling in a picture of blame I've officially rejected; if blame doesn't actually require a target who deserves it, maybe my framing is confused rather than merely exposed, and I'd rather hear that now. No bibliography needed, I want the arguments and not the references.
