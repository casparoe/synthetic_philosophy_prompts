I've been reading LessWrong and the EA Forum since roughly the Sequences era, and I've absorbed the habit of treating a money-pump story as a conversation-ender; lately I'm less sure the habit is earned. Last month two people from my local meetup group — Vera, a data scientist who did a philosophy major, and Marcus, an actuary — spent three weeks arguing in the group chat and then an hour on a call about a case Vera constructed, and I mostly lurked. Our friend Ilana is deciding between staying in a research job she loves and taking a series of progressively better-paid, progressively more soulless corporate roles, and Vera turned that into a dial to make the structure explicit. Her setup: careers C₁ through C₁₀₀₀. C₁ is twenty hours a week of work Ilana finds genuinely absorbing, at $45,000 a year. C₁₀₀₀ is about one minute a week of absorbing work at roughly $450,000. Each single step down the dial trades exactly 72 seconds per week of absorbing work for exactly $405 per year of income. Vera asserts two things: (a) for every i, C_{i+1} is at least as good as C_i — who turns down $405 a year in order to keep 72 seconds of interesting work? — and (b) C₁ is clearly better than C₁₀₀₀, all things considered. Since "at least as good as" is supposed to be transitive, (a) and (b) cannot both be true, and the whole argument was about which one to give up. It went like this.

**M1 (Marcus):** "This is a Sorites in a business suit. 'For every i, C_{i+1} is at least as good as C_i' is a tolerance premise, and tolerance premises are false for every vague predicate we have. There is some k where the swap from C_k to C_{k+1} genuinely goes the other way — the 72 seconds finally outweigh the $405 — and the fact that neither of us can name k is no objection. I can't say at how many hairs a man stops being bald either. Vagueness is ignorance, not indeterminacy."

**V1 (Vera):** "You've smuggled two things in. First, my premise isn't a psychological claim about what I can discriminate; it's a claim about the balance of reasons at the margin, and denying it commits you to holding that there's a fact about exactly where 72 seconds of meaningful work starts to be worth more than $405 — a fact you'd have to be able to state, not merely posit. Second, your baldness analogy is disanalogous in the way that matters. 'Bald' is unsharpened because nothing turns on it. 'Better than' is the relation that's supposed to settle what an agent has most reason to choose. If there's a sharp k, then at k Ilana's reasons flip decisively, and you can't tell her why, and she can't plan around it."

**M2:** "Then let the planning worry show why your view is the worse one. Suppose you're right and the relation is intransitive here. Now her ranking contains a cycle: C₁ strictly better than C₁₀₀₀, which is at least as good as everything back up the chain to C₁. Anyone carrying that ranking can be pumped. Hand her C₁, offer C₂ for a dollar, offer C₃ for a dollar, all the way round, offer C₁ back for a dollar, and she has paid a thousand dollars to end exactly where she started. Cyclic preference isn't an exotic logical possibility, it's a defect with a price tag, and a price tag is the nearest thing decision theory has to a reductio."

**V2:** "Three problems with that. The pump only runs on a myopic agent who takes each swap as it comes without seeing the pattern; Ilana would refuse the sequence, and refusing a sequence can be perfectly rational even when each step looks acceptable in isolation. Second, your pump charges her a dollar at every step, but I only ever said each step was 'at least as good,' never 'better' — a rational agent doesn't pay to move sideways, so your pump needs strict cycles and I never granted one. Third, and this is the deep point you keep skating past: your whole reductio assumes that a transitive and complete ranking must be representable by a single real number, and that is exactly what fails here. Any real-valued score for these careers has to fix an exchange rate between dollars and absorbing-hours, and for any rate you name I can construct a pair of careers where it delivers the wrong verdict. No number, hence no transitive ordering."

**M3:** "If that's your line, Ilana is in worse shape than you're admitting. Without a representation you can't even state (b). 'All things considered better' just means the separate considerations have been aggregated into a single verdict, and you've declared aggregation impossible. So either you do have an aggregate ranking — in which case transitivity is back on the table and you still owe me a diagnosis of your tolerance premise — or you have a pile of dimension-specific rankings that never combine, in which case there is no fact about whether C₁ is better than C₁₀₀₀ and your endpoint intuition is a mood, not a premise."

**V4 (Vera):** "Not a mood, and not an aggregate either — you're excluding a third option by assumption. When two careers are adjacent on the dial, the true verdict isn't 'equally good,' because that would require the exchange rate I've just denied you; and it isn't 'better' or 'worse.' They're on a par. Parity is a real comparative relation, distinct from all three members of your trichotomy — Vera said she was taking this from Ruth Chang, whose work I haven't read — and it's intransitive in exactly the way the Sorites requires: C₁ and C₂ on a par, C₂ and C₃ on a par, C₁ and C₁₀₀₀ not on a par, no contradiction anywhere, because parity was never claimed to be transitive. 'Better than' stays perfectly transitive; there simply is no adjacent pair for which it holds. That blocks your pump, keeps my endpoint claim, and needs no k."

**M4:** "Parity is a name for your ignorance, and it's unstable by your own logic. If C₁ and C₂ are on a par, then improve C₂ slightly — add ten dollars a year — and on your own story about the margin it's now better than C₁. Run that 999 times and you've rebuilt the Sorites with 'on a par' sitting where 'at least as good as' used to sit, because parity can't be tolerant at every step and violated at the ends without a cutoff of its own. So you're either back at a sharp k, or parity is intransitive and you're back where you started — with the extra burden of explaining how a relation that behaves like indifference in every local test licenses a decisive verdict at the endpoints."

**V5:** "It doesn't behave like indifference, and that's the entire point: 'equally good' implies an exchange rate, parity doesn't. As for the cutoff — yes, parity is intransitive, I've never said otherwise, and intransitivity in a comparative relation is only a scandal if you assume the relation has to be a ranking. But here's what I actually think is going on, and it's neither vagueness nor a fourth relation. 'Better than' is a three-place predicate: better(x, y, K), where K is the contrast class that fixes what counts as a relevant difference. Adjacent pairs get compared under a local K in which both 72 seconds and $405 fall below the threshold of relevance, so neither is better and they aren't on a par either — the question simply doesn't arise. C₁ and C₁₀₀₀ get compared under the global K fixed by the choice Ilana actually faces, and there the verdict is decisive. Transitivity is a theorem about a fixed K. My premises were never all evaluated under one K, so there's no contradiction and no cycle to pump."

**M5:** "Then you've conceded. Under the global K — the only one that bears on what Ilana should do — the ordering is one transitive ranking, which is what I've been saying since minute one. And any procedure that consults the local Ks walks her, one costless-feeling step at a time, from C₁ to C₁₀₀₀, with each step endorsed by the relation you call authoritative at that step. Relativism doesn't block the pump, it supplies the engine room. If the local verdicts have no authority over choice they're decoration; if they do, she's pumpable by construction."

**V6:** "Take the first horn. Local verdicts are claims about relevance, not recommendations, and an agent who knows the contrast classes shift plans against the sequence as a whole and never makes a choice she endorses. I'll grant you this much: if she's forced to decide one step at a time with no memory of the earlier steps, she lands at C₁₀₀₀, and that's a real cost of my view. The question is whether your sharp k does better, and it doesn't, because your k is invisible to her too. Both accounts leave her walking into the grind. Only one of them can also say she was never wrong at any single step."

**M6:** "That sentence is the actual disagreement, and I'll take it head-on. My view says she went wrong at exactly one step and neither of us can say which; yours says she went wrong at no step and ended up somewhere she herself calls clearly worse. I'll take the first. A theory on which an agent can be led from a life she prefers to a life she finds obviously worse without a single defective step isn't a refinement of decision theory — it's a proof that she shouldn't have been consulting 'better than' at each step in the first place. Which is my conclusion, not yours."
