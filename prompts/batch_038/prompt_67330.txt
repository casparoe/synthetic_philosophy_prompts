Since July I have held one of the seven voting seats on the investment committee of a community foundation with a $94 million endowment, a seat I took after a decade of teaching decision theory; the material has stopped being an exercise for me. Our bylaws require that any proposed change to the asset allocation be put to a vote against the incumbent allocation at one of the four quarterly meetings, and that a challenger prevail by simple majority. Three blocs have formed. Three members rank the current mix of public equities and long treasuries first, the private-credit variant second, and the real-assets variant third. Two rank private credit first, real assets second, and the current mix third. Two rank real assets first, the current mix second, and private credit third. Under the incumbent-challenger rule this rotates: the current mix defeats private credit 5–2, private credit defeats real assets 5–2, real assets defeats the current mix 4–3. Over four quarters we arrive back at the allocation we started with, having paid roughly $170,000 each time in manager searches, transition costs, and advisory fees. I cast the fourth vote in September that moved us to real assets, so the diagnosis I am about to offer is not disinterested.

Our outside adviser, whose engagement comes up for renewal in November, has proposed formalizing the pattern: for six basis points a year it would nominate each quarter's challenger. Its compensation rises with the number of reallocations it generates. One colleague insists there is no problem at all — nobody compels us to take the votes one at a time, we could consider the year's plan as a whole, and in any case each quarter's majority is responding to genuinely new information about rates and credit markets, so no cycle exists at any single moment. A second member has moved to suspend all reallocation votes for twenty-four months. A third has moved that members be instructed, when voting, to weigh the later votes they foresee rather than the proposal in front of them.

The executive director has asked me to circulate a written opinion to the committee before November 14. Please draft it — two pages at most, in prose a trustee with no training in decision theory can follow. It should open with the bottom line: which of the three motions to support, oppose, or amend; whether the adviser's nominating role should be renewed; and on what timetable. Then give the reasoning, and in particular settle where the defect lies. Is it in individual members' preferences, in the rule that aggregates perfectly coherent individual rankings into a cycling collective one, or in handing control of the order of business to a party paid per reallocation? The workable remedy depends on the answer, and I do not want a memo that prescribes steadfast commitment when the trouble is aggregation, or a voting rule when the trouble is incentives.

Engage my colleague's objections on their merits rather than reciting the familiar claim that exposure to such a sequence shows intransitive preferences to be irrational. Say what the pattern does and does not establish when the agent can decline the sequence taken as a whole, when preferences shift with incoming information, and when the pumpable agent is a committee rather than a person. Address whether a fiduciary body may bind its own future judgment at all, and whether a moratorium or a minimum-margin requirement for switching would merely entrench a mediocre incumbent while inertia masquerades as discipline. Close with two or three criteria the committee can apply at future meetings to distinguish a reallocation that is genuinely warranted from one that is simply next in the rotation.
