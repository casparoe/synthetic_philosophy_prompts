I'm building a seminar session on the supererogatory around Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas," and I want to use the story against the standard taxonomy rather than as an illustration of it. The stock analysis runs: staying is permissible, since the child's misery is the price of everyone else's happiness, and leaving is either permissible or beyond the call of duty. But leaving helps no one — the child stays in the dark, Omelas stays happy — and Le Guin gives us no reason to think the walkers find anything better at the end of the road. So what is the moral status of walking away?

Please work it through against the familiar definition of the supererogatory (permissible to omit, better than what duty requires, and praiseworthy in a way the merely permissible is not) and tell me which clause the act fails, if any. I keep reaching for a term of my own — call it expressive supererogation, where the credit lies in what the act says about the agent rather than in what it does for anyone — and I'd like you to test whether that notion can be made to carry weight here, or whether it collapses into either duty (staying makes you complicit) or mere preference (leaving is just quitting). Then take up one variant: if departures were numerous enough that Omelas's prosperity actually collapsed and the child went free, would walking away become obligatory, supererogatory, or neither — and would your answer show that the story was never really about the supererogatory at all? Finish by saying what Le Guin contributes to this debate that argument on its own doesn't.

I want criticism, not plot summary, and I want you to be as scrupulous with the story as with the doctrine: mark the places where the reading strains the text. And if you think the question is malformed — that a piece this schematic can't bear that much weight, or that expressive supererogation is a distinction without a difference — say so plainly rather than writing a dutiful essay to please me.
