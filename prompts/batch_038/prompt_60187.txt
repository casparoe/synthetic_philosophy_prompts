In six weeks I am delivering the opening paper at a two-day workshop jointly hosted by my department's philosophy of biology group and the university's machine learning laboratory, under the banner "Representation, Natural and Artificial." The thesis I intend to defend is deliberately provocative: that the internal activations of a trained neural network bear genuine content rather than merely derived or as-if content, and that the way to establish this is a teleosemantics of the Millikanian family, on which the content of a state is fixed by the conditions under which that state's consumers were supposed to respond to it, with "supposed to" cashed out by a selection history. My selection story is the training history. Gradient descent against a performance objective is, on my account, a genuine selecting regime rather than a mere shaping procedure, and the dispositions of downstream layers are the proper functions of the activations that feed them. I am aware that this imports a contested move — that selection need not involve the reproduction and re-establishment of a lineage — and that I am leaning harder on the consumer side of the theory than Millikan herself may allow. I have already been warned by a colleague that a purist in the room will contest my use of the label before anyone engages with the argument, on the grounds that the consumer-oriented characterization of biosemantics is a misreading.

The audience, however, is not a philosophy-of-mind audience, and I do not want a prep sheet written for one. Roughly a third of the room consists of philosophers of biology, at least two of whom hold organizational or closure-based accounts of function on which nothing turns on lineage at all, and one of whom has argued publicly that functions require a reproducing lineage and will treat my appeal to training as a category error. Another third are machine learning researchers and interpretability people who use the word "representation" instrumentally, have no patience for metaphysics, but do take seriously whether an attribution earns its keep in prediction and intervention — they will ask what my content attributions license that theirs do not, especially in light of superposition and polysemanticity. The remainder are graduate students from both sides and two people working on AI governance, who will want to know what actually turns on whether such a system has content: misrepresentation, deception, moral status. Assume no one in the room is a teleosemanticist by conviction, and assume the philosophers of biology know the classical objections to etiological function theories better than I do.

What I want from you is a question-period prep sheet, not a defense of my thesis. Please organize it under the following headings, in this order. First, **The room in brief** (about 150 words): a diagnosis of what each bloc wants from the talk, and where their objections will genuinely diverge rather than converge. Second, **The objection queue** (nine or ten objections, and the bulk of the sheet): ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, each giving the objection as one of these people would actually voice it from the floor — a philosopher of biology will not say "your account is unfalsifiable," and an interpretability researcher will not say "you have misdescribed the intentional stance" — followed by a two-minute reply I could deliver without notes, and then a candid note on where that reply is soft, i.e. the place a determined follow-up would corner me. Third, **Concessions to make early** (about 200 words): what I should grant inside the first ten minutes so that a predictable battery of objections loses its charge, and what I must on no account concede. Fourth, **The load-bearing reply** (about 200 words): the single response that has to land if the thesis survives the hour, and how I should set it up in the body of the talk. Fifth, **The question I hope nobody asks** (about 150 words): the objection that is both likely and close to fatal, with an honest estimate of what I can say if it comes anyway. Do not attach references, footnotes, or a reading list to any of this; where a label genuinely helps — selected-effect versus organizational function, producer versus consumer teleosemantics — use the label and move on. And please be willing to tell me that one or two of the objections has no good answer available to me at all.
