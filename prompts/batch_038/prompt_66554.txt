I run user research at a mid-size consumer app, and since the new privacy rules landed every design change — defaults, consent screens, pricing tiers, notification cadence — gets argued about in a weekly review with legal sitting in. It always collapses into the same fight: someone says "users have revealed they don't care about X, look at the acceptance rate," someone else says "that's not a preference, that's a dark pattern," and I'm the one who puts my name on the research note that's supposed to settle it. Got badly burned on one of these last quarter.

Can you build me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in what revealed-preference theory actually commits you to, that takes a set of observed choices and answers "does this license a claim about what these people value?" Steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each one, and where it just terminates in "you can't say that." It has to be runnable by a PM with no economics in about 15 minutes. Things it needs to genuinely handle: whether "preference" here is just a relabeling of the choice, or a claim about a state of theirs that could have come out differently; what has to hold about the choice situation for the inference to go through at all (defaults, framing, what else was on the menu, whether opting out cost anything, whether they knew what they were accepting); whether there's any consistency check we can honestly run on logs to separate pattern from noise; and where "they chose it" comes apart from "it's good for them," since half these fights are secretly about the second thing. Flag which steps are judgment wearing a procedure costume.

Then run it on three live ones:
1. 92% accept full data-sharing consent on first open — single "Agree" button, the alternatives behind a "More options" link.
2. Free-tier users who sit through ads still don't upgrade after we dropped the ad-free tier below the price they themselves named in a survey.
3. We A/B'd two versions of a streak feature, the more aggressive one retained better, and the team now wants to call that evidence users prefer it.

At least one of these comes out badly for us, I know. Say which, don't soften it, and say what would have to change in the design or the data for it to come out the other way. Finish with a short list of what the procedure can never deliver, however clean the data gets.
