I am finishing a position paper for a philosophy-of-medicine workshop and want a consistency audit on my own commitments before I have to defend them in public. The package is a naturalist account of disease in the biostatistical tradition — Boorse's version is the paradigm, and mine sits close to it — together with a set of case verdicts and practical claims I am not willing to trade away casually. They are:

1. Disease is an objective, theoretical category: an internal state of an organism that constitutes a deviation from normal functioning, where normal functioning is the statistically typical contribution of a part or process to survival and reproduction.
2. Typicality is always indexed to a reference class, at minimum age and sex, and the reference classes are fixed by where the biological generalizations actually hold, not by our interests, our sympathies, or whom we would like to protect.
3. Normativity enters downstream. Illness is a disease that the patient or the society judges undesirable or incapacitating. Disease itself is value-free. I am not a normativist and I reject any account on which a condition's being bad for the person is what makes it a disease.
4. That value-freedom is precisely why the concept is worth having: distress, inconvenience, and social disapproval are never sufficient for diseasehood, and the extension of the term is settled by biology rather than by negotiation. I use the account to push back on medicalization.
5. I also hold that whether medicine has any legitimate business with a condition should track whether the condition is a disease. This is a large part of why the naturalist account appeals to me; it gives the profession a jurisdiction that is not merely a matter of what people happen to demand.
6. Function is assessed against the environment the organism is actually living in, not against an ancestral baseline. Otherwise the ordinary conditions of contemporary life would render nearly the whole population pathological, and the account would lose its descriptive grip.
7. Case verdicts I want to keep:
 (a) infertility in a young adult is a disease;
 (b) presbyopia and age-related hearing loss are diseases, not mere variations;
 (c) endemic goiter among people with chronically iodine-poor diets is a disease in those individuals even though the state is statistically typical in that population — public health has uncovered genuine pathology there, not invented it;
 (d) male pattern baldness is not a disease;
 (e) senescent decline as such is not a disease, though particular diseases are common among the old;
 (f) a sexual orientation is not a disease, whatever its aggregate reproductive consequences, because it involves no reduction in the functioning of any psychological mechanism.
8. And yet it is obvious to me that medicine properly provides contraception and that a health system which declined to do so on the grounds that contraception answers to no disease would be failing at its purpose.
9. I want to deny both the expansive formulation of health as complete physical, mental and social well-being, which strikes me as indefensible, and the reduction of health to the mere absence of disease. Health is something positive on my view, not a complement set.
10. One thing already bothers me and I want it addressed rather than avoided: every case I call a disease is a case in which the person is harmed, and most of what I refuse to call a disease is a case in which they are not. A critic will say my supposedly value-free criterion does no independent work and is quietly tracking desirability.

Please go through these and tell me which of them genuinely cannot stand together and which merely look as though they cannot. I am not looking for a survey of the naturalism versus normativism debate, and you can assume I already know the stock objections — that survival and reproduction are themselves goals someone selected, that statistical typicality cannot handle widespread pathology, that medical judgment is irreducibly evaluative. What I need is an audit of this particular set.

Specifically: identify each real collision, saying exactly which numbered items are involved and what in their usual motivations makes them incompatible; order the collisions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad; for each, state the minimum revision that would remove it and what that revision costs me — which verdicts, which methodological commitments, or which of the reasons I gave for preferring naturalism in the first place. Then separately identify the pairs that only appear to conflict, and say why the appearance arises and how it dissolves. Item 10 should be handled in that second category or in the first, whichever is correct, and you should say which. Finish by naming the single revision that would buy me the most consistency overall, and be candid if it is a revision to something I have said I care about most.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the sharpest thing wrong with the package — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my question, no summary of the debate before you get to the audit.
