So — I'm drafting a personal essay for a public-philosophy site about superorganisms and insect minds, and the editor has asked me to actually stake out a position rather than survey other people's. Problem is I'm not sure my position is internally coherent, and I'd rather find out now than in print. In my circle this topic comes up constantly and everyone immediately reaches for Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary, which mostly just produces two camps: the "that's obviously just poetry, anthills don't believe anything" people and the "sure, why not, minds all the way down" people. I'm somewhere in between and I suspect my somewhere-in-between is held together by vibes. My partner, who does behavioral ecology, also keeps muttering that I'm romanticizing colony harmony — apparently workers lay their own eggs in a lot of species and there's a standing fight over the sex ratio, which she thinks undercuts my picture of the colony as a single will. I don't know if that objection lands or not.

Here's the package, as honestly as I can state it. (1) I'm a functionalist: what makes a state a belief or a pain is the causal job it does, not the stuff doing it. (2) An individual ant has some experience — very dim, mostly a few channels of smell and contact, but not zero. (3) A colony can literally have beliefs and goals, not just as a figure of speech: it tracks patch quality, reallocates workers, holds a foraging territory, defends it, and you can predict its behavior as though it had weighed options. (4) But there is nothing it's like to be the colony. No unified subject sitting on top of the ants. (5) Correspondingly, I reject group minds for human institutions — "the committee believes X" is shorthand for what members believe and do; a corporation, a faculty, a nation has no beliefs of its own. (6) The reason (3) and (5) can coexist, I think, is unity: a colony is one reproductive unit, one gene pool, workers with no agenda of their own, chemical signaling that locks the whole system into a single pattern, whereas a committee is a heap of agents each with separate ends. (7) I also think consciousness isn't just functional organization of any old kind — it needs the system's information bound into one perspective at one time, whatever that amounts to mechanically. (8) Morally I think what matters is sentience, so no amount of colony-level cleverness gives a colony any claim on us; the ants matter, the colony doesn't. (9) And I want to be consistent about the artificial version too: if I built a few hundred little robots that exchanged short local messages and collectively mapped a burning building, avoided obstacles, and redistributed themselves toward the exits, I'd say the swarm has beliefs about the building — while also saying there's nothing it's like to be the swarm.

Could you audit this for me? What I want is the tensions ordered by severity, not a flat list where everything is equally worrying — some of these probably only look like they conflict and dissolve once you say what's actually being claimed, and I'd like to know which ones those are so I stop defending them. For each real tension, tell me what would have to go, what I'd lose by giving it up, and whether there's a cheaper repair (a distinction I'm allowed to draw, a commitment I could weaken instead of drop). I especially want to know two things: whether (2) and (4) really are as comfortable together as I assume, and whether the unity story in (6) is doing principled work or just naming my intuition — note that in (9) the swarm has no gene pool, no reproductive unity, nothing like an organism behind it, and I still want to give it beliefs, so you can use that against me if it works. Then finish with the single revision that would clean up the most damage. Don't try to protect whichever commitment seems to be my favorite, and please no reading list or suggestions of what to go read — I can't chase sources right now, I just want the analysis.
