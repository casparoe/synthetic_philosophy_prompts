I am writing the affirmative paper for a graduate seminar on causation that meets fortnightly with the two sides alternating, and my slot is next. My assignment is to argue, in a self-contained way, that there is genuine causation by omission: that when a night nurse fails to check an infusion pump and the patient dies, her failure is not a mere background condition, not a negative state of affairs doing rhetorical work, and not a façon de parler, but a cause of the death on a par with the occlusion that killed him. I do not want a survey of positions or a catalogue of who says what; I want one sustained argument, laid out as numbered premises and inferences, that a well-prepared skeptic can attack line by line, with the load-bearing premises flagged so that I know where to expect pressure. My opponent presents the negative side, draws on Helen Beebee's scepticism about absence causation and on Jonathan Schaffer's contrastive reconstruction of causal claims, and is scrupulous about never letting a speaker slide from the truth of a causal sentence to the existence of a causal fact, so please write accordingly.

Build the argument around the following case; you may smooth the details but not the structure. Wren is the only nurse assigned overnight to a small step-down unit. Hospital policy requires a pump check at 03:00. At 23:00 a physician redirects her to a neighbouring ward for the remainder of the shift, and so she does not check the pump. At 04:10 a patient dies from an occluded line. Stipulate, and state out loud so that the usual escapes are closed: there is no backup of any kind — no second nurse, no alarm, no colleague who might have noticed — so the counterfactual dependence of the death on the check is robust and there is no preemption, overdetermination, or switching structure anywhere in the case; the effect is fixed at a coarse grain, the death occurring at 04:10, and the contrast everyone in the room accepts as relevant, my opponent included, is check versus no check, so nothing can be made to turn on locating a cleverer contrast class; and the occlusion is itself a positive event with an unremarkable causal history. Note also that the Lewisian counterfactual account already concedes absence causation and contains the resulting proliferation by appeal to contextual salience, so simply invoking Lewis would beg the question against a skeptic who thinks salience is doing pragmatic, not metaphysical, work.

The argument must do four things. First, show that denying omission causation severs the physician's 23:00 instruction from the death, since on the negative view the instruction causes only Wren's being elsewhere, which is not what killed anyone; then answer the reply that this is an expressive cost we can absorb, given that the instruction still makes a difference to what we say and to whom we blame. Second, meet the promiscuity objection — my not watering your plants on another continent apparently caused their death — not by fiat but by developing and defending a distinction I want you to name and argue for: call an absence repertoire-relative to an effect when the omitted act lay within the localized set of options actually available to the system surrounding the effect at the relevant time, and merely global otherwise; explain why Wren's failure is repertoire-relative to the death and my horticultural inaction is not, and then confront the obvious worry that repertoire-relativity is a fact about our interests rather than about the world, which would mean I have traded a metaphysical thesis for a pragmatic one. Third, address the process objection from Salmon–Dowe style theories — nothing is transferred, no continuous process links the failure to the death — and say explicitly whether the right response is to deny that causation requires any such connection or to reinterpret what a process theory was ever supposed to be for. Fourth, take up the duty-and-expectation route, on which her failure is a cause because policy required the check, and explain why I should not lean on it, since grounding causation in norms threatens to make causation mind-dependent; if you think norms can be rehabilitated here, say in what non-mind-dependent form. Close with a short, honest cost section stating what the defender of omission causation must give up in return — including whether my case, if it fails to convert the skeptic, would suggest that at least part of the dispute is verbal rather than substantive.
