I'm three weeks into an upper-level epistemology seminar whose second half has been devoted entirely to classical Indian theories of knowledge, and for my final paper — about five thousand words — I've persuaded my instructor to let me write on Kurosawa's *Rashōmon* (1950) as a case in Nyāya epistemology. I saw the film again at a campus screening last weekend, and my roommate's reaction was that it is simply the cinematic version of the claim that there is no such thing as objective truth, that everyone has their own version of events. That strikes me as precisely the lazy gloss I want to argue against, but I don't yet have the resources to say what the better gloss would be. So: give me a reading of the film through Nyāya's account of the pramāṇas, treating both the film and the tradition as seriously as each deserves.

The paper needs to be organized around testimony, because that is where the film bites. Nyāya admits four sources of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — and, unlike the Buddhist reduction Dignāga proposes, it refuses to collapse testimony into inference; instead it makes testimonial knowledge conditional on the speaker being an āpta. The film looks engineered to defeat that condition. All four accounts of the killing in the bamboo grove come from speakers with something to protect; the samurai's testimony arrives through a medium, which raises the awkward question of who the speaker even is and whose reliability is being vouched for; and the woodcutter, the one witness with the least obvious stake, turns out to have stolen the dagger and to have been suppressing his own version all along. Does the film therefore show that testimony really does collapse into an inference about the trustworthiness of the testifier, and that the inference is simply unavailable here — i.e., does it vindicate Dignāga against Nyāya? Or does Nyāya have a better answer: since being an āpta is a matter of knowing the thing and saying what one knows rather than of general moral character, a speaker might be an āpta about the duel and unreliable about everything surrounding it. I'd like you to push hard on that distinction and tell me whether it survives contact with the film. And separately: three-plus-one mutually incompatible reports about a single event look like a textbook case of the situation in which a reason is defeated not by a perception but by an equally good opposing reason, for which Nyāya has a name and a prescribed procedure. Tell me what that procedure amounts to, and whether the film follows it or forecloses it — whether we are left in a doubt Nyāya would consider legitimate, or in a skepticism it would reject as incoherent.

Two further features matter most to me. First, the flashbacks: the film presents each testimony as vivid, apparently perceptual footage, so the audience is quietly given the epistemic standing of a witness to events that, inside the story, are only reported. Is that a cinematic cheat, one that makes the film's skepticism available solely to viewers who have been granted perception they have no right to — or does it model something Nyāya would recognize about the phenomenology of taking in testimony? Second, the ending at the gate: the commoner's cynical speech, the woodcutter taking the abandoned baby home, the rain stopping. Nyāya holds that a cognition's validity is neither self-guaranteeing nor self-disclosing, and that you find out whether a cognition was veridical by whether acting on it succeeds. Does the film end by substituting practical commitment for evidential resolution, and is that a satisfying epistemology or an evasion of the question it spent ninety minutes raising? Please be frank about where the reading strains: I don't want the film reduced to an illustration of a doctrine, and I don't want Nyāya stretched to fit it, so tell me where the two genuinely resist each other and where a careful critic has to concede that the film is doing something the framework cannot capture. If comparison — the fourth source, learning what a thing is by being told it resembles something you already know — has any real purchase on a narrative built out of resemblances between incompatible accounts, say so; otherwise leave it out rather than force it. Finally, tell me what, if anything, *Rashōmon* contributes to the debate over testimony that argument alone could not deliver. Two housekeeping notes: I can't chase references, so please no bibliography and no citations — gloss Sanskrit terms on first use instead — and don't put lines of dialogue or the sūtras in quotation marks, since I'm working from memory of both and would have no way to verify a single word.
