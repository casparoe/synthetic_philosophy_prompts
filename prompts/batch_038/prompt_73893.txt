I sit on my faculty's graduate research conduct panel and I have to circulate something before we vote Friday. A PhD student alleges her supervisor talked over and talked past her in group meetings for two years, presented archival findings she'd dug up at a conference as part of his own project with no mention of her, and then wrote her a lukewarm reference letter. There's no smoking gun anywhere. The conference program just lists his name, the letter is only "meh" in a way you can't pin on bias, and the meeting behaviour rests mostly on her own contemporaneous notes. On top of that, two of her earlier reports (one about a different faculty member) came back unsubstantiated, and our procedure says prior unsubstantiated reports may be taken into account when weighing a complainant's reliability.

The panel is split. Two of us want to sustain in part. Two want to dismiss for insufficient corroboration and note we can't take her word over his on the letter. The fifth thinks the procedure is rigged from the start — that a corroboration requirement disqualifies exactly the complaints it was written to hear, because this kind of harm doesn't leave paperwork.

Draft the memo I'll send round: a recommended disposition on each of the three allegations, what standard the panel should apply to evidence like her notes and to the prior-reports clause, and criteria we could reuse next time instead of relitigating this from scratch. Please dont let it collapse into just naming the procedure as some species of epistemic injustice and stopping there — I want the dismissal side's worry handled at full strength, including the possibility that we've swung into crediting her more than the evidence warrants because of who she is and how the story reads. Reasons over labels, no bibliography needed.
