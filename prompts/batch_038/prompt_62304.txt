I'm working on the theory chapter of my dissertation in archaeological anthropology, on the concept of societal collapse, and I want to pressure-test a thesis I have come to hold but cannot yet defend properly. Please argue for it: that the dispute between Tainter's account, on which collapse is a rapid loss of sociopolitical complexity driven by diminishing marginal returns on investment in that complexity, and the sceptical camp represented by Middleton and by Yoffee and his collaborators, on which so-called collapses are usually regional reorganisations in which ruling institutions fall while most of the population carries on more or less as before, is not a substantive disagreement at all. Both sides write as though the unit that collapses or persists — the polity, the dynasty, the settlement system, the population, the cultural tradition — were something to be read off the record, when on my reading it is fixed in advance by the explanandum each side has chosen; the appearance of contradiction is then an artefact of two different units being described with a single word.

Make the case by working through two concrete examples, the southern Maya lowlands in the ninth century and the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age, and for each state explicitly what the two competing diagnoses amount to once the unit is named rather than assumed. The argument has to do real work rather than merely relabel: show that fixing the unit changes what counts as relevant evidence, or as a successful explanation, in at least one of the two cases. Then take seriously the strongest objection I can think of — that demographic decline is not unit-relative, so at least some collapse claims are true or false independently of any choice of unit — and say whether it defeats the thesis or only limits its scope. Throughout, please separate the points on which specialists in this literature are broadly agreed from those that remain genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as though it were the consensus.
