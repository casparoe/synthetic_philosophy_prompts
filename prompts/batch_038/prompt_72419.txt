I teach engineering ethics to aerospace people and I'm tired of running Challenger through Kant and the standard whistleblowing codes. I want to run the January 1986 launch decision through Confucian role ethics instead — the Ames and Rosemont version, where a person just is a center of a field of roles rather than an autonomous chooser armed with principles.

The episode as I have it: Boisjoly and the other Morton Thiokol engineers had been warning about O-ring erosion in cold weather since the previous summer, the memo went nowhere, the task force they got had no power, and on the night-before go/no-go teleconference Thiokol first recommended delaying, then went into a private caucus after NASA pushed back hard and came out recommending launch. Somewhere in there a manager was told to stop thinking like an engineer and start thinking like a manager. Boisjoly ended up testifying to the Rogers commission and spent the rest of his career giving ethics talks.

Map it honestly: who the persons-as-roles actually are, what the rectification of names does with that engineering-hat instruction, and where the remonstration tradition lands the objectors — the Analects bit about gently remonstrating with your parents, the filial-piety classics insistence that a ruler with remonstrating ministers doesn't lose his state, and (Xunzi, the chapter on the way of the son, I think, though I may be mangling it) the thought that the great thing a person does is follow the Way rather than the ruler.
