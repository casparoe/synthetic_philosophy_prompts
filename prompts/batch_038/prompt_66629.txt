Roommate of mine collects vinyl and has latched onto lab-grown diamonds as his go-to analogy for why authenticity is a scam. His pitch: a lab diamond is chemically, optically, structurally identical to a mined one — every intrinsic property matches — so the price gap is pure snobbery, you're paying for a story about where the thing came from rather than for the thing itself. And he says the same has to hold for a perfect forgery: stipulate a fake Rembrandt that no expert, no X-ray, no pigment analysis can tell from the real one, and then insisting the real one has aesthetic value the fake lacks is the exact same error, provenance fetishism. From which he concludes that the whole notion of originals being worth more is not just overpriced but conceptually confused.

I dont buy it, but I can't locate the flaw, so I want the analogy audited rather than argued with. Correspondence table first — what plays the mine, the lab, the grading certificate, the resale market, the wearer knowing which one they've got. Then the disanalogies, sorted: which ones actually break his inference and which are differences that make no difference. Two candidates I already suspect, and I want to know if they're load-bearing: a mined stone and a lab stone are both sold honestly as what they are, whereas a forgery is partly constituted by the intent to pass as something it isn't; and a diamond is an instance of a kind while a panel is a particular thing made by a particular person at a particular time, so "the same kind of thing" may not even be an available description. Also — does the no-expert-could-tell premise do the work he thinks it does? I've got a half-memory of an argument that the mere possibility of learning to tell them is enough, that once you know which is which you can train yourself to see the difference, so the two aren't aesthetically on a par even while they're momentarily indistinguishable.

And here's the bit I think his analogy has no room for at all: the middle cases. The studio assistant's panel signed by the master, the pupil's copy painted as an exercise, the picture where a restorer repainted a third of the surface in 1890. Diamonds come in exactly two bins and the trade has a label for each; paintings come on a continuum and museums have a whole graded vocabulary for it. What does his story say about those, and is the fact that it cant say anything a genuine problem for the analogy or just an artifact of the diamond case being simpler than the target?

Commit to something. I want your verdict on whether his conclusion survives, with reasons, not a balanced survey that hands the question back to me. And if the analogy collapses, give me a better one for the original/forgery difference — one that doesn't just smuggle in whichever conclusion you land on.
