I'm TA-ing a political philosophy seminar in the spring — 22 undergrads, mostly second-years, one 80-minute section a week, and I got assigned the third week, which is where the syllabus parks distributive justice. I want to run something that makes them feel the difference between equalizing resources, equalizing welfare or reported satisfaction, and equalizing what people can actually do and be, without me just drawing three columns on the whiteboard. Ideally they run into the disagreement themselves, from inside the game, and nobody says the words "capability" or "primary goods" until the debrief. My supervisor thinks games are a waste of section time, so if this doesn't land I will absolutely hear about it.

The rough setup I have in mind (improve it or throw it out, I'm not attached): everyone starts with an identical bundle of tokens, but privately, from a card only they see, they learn something about thier own conversion rate — how many tokens it costs them to produce one unit of the thing that gets scored. Some cards are cheap converters, some expensive; a couple carry an ongoing maintenance cost where you have to burn tokens just to stay in the game at all; and one or two carry an expensive taste, meaning the student needs a fancier version of the good and it costs more through no fault of circumstance, just because that's what they want. Then partway through I collect a private satisfaction rating from everyone, and the moment I most want them to hit is when the low-conversion group turns out to have quietly adjusted their ratings downward across rounds — because that breaks the welfarist metric from the inside rather than from outside, and I don't think a lecture can deliver that. I'd also like at least one student to end up with a genuinely equal resource bundle and a wildly unequal life, and for the class to have to argue about whether that counts as unfair.
