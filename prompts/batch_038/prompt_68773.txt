So I'm the treasurer of the community foundation in a town of about 3,800 people and I just spent three hours arguing with the guy who sits next to me on the board, and the infuriating part is I can't put my finger on why I think he's wrong. A donor has offered $1.4 million to build a real performing arts center on the lot behind the library — 380 seats, proscenium arch, fly system, actual dressing rooms — on the condition that we commit in perpetuity to booking a touring season: a play out of the city every couple of months, a chamber group, an occasional comedy night. His pitch is that the nearest legitimate theater is two hours away, that a kid growing up here will otherwise never see a good production of anything except whatever the high school drama club can cobble together in the gym, and that seeing great plays makes people more thoughtful and less cruel to each other. He called it a moral education, then quoted something about tragedy purging pity and fear, and I couldnt rebut it because the claim sounds obviously true and also obviously not true and I don't know which half to attack.

The thing I keep pointing at instead is Founders' Weekend, which we already have and which costs maybe $30,000 a year. For three days Main Street closes, the volunteer fire department runs the grill, the middle school band is genuinely bad, somebody's uncle does the same card trick he's done since 1998, and everyone who isn't performing is judging the pie contest or hauling tables. Turnout is close to ninety percent of the town, including people who haven't set foot in a public building in years. My case for it is embarrassingly vague — something about the difference between being an audience and being a participant, and about the fact that a touring season turns us into consumers of other people's culture rather than makers of our own. He says that's just aestheticized parochialism, that I'm romanticizing a magic act because it's ours, and that the honest answer is that what people want is to be entertained well rather than to watch their neighbors be mediocre for the fourth year running.

There's one more detail I kept out of the argument because it sounded like gossip, but I think it's load-bearing and I want you to tell me whether it is. Our drama club's best actor is, frankly, a rat — he borrowed money from half the town and hasn't paid a cent of it back — and the more dazzling he is on stage the more people seem to forgive him off it. Last spring he played a charming hypocrite and the whole town left the gym talking about what a joy he was to watch, and two weeks later he was collecting donations for a youth trip. On the other hand, our fire department chief is a saint and nobody notices. So: which of us is right about whether the building is a moral good, what should the board actually do (take the money, take it with modifications, turn it down), and can you give me the version of my own unease that I should have said out loud instead of the mush I said? I care more about whether his moral-education claim survives contact with a town this size than about the budgeting logistics, so please take a side and defend it. And write it to me like a letter — straight prose, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs the way you'd email a friend, and tell me where you land near the top so I know what I'm reading.
