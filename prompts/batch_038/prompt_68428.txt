---

(a) The method of elimination is Chalmers' test for whether a dispute is merely verbal or substantive. You take the term the two sides are fighting over -- call it T -- and you tell them to argue without using it. If, once T is banned, the parties turn out to agree on everything they can still say in the remaining vocabulary, then the original dispute was verbal: they were just attaching different meanings to T and there was no real disagreement hiding underneath the words. If they can restate their positions without T, agree on all of that, and still disagree about T itself, then the dispute is substantive, because now there's something they disagree about that isn't the word. You also have to watch out for the leftover vocabulary containing another term that's doing the same work, so you iterate: pick a replacement term whose meaning isn't in dispute and see whether the disagreement turns up again there. This is basically the positivist programme with better tools -- the point is to expose the traditional questions of metaphysics as pseudo-problems that only looked meaningful because of the vocabulary they happened to be asked in.

(b) With the statue and the lump, both sides can agree on every non-ontological fact: which matter is arranged statuewise where, how it got arranged that way, what would happen to it if you squashed it or melted it down. None of that is contested. But one side says there's one object here and the other says there are two. So the dispute survives eliminating statue and lump, which means it's substantive rather than verbal.

What that shows is that the disagreement is about the world and not about language -- the two sides are giving different answers to a question with a determinate content. What it doesn't show is which side is right, and it doesn't show that there's any fact of the matter about how many objects there are. A dispute can be non-verbal and still fail to have a true answer, which is more or less what the deflationist says about ontology: a sentence like "there are statues" isn't true or false, it's empty, because it looks like it's describing the world when really it just restates rules belonging to the framework we picked.

A deflationist wouldn't accept (b) as I've set it out. For them, if "there are statues" follows automatically from the ordinary facts everybody already accepts, then the question is settled a priori by our linguistic practice and there's nothing left over for metaontology to investigate. So metaontology collapses into philosophy of language: once you've pinned down what "there is" means, the question of what exists is closed.
