I have been auditing a graduate reading group on nationalism for two years now, and the same argument recurs every autumn without anyone's position changing by a millimetre. On one side are the modernists: the nation is a specifically modern political form, the product of the centralized state, industrial society, mass literacy and standardized vernacular print — Anderson's imagined community made thinkable by print capitalism, Gellner's insistence that nationalism is a functional requirement of a society in which the polity and a homogeneous high culture must be congruent, Hobsbawm's traditions invented from above, plus the Weber-style observation that a French peasant of 1780 had a village, a parish and a king but no France. On the other side are the ethnosymbolists and medievalists: Smith's claim that nations are built out of pre-existing ethnies, defined by a name, a myth of common descent, shared memories, attachment to a homeland and some measure of solidarity, and Hastings's stronger case that England was already a nation in the Anglo-Saxon and early medieval period — Bede writing in the early eighth century of the *gens Anglorum* as a people distinguished by language, law and territory, a vernacular literary culture and vernacular scripture, a single unified church, royal taxation and a common system of law, and recurrent war against outsiders, all of which he reads as the standard equipment of nationhood. Hastings then adds the claim I find most interesting and least discussed in the room: that the model of the nation Europe inherited was the biblical Israelite one, a fusion of land, people, law, language and covenant transmitted through the church, so that the modernist criterion-set is not a discovery about nations but a parochial preference for a secular, sovereign, post-1789 specimen.

What makes the exchange go in circles is that each side treats the other's best cases as equivocations rather than counterexamples. When the medievalist produces Bede's *gens*, the modernist says the word does not mean what "nation" means — a *gens* in an ecclesiastical Latin taxonomy of peoples is not a community whose members feel they belong to a sovereign political unit, and to call it one is to date the nation to the Middle Ages by redefining it. When the modernist then stipulates that nations require sovereignty, mass participation or industrial-scale cultural homogeneity, the medievalist says the conclusion has been smuggled into the definition: on a sufficiently political and sufficiently modern criterion, "nations are recent" is not a historical finding but a tautology, and the interesting historical question — how much cultural raw material was already in place — has been ruled out of court by stipulation. Neither charge is ever answered, only repeated at greater length, and each new case (Hroch's phases of the small-nation movements, the nineteenth-century vernacular revivals, the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, the English of the law courts and of Parliament in the fourteenth century, the printing of vernacular bibles) is absorbed by both camps as confirmation of their own view. My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than endorse, has three parts: that the surface disagreement is largely verbal, a dispute about whether "nation" is a checklist term or a functional one; that underneath it the two camps are answering different questions — Gellner asking what conditions make a polity require a homogeneous high culture, Hastings and Smith asking where the cultural material for any such polity came from — so that many of their central claims never actually contradict one another; and that the "invented tradition" concept is doing unfalsifiable work, since any apparent continuity can be redescribed as a recent invention dressed in archaic costume, which would mean that no evidence could in principle refute one side. I also suspect a sorites structure: nationhood is vague, each individual case can be won by either camp on its own criteria, and the aggregate claim — nations are modern, or nations are ancient — is not the kind of thing individual cases could settle.

So: diagnose the disagreement, using what each side actually says rather than a survey of who said what. Is this a verbal dispute about the extension of "nation," a substantive disagreement about which properties constitute a nation, a difference in the standards of evidence that count (a tax roll and a homily versus census literacy and electoral mobilization), two different explanatory questions travelling under one label, or an impasse produced by an unfalsifiable element on one side? Be explicit about where my three-part hypothesis fails, if it does — in particular whether the Hastings claim about the biblical prototype of nationhood is a fourth thing altogether, a dispute about which exemplar the concept is anchored to, in which case the usual verbal/substantive taxonomy may be the wrong instrument. Then tell me whether there is a hidden empirical question capable of deciding anything here: what would have to be established about, say, the depth and social range of medieval English self-identification, or about the reach of premodern state and church institutions, for one side to lose? Finally, say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a stipulated criterion both camps could accept, a formal account of vagueness applied to nationhood, a reframing of the question away from "how old are nations," or the honest conclusion that the debate cannot be expected to progress, and why it nonetheless persists as a productive one.
