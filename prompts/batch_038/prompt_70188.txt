I'm a software engineer with no training in political science or philosophy — my day job is distributed systems — and for about two years I've volunteered Tuesday evenings on a small civic-tech project that maintains a public dashboard. A five-person panel (two academics, two former election observers, one retired judge) decides whether a given situation counts as democratic backsliding; I write up the case files from primary documents, build the pipeline, and publish the panel's reasoning text alongside each verdict, which is how I came to have their stated reasons and not just their votes. To keep the panel from anchoring on country names, I anonymize everything before it reaches them: countries become letters, dates become "eight months before the election," and identifying details get stripped. I'd ask you to do the same — reason from my descriptions rather than from anything you may recognize behind them. There is a literature with terms of art for some of this; I haven't read it, and I'd rather have your reasoning about these nine files than a map of the field or a list of things to go read.

Last month I tried to encode the panel's stated reasons as a decision tree for our public methodology page, and I couldn't get it to close: every rule I wrote that reproduced their affirmative verdicts also fired on situations they had rejected. Here are the eight decided cases. **(A)** A ruling-party majority expands the constitutional court by four seats and fills all four within three weeks, using a procedure that was lawful under the existing rules — unanimous, backsliding. **(B)** The executive declines to implement a constitutional court decision striking down part of a tax law and keeps collecting under the struck-down provision; the court has no enforcement power — unanimous, backsliding. **(C)** Businessmen allied with the ruling party acquire a majority of the regional daily press with private capital; no law changes, no preferential state advertising is documented, and a content study the panel commissioned finds no measurable shift in regional coverage over the following year — 4–1, not backsliding, the chair dissenting on the ground that a change in who owns the means of political communication is itself the harm whether or not content shifts. **(D)** Parliament transfers certification of national election results from an independent commission to a ministry headed by a party loyalist, by ordinary legislation — 3–2, backsliding. **(E)** The government merges the public broadcaster into the information ministry as a department — unanimous, backsliding. **(F)** Standing rules require publication of an audit office's report on a large procurement program; the government simply does not publish it, for fourteen months, and changes no rule — unanimous, backsliding. **(G)** A newly elected opposition government replaces the top two levels of the civil service in three line ministries, within existing appointment rules, with its own people — 5–0, not backsliding, with the recorded note that every government does this. **(H)** Parliament raises the signature threshold for ballot access from five thousand to ten thousand on the electoral commission's own recommendation, applying to all parties, eighteen months before the election, with a published impact assessment finding no party would be excluded; the number of parties on the ballot is unchanged at the next election — 3–2, not backsliding. The ninth file goes to the panel next week: **(J)** the electoral commission has always outsourced the voter-registration database and the results-transmission software to a private vendor chosen by open tender; the vendor is acquired by a holding company whose majority owner is a longstanding friend and donor of the prime minister; the competition authority clears the acquisition without conditions; the tender rules are unchanged and the commission renews the contract at the same price after a normal tender. Six months later, at the commission's request, the vendor enables a feature that flags registration changes made in the thirty days before an election for manual review by commission staff. The commission calls this an anti-fraud measure. The flagging criteria are not published, the reviewers are appointees of the commission's chair, and no election has yet been held under the arrangement, so there is no data on how many flags were raised or how any were resolved. No law has changed and the commission remains formally independent.

What I want, in rough order of importance: first, the candidate rules that reproduce all eight verdicts, with an honest account of where each one strains — I suspect at least three different rules fit, and I suspect one of them is running only in the chair's head, recoverable from her dissent in (C); tell me if any of your candidates gets one of the eight wrong, and what the panel would have to say to keep it. Second, a prediction on (J): the verdict, the likely vote split, and which rule each predicted vote corresponds to, including how you'd expect the two members who dissented in (D) and (H) to go. Third, which of (A) through (H) is the closest analog to (J) — my own instinct keeps sliding between (C), because the ownership is private and no effect has been measured, and (F), because the state is withholding the information an outsider would need to check it — and what turns on that choice. Fourth, if the precedents genuinely underdetermine (J), spell out what the panel would be committing itself to by ruling each way: for each outcome, name a future situation that the ruling would then force it to decide the same way and that I think it would want to decide differently. Fifth, design one further hypothetical case file that would discriminate between your leading candidate rules, something concrete enough that I could put it to the panel next quarter and get a clean answer. And finally, the thing that has actually stopped me from finishing the methodology page: the chair insists the concept ought to be mechanism-neutral, and I cannot tell whether that is a substantive claim about what makes something backsliding or a way of not saying what the mechanism is. If three or more incompatible rules account for every verdict the panel has issued, is the label doing any work beyond ratifying five people's reactions — or is the underdetermination just an artifact of my having eight data points instead of eighty? I'd want your answer to that last question to be argued from the cases above rather than from general worries about normative concepts.
