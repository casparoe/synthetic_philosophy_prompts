So my mum got a diagnosis of moderate dementia about a year ago and we're right in the middle of the advance directive mess. She wrote one when she was 62, very crisply: no tube feeding, no hospital transfers, let me die at home. She's 81 now. And the thing that's wrecking my head is that she's... fine? Not fine, obviously. But she's content. She sings all day, she eats everything in sight, she gets upset when anyone mentions doctors. So we have this document written by someone who's continuous with her but not obviously identical to the person in the chair, and it says one thing, and the person in the chair says another, nonverbally.

I've read enough of the standard stuff to know the lay of the land — Dworkin on critical vs. experiential interests and precedent autonomy, Dresser's Margo case and the charge that we're letting a vanished person veto a living one, the identity-based worry about whether there's anyone left for the directive to bind. I don't need a tour of that, please don't give me one. What I want is a comparison of two ways of construing what the document is actually doing, because I think the whole dispute changes shape depending on which you pick and I haven't seen anyone spell the contrast out cleanly.

Call them the command model and the evidence model.

Command model: the directive is an exercise of authority. The earlier self issues a standing order, the way you can leave instructions in a will. Its force comes from the fact that it was issued by the right person at the right time, not from anything about its content or its predictive accuracy. Under this model it doesn't much matter whether she was right about what late dementia feels like from the inside; the point is that it was hers to decide.

Evidence model: the directive is testimony. The earlier self isn't ordering anything, she's reporting — reporting on what the person in the chair would want if she could still weigh her own life by her own lights. So the document carries weight only insofar as it's reliable, and reliability decays. If her present behaviour is better evidence about her present values than a piece of paper from twenty years ago, the paper gets discounted. It isn't overruled by a competing authority, it's outbid by fresher data.

Here's what I want you to do:

1. Set the two against each other properly. I'm mostly interested in whether they genuinely come apart or whether the evidence model is just the command model with the volume down. Someone will say every appeal to precedent autonomy is covertly evidential (we honour it because she knew best), and someone else will say every evidential appeal is covertly a claim about authority (we trust the fresh data because the current self owns the decision). Give me a case where the two models give opposite verdicts and not just different confidence levels in the same verdict — one says treat, the other says don't. If you can't build such a case, say so and explain what that would show.

2. Run both against a Ulysses-style directive, where the earlier self explicitly anticipates the change in her own values and precommits against it: when I stop recogising this as my life I will look happy, and that will not mean I'm better, don't be fooled. My instinct is that this is where the evidence model should break, because the testimony was calibrated in advance to the future self's testimony being worthless. But it also looks like the moment where it's least defensible from the other side, since she's claiming epistemic authority over an experience she's never had, which is exactly what we refuse to let people do about everything else. Tell me whether the asymmetry really cuts that way or whether I'm flattering my own position.

3. Tell me which model the actual machinery is quietly running on. My sense from trying to use this document is that the forms pretend to be command-model — her signature, her authority, her wishes — while the practice is evidence-model, because the clinician asks us what she would want and treats our answer as a hypothesis about her values rather than as an order. If that's right, does the gap matter morally, or is it administrative fiction doing useful work?

Also please be straight with me about how sure you are. At the end, say how confident you're feeling about your main claims — are you confident, or are you reasoning your way to something merely plausible — and single out the one point in your whole answer you're least sure of and say why it's shaky. Don't spread equal hedging over everything, that's no use to me at all. I need to know which bit to go and double-check before I take this to the family meeting.
