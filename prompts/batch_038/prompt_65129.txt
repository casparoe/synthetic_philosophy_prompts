our company's new head of wellbeing gave a talk last week and the whole thing rested on a single comparison: we're like endurance athletes. Her line was that athletes don't get broken by training hard, they get broken by not recovering — overtraining syndrome, not overwork. So what's wrecking people in offices isn't the hours, it's the missing recovery: no real weekends, sleep debt, holidays spent in the inbox. What she proposed was sleep workshops, a hard stop on messages after 7pm, and enforced leave — and explicitly not fewer hours, because cutting hours would just compress the same load into less time.

my union rep says that analogy is doing all the work and it's doing it in management's favour, and that it's basically the reasoning behind every karoshi-prevention program that ended up changing nothing. He also reckons the people who fought for the 35 hour week were onto something the wellbeing crowd can't say out loud. I want to work it out for myself before the next consultation, so I'd rather get the analysis than a set of slogans.

Can you audit the athlete analogy properly? Start with the correspondence table — who's the athlete, who's the coach, what plays the part of the training session, the taper, the competition, the injury, the off-season — and then the places the mapping breaks. Not all of the breaks will matter, so sort them for me: which disanalogies actually bite against the use she's making of it, and which ones are decorative. I have a suspicion the analogy quietly assumes things office work doesn't have — something about who sets the load, who the performance is for, and whether the person being depleted is also the person spending the output — but say that better than I can and tell me if I've got the wrong thing.

Also check the analogy on its own home turf: my vague understanding is that a coach dealing with an athlete in real trouble cuts volume rather than adding sleep hygiene, in which case her conclusion doesn't even follow from the source domain. I could be wrong about that, and I'm not asking you to fact check sports science, just to say whether the internal logic of the comparison supports what she took from it.

Then the verdict, and please don't hedge or give me both-sides mush: does more recovery at the same hours survive once the disanalogies are on the table? Is there a narrower version of it that does survive, and if so what has to be added to make it non-cosmetic? And if the athlete frame is just wrong, what would be a better analogy for work and exhaustion — one that doesn't relocate the problem onto the worker's habits — and what would that one license instead?

No references or reading list needed, I want the reasoning.
