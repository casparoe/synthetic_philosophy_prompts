I'm on the animal welfare committee at a marine institute on the coast — think half working labs, half small public aquarium, with an IACUC-ish panel that we've padded out with two of the curators because the regs we answer to basically stop at vertebrates and everything past that line is voluntary. The question in front of us for the meeting on the 14th is whether to extend our anesthesia/analgesia and enrichment requirements to the cephalopods and decapods we hold: two common octopuses in the learning lab, a batch of cuttlefish that come in every summer for a teaching course, and about a dozen lobsters in the touch tank. Our standing policy says extra protections attach where there is "credible evidence of aversive experience," which sounds rigorous until you notice nobody ever wrote down what counts as credible, so in practice the clause has been read as "whatever the mammals get, plus whatever's convenient."

The thing that got the committee stuck is a specific incident plus a specific proposal. In the spring one of the octopuses took a bad laceration during a tank transfer; for about four days it shifted its den, kept the injured arm tucked and guarded, and ate roughly half its normal ration, then went back to baseline. Our head vet, who I think is arguing in good faith and is way better at comparative physiology than I am, says that's exactly what peripheral nociception plus a stress response predicts, that comparable guarding-and-withdrawal patterns show up in preparations far too simple for anyone to want to credit with experience, and that the honest move is what she calls a reflex sufficiency review: protections only attach when we can demonstrate some behavior that a fixed reflex story can't handle, judged by a named reviewer. One of the postdocs shot back that this is Descartes' beast-machine argument in a lab coat — the same move where any counterexample can be absorbed by positing more elaborate internal machinery, so the hypothesis never actually risks anything and the burden sits permanently on the animal — and pointed out that Descartes at least got something out of it theologically, whereas we'd just be getting a seperate budget line out of it. I keep wanting to run expected-value math on the two error types and I know that's a cheap move when both errors are genuinely bad, so I'd rather have the argument done properly. There are real costs too: cephalopod anesthesia is fiddly and the enrichment program is something like a quarter of an FTE, and if the lobsters have to come out of the touch tank we lose the school-group program that does most of our local goodwill. Plus a consistency worry from one of the curators — if the criterion is flexible, context-sensitive behavior, we owe an answer about cleaner shrimp and anemones, not just the charismatic invertebrates.

Draft the memo I'll circulate: a clear recommendation (adopt, reject, or adopt with conditions spelled out), a time frame the committee can actually execute in before the summer intake, and — this is the part I care about most — a written decision rule for the *next* species that comes up, since we don't want to relitigate first principles every year. Take the head vet's reflex-sufficiency proposal seriously as a proposal, not as a villain position; if the memo reads like a hit piece she won't sign it and nothing moves, and honestly the unfalsifiability charge needs to be earned rather than asserted, because there's a version of her view that specifies what would count as evidence and isn't question-begging. And do engage the Descartes framing: what the actual seventeenth-century argument was, which parts of it are still doing work in our debate, and where the analogy breaks down — I don't think it holds together cleanly, but I want the memo to show its work.
