The question, out of 25:

> **3.** (a) State the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem and explain why, applied to ZFC, it seems to generate a contradiction with Cantor's theorem. [8]
> (b) Give the standard resolution of Skolem's paradox, and say precisely which set-theoretic notions it makes model-relative and which it leaves alone. [9]
> (c) Does the resolution actually dispose of the philosophical worry? Discuss, including how Putnam ran the same model-theoretic machinery into an argument against metaphysical realism. [8]

The student's answer:

> (a) The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem says that a first-order theory in a countable language which has an infinite model also has a countable model. ZFC is such a theory, and if it is consistent then by the completeness theorem it has a model, so by Löwenheim–Skolem it has a countable model M. But ZFC also proves Cantor's theorem — for any set x there is no surjection from x onto P(x) — and so proves in particular that there is no bijection between ω and P(ω). So M is a countable structure, its domain can be listed out in an ω-sequence, and yet M satisfies the formal negation of exactly that: it satisfies the sentence asserting that no bijection exists from ω onto the power set of ω. From outside we can enumerate everything M contains, including everything M counts as a subset of ω; from inside, M denies that this can be done. The paradox is that M looks like it is right and wrong about the same thing at once.
>
> (b) The resolution, which is Putnam's, is that uncountable is not absolute. What the relevant sentence says as interpreted in M is only that no member of M's domain is a function that M recognizes as a bijection between ω^M and the object of M that plays the role of P(ω). From outside there is such a function, but it is not one of M's sets, and M's quantifiers range only over M's sets, so the existential is not satisfied in M. Cantor's theorem is untouched: it is a theorem of ZFC, so it holds in every model of ZFC, M included. What gets relativized is how many subsets a set has, and hence how big sets are; what does not is the truth of the axioms, which M satisfies in full.
>
> One might try to escape by insisting on transitive models. By the Mostowski collapse, a countable model of ZFC can be turned into a countable transitive model M', and there is nothing coded or artifactual about M': if x is in M' then every element of x is in M', so its members are the real sets. But this does not restore absoluteness. The collapse is an isomorphism, so it adds no sets, and M' is still countable and P(ω)^M' is still countable. Transitivity does make some things absolute — being an ordinal, being a function, being a member of — but the claim that a bijection between two sets exists is existential, and existential claims can hold in the ambient universe while failing in a transitive substructure. That is why the countable transitive models that set theorists work with all the time are unobjectionable: they are wrong about nothing they assert, they just do not contain everything.
>
> (c) Where I think the resolution falls short is that it buys consistency at the price of giving up on ZFC being about a single determinate universe. If uncountable always means uncountable-in-a-model, then there is no such thing as the power set of ω simpliciter, only the various objects that various models contain under that name. The same model-building machinery gives us, via the independence results, models where CH holds and models where it fails, so CH has no determinate truth value either: it is not that we are ignorant of which model is intended, it is that none of them is. Skolem himself was unbothered by all this — he took the result to show only that first-order axioms are too weak to pin down the intended universe, which he regarded as no threat to Cantor's theorem or to realism about sets. That seems to me too complacent.
>
> Putnam's model-theoretic argument runs the same trick on our language as a whole rather than on set theory in particular. Take the complete true theory of the world, T. If T is consistent it has a countable model; and more generally, given a consistent T and a countable domain, one can find an assignment of references that makes T come out true of that domain. So the facts of the world do not single out one interpretation of our terms as the intended one. The metaphysical realist wants reference to be fixed by a word-world relation that holds independently of what we say or think, but on this picture any consistent theory can be made true by a suitable reassignment, so truth cannot mean correspondence under a unique intended interpretation. Putnam's conclusion is that metaphysical realism is not merely false but unstatable, since stating it requires the determinate reference it is meant to explain. The obvious rejoinder — that we can simply formulate the constraint that our interpretation is the intended one — he answers by pointing out that any such constraint is itself just more theory, and so is available for reinterpretation in its turn.
