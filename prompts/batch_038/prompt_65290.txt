My roommate Dara and her cousin Ivo had a massive argument at my place on Saturday and I ended up keeping score on the back of an envelope, so now I want an actual referee.

It started over a documentary — some war, I don't remember which and it doesn't matter, the argument went general within five minutes. The point of the film was that the aggressor side's conscripts had mostly behaved impeccably (took prisoners, no reprisals), while a chunk of the volunteers on the defending side had done genuinely appalling things in the field. Dara said the documentary had the moral labels backwards: the conscripts were the decent ones and some of the "heroes" were the monsters. Ivo said that was a category error — a man who fights for the agressor is doing wrong even if he does it politely, and a man who fights for the just side and then commits atrocities is doing two wrongs, not one and a half.

Then it got abstract. Rough order of moves, best I can reconstruct:

1. Dara: fighting isn't the soldier's crime to answer for. Soldiers on both sides are on a moral par, because each is doing what he's been told, each has been fed a story about why his side is right, and you can't reasonably expect a nineteen-year-old to work out who started it. The wrongness sits with the people who started the war, not the ones holding the rifle.

2. Ivo: that slides between excuse and justification. Even granting the conscript is excused — young, saturated in propaganda, no realistic exit — what he's doing is still unjustified killing. Excused isn't permitted. On Dara's standard, a cop who shoots an innocent man because he was reliably told the man was a bomber is also "on a moral par" with the guy defending his own family.

3. Dara: fine, say excused rather than equal. But look where Ivo's view lands. If everyone on the unjust side is already a wrongdoer merely for showing up, what does the next wrong cost him? A soldier who knows he's guilty of aggression has almost nothing left to gain from restraint. And you stop treating the people you capture as soldiers and start treating them as criminals, which is exactly how you get show trials and no prisoner protections. The idea that combatants are moral equals is what makes the laws of war stick at all.

4. Ivo: that's a hostage note, not an argument. If a false moral belief produces better behavior, we may as well also tell soldiers the enemy are demons, since that's good for morale. And as a matter of fact the rules hold because of training, unit discipline, and fear of reciprocity, not because every private has run his own audit of the casus belli. You can seperate the questions regardless: whether someone may be targeted isn't the same question as whether what he's doing is permissible. An aggressor's soldier is liable to be killed even though he's doing wrong; a defending soldier is not liable even though he's doing right.

5. Dara: and there's the trick. If the aggressor's soldier may be shot, then the man who shoots him does nothing wrong, and a man who does nothing wrong isn't a wrongdoer either. So in the only currency that has any practical weight — who may be killed, and who may kill — we're right back to symmetry, and Ivo keeps the word "unjust" purely so he can feel clean. Also, "liable without being at fault" can't explain why a conscript who reasonably believes his own side is just should be liable at all.

6. Ivo: liability was never about fault, it's about being the source of an unjust threat. A man shoved off a roof who's about to crush you has done nothing whatsoever wrong, and you may still vaporize him on the way down. That case gets both halves at once — liable and blameless, which is precisely Dara's conscript — and Dara has no counterpart for the defending soldier.

They went another round after that about whether it changes anything that one side won, and I lost the thread. Can you take moves 1 through 6 in order and say who's right at each one, and where they're both wrong? I especially want to know whether 3 is a genuine objection or just consequences-mongering, and whether 6 actually answers 5 or only renames the problem.
