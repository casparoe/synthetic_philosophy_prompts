I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a piece on what becomes of credit when several groups converge on the same result at once — the Newton-and-Leibniz shape, in which the person who had the idea first and the person who got it into print first turn out not to be the same person. For the piece I have been given the internal precedent book of a private foundation that awards an annual prize for a single discovery in the physical sciences. Its Committee on Priority settles contested nominations by appeal to its own earlier rulings, and I have the announced rationale for the last five. I want your prediction about the sixth.

The precedents, in the committee's own terms as reported in the book:

2011 — Two groups produced the same result within five months, each unaware of the other. The prize was divided. The ruling said the result was in the air, and that where two independent paths converge, neither is more the discovery than the other.

2014 — A laboratory director was nominated on the strength of a paper on which he was first author; the doctoral student who designed the decisive experiment and pressed the interpretation was not nominated. The committee awarded the prize to the student, holding that the prize tracks the person who supplied the pivotal thought, not the person who supplied the laboratory.

2017 — A researcher posted a complete account of a result as a preprint. Four months later a larger group published the same result with fuller confirmation, and it is the second paper the field cites. The committee awarded to the author of the preprint, holding that public availability starts the clock and that later completion does not undo earlier disclosure.

2019 — A theorist derived the result in a private notebook six years earlier and mentioned it to two colleagues, but did not believe it and never wrote it up. Another derived it, published it, and built a program of work on it. The committee awarded to the second, holding that a derivation its own author declines to stand behind is not a discovery, and that the prize exists to mark what a field could actually use.

2022 — A cluster of near-simultaneous results was documented, and the losing nominee argued that the discovery was inevitable and therefore no one's in particular. The committee named a single laureate, writing that the fact that a result was coming does not make it unowned.

This year's contested nomination concerns a method for stabilizing a class of materials at ambient pressure that had previously been observed only under extreme conditions. There are three claimants.

A, then a postdoctoral researcher, gave a departmental seminar setting out the reformulation that everyone now agrees made the method possible. The seminar was not recorded. The only surviving written trace is the handwritten notes of one attendee, who is now a co-author on B's paper. A left academic research four months later, never published any part of the work, and has told the committee she does not want the money but would like the seminar acknowledged in the ruling.

B, a senior laboratory head, produced a working method eleven months later and published it in a leading journal. The paper does not mention A's seminar. B's group notebooks contain entries dated two months before that seminar which, on the reading of the two experts the committee commissioned, are consistent either with independent arrival or with having taken something from the talk. Every version of the method now in use descends from B's paper. B is the nominee.

C, a small team in another country, published a functionally equivalent method eleven days before B, in a regional journal, with weaker supporting data and a derivation that does not contain A's reformulation. It went uncited for six years. C was unknown to both A and B.

The inevitability evidence here is considerably stronger than it was in 2022: four other groups published partial results inside an eighteen-month window, and a review article two years before B's paper named this stabilization as the field's obvious next step. The foundation's charter permits dividing the prize between at most two laureates, and permits withholding it for a year.

What I need from you:

Your prediction of the committee's verdict — A, B, C, some division, or a withholding — with reasoning drawn from these five precedents rather than from general intuitions about fairness. I want to see which ruling you think controls and why.

The candidate rules that fit all five precedents about equally well. I expect there are at least three; give each one, and give what each predicts for this case. I am trying to determine whether five rulings genuinely pin down a rule or only look as though they do.

The closest analog among the precedents to this case, and the point at which that analogy breaks.

If the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say what principle the committee would be adopting with each available choice. I intend to write the piece around what the ruling will commit them to, not around who wins, so be explicit about the doctrine each option would create for the next decade of disputes.

One further hypothetical case, specified precisely enough that I could put it to the committee chair in an interview, that would best discriminate among the candidate rules you identify.

A note on the inevitability variable: at what point, if any, does evidence that a result was coming from four directions tip a committee of this kind from naming a laureate to withholding the prize, and does the evidence in this case cross that line?

Lead with your predicted verdict in the first sentence and give the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Then tell me how confident you are in that prediction and single out the point in your reasoning you are least sure of — whether that is which precedent controls, which rule the committee is really running, or whether the five rulings are mutually consistent at all.
