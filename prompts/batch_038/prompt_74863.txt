I'm six weeks into a research internship at a small AI safety group, and on Fridays we hold a design review in which someone presents an idea and the rest of us spend an hour trying to break it. Last week's proposal came out of the work on mechanistic anomaly detection: rather than specifying what good behavior looks like and checking outputs against it, you record how a model implements a task during trusted evaluations, and then in deployment you flag it whenever it produces an acceptable-looking output by a mechanism that differs from the recorded one. The selling point is that this is supposed to catch cases where the output is indistinguishable from a benign output but the process behind it is not — which matters most in exactly the situation where you do not trust the model and cannot afford to write down in advance what you want from it. Our one theoretically-minded researcher, Wen, and the engineer who actually builds and runs the probes, Sadie, went at it for forty minutes, and since I was assigned to write up the minutes I want to get the analysis right before I do.

Here is the exchange as I recorded it. (1) Wen opened by saying the proposal has no answer to the prior question of when two mechanisms are the same mechanism: one output type can be produced by quite different circuits (the model solves the item stepwise on Monday and by a shortcut on Tuesday, and both answers are correct), while a genuinely different underlying aim can reuse nearly all of the same circuitry and diverge only in the final layers. So differing from the recorded mechanism is neither necessary nor sufficient for anything worth catching, and the proposal needs an account of mechanism identity before it needs a detection threshold. (2) Sadie replied that she never needs identity, only comparison: measure internal activations conditional on matched inputs, compare the deployment distribution to the recorded one, and estimate how far those activations typically move under nuisance perturbations — reseeding, paraphrasing the prompt, changing decoding temperature, reordering few-shot exemplars — flagging only shifts larger than that baseline spread. On her account the phrase same mechanism is never actually adjudicated; it is replaced by a calibrated statistical distance, and Wen's multiple-realizability objection is just the observation that nuisance variance is large, which is a sensitivity problem rather than a conceptual one. (3) Wen countered that the calibration relocates the problem instead of dissolving it, because the verdict now depends entirely on which transformations were classified as nuisances: include prompt paraphrase and you buy robustness, but include something like a system message instructing the model to prioritize speed over thoroughness and you have quietly licensed precisely the shift the monitor was meant to catch. Specifying the nuisance set therefore requires the judgment about which variations are irrelevant that the mechanism-level framing was advertised as letting us avoid, so the proposal is not specification-free; it hides the specification in an unexamined list of transformations. (4) Sadie conceded part of this and reframed: every monitor has a null hypothesis, and a null built from observed benign variation is more honest than one built from a hand-written description of good behavior, because you can measure its false-alarm rate, whereas you cannot measure how often a prose definition fires wrongly; if the price is that the claim weakens from detecting anything anomalous to detecting anything off by more than the benign spread, she would pay it. She then added that on Wen's standard no monitor could ever be built, since any operational criterion for sameness of process amounts to a choice of invariances, and demanding an answer to what a mechanism really is before constructing one is perfectionism dressed as rigor. (5) Wen's last move was that if the criterion is merely off by more than the benign spread, then what makes a spread benign is not a fact about mechanisms at all but a fact about which variations we have decided not to care about — so the proposal collapses back into behavioral monitoring with a great deal of extra machinery, and its distinctive promise, that it can see aims the behavior conceals, was never delivered.

Please go through moves (1) to (5) in order and rate each one — something like clearly mistaken, partly right, clearly right — with a brief justification of the rating rather than of your overall impression, and say explicitly which of Wen's two distinct objections (the necessity/sufficiency failure in (1), and the smuggled-specification charge in (3)) survives Sadie's calibration answer and which does not. I would also like you to test the two ends of the exchange rather than assume them: whether Sadie's appeal to measurable false-alarm rates really buys the honesty she claims when the null hypothesis is itself derived from data, and whether Wen's talk of collapse into behavioral monitoring is fair given that internal activation statistics are not behavior. Then tell me what neither of them raised that would change the shape of the dispute. Two candidates I have in mind are whether mechanisms might be individuated relative to an explanatory purpose or level of description rather than absolutely, and whether a monitor could be built on invariances chosen and defended deliberately instead of inherited from a benign dataset — but I want to know whether those are genuine third positions or just restatements of one side, and whether any version of the proposal can keep its claim to need no behavioral specification while answering Wen.
