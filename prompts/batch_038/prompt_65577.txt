A colleague of mine argues that the socialist calculation debate was conducted under a false description: that it was never really about property, incentives or politics, but about computation — and that the analogy which reveals this is that the price system is a computer. On his account, the dispersed, local, partly inarticulable bits of knowledge Hayek describes are the input data; prices are the compressed representation the system produces; buying and selling are the operations; profit and loss are the error signal that drives the update; and the resulting allocation is the output — an approximate, continuously revised solution to a problem far too large ever to be written down in full. Read this way, Mises's impossibility claim collapses into the assertion that a computation cannot be performed unless a single processor holds all of the inputs, which is simply false of distributed computation; and Hayek's own argument — his celebrated illustration of a rise in the price of tin propagating through substitutes and complements without anyone along the chain knowing why — becomes the description of a communications protocol rather than a proof that conscious direction is impossible.

He then makes two further moves. First, that Lange's proposal for a planning board imitating the market by parametric trial and error was not a mistake but a preview of iterative numerical methods, and that we now possess both the compute and the machine-learning techniques to run such methods over vast, noisy, real-time datasets. Second, that the question is settled empirically by the internal organisation of a large logistics or retail firm, which allocates scarce means among competing ends at national scale without internal markets, using forecasting, optimisation and replenishment rules — and he cites the Soviet-era work on optimal allocation by linear programming, whose principal author later shared a Nobel prize for it, as evidence that the allocation problem was always an optimisation problem wearing institutional clothing. His conclusion is that the Mises–Hayek objection survives, at most, as a claim about the computing technology available in the 1930s.

I do not want the politics adjudicated, and I do not want a survey of the debate or a reading list; I want an audit of the analogy, on the assumption that it is doing genuine argumentative work. Please take the following in order.

1. Set out the correspondence table explicitly. For at least the processor or processors, the memory, the input data, the objective function, the error signal, the update procedure, the output and the stopping criterion, say what in a market economy plays each role — and flag any slot for which there is no plausible counterpart at all.

2. Separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for his conclusion from those that are merely decorative. In particular, consider whether the source domain quietly imports four features the target lacks: a fixed set of inputs supplied from outside the system; a stationary objective that does not shift in response to the model's own predictions being acted on; a standard of correctness handed down from outside, where in an economy the ranking of candidate allocations is constituted by the very exchanges that generate the data; and an output that can be read off, evaluated and then implemented by someone standing outside the process that produced it.

3. Consider whether the analogy's plausibility depends on holding the number and character of distinct goods fixed — a firm's catalogue of stock-keeping units against an economy's indefinitely heterogeneous, constantly re-described set of goods and services, many of which have no stable unit in which they could be counted.

4. Assess whether the internal-planning example supports or undermines the analogy, given that such a firm plans *between* market transactions, takes many of its input and output prices from markets it does not control, and is disciplined by a profit-and-loss test denominated in prices set elsewhere.

5. Say whether his conclusion survives — wholly, in weakened form, or not at all — and, if weakened, state precisely what residue of the Mises–Hayek objection the analogy cannot absorb.

6. If the analogy must be replaced, propose a better one, state what it licenses, and say what it cannot carry.

Finally, comment briefly on the rhetorical work performed by recasting an institutional claim as a technical one: what does describing the price system as a computer make it structurally difficult to say?

Please write in British English.
