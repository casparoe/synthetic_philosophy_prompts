Ok so this came up at a giving-group meeting I go to and I still haven't settled it in my own head.

The guy who hosts us — call him R — was pushing back on the standard effective-giving claim that you're required to keep donating until you're roughly down at the level of the people you're helping. His whole pushback run on a single analogy: morality is a loan. You owe a fixed amount, you make payments, once the principal is cleared you're square, and anything past that point is a gift rather than a payment. He said this isn't even a fancy metaphor, that supererogatory is literally a payments word — paying out over and above what was asked — so the idea that there's a line and that goodness can sit past it is built into the only vocabulary we've got. Then he added two more pieces of the picture. First, unpaid debts accrue interest, so sitting on a duty you already have is worse than the original failure, while an act past the line accrues nothing and therefore can't decay through delay. Second, an honestly insolvent debtor is released, so somebody with nothing owes nothing. From all that he concluded that the no-cap position isn't really a moral view at all, it's a refusal to read the ledger: you can't owe more than you borrowed.

Another person in the group said he was smuggling the entire answer in. A loan has a principal because somebody lent a determinate sum, and the thing we were actually arguing about was whether the amount owed is fixed up front or scales with what youve got; and she pointed out that on the religious version of the same image the debt grows with the gifts you've received, which pushes the other way. R said she was just describing a different metaphor, not objecting to his.

Can you run the audit for me? I want the mapping laid out explicitly — who or what is the lender, what's the principal, what counts as a payment, what's the interest, what's insolvency, and what foreclosure or discharge would correspond to on the moral side — and then I want the breakages sorted into the ones that actually bite for the use R made of it versus the ones that are just surface mismatch. Especially: do his interest point and his insolvency point survive, because those felt like the strongest parts and nobody in the room engaged with them. Then tell me whether the conclusion holds up once the disanalogies are counted — can the ledger picture actually deliver a determinate cap, or does it need something else bolted on to do that, and if so what exactly? And if you think the whole image is the wrong one, say what a better analogy for the duty/supererogation boundary would look like and why it doesn't collapse the boundary the way this one seems to.

One more thing: tell me how confident you are in the verdict you land on, and single out the step in your own reasoning you'd be least surprised to find was wrong.
