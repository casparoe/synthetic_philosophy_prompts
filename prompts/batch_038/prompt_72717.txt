For eleven months now I have been living inside a medical situation that has quietly dismantled every working assumption I had about my own subject matter, and I find that I cannot think my way out of it alone. My wife suffered a small stroke last winter; the imaging showed damage in a region the neurologist described, with some care, as belonging to the machinery by which an injury comes to matter to a person rather than the machinery by which it is located. She has not lost the ability to tell me where she hurts or how badly. She is scrupulous about this. Kneeling on gravel in the garden, or lifting the lid of the stove with a bare hand, she will say that it hurts, and she will rate it accurately, and she will sometimes even describe the quality of it with more precision than I could wish for; then she goes back to what she was doing. She does not guard the knee. She does not pull her hand away. She has declined the analgesic she was prescribed on the grounds that it makes her slow and that there is, in her phrase, nothing there to relieve. She is a woman of unusual honesty and she has never in twenty-six years understated anything to spare my feelings, so I do not believe she is performing stoicism for me.

What I cannot assemble into a coherent picture is the following. First, she appears to be in pain, and pain is supposed to be the paradigm of a thing that is bad for the person whose pain it is; yet nothing about her life looks worsened by it — she is cheerful, she works, she sleeps, and when I ask whether she wants it to stop she considers the question as though I had asked whether she would like the weather to be different. Second, I am in a state of near-continuous misery about her condition, to the point that I have begun to resent her composure, and I cannot tell whether my distress is a genuine harm in the situation, or evidence of a harm to her that she can no longer detect, or merely a fact about me with no bearing on her interests at all. Third, a friend of ours who has practised contemplative disciplines for thirty years told me, with some satisfaction, that my wife has been given by lesion what he has spent his life trying to earn by discipline, and that I should regard her as fortunate. I have been turning this over ever since, because it strikes me as both consoling and somehow obscene, and I cannot articulate why the two reactions should coexist. I also ought to mention that she has lately said she notices she is slower to be moved by other people's pain as well — a grandchild's scraped knee, a friend's diagnosis — and she has asked me whether that is the same mechanism, and I had no answer for her.

I am in the fourth month of a stalled chapter in a novel whose protagonist is a woman in precisely my wife's situation, confronted with an offer of a procedure that would restore her ordinary relation to her own injuries. I cannot write the scene because I cannot decide what she should say, and I suspect the reason I cannot decide is that I do not know what I think about the person I live with. So I should like your view, stated plainly and argued rather than hedged: what is actually going on in a case of this shape, described in whatever terms you take to be most exact? Is her condition bad for her, and if so, in what sense, given that she reports no objection to it and appears to have lost nothing she values? Is she wronged or harmed by declining treatment, and would I be wronging her by pressing the matter, or by removing the stove and the gravel on my own initiative while she is out of the house? Is the equanimity my wife has acquired by damage the same thing as, or a different thing from, the equanimity a practitioner acquires by training, and does the difference matter to whether either is a good? And is my own suffering a reason to intervene in her life, or only a symptom I should manage elsewhere? Please also tell me where you think I am reasoning badly, if I am, and then tell me what to do on Tuesday morning.
