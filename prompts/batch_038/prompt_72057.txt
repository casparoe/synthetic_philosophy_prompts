I run a volunteer transcription program at a county historical society in a small city in upstate New York — twenty-four regulars, mostly retirees, three high-school juniors, one retired shop teacher who is the only person in the room who has ever taken a history course beyond the twelfth grade. We meet Saturday mornings, and this coming month I have been given a hundred minutes in our usual room: six round tables, a whiteboard, a printer in the hallway, and no projector. The reason I want to spend that time on something other than transcription technique is personal. Last spring, while helping clear out my great-great-aunt's house, I tried to find out anything at all about my great-great-grandmother, who appears in family lore only as the woman who "kept the boardinghouse." What actually survives of her is her name on a line in a county almshouse admission register, one entry in a liquor-commissioner's docket, and a sentence in a local paper about a complaint against her. I spent a weekend looking for anything in her own hand and found nothing — not a letter, not a signature on a petition, nothing. That experience has reframed the whole project for me: our volunteers are spending their evenings making searchable the paperwork of poor-law officers, coroners, justices, church disciplinary committees, and tax assessors, and almost none of that work makes searchable what the people named in those documents thought of themselves. I want the group to feel that asymmetry rather than be told about it.

So I would like you to design a runnable workshop activity — not a lecture, not a slide sequence, and not a discussion built on a handout of questions — in which the volunteers reconstruct the life of one invented ordinary person from a fabricated dossier of institutional records, and discover through the work itself how much of what they end up saying is a property of the record-keepers rather than of the person. Please invent the person and every document from scratch; I specifically do not want this built around a real, famous case, because I know enough about the well-known microhistories to sense that the workshop would slide into a talk about books nobody in the room has read. I know the labels that attach to this territory — history from below, the microhistorical approach, the argument over whether an unusual case can be made to stand for a normal one — and I want the participants to run into the substance of those problems before any of them hears a term for what they have just done. A few mechanics I would like you to build in, though you should feel free to improve on them or tell me why one will not work: the same dossier goes to several tables, but each table receives a different governing question, and the divergence in the resulting biographies should be unmistakable when the tables read out; one document should be about a different person whose name is nearly identical, so that a careless group will silently merge two lives; exactly one item should be self-authored by the subject, and it should be fragmentary and easy to over-read; two documents should contradict each other in a way that cannot be resolved from inside the dossier; and one detail should be attested only once, in the least respectable source, but should turn out to matter enormously, so that groups have to decide whether a single attestation is evidence or noise. Give me the printable text of each document in full (short enough that the whole dossier can be read in under twenty-five minutes by a slow reader), the table assignments and roles, a minute-by-minute timeline including a mid-activity turn and the exact sentences I should say when I reveal it, whatever incentive or scoring device creates the pressure that makes this more than a reading exercise, and a debrief that maps particular moments in the room onto particular claims about evidence, silence, and representativeness — supplying the technical vocabulary only at the very end, and only where it genuinely helps.

Two further things, and please be candid rather than reassuring. First, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with this particular room — where a table will stall, which group will short-circuit the exercise by refusing to assert anything at all, which reveal will land flat or land too hard, and what I do in the moment with a participant who objects that this is all just speculation dressed up as history. Second, be explicit about what the activity cannot reproduce and what I therefore have to supply myself: I suspect at least that the participants will know going in that a person really is behind the dossier and that the dossier is closed, whereas an actual researcher knows neither. Finally, I want the workshop to close by turning what the room just felt back onto the decisions our volunteers actually make at their keyboards — whether to transcribe a derogatory term exactly as written, whether to normalize a name, whether to index a woman who is recorded only as someone's wife under her own name — so please give me three or four of those decisions staged as a quick round of the same kind of forced choice. Write the whole thing in American English, and do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; if the group asks afterward what to read, I will handle that separately and do not want the workshop materials to gesture at sources they cannot verify.
