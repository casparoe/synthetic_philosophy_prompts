I sit on the elections committee of a mid-sized professional association, and this year I am also auditing a graduate seminar in social choice, which has made our committee meetings considerably more contentious than usual. We elect a single officer, usually from three or four nominees, by plurality. Two cycles ago an organized bloc abandoned its genuine favorite — a nominee with no realistic path to first place — and voted as a unit for its second choice in order to block a front-runner it disliked; the resulting winner, according to our own post-election survey, was ranked last by a majority of the membership. One committee member, citing the theorem of Gibbard and Satterthwaite, moved that the charter be amended to require that any rule we adopt be strategy-proof. Another member observed that with three or more nominees and no restriction on what members' preferences may look like, the theorem appears to leave dictatorship as the only strategy-proof surjective option, so the amendment would be either vacuous or absurd, and in any case would enshrine a mathematical result as a matter of constitutional principle without anyone being able to say what value the principle serves. A third member then circulated the proposal described below, which she presented as a way to capture whatever protection strategy-proofness was supposed to provide without running into the impossibility. My seminar classmates and I cannot agree about whether it is a serious idea or a confusion dressed in procedural language, and I would like the question argued properly rather than settled by enthusiasm.

The proposal, which I will call the Sincerity Declaration scheme, works as follows. Each member submits, as now, a complete ranking of the nominees. Alongside the ranking, the member files a declaration in one of two categories: S, meaning that the submitted ranking is exactly the member's true preference order, or T, meaning that the submitted ranking departs from the member's true order in at least one adjacent pair of nominees. The declarations are sealed until the polls close and are never published individually, only in aggregate. Counting proceeds by our ordinary positional rule — plurality now, though the scheme generalizes — except that ballots filed as T are weighted by a fixed coefficient λ set in the charter, currently proposed at two-thirds, while ballots filed as S carry weight one; under plurality this means a T ballot counts as two-thirds of a vote for its top-ranked nominee. The rationale is that tactical voting is not prohibited but priced: nobody is forced to be sincere, and nobody is punished for being sincere, since declaring S while voting sincerely costs nothing and gains full weight. A later variant, SD-Public, would make the declaration public while keeping the ranking secret, on the theory that a false declaration then becomes socially checkable in a way a false ballot cannot be. The proposer makes three claims on its behalf: first, that it restores the protection plurality lacks, because sincerity is now subsidized rather than exploited; second, that the impossibility does not reach it, since Gibbard and Satterthwaite concerns rules whose only input is a ranking, whereas this rule's message space is the pair (ranking, declaration), and a scheme on an enlarged message space simply is not the kind of object the theorem rules out; third, that lying on the declaration is not tactical voting but false testimony about one's own mind, a matter for an honor code rather than for mechanism design. Opponents on the committee answer that the λ discount is merely a price on tactical voting, and prices are paid by precisely those with the most to gain, so the scheme filters for the most determined tacticians rather than deterring them; that since a declaration cannot be verified without abandoning the secret ballot, and SD-Public's public declarations would let blocs and employers pressure members into signaling loyalty, claim three collapses into either unenforceability or coercion; and that "sincere" may not even be a determinate property of an individual ballot under uncertainty, because whether a ranking counts as tactical depends on one's beliefs about other voters — the very dependence that Farquharson's older distinction between sincere and sophisticated voting was designed to handle under assumptions of common knowledge that no real membership satisfies — so the declaration asks members to report something that may not exist to be reported.

Please write an essay of roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words that adjudicates this controversy and reaches a verdict rather than a survey. At minimum it should: say explicitly what the requirement of strategy-proofness is actually protecting, normatively — whether it amounts to a right to sincerity, a fairness constraint against exploiting the honest, an informational or efficiency property, or something else — since the answer determines whether the Sincerity Declaration scheme captures the target or merely resembles it; rule on the proposer's second claim, explaining whether enlarging the message space is a genuine escape from the impossibility or a redescription of it, and whether the relevant impossibility reappears one level up in the declaration game itself; assess whether "sincere" is well-defined for a single voter with incomplete information about the electorate, and what follows for the scheme if it is not; and address the pricing objection, including the possibility that the discount is not a deterrent at all but a fee that sorts voters by how much they stand to gain. Work with our actual situation — one winner, three or four nominees, a membership containing at least one coordinated bloc — rather than with an idealized electorate, and be concrete about whether you would recommend adopting the scheme, adopting a modified version of it, or rejecting it. Do not end on a non-committal note: if your conclusion is conditional, state the conditions precisely and say which of them you think hold for us.

After the essay, and in a separate fenced code block, please give a brief structured summary as a single JSON object with exactly these fields: "verdict_label", one of "genuine_third_option", "impossibility_in_disguise", "worse_than_both_alternatives", "workable_only_under_stated_conditions", or "other"; "verdict", your conclusion in one or two sentences; "what_strategy_proofness_protects", the normative content you attribute to the requirement, in one sentence; "message_space_escape", an object with the boolean field "escapes_impossibility" and the string field "reason"; "strongest_objection", the single objection you take to be most damaging to the scheme; "proposers_best_reply", the strongest available answer to that objection; "reply_succeeds", a boolean; "salvage_conditions", an array of strings giving the conditions under which some declaration-based scheme could work; "recommendation_to_committee", one sentence of practical advice; and "confidence", one of "low", "medium", or "high".
