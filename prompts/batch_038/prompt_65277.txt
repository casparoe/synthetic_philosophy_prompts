I teach a 200-level philosophy of mind section and next week's class is on Dennett's attack on the Cartesian theater. Half the room will be engineering majors who've never heard the phrase, so I want to open by showing the first ten minutes of Inside Out — Headquarters, the console, the memory orbs, the Islands of Personality — as a picture of exactly the model he's after. Then I want to turn on the film, but I don't want to teach them that a smart movie is naive just because it isn't a philosophy paper.

Where I'm stuck, three things:

1. The obvious charge is that Headquarters is a viewing room with little people in it, so the regress never gets cashed out. But rewatching it, I keep noticing that nobody in there ever seems to be *having* the experience. The emotions aren't subjects, they're operators watching a monitor and shoving buttons around, and the whole plot is a fight over who gets the controls. So maybe it isn't a theater at all but what I've been lazily calling Cartesian middle management — a control-room problem rather than a viewer problem. Is that an actual distinction or am I just renaming the same mistake? And if there's no viewer in there, does the film still owe us an account of why Riley's experience is unified, or does dodging the homunculus let it off the hook? Relatedly: the film literally shows the theater getting assembled, baby Riley with one button and one emotion and more arriving one at a time. That looks like the one thing Dennett says you're allowed to do — decompose the little man into smaller, stupider ones until they're dumb enough to be machinery. Does the film get credit for that?

2. Everybody makes the theater joke about this movie. Nobody seems to make the Cartesian materialism one, where the worry isn't a screen but a finish line — the idea that a memory has a definite, allready-fixed color sitting on a shelf in Long Term Memory waiting to be inspected. The film hands itself a test case: Sadness touches a stored core memory and it turns blue, and the memory is allready in storage, not on the screen. That's either an Orwellian rewrite (it was one thing, got edited afterward, and there's no fact about what Riley really remembered) or a Stalinesque one, and I don't think the film knows which and I'm not sure it needs to. Should I bring color phi in here — the two-flash case where people report continuous motion with the color changing midway, which couldn't have been assembled in time — or does that lean on timing facts the film never commits to?

3. What would a genuine multiple-drafts version of this movie look like, and could you film it at all if there's no screen and no finish line? I keep thinking the ending gestures at it: the console gets rebuilt bigger with way more buttons, all five emotions crowded around it, and the mixed gold-and-blue core memory is the thing that finally lets Riley feel like herself. Distributed processes in a theater costume, or am I reading my own view in?

Tell me where I'm wrong and where the film resists all this. No plot summary and no citations or reading lists — just the reading, scene by scene where it counts, and your honest take on whether I should still show the clip.
