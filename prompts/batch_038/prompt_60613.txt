Background first, because I think it matters for what I'm asking. I retired four years ago after thirty-eight years of invertebrate neurophysiology, most of it on the stomatogastric ganglion of the crab, which as you may know will sit in a dish with nothing attached to it and throw off perfectly patterned rhythmic motor output for hours on end. So I have a rather personal interest in how much machinery it takes to produce something that looks, from outside, like purposeful behaviour — I've watched a great deal of that machinery work. Since retiring I've been going slowly through Descartes, the Meditations and the Discourse and some of the letters in translation, and I've hit a wall at his view of animals, the bête-machine business. I don't want another summary of it. I want it run against a real episode that I think is underused for this purpose. The episode: in early 2018 Switzerland made it a requirement that lobsters and other decapod crustaceans be stunned, electrically or by mechanical destruction of the brain, before being killed, which in practise ended the custom of dropping them alive into boiling water; and in late 2021 the UK government extended the reach of its animal sentience legislation to all decapod crustaceans and cephalopod molluscs after commissioning a review of the scientific literature that concluded there was strong evidence these animals can experience pain and distress. Both decisions leaned overwhelmingly on a particular kind of evidence — prolonged rubbing and favouring of injured sites, avoidance learning, long-lived trade-offs where an animal will pay a cost to escape or give up a shelter it likes if that shelter is where it gets shocked, responsiveness to anaesthetics and to painkillers, that sort of thing. Take those facts as given; you don't need to look anything up and I'd rather you didn't try.

Now the analysis, and I want it disciplined about the mapping rather than impressionistic. Start with what the position actually commits him to about a lobster — the commitments, not the caricature — and keep his two reasons seperate: the absence of language, and the absence of behaviour flexible enough to meet novel situations without a mechanism tailor-made for each. People always run those together into one big "animals are automata" gesture, and I suspect that's why the view looks weaker than it is. Tell me which of the two the crustacean case really presses on, and whether it presses on them in the same direction or in opposite ones. Then the part I care about most. My half-formed suspicion is that Descartes is usually treated as having made a wild empirical guess which science has since refuted, and that this is a category mistake. On my reading his real move was to deny that behaviour, however complex, counts as evidence about inner states at all: the automaton hypothesis isn't a rival explanation of the data, it's a claim that the data cannot speak. If that's right then the lobster law didn't refute him, it simply declined to play his game, and that's a very different achievement from the one people credit it with. Tell me whether you think that reading is fair, and then tell me whether it makes his position unfalsifiable in a way that should be fatal to it or in a way a working scientist ought to recognise and respect — I'm thinking of the perfectly ordinary situation where someone says your instrument isn't measuring what you assume it's measuring, which is not an empirical competitor to your result and can nonetheless be correct. Test that analogy rather than waving it: say where it holds, where it strains, and what would have to be true for it to break down completely. And then a question I genuinely can't settle myself. Does the crustacean case make his position easier or harder than the dog case does? There's a pull in both directions and I want it teased apart — a lobster's repertoire is thin and stereotyped, which is precisely what he'd predict from a machine, but the phylogenetic distance is so vast that the "it's built roughly like me so it's probably roughly like me" inference that carries so much weight with a mammal loses most of its purchase. Does the extra distance help him or hurt him, and where does the octopus sit, since it seems to combine the distance with a flexibility that's much harder to wave away?

After that, tell me where the theory goes silent instead of making it talk. Both of those decisions were made under openly acknowledged uncertainty with lopsided stakes: you don't have to settle the nature of consciousness to notice that stunning costs a restaurant very little and boiling costs the animal a great deal if it can feel anything. I can't find any resource in the machine doctrine for that style of reasoning whatsoever — no way for the framework to even formulate "act as though you might be wrong, cheaply." Confirm or correct me on that, and say what a Cartesian could have offered the Swiss and British officials if they'd been consulted. Finish with a verdict: does this episode refute the doctrine, sideline it, or show it was never a candidate for refutation by this kind of evidence — and what would have to be the case, in principle, for something to defeat it empirically? If it's a mixture, say which parts get which verdict and why they come apart. Two ground rules. Don't cite anything and don't put words in his mouth between quotation marks; I've been burned before by confident-looking quotes that turned out to be somebody's paraphrase, and I have no way of checking you from where I sit. Paraphrase, tell me what he's committed to, and I'll chase the passages down myself. And I'm aware there's a live dispute among readers about whether his animals feel anything in some non-reflective sense or whether he meant them to be wholly without experience — flag which side you're assuming, note where the analysis would change if you assumed the other, but don't stake the whole thing on resolving it. One last thing on form: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just continuous prose in paragraphs, the way you'd write a long letter to a colleague who's asked a complicated question and deserves a considered answer rather than a formatted one. As long as it needs to be, but I'd like to be able to read it in one sitting with a cup of coffee.
