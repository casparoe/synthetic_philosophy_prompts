Last night I spent three hours arguing with a friend who directs analytics at a consumer lender, and we ended nowhere. His claim, delivered with total confidence, was that the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness are a measurement artifact: the metrics were never designed to hold together, and once an institution settles on the one that expresses its actual normative question — for him, calibration within groups, on the grounds that a score of 0.3 had better mean 0.3 for everybody — the apparent conflict evaporates, and whatever disparities remain are simply the base rates speaking, which is a fact about the world rather than an injustice anybody committed. My claim was that the conflict is structural, and that his "settle on calibration" move carries a cost he would not care to defend aloud in a board meeting. Neither of us budged, and I have decided the way to break the deadlock is to put a fully worked case in front of him rather than more adjectives. I want you to build that case to a specification. Let me be blunt about what would make it useless to me: a scenario that merely announces a disparity has been designed to reach its conclusion, and he will spot that in a minute. The numbers have to generate the conflict out of the definitions of the metrics and nothing else.

Here is the specification. The setting should be a credit union that must, for each applicant, either extend a loan or decline it, predicting default within two years; the two groups are applicants with thin credit files and applicants with established ones, though you may substitute another realistic screening setting if you can tell me why it is better. Give each group exactly sixty applicants. Let the risk score take exactly three distinct values, and make every cell of both two-by-two tables a whole number, so that a reader with a pencil can verify everything. The case must satisfy all of the following at once: the score is exactly calibrated within each group at each of its three values; the base rate of default is higher among thin-file applicants; a single common threshold governs both groups; and at that threshold the false positive rate — the share of applicants who would have repaid but are declined — is materially higher for the thin-file group, while the false negative rate runs the other way, so that thin-file defaulters are caught more often than established-file defaulters. Finally, stipulate away every escape hatch he will reach for: the outcome is recorded identically for both groups, there is no feedback from the decision to the outcome, there is no sample selection, and the score is not standing in for anything it should not be a proxy for. State those stipulations in the scenario itself, which should run to no more than a paragraph before the arithmetic begins.

Then do the work in three movements. First, lay out the construction cell by cell and confirm that calibration, the unequal base rates, and both rate gaps are present simultaneously rather than traded off against one another. Second, take up the two repairs available to someone who wants to keep calibration and equalize one of the rates by means of group-specific thresholds: the adjustment that pulls the false positive rates together, and the adjustment that pulls the false negative rates together. For each, report the resulting counts — how many thin-file applicants receive a loan, how many of those would have defaulted, how many would-have-repaid thin-file applicants are now declined, and what the credit union's expected losses are — and tell me plainly which of the two repairs leaves thin-file applicants worse off on the very harm the repair was meant to address. If exact equalization is unattainable at any threshold on a three-valued score, do not declare it impossible after one attempt; give me the nearest attainable thresholds on either side and the size of the residue, since I suspect that residue is part of what my friend glosses over. Third, settle a side bet. He asserts that if the two groups had the same base rate of default, exact calibration within groups would automatically deliver equal false positive rates. Either build me a sixty-and-sixty case with equal base rates, three score values, exact calibration in both groups, and unequal false positive rates, or tell me that no such case exists and identify the general fact that rules it out.

Close with three things. Tell me which single feature of the construction is doing the work, specified precisely enough that I could delete it and watch the case fall apart — and then actually delete one candidate: say what happens if I drop the stipulation that the outcome is recorded identically for both groups, and whether the case then collapses into the familiar dispute about biased labels with nothing left of the structural point. Tell me what the case is evidence for in the argument I was having: whether it shows that choosing a metric is a substantive moral decision rather than technical housekeeping, or something weaker than that. And tell me how a defender of my friend's position would attack the case rather than answer it — what he would say about the choice of threshold, about what declining an applicant actually costs, or about whether the thin-file base rate is a fact no metric can argue with.

One last thing about presentation. I want the answer as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. That includes the counts — give me each cell of each two-by-two in a sentence, spelled out, rather than in a grid, because I will be reading it on a train and I want to hand it to him afterwards as something he can read straight through. Do not trouble yourself with citations, references, or a list of the relevant papers; I know the literature and will add whatever I need.
