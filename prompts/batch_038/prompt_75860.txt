I retired three years back after 35 years doing precision measurements — mostly building sensitive detectors and then spending my actual working life arguing about noise floors, coupling, and whether a signal was real. Philosophy is the new hobby. I've gotten stuck on epiphenomenalism and on the argument people call the paradox of phenomenal judgment: roughly, that if experience makes no causal difference to anything, then our talking and theorizing about experience can't have been caused by the experience, so the fact that we're sitting here arguing about it is either a miracle or evidence that the view undercuts its own warrant.

Here's what I want. Translate the whole thing — the epiphenomenalist's positive position and the objection to it — into the vocabulary I actually used at the bench: coupling strength, detector efficiency, noise floor, calibration, systematic versus statistical error, back-action, closed versus open systems, and the measurement chain running from the thing measured through the readout to the logged number. Be as faithful as you can. I'd rather have an honest translation than a flattering one.

Some specific things I can't settle on my own, and I want you to take each one on directly:

1. If experience has exactly zero coupling into the physical chain, what is my report of it? In my world a readout with zero coupling to the thing it claims to register isn't a weak measurement or a noisy measurement, it isn't a measurement of that thing at all. So is the epiphenomenalist entitled to words like "tracking" or "accurate"? Can a channel stipulated to carry no influence still be said to be calibrated against something?

2. Suppose I stipulate two setups that produce bit-for-bit identical logged data, and claim that in one of them there is additionally an experience that does nothing. Does "identical data" even have a determinate meaning there, given that what makes a logged number a number *about* something usually depends on the causal history of the chain that produced it? I want to know whether your translation preserves that stipulation or quietly destroys it.

3. Where along the chain is the coupling supposed to vanish — at the level of one cell influencing the next, at the level of the whole system's behavior, somewhere else? Does the location change whether the paradox bites, or does it bite wherever you put it?

4. Is there any analogue in my own trade of a quantity that's perfectly real, enters no equation of motion, and is nonetheless something we can be said to know — a gauge freedom, an overall phase no detector can see, a conserved thing that never couples to anything? If such cases exist, do they help the epiphenomenalist or hurt them? This matters because I want to know whether "no coupling, no knowledge" is a hard constraint on knowledge or just a habit my trade drilled into me.

Then give me the residue report, and be systematic about it: which claims in this debate map cleanly onto coupling/measurment language, which ones only come across if you bend them (say what you bent), and which ones have no expression at all in my vocabulary. And don't cheat — if you render "experience" as "an internal state the system has but makes no use of," you've assumed the disputed point and the whole exercise is worthless. Flag anywhere you're tempted to do that instead of doing it.

Last thing, and its the part I actually care about: once the residue is on the table, tell me whose fault it is. Is the untranslatable remainder a limitation of a bench-top way of thinking — my vocabulary assumes every fact is a fact about something registered somewhere — or is it a sign the original argument was less clear than it sounded, and that the paradox is doing a bit of sleight of hand I should have spotted?

Format: first sentence is your bottom line. Then the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate my question back to me, and no citations or reading lists — I want your own reconstruction, not a bibliography.
