I sit on a seven-member licensing panel that decides compound questions: whether a novel medical device should be approved, where approval turns on three separate findings — that it performs as claimed, that its risks are acceptable given the intended use, and that no less invasive alternative exists. Our last three decisions have come out differently depending on whether the secretary tallied the conclusion or tallied the premises first and let the conclusion follow, and the chair has now proposed that we vote premise by premise in a fixed order, which one member objects will make the result depend on the order in which items are taken. Before we amend our standing orders I am running a half-day workshop to establish what my colleagues actually disagree about, because I suspect several of them are using the same vocabulary for incompatible commitments. What I need is a diagnostic instrument for that workshop, not a survey of the literature and not an argument for one procedure.

Please write a decision tree — or, if you prefer, a scored quiz that amounts to the same thing — of nine to eleven questions, each a concrete voting scenario with a small table of individual member votes that you invent. Make the numbers checkable: show explicitly what the panel's published finding would be under each procedure the question contrasts, and keep the arithmetic simple enough that a reader can verify it in the margin. Two or three answer options per question, phrased in the everyday language of committees (for example, what the panel ought to be recorded as having found, or what the secretary should write in the minutes), never in the technical vocabulary of aggregation theory, and never in a form that lets someone answer by recognising a label rather than by committing to an outcome. The leaves of your tree should be positions on how a group's judgment is to be constituted, each carrying a name where one is standard and a one-sentence statement of what accepting it commits you to, or the marker "unnamed" where you think nobody defends it. At a minimum the leaves must include: the view that the conclusion vote settles it and the premises are only reasons; the view that the premises settle it and the conclusion is read off them; a sequential or agenda-driven view; a view on which the panel has no judgment at all beyond the distribution of its members' votes; some version of picking the most representative consistent set of findings under a measure of distance from the profile, if you think that cell is genuinely occupiable rather than decorative; a view on which members' votes carry different weights by expertise; and a view on which the panel should decline to issue a single finding and publish the pattern of disagreement instead.

Two of my scenarios are the ones I most want handled properly, so build them in. First, include a question where one of the premises is itself a disjunction of two sub-premises on which the panel splits differently, so that taking the disjunction first or its disjuncts first yields opposite published findings; say in the scoring key what that question is diagnosing and what someone who is unmoved by it thereby concedes. Second, include a question where one member is recused on a single premise but not on the others, so that the majorities are computed over different denominators; note whether that merely exposes an ambiguity in the existing leaves or whether it requires a new one. Then flag honestly any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate — I expect at least two procedures to coincide on a simple conjunctive agenda of the kind we run, and I would rather see that stated than concealed behind a question that pretends to distinguish them. Finally, add a short closing section listing the combinations of answers your tree permits but no position occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive once its consequences are spelled out, or simply because nobody has thought to defend it. My audience is clinicians, an engineer, two lay members and a lawyer, and they should be able to complete the instrument in about twenty-five minutes with no background in social choice theory. Please write the whole thing in British English, and do not give me references, a bibliography or citations anywhere — the point is that my colleagues are sorted by what they say about cases, not by what they have read.
