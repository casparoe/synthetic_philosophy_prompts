Two guys from my game-night group got into a long argument in my kitchen last week and I was just sitting there eating cereal, so I'm the one stuck describing it. Neither of them is a philosopher, there both just people who play a lot of games and have thought about this.

The setup: Dev plays an open-world game where the villagers beg for mercy and scream and so on, and he deliberately spends whole sessions doing the worst stuff available — burning down houses with people inside, that kind of thing. Not as a challenge run or for loot, just because it's fun.

1. Marcus said that's morally wrong, not just gross. Wrong even though no real person is affected.
2. Dev said wrongness needs a victim and there isn't one. A villager isn't the sort of thing that can be harmed, any more than the word "cat" on a page can be scratched.
3. Marcus said the victim is Dev: doing that for hours shapes what kind of person you are, the same way people say cruelty to animals matters because of what it does to the cruel person rather than the animal.
4. Dev said that's not an argument that his in-game acts are wrong, it's an argument that his hobby might be bad for him, like doomscrolling or eating garbage — and Marcus opened by calling it wrong, not unwise. He added that if shaping your character is the test, then reading a novel from the villain's point of view does it too, and Marcus reads piles of those.
5. Marcus said there's a real difference: a reader just watches, but in the game you're the one deciding, and you enjoy deciding it. Enjoying pretend cruelty is enjoying cruelty.
6. Dev said it isn't, the same way enjoying a horror movie isn't being scared — it's make-believe, you enjoy the fiction, and a killing on stage isn't a killing. Then he made another move I couldn't answer at the time: the begging is a scripted voice line that fires when you get within a few meters, and the village resets the next morning with everybody alive and no memory of anything. So there isn't even a fictional victim in there, because the story never says anyone suffered — it just plays a scream.
7. Marcus said fine, then explain why a photorealistic game where you abuse a child-shaped character is different from one with cartoon blobs. Dev said it isn't different morally, only aesthetically, since realism is about graphics, and Marcus can't have morality depend on how good the rendering is. Marcus said realism isn't about graphics, it's about what you're imagining, and that's a moral difference, not a technical one.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step? Not just a final score — I want to know which of those seven actually land and which don't, and whether there's something both of them are walking straight past (I have a feeling there is, I just can't say what it'd be). And please be straight with me about which parts of this people who work on it have basically settled versus which parts are still live fights where the smart people are split — I don't want somebody's contested opinion handed to me as if it were the consensus.
