I'm a third-year and I'm in a seminar on the ethics of war this term — we did the usual deterrence weeks, so a bit of Schelling-style rational choice (commitment tactics, brinkmanship, threats that deliberately leave something to chance) and then the moral problem, which as I understand it is that a deterrent only works if you actually intend to retaliate, and retaliating after the fact would be pointless slaughter, so you're required to intend something you'd be forbidden to do. The standard-ish reply in my reading group is that it's fine to form a *conditional* intention — you intend to retaliate if attacked, you hope the condition never arises, and you'd never bring it about yourself — so the intention isn't an intention to do wrong, it's an intention to do wrong only in a world you're trying to prevent. That reply has been bugging me and I think I've landed on why, but I want to test it against an actual constructed case rather than argue it in the abstract. Roughly, I've been sorting deterrent threats into *sealed* ones, where the trigger sits entirely inside the threatener's own decision (they decide whether the condition has been met and they decide whether to act), and *leaky* ones, where the triggering condition can also be satisfied by events nobody chose — a misread early-warning return, a warning system fault, a third state's miscalculation that produces the same observable signature as the attack. The conditional-intention reply feels a lot weaker for leaky threats, because what you're accepting isn't a conditional plan so much as a standing lottery over catastrophe that you keep paying into, and the "I'd never bring it about" clause stops doing any work.

So: build me a thought experiment where a leaky threat is the only deterrent available, it genuinely works, and yet the leader can't honestly hold the conditional intention that's supposed to make it okay. One paragraph of scenario, realistic statecraft — I've vaguely got in my head something like a disputed archipelago or a jointly-staffed radar site, but you can set it wherever, just no doomsday machines, no automated launch systems, nothing that couldn't have happened in the 80s or next tuesday. Then the constraints, and please check each one explicitly afterwards rather than just assuming: both sides are fully informed and instrumentally rational, so the case can't lean on misperception, a madman leader, garbled communications, or domestic audience pressure; the leak has to be in the *trigger* and not in the *response* — the leader keeps full control over whether their own forces ever fire, so the puzzle can't just be loss of control over retaliation; the deterrent has to actually succeed in the scenario, with the adversary's reasoning shown, not just asserted as credible; and the squeamish option has to come out worse, i.e. a leader who refuses to hold the leaky threat and cleans up the entanglement ends up with an outcome they have more reason to regret. No third option falling out of the sky. One extra constraint that matters for my paper specifically: the thing has to still count as a threat and not just a warning. I know that distinction is in the literature, and my seminar partner thinks "leaky" collapses straight into "that's not a threat, it's a prediction about how things might go badly", so the case needs to be one where the leader is doing something, publicly and deliberately, and not merely describing a risk.

After the scenario, walk me through: (a) the adversary's reasoning and why exactly they back down, (b) whether the leader can form the conditional intention and if not which piece of it fails — the condition, the act, or the sincerity — because I suspect it's the condition and I want to see if a case can be built where it isn't, (c) which single feature of your construction is doing the work, and what happens if I delete it — does it collapse back into a sealed-threat case, or into a warning?, and (d) how someone defending the conditional-intention reply would attack your case and whether that attack actually works. Also be straight with me about whether these specs are jointly satisfiable; if one of them can't be met alongside the others, say which one and why rather than quietly dropping it, because "you can't have all of that at once" would itself be a finding I could use. To be clear about what the case is *for*: I'm not trying to show deterrence is immoral or to get a story about how scary nukes are, I'm trying to find out whether sealed/leaky cuts a real joint in the structure of these threats. So a case that just stipulates the leader's mental state, or that has a character announce that the threat is credible, doesn't count — the intentions and the credibility have to fall out of the setup you built.
