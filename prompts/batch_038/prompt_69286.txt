so I'm a philosophy undergrad auditing a seminar on spacetime this semester, and there's one argument I keep chewing on that I can't get anyone to fully answer. Our prof walked us through Newton's bucket — the pail hanging on a twisted rope, where the surface of the water stays flat while the bucket is spinning but the water isn't yet, and then goes concave once the water catches up and is at rest relative to the bucket. When I first read it I thought it totally killed relationism, because the dip in the surface is just something you can measure with a ruler, which means we have a way of detecting our own motion through space itself. Like we'd finally know our absolute velocity and not just our velocity relative to the other junk in the lab.

But then someone in the seminar said special relativity "gets rid of absolute motion," and that flattened me, because if a bucket of water on a lazy susan can read off your absolute velocity, doesn't that just refute SR on the spot? Feels like Einstein could have spared himself a lot of trouble by reading the Principia with more care, since Newton basically had the same experiment two ways — the bucket, and then that follow-up with two globes tied together by a cord spinning about their shared center of gravity out in an otherwise empty region, where he says the pull in the cord tells you about the circling even though there's nothing else around to compare it to. Am I missing something obvious here?

And a variant I've been mulling that I'd love someone to poke holes in: imagine two identical buckets in an otherwise empty universe, spinning at the same rate in opposite directions, then bolted together with a rigid bar so the whole contraption has zero net angular momentum — nothing about the combined system is rotating in any sense I can put my finger on. My intution is the water in each bucket is still concave, which would mean the concavity can't be a fact about the state of the two-bucket system as a whole, only about each bucket's relation to... something else entirely. Is that intuition right? And if it is, why doesn't it end the substantivalism debate for good? Where exactly do people who still defend some version of relationism think this line of reasoning breaks — do they deny the water would be concave, deny it means anything about space being a substance, or something more subtle that I'm just not seeing?
