Write an essay adjudicating a dispute about what al-Ghazālī is actually doing in the seventeenth discussion of *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahāfut al-Falāsifa*), the discussion aimed at the philosophers' claim that a departure from the ordinary course of natural events is impossible. Your audience knows Ashʿarite kalām and Avicenna's modal metaphysics well enough that you need not define terms like necessity-through-another, but has not read the discussion closely.

Begin from what the discussion itself does. Al-Ghazālī holds that the connection between what are customarily called cause and effect is not necessary: neither term's existence or non-existence entails the other's, and the sequence we observe holds because a prior divine decree fixed it, not because the connection is indissoluble in itself. His central example is cotton that burns on contact with fire, and he concedes the possibility both of contact without burning and of burning without contact. Against the claim that fire is an agent acting by nature, he distinguishes an agent that acts by nature from one that acts by choice, and argues that observation establishes only that burning occurs *with* contact, not that it occurs *by* contact; he adds that observed antecedents may conceal an unobserved producer, illustrating this with a man born blind who, on recovering sight, credits the opening of his eyes until sunset teaches him otherwise, and noting that the philosophers' own appeal to a Giver of Forms already reduces sunrise, a sound eye, and a coloured object to merely preparatory conditions.

Now weigh three readings of this material.

Reading A treats the argument as fundamentally evidential and epistemological: the inference from observed accompaniment to necessary production is unsupported, so al-Ghazālī anticipates Hume on the missing ingredient in our idea of causation, and the theological apparatus — the decree, the angels, the Giver of Forms — is scaffolding that could be removed without damaging the argument.

Reading B denies that the comparison survives scrutiny: al-Ghazālī is not a skeptic who leaves the ordinary causal vocabulary intact while doubting its modal force, but a metaphysician who relocates agency altogether, so that the regularity is a regularity in a divine willing rather than a feature of the world's furniture, and so that the fire-and-cotton case is a claim about what there is, not about what we may infer.

Reading C, which I stipulate for you here and which you should not go looking for under that name, treats both readings as missing the discussion's dialectical function. On this view, the argument's work is done entirely by narrowing the modal claim the philosophers require — that a departure from the ordinary course is impossible — and by showing that they cannot secure necessity for the natural connection without already treating the observed antecedent as a mere condition, which is their own doctrine. Al-Ghazālī's apparently concessive remarks, that we nevertheless know what will happen because the habitual course is fixed and does not change, are then not a retreat from occasionalism but the strategy itself: prediction is preserved while the label of necessity is withdrawn from the natural domain, and his positive commitments are left deliberately unstated because the book is a refutation rather than a system.

Say which reading you accept, and do so with a definite verdict rather than a survey; a split verdict of the form "A on this question, C on that one" is acceptable only if you identify precisely which questions receive which answers and why the split is not ad hoc.

Then settle a crux that should govern your choice. Does al-Ghazālī's position license the claim that a world in which fire reliably failed to burn cotton would be a world in which fire had ceased to be fire — because producing burning is constitutive of the nature — or only the weaker claim that fire would remain fire while the divine habit had changed? Argue which option is genuinely available to him, and price each: the first seems to restore to natures exactly the productive work he has denied them, while the second threatens to leave "fire" as a bare name for a position in a sequence, with no content independent of what has been observed to follow it.

Finally, state what your verdict implies for Ibn Rushd's rebuttal in the *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*. If Reading C is right, a substantial part of that rebuttal — the charge that abolishing causes empties natures and with them demonstrative knowledge — would be aimed at a doctrine the seventeenth discussion never asserts; if Reading B is right, the charge lands squarely. Do not leave this as an aside: treat the fit between your chosen reading and Ibn Rushd's target as evidence bearing on the choice.

Between 1,400 and 1,900 words. Argue from the content of the positions themselves; you should not need secondary literature, and you must not append a bibliography or a list of further reading. Avoid historical scene-setting about the madrasa, the vizierate, or the reception of kalām, and avoid closing with a reflection on the debate's contemporary resonance. Spend your words on whether the argument works.
