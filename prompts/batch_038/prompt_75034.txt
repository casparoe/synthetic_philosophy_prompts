I referee for a journal that publishes economic history and comparative politics, and a striking share of the manuscripts I see assert that some outcome was path-dependent, then let the assertion carry the argument. Before I write the same report again, I want the specification I ought to be applying. Lay out what a claim that an outcome was locked in by its own history must deliver in order to be more than a way of saying that it changed slowly: the conditions it has to establish, the distinctions it may not blur — path dependence against mere hysteresis, lock-in against plain durability, a critical juncture against a slow drift — and, for each item, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a matter of taste. Say which requirements are non-negotiable if the claim is to be intelligible at all and which bind only particular schools of explanation. Then take a position on two problems I cannot settle for myself. First, what would count as evidence distinguishing genuine lock-in from the rival thesis that any of several routes would have converged on a functionally equivalent outcome anyway? Second, is the demand that the branching event be identified compatible with the demand that the outcome be robust once reached — if a trajectory is that sensitive to small events, can any one small event properly be called its cause? Close with a sentence or two on which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidate accounts. Please answer in continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
