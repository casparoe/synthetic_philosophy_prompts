I am drafting the methods section for a comparative project that will assess subsistence transitions in several regions against a single set of criteria, and before I choose among the existing verdicts on the shift from foraging to farming I want to know what an adequate framework for rendering such a verdict would have to deliver — so that I can distinguish the published answers that actually answer the question from the axiologies that have merely acquired a case study.

Suppose, as a case I am constructing rather than reporting, two adjacent valleys with the same climate and the same foraging baseline. Valley A adopts a cereal package early. Valley B remains a foraging economy for another fifteen hundred years and then adopts the same package on its own initiative, with no sign of conquest or displacement. In A, five centuries into cultivation, adult stature has fallen by several centimetres, dental enamel defects are several times as frequent as in the foraging series that precede them, and there are clear markers of periodic nutritional stress; but surviving children per woman have roughly tripled, the mortuary record suggests fewer deaths in the first year of life, and from the third century onward a minority of burials carry goods the rest lack. In B, stature and dental health are flat across the same interval while markers of violent trauma rise steadily. Everything available to me is bone and burial, which samples the buried, the sedentary, and those who survived long enough to be interred as adults.

Write the requirements list for a framework that is to say whether the transition made A worse off, giving for each requirement the case or consideration that motivates it. I want the list to cover at least: the choice of welfare metric and what could justify one over another, as against simply stipulating it (health, longevity, hours of work, nutritional security, autonomy, number of people); the fact that A's later generations would not otherwise have existed, and what a framework may say about them; a population several times larger with somewhat worse lives each; the visible minority who did better, and whether inequality is a harm in itself or only through its effects; the absence of any chooser, which may leave the language of mistake without a subject; the fact that once A's numbers rose, the alternative to transition was not stasis but collapse, so the comparison may be between two harms rather than between a harm and a neutral baseline; B's independent adoption, which any framework must be able to read as evidence of something; the bias structure of skeletal and mortuary data; the fact that the transition is a process across many generations rather than an event, so a verdict requires a rule for weighting them; and the fact that we know how things turned out downstream, which a framework must either exclude from the assessment or own as part of it. A framework that cannot say what further evidence would move its verdict should also fail, since the project is comparative and will be run on regions with poorer data than this.

State the distinctions a framework must not collapse, and handle explicitly the one between the transition's having been a mistake and its having made people worse off. Then mark which of your requirements are non-negotiable and which are commitments of one camp masquerading as requirements — I expect at least one of them to be. Say plainly whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable and, if so, which subset; that is the part I most want to see, since a list that cannot be met is itself a result about the question. Finally, identify the single requirement that eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive frameworks, and close with one paragraph — no citations and no reading list, I am not asking for either — locating the two familiar verdicts, catastrophe and net gain, against the list you have produced.
