I'm marking in-class essays for a third-year philosophy of language course and would like a second reader's judgment on one of them.

The question was:

*Quine's jungle linguist, working only from observable behaviour, hears a native utter "Gavagai" whenever a rabbit scurries past. Explain how Quine uses this case to argue for the indeterminacy of radical translation, and evaluate his claim that there is therefore no fact of the matter about what the native's words mean.* (45 minutes, 20 marks; marked both for accuracy about the argument and for the quality of the evaluation.)

The student wrote:

Quine asks us to imagine a linguist attempting radical translation: she is working on a language nobody has previously studied, with no bilingual informants and no vocabulary in common to begin from. Her only evidence is the natives' overt behaviour — what they utter, and whether they assent or dissent when she ventures a rendering while some particular stimulation is in play. A rabbit scurries past, a native says "Gavagai," and the linguist notes the correlation; she confirms it by watching for further rabbit occasions and checking that the same utterance recurs, and she builds her first analytical hypotheses on that basis.

Quine's point is that this procedure cannot single out one translation. Everything the linguist could observe is equally consistent with taking the utterance to be about an undetached part of a rabbit, about a momentary stage of a rabbit, or about an abstract entity such as rabbithood, since whenever a rabbit is present each of these is present too. Which of them the native is talking about is never settled by the evidence, even in principle, because the evidence is exhaustively behavioural. Quine's behaviourism is what makes the conclusion stick: he treats meanings as items inwardly grasped by the speaker, and argues that since the linguist could never observe such items they cannot be what makes one manual right and another wrong. There is nothing to meaning beyond dispositions to respond to stimulations, and those dispositions admit of more than one equally good systematisation. Hence the linguist can never know which manual is correct, and Quine concludes that there is no fact of the matter about what the native's words mean.

Two limits are worth noting. First, the problem is specific to the radical case: where the linguist has a bilingual informant, or is translating between languages she already speaks, there is enough evidence to fix a unique manual, and the thought experiment mainly shows how much of our ordinary interpretive practice is extrapolation from scant data. Second, the conclusion is sceptical in a familiar shape. If the evidence never decides between manuals, speakers cannot be sure that they understand one another and all communication must fail to some degree; Quine's position about meaning resembles the traditional sceptic's position about the external world.

Both limits depend on accepting Quine's behaviourism. A theorist who holds that understanding consists in an internally represented rule rather than in dispositions to assent can deny that the absence of decisive behavioural evidence shows there is no fact of the matter, which suggests the thesis is less radical than it first appears.
