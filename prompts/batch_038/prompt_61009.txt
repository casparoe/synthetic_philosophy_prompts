Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words, with a title, that puts the multiple realizability argument for functionalism under pressure. The philosophy must be carried entirely by situation and dialogue; the reader should finish able to say what went wrong without ever having been told it.

Use the following setting, which is mine and not a familiar one.

A survey authority certifies whether an artificial system instantiates a given mental kind — in this story, being in pain — before that system may be licensed, protected, or decommissioned. Certification proceeds by functional specification alone: the auditor checks whether the candidate's states stand in the right causal relations to inputs, to other states, and to outputs, and is instructed to treat the medium that fills those roles as irrelevant. Your protagonist is an auditor arriving at a remote outer-system installation with two certifications to complete before a decommissioning deadline that the story should make the reader feel.

Both candidates must appear on the page:

The first is a sprawling lattice of habitats, chemical vats, and relay beacons distributed across a distance that light takes hours to cross. On paper its causal map is exactly right: the same roles, the same relations, filled by the same topology. But because of the transit time between its parts, there is no moment at which the whole system is in any single state, and an auditor who watches its outputs is always seeing the consequences of conditions that have long since changed. Nobody can catch it being in anything.

The second is a compact machine in a sealed room that passes every functional test instantly and without inconsistency. Its defect, which the auditor discovers late, is that its state transitions are not produced by its own internal wiring: a scheduler outside the machine, sitting between input and output, supplies each transition from a table prepared in advance. The machine is functionally flawless precisely because nothing inside it is ever required to do anything.

Constraints on execution:

- Write in third person limited, close to the auditor, in a plain procedural register. The strangeness should come from the facts, not the prose.
- The words *functionalism*, *multiple realizability*, *qualia*, *substrate*, and *consciousness* must not appear anywhere in the story. The characters should talk like engineers and administrators who have stakes and opinions, not like participants in a seminar.
- Include one scene in which a character explains the certification standard to a layperson by means of an analogy, and make the analogy wrong in a way an attentive reader can identify. No one in the story corrects it.
- At least half the story should be dialogue. The auditor's view must change, or must conspicuously fail to change, for reasons the reader can inspect rather than infer from mood.
- End on the signature. The auditor must certify one candidate, refuse the other, or do neither, and the closing pages must leave it genuinely arguable which choice the standard itself licenses.

After the story, add an author's note of no more than 300 words, addressed to me rather than to the reader, and naming no philosopher, school, or published work. In it, do three things. First, identify the single premise about realization that the two candidates attack from opposite directions, and explain why they are two versions of one problem rather than two unrelated problems. Second, give the strongest reply a defender of specification-by-function would make to each candidate, and say plainly whether either reply can be made without abandoning the standard. Third, name one change you would make to the setup that would dissolve the tension, and state what the fact that it dissolves the tension shows about where the argument's weight was actually resting.
