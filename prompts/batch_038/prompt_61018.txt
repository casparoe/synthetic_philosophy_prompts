For the second session of my graduate seminar in the philosophy of technology, I want to hand out a diagnostic instrument rather than another reading summary: a decision tree that sorts students into a position on whether artifacts have politics, on the basis of what they say about concrete cases rather than which author they admire.

Please build it in the following order.

**First, the space.** The debate as it is usually taught collapses at least three questions that can be answered independently, and I want the tree built out of them rather than out of a list of names. Let a position be a tuple, not a label:

- (i) *Causal*: does the technical configuration of an artifact make a difference to social outcomes that could not have been made by the human decisions surrounding it — or does it only transmit differences already present in those decisions?
- (ii) *Political in the strict sense*: can an artifact embody or enact power, authority, or a form of life, in a sense stronger than "people use it politically"?
- (iii) *Developmental*: is the sequence of technical change internally constrained — trajectory, lock-in, path dependence — so that social choice only selects among variants the technology already licenses?

Add a fourth axis for *locus*: if there is politics in the artifact at all, where does it sit — in the designer's intention, in the operational requirements of the design, in the interpretive work of the relevant social groups, in the network of human and non-human actants, or in the wider socio-technical regime?

**Second, the questions.** Nine branch nodes, each presenting a case with two or three answer options. No question may ask whether artifacts have politics, whether technology is neutral, or which camp the student belongs to; every node must be answerable by someone who has never encountered the literature. Use these cases (you may split one across two nodes or add a tenth of your own):

1. A parkway whose overpasses were built to a clearance too low for public buses, so the beach at the end of it was in practice reachable only by private car — and the designer wanted it that way.
2. A mechanical tomato harvester that became viable only once a fruit variety had been bred to survive machine handling and only on large, level, capital-intensive acreage; developed at a public experiment station funded by growers, its spread coincided with the disappearance of thousands of small holdings.
3. The stirrup argument — that mounted shock combat became possible only with the stirrup and that the cost of the equipment helped produce a landed warrior class — together with the historians' standard reply that the chain contains too many optional steps.
4. A hiring or eligibility algorithm that reproduces a disparity visible in its training data; no one intended it, it is not in the specification, and it arguably tracks an existing social pattern faithfully.
5. A spring-and-hydraulic door closer replacing the junior colleague who used to shut the door, and a road hump replacing the traffic warden.
6. A ranking system optimized for advertising revenue that amplifies outrage, where the amplification appears in no one's plan and no one can point to the responsible component.
7. A city replacing paper ballots with electronic voting machines, where officials, disability advocates, security researchers and the vendor describe the same device incompatibly, and the dispute ends only when one description wins.
8. A large centralized generating technology whose safe operation demands hierarchical, security-conscious, expert management, versus the same nominal service delivered by small distributed units — politics located in requirements rather than intentions.

**Third, the leaves.** Ten to fourteen positions, named where a name exists (hard or autonomous-technology determinism; soft or leading-cause determinism; the interpretive-flexibility-and-closure view; the symmetric delegation-and-prescription view; critical theories that keep the artifact's ambivalence but politicize the design code; the dual-nature program treating artifacts as having both physical and intentional properties; affordance- or disposition-based middle positions; the deflationary view, associated with Woolgar and Cooper, that artifacts are irreducibly ambivalent and that the celebrated bridge story functions as an urban legend of the field; a historical-materialist variant on which technology is a site of class struggle rather than an independent cause), and marked *unnamed* where nobody defends the combination. Note that the two classic modes of artifact politics — politics deliberately built into a design, and politics implied by a technology's operational requirements — should be separate leaves, not one. For every leaf give one sentence stating what it is committed to saying about case 4; using the same probe for all of them is what makes the leaves comparable.

**Fourth, the gaps.** Mark at least three points in the cross-product that no position occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored. My suspicion is that the third category is badly under-populated here, and I want the students to see the hole rather than assume the map is complete.

**Fifth, the failures.** Flag at least two pairs of leaves your instrument cannot actually separate, and say whether some further case question would separate them or whether the distinction is only verbal. I expect the delegation and prescription views to be trouble, and the affordance view against soft determinism as well — but report where you find the trouble rather than taking my guess for granted.

Close with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, and tell me which single question does most of the sorting work.

Two to three pages. No bibliography, reading list, or citations: the students have done the reading, and I do not want the handout to drift into being a reference section. Names and author attributions belong only where the name is part of the position.
