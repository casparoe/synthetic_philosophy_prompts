I'm applying for a nine-month postdoc on a small seed fund attached to an AI governance institute, and they want a proposal of about 1,200 words, length strictly enforced. Here's my half-formed idea — can you turn it into an actual proposal?

The setup: everyone talks about a "responsibility gap" for autonomous systems. The system acts, somebody gets hurt, and there's no one you can justly blame, because the developer followed the validation protocol, the operator followed the manual, and the organisation's procedures were all in place. My hunch is that the debate has stalled because both sides are arguing about whether a blameworthy *agent* exists, and that's the wrong question. What's actually missing in the bad cases isn't a culprit, it's a transaction: the ability of the person who was harmed to address a demand to somebody, get an intelligible answer back, and see something happen in response. If that loop can't be completed there's a gap even when plenty of people are technically blameworthy, and if it can be completed there's no gap even when nobody is culpable. So I want to pull apart gaps of locus (nobody to point at) from gaps of discharge (somebody to point at, but no process that could actually receive the demand and answer it), and argue that only the second kind is interesting.

The two cases I'd work through. One: an autonomous cargo vessel alters course in a way that's legal and sensible in isolation, a smaller boat grounds avoiding it, and afterwards the flag state, the software vendor, the classification society and the shore operator can each show they did exactly what they were supposed to. Two: a hospital's triage algorithm quietly deprioritises a patient group, it's found a year later, the model was validated per protocol and the clinicians never saw the score so they never had anything to override. In both, the insurer paid out, nobody was blamed, and if you ask the family "who do I put the question to?", there's no non-arbitrary answer.

What I need from you:

- the research question stated precisely enough that it could genuinely come out either way;
- why the existing positions leave it open — including the "the gap is an illusion, blame distributes fine" line, the strict-liability and compensation-scheme answers, and the problem-of-many-hands material — and specifically why none of them is actually about the transaction;
- the approach, in three phases across the nine months, and why this rather than the two obvious alternatives (build a better account of distributed blame, or go fully institutional and just design an oversight regime);
- what a successful result looks like: I have in mind a test, specifiable before a system is deployed, for whether an institution can take and answer a demand about that system's behaviour. The proposal should say what this test has to do that existing due-diligence and audit standards don't;
- an honest account of what would count as the project failing — e.g. if the conditions turn out to be satisfied by any org with a complaints mailbox, if the test can't be run ex ante, or if it collapses into a relabelling of strict liability;
- a short paragraph on scope: nine months, one person, no fieldwork budget.

One more thing: if there's already a distinction in the literature that does the work I'm gesturing at — something around answerability versus attributability, or responsibility voids, or whatever — say so inside the proposal rather than letting me find out from a reviewer, and explain what's left over for me to do.
