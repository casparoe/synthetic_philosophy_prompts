i supervise the call-taking floor at a regional emergency dispatch centre and there's an inconsistency in how we're run that i keep failing to explain to new hires. here's the setup: every call is worked from a scripted protocol — a tree of preset questions in a fixed order, where the answers map onto a code, and the code determines what we send, how fast, and what instructions the caller gets (start compressions, don't move them, unlock the front door, stay on the line). we're explicitly forbidden from adding our own questions, reordering them, or going off script; deviating is a disciplinable matter, and the line they feed you in training is that your own judgement is the least reliable component in the whole system. but the same organisation assesses us as individuals. certification and annual recertification include a written exam on anatomy, on red flags, on spotting an atypical presentation — most of which the script already encodes — and the QA reviewers pull recorded calls and score whether *you* reached the right code, and will sit you down afterwards to walk through your reasoning. so the floor tells us the thinking lives in the card, and the HR process tells us it lives in the person, and both are treated as non-negotiable.

i want a proper unpacking of what the whole arrangement presupposes about where cognition sits. does the floor practice commit us to something like the extended mind — call-taker plus card plus the caller on the other end of the line as one cognitive system — or can a more modest picture do the work, one where the card is just a reliable external scaffold for a process that's still wholly inside the skull? i know there's a standing objection that coupling isn't constitution (using a thing doesn't make it part of your mind, or the whole world floods in), and something about the conditions an external resource has to meet to count — availability, trust, automatic endorsement — and i'd like to know whether our card actually meets them or whether it fails on some of them in an interesting way. relatedly: our QA scoring looks to me like it violates the parity idea, since the same input-output pattern gets praised when it happens in someone's head and marked down when it comes out of reading the card aloud correctly. is that reading fair? and is the training slogan — the algorithm beats your gut — a separate commitment that could be held by a strict internalist, or is it doing quiet work for the extended picture? i also wonder whether the certification regime presupposes a reliabilist epistemology in which the belief-forming process is allowed to include a document, because if so the written exam is testing something the process doesn't need.

what i'm after: a reconstruction of the implicit theory, a clear separation of what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and an honest account of where it's philosophically underdetermined — my suspicion is several incompatible views license exactly this setup. tell me whether there's a coherent reading on which both halves make sense; my own hunch is that the exam survives because the card can't notice when the caller's words don't fit its categories, which sounds like the person is doing something the script structurally can't, but then i can't tell whether that's internal competence or just another link in the same distributed system. and finally, what would actually have to change — in certification, in hiring, in who gets blamed after a bad outcome — if the org took the extended view seriously instead of merely behaving as if it were true. flag anything about responsibility i'm not seeing, because after a miss, "the code was wrong" and "she was wrong" seem to come apart and i dont have the vocabulary for that gap.
