ok so i'm helping run a one-off evening thing — half philosophy reading group, half open mic — and the theme is counterfactual history: whether the big outcomes were coming anyway, or whether everything hinged on a bullet in Sarajevo and a bad storm in the channel. I want to open the night with a poem and i'd like you to write it. Play the part of someone who's both a working poet and a philosopher of history — equally at home with a sestina and with the literature on causation. Think carefully, don't just start versifying.

Form first: a full sestina. Six stanzas of six lines plus the three-line envoi, 39 lines total, in the standard rotation — the one where each end-word occupies each of the six positions exactly once across the six stanzas, and the envoi picks up all six words again. The rotation isn't decoration here, it's the thesis: a counterfactual doesn't add or remove any furniture from the world, it just rearranges what was already in it. Same six things, every time, in a different order. If a reader who knows the form can feel an argument happening *because* the words keep coming back in new slots, it's working; if the permutation is just noise underneath a generic poem about the past, it isn't.

Before the poem, brainstorm out loud for a few lines: name your six end-words, say in a sentence each what job it's doing, and flag which ones are meant to carry double duty — i want at least two that can be read as both a single event and a standing structure (a "current," a "track," a "harvest," whatever you land on). That ambiguity is where the whole controversy lives. Then sketch the order you want the ideas to arrive in, then write it.

On content, actually engage with these rather than gesturing at them:

- the gap between a trigger being contingent and an outcome being overdetermined — enough separate pressures pointing at the same terminus that the war finds another door even if the driver never takes that wrong turn.
- the awkward fact that you can't cleanly delete one event and keep the rest of the century intact. either the whole upstream past shifts with it, or you need something like a small miracle to hold the past fixed while the future forks. let the poem pay that cost instead of pretending it's free.
- the suspicion that inevitability talk can't lose: whatever happens gets redescribed as what was always coming, because the weighting we assign to which factors matter is already doing the work before we look.

What I don't want: name-dropping. No footnotes, no philosopher's name inside the poem, no "as the old maxim has it." A famous nose can absolutely appear as an image, just unattributed. Also no sermonizing and no shrugging about the tapestry of time — the envoi is allowed to land somewhere, but it should be a small earned landing, not a moral.

American English spelling throughout, please.

Then after the poem, add a short note in plain prose, maybe 150 words: tell me how confident you are that the poem actually gets these ideas right rather than just sounding like it does, single out the point you're least sure of — the image or claim you'd most expect a specialist to object to — and own up to any liberties, like collapsing two distinct senses of "inevitable" into one word because the form demanded it.
