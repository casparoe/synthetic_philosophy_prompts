So I'm in this informal reading group where we're going through political texts written by working scientists, and this week it was Einstein's 1949 essay Why Socialism? Half of us had read it before and half hadn't, and the discussion kind of derailed into an argument I want your take on. One friend insisted the whole essay is just the socialist calculation debate repackaged — that when Einstein complains about the anarchic character of an economy coordinated by private capital and suggests a planned economy is the obvious corrective, he's repeating what Lange and Lerner already argued, namely that a planning board could simulate market prices by trial and error, and that Hayek's reply about dispersed and largely tacit knowledge being uncollectable by any central body lands on Einstein exactly as hard as on anyone else. I disagreed, but I couldn't articulate why beyond a vague feeling that Einstein is worried about something else entirely, and I got talked down.

Here's what I think the actual difference is, and I'd like you to tell me whether I'm onto something or just confusing myself. Near the end Einstein raises what he treats as the genuinely hard problem: a planned economy is not automatically a socialist one, because concentrating economic power in a single bureaucracy creates an apparatus the individual citizen has no effective way of checking, so the protection of individual rights and of meaningful democratic decision-making becomes the part that stays unsolved. That reads to me like a claim about domination and about who can hold power accountable, not a claim about whether the right prices can be computed. So: compare Einstein's worry against the two poles of the calculation debate — Hayek's near-impossibility conclusion about knowledge, and the optimistc Langean answer about iterative price-setting — and say precisely where Einstein's objection lands. Is it a feasibility objection, an efficiency objection, or a liberty objection in disguise? And can a market socialist just respond that keeping consumer goods markets and socializing only investment means Einstein's concern never bites, or does the concern survive even a scheme that leaves most of the price system intact?

The reason I care about pinning this down is that the same shape of argument keeps getting deployed about technology companies. A huge retailer or platform coordinates production, logistics and internal pricing without using market prices at all, so people like to say it's effectively a planned economy roughly the size of a mid-sized country; throw in modern machine learning and the claim becomes that Lange's board is finally technically feasible rather than merely imaginable. If that's right, does Einstein's problem dissolve, or does it just relocate from the state to whoever operates the planner? Be concrete about what would have to be true institutionally for each of those answers to hold — who appoints the planners, what exit options workers and consumers actually have, how you'd know the thing had gone bad. No need for sources or further reading on this, I mainly want the conceptual mapping and where the disanalogies bite.
