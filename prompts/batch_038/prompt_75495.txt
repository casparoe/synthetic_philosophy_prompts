You are a classicist with a working background in folklore studies and the methodology of oral tradition, and you are good at taking a popular analogy apart without being dismissive of it. Think carefully, and before you judge the analogy, list the candidate correspondences it invites rather than leaping to the verdict.

Context: I am a PhD student working on the evidential value of legendary narrative. At a recent workshop a speaker argued against reading anything of the Late Bronze Age out of Homer by invoking the children's game of telephone — a message passes down a chain of retellers, each introducing small errors, so what reaches the end bears no reliable relation to where it started; hence any match between the Iliad and the archaeology at Hisarlik is coincidence, and appeals to the Hittite references to Wilusa and Ahhiyawa cannot rehabilitate the poems. Versions of the same analogy do real work in skeptical treatments of the Exodus and in dismissals of the Arthurian material. My suspicion is that it fits some of these traditions far better than others, since a manuscript tradition copied by literate scribes and elaborated by a named author like Geoffrey of Monmouth is not the same transmission medium as metered oral performance, and that meter and formulaic diction may act as something like error-correction — though I suspect that reply overstates itself in turn.

Please audit the analogy and deliver three things.

First, a correspondence table with columns: `source_feature` (an element of the telephone game — the whisper, the fixed initial message, the linear chain, the lack of stakes, the audience who never hears the original, the scoring by fidelity); `proposed_counterpart` (what in oral-traditional transmission it is taken to be); `exists_in_target` (yes / partial / no); `why_it_matters`; `severity` (fatal / weakening / cosmetic). Here, and explicitly in prose, flag what the analogy smuggles in that the target may lack — above all any tacit assumption that there was a determinate original message, and that fidelity means preserving it rather than preserving a genre or a set of salient names and places.

Second, a per-case table with columns: `case` (Homeric Troy; King Arthur; the Exodus); `actual_transmission_medium`; `where_telephone_fits`; `where_it_breaks`; `net_signal_about_historicity`. Cases may get opposite answers, and I expect them to.

Third, a bottom line of three to five sentences: whether the conclusion the speaker drew survives, and for which cases; and if the analogy should be replaced, name a better source domain and say what it does better.
