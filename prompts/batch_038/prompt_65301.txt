Two years ago my business partner and I agreed that she could buy a slice of the shares I hold in the company we founded. We did it again last month, at a price I thought fair, and I expect we will do it again. There is, so far as I can tell, no gap between what she knows and what I know: we sit in the same board meetings, read the same memos, have called the same customers, and watch the same numbers.

A friend tells me I should be uneasy regardless. Her reasoning is that if my partner knew exactly what I know and still wanted my shares rather than her own, she would have to think those shares worth more than I do, and the only way she could think that is by having some reason I lack — so her continuing to make the offer is itself a warning sign, whatever else she says. My partner's own explanation is that she has a longer horizon than I do and no near-term need for cash, which I find plausible but which my friend dismisses as cover. Then my friend's argument seems to me to prove far too much: on her reasoning nobody would ever buy anything from anybody, and yet people trade constantly and mostly without regret.

Write back the way you would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, continuous prose only. Give me your verdict on my friend's argument in your first few sentences, and only then the reasoning — above all, what would have to be true of our situation for her argument to go through, and what feature of it might make it fail.
