I am a senior at a small school with an elective in practical philosophy, and the term ends with a student symposium in which each of us presents and then defends one argument of our own construction. My assigned theme is decision-making under deep uncertainty, and I have built a case against the precautionary principle that I believe is valid but which I am now reluctant to present, because its conclusion is far stronger than I intended: it appears to condemn not only the precautionary principle but also the expected-utility-based cost-benefit analysis that is normally proposed as its replacement. I would like a diagnosis before the symposium rather than a defence of my conclusion. To fix the target, I am working from Principle 15 of the 1992 Rio Declaration: "Where there are threats of serious or irreversible damage, lack of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent environmental degradation." Here is the argument as I have written it out.

(1) A principle of practical rationality is admissible only if, in the decision situations it is designed to govern, it yields at least one permissible option and does not issue incompatible demands. (2) The precautionary principle requires that, where an activity poses a plausible threat of serious or irreversible harm, precautionary measures be taken even though the probability of that harm has not been scientifically established, and it does not permit postponing the decision until the probabilities are in. (3) The standard answer to the charge that such a principle is paralyzing is to point at the proportionality clause already present in formulations like the one above: measures must be cost-effective and proportionate to the damage they avert. (4) Cost-effectiveness and proportionality are comparative notions; applying them requires that the magnitude of the harm averted and the magnitude of the cost incurred be commensurable on some common scale. (5) But in exactly the situations that call the principle into play, the averted harm is unquantified, and by the same token so is the harm the precautionary measure brings about — forgone treatments, suppressed yields, research displaced into less scrupulous jurisdictions, benefits that never arrive because the technology was frozen early. (6) Therefore the proportionality clause cannot be applied in the situations where it is supposed to rescue the principle from paralysis. (7) With proportionality unavailable, the principle collapses into a demand for some precautionary measure against every activity carrying a plausible unquantified threat — and precautionary measures are themselves activities carrying plausible unquantified threats. (8) Hence the principle requires precaution against the precautions it requires, and, since nothing changes at the next level up, it generates an endless hierarchy of requirements instead of terminating in a determinate set of permissible options. (9) Therefore, by (1), the precautionary principle is inadmissible precisely in the circumstances for which it was designed. (10) Yet the reasoning at (4) through (6) is not specific to that principle: any rule directing us to weigh harms whose probabilities are not established is inadmissible by (1), which includes expected-utility-based regulatory analysis, since expected utility likewise cannot be computed when the probabilities have not been established. So either (1) condemns practical rationality as such under deep uncertainty, or it does not condemn the precautionary principle.

My teacher tells me that steps (7) through (9) are a familiar objection that I should not expect credit for, and that a version worth presenting has to survive the proportionality reply; I think (3) through (6) is that version, but I am not confident. So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I can preserve only two of (1), (2), and the block (3)–(6), I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since a precautionary principle that is not triggered until the probabilities are known is not the principle anyone actually invokes, and since I do not want to abandon the idea that a decision rule must be action-guiding. Four specific questions. First, does the trigger condition in (2) — a plausible threat of a certain gravity — get satisfied by the mere fact that harms on both sides are unquantified, or does my (5) need something further to run? Second, is there a formulation stated in terms of uncertainty rather than risk, one that never mentions probabilities at all, which would block (7)? Third, is the defect in (8) really the defect (1) prohibits: is a principle that never finishes issuing demands thereby in the same trouble as one that issues contradictory demands, and does it matter that (1) seems written for decision procedures rather than for standards, which are routinely applied with judgment supplying what the standard does not? Fourth, a defender who keeps both (1) and (2) owes an account of the asymmetry — why the unquantified threat of a novel technology counts while the unquantified cost of blocking it does not — and I want to know the least ad hoc asymmetry available to them, whether it rests on irreversibility, on the burden of proof sitting with the proponent of the activity, or on something else entirely.
