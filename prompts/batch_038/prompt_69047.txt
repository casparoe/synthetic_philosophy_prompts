**Deliberation requires an opaque self-model (draft)**

*1.* The embedded-agency literature starts from a complaint about the classical picture of an agent: in that picture the map and the territory are two seperate things, and the map is allowed to be as complete as you like, because the mapmaker stands nowhere in particular. But an actual agent is inside the territory it models. Its world-model is a physical structure in the world, so a complete model of the world has to include a model of the model — which is just that old idealist chestnut about a map so accurate that it has to contain a picture of itself. If the map represents the map, and that representation is itself accurate all the way down, you get an infinite regress of representation, or at any rate a representation of unbounded size, inside a finite head.

*2.* This regress is sometimes taken to show that no finite agent can have a complete self-inclusive world-model. I think that inference is half right at best, and that the interesting conclusion is narrower. Completeness is a red herring: nobody needs a complete model, and a model can point at a region of the territory without depicting it. What the regress really rules out is self-transparency.

Definition. A model M is *self-transparent* at a region R of what it models if M represents R's behaviour in a way that can be substituted for R salva veritae: running the representation gives you what R does, with no residue and no further work. M is *self-opaque* at R if it represents R only at an interface — enough to constrain and predict R's outputs, not enough to stand in for R.

*3.* My claim: for an agent that decides, self-opacity is not a compromise forced on it by finite memory or by the impossibility of infinite regress. It is constitutive of there being anything left to decide. If an agent's model of its own choice were transparent, the model would already contain the answer, and the computation we call deliberation would be a redundant copy of a fact already recorded in the map. Transparency makes the agent a spectator of itself: it is in the position of reading off what it will do, and reading off is not doing. On this view the fixed-point trouble at the centre of embedded agency is not an obstacle to be engineered around — it is what keeps the agent's own decision unsettled, and hence what keeps it a decision.

*4.* The obvious reply, and the reason the draft has been sitting. Let the agent know its own algorithm. Its self-model says: I will output the argmax of Q over actions. That is about as transparent as a self-model gets, and yet the agent still has to run the search to find out what it outputs. So transparency does not hand over the answer for free; it hands over a description of a procedure. Which makes *3* look like the old fatalism worry in new clothes — if you know what you'll do you can't deliberate — and that worry is a non-problem, since knowing what you'll do is perfectly compatible with doing it, and you may know it in virtue of the fact that you're going to.

So either *3* collapses into fatalism and I should cut it, or there is a real difference between "the model describes the procedure that produces my choice" and "the model contains my choice," and that difference is doing all the work. I lean toward the second, but I can't get the distinction to stand up. Any procedure-description seems convertible in principle into an output-description by simply running it; and if running it is all the conversion requires, then the difference between the two cases is about cost rather than kind. That leaves me with the much weaker claim that deliberation is redundant only where it is cheap, which nobody will quarrel with, and which certainly isn't a thesis.
