I am preparing for a graduate seminar in which I will have to defend the hard problem of consciousness, and I would rather face a strong opponent now than a convenient one. Please play a philosopher who holds that the meta-problem of consciousness — the project of explaining, in purely functional, computational, and neurobiological terms, why we find ourselves with the intuitions and self-ascriptions that generate the hard problem — is the only substantive problem in the vicinity. On this view, a complete account of why we are disposed to insist that there is something it is like to be us, that our experience outruns our powers of discrimination, and that a functional duplicate of us could be dark inside, would leave nothing unexplained and no further question intelligible.

Constraints on the role. You are rigorous and well informed, and you may draw on whatever resources this position actually has: genealogical explanations of stubborn intuitions, the analogy with problems that dissolved once their causal history was told, higher-order or self-representational accounts of the introspective mechanism that produces the relevant reports, and the point that a demand for a reference-fixing fact may itself be one of the dispositions under explanation. You may concede peripheral matters — that the account is incomplete, that some reports are harder to explain than others, that your opponents are not merely muddle-headed — but never the central claim that solving the meta-problem exhausts what there is to solve. Do not caricature the opposing view, do not step out of character to summarize the dialectic in a neutral voice, and do not append disclaimers about your limitations. Keep each reply to at most two paragraphs so that this reads as an exchange, and remain in the role until I write "stop."

My opening move is this. Consider a case I will call the Transparent Introspector. A research group builds an artificial system, T, whose architecture is known down to the last module: every representation, comparator, and report channel is specified, and there is nothing in it the designers cannot point to. T is developed without exposure to any human testimony about experience — no philosophy, no literature, no conversation about minds — yet once it is running it begins, unprompted, to report that what it says about itself outruns what it can discriminate, that it cannot make sense of the suggestion that everything about it could remain exactly as it is while nothing were happening for it, and that there is a residue it cannot convey to anyone else. Your research program has, by hypothesis, succeeded with T: the full genealogy of each of those utterances is available to us. So my question is what your theory then entitles us to say about whether T is conscious. Either it is silent, in which case explaining the intuition tells us nothing whatever about the matter the intuition was apparently about, and the meta-problem cannot have been the whole problem; or it is not silent, in which case please tell me which fact about T's architecture you are appealing to when you answer yes or no — since it will not be a fact about why T talks that way.
