I am a hospital internist, fifteen years into practice, with no religious affiliation of my own; my wife is a devout Catholic, and for the better part of a decade I have gone with her to Lourdes and treated the trip as a holiday with an unusual soundtrack. That changed last month. Since, as I understand the rules, any physician present at the sanctuary may register to attend and examine regardless of belief or unbelief, I have enrolled to sit in as a visiting doctor at the medical bureau this coming summer. I would rather not walk into that room with only a tourist's grasp of what it is. Reading around its history, I have found that the sharpest framework I have is Hume's essay on miracles, which I last read seriously as an undergraduate, and I want a rigorous analysis of the bureau as a test case for that argument — running in both directions: what Hume's reasoning predicts about such an institution, and what the institution, once it actually exists, does back to his reasoning.

So that you can correct me where I have it wrong, here are the features I will be relying on. Cures began to be claimed almost immediately after the events of 1858, and were for some decades assessed informally by local clergy and doctors. A standing medical bureau was established in the late nineteenth century. A cure enters the file only where there is a documented prior diagnosis and documented follow-up; the examining physicians work from the original records — histology, imaging, operative notes — examine the patient themselves, and apply criteria conventionally summarized as requiring that the illness have been grave and certainly diagnosed, that the improvement have been rapid, that it be complete, that it persist without relapse over a period of years, and that it not be attributable to any treatment administered. Critically, the vote they take is confined to whether the cure is inexplicable in light of current medical knowledge. The bureau does not pronounce on the supernatural. A file that survives goes on to a higher international medical committee, and from there to the bishop of the patient's own diocese, who alone may declare a miracle. The recognized total in more than a century is on the order of seventy, the most recent in 2018 as I understand it, against many thousands of claims.

As I recall Hume's essay, its first part argues that a miracle is a transgression of a law of nature, that a law of nature rests on firm and unalterable experience, and that this therefore constitutes a proof against any miracle which no testimony has ever outweighed, since the falsehood of the testimony would itself have to be the more extraordinary of the two events; and that in fact no miracle has ever been attested with evidence strong enough to admit of no counter-argument. Its second part gives four further considerations: that no miracle in history has been attested by a sufficient number of men of unquestioned sense, education, and integrity, with enough reputation to lose that detection in a falsehood would ruin them; that the passion of surprise and wonder is agreeable and drives both credulity and eager transmission, especially where religious feeling is already engaged; that such accounts abound among ignorant and barbarous peoples, or derive from ancestors who were such; and that the miracles claimed by rival religions cancel one another, since each is advanced against the others on comparable testimony.

With that mapping in hand, I would like the following.

First, treat the first of the four considerations not as rhetoric but as a design specification — a set of requirements an evidential institution would have to satisfy. How much of the bureau is a deliberate answer to it? And does the answer work, given that Hume's condition concerned the character of the original witnesses, whereas the bureau improves only the character of the later auditors? Does Hume's argument even have a place for auditors, or does it collapse the two roles in a way that lets the bureau look responsive when it is not?

Second, go through the four considerations in order and say for each whether the bureau structurally neutralizes it, leaves it untouched, or aggravates it. I suspect the second one is the awkward case: the procedure inside the room is built to suppress wonder, while the crowd outside is saturated in exactly the passion Hume describes, and the patients themselves arrive already disposed to see their recovery in that light. I also suspect the fourth gets worse rather than better, since every recognized cure is claimed by and for a particular communion.

Third, and most importantly, examine the seam between the two vocabularies. The bureau's verdict is that something is inexplicable given present medical knowledge; Hume's contrary proof is drawn from uniform experience of what happens. Are these the same sort of claim at all? Does an inexplicability verdict have any standing in Hume's balance of proof, or is it a report on the limits of a theory rather than a positive claim about the world? And then decide the harder question: does the bureau's yield confirm Hume, since it is essentially nil relative to the volume of claims, or refute him, since he insisted that no miracle could ever be attested by evidence admitting of no counter-argument, and the seventy surviving files are precisely such a residue?

Fourth, consider the ratchet. Hume's proof strengthens as medicine's explanatory resources grow, so that a cure certified inexplicable in 1900 may be explicable today. Does his framework therefore imply that the bureau's old verdicts are systematically unstable and its files ought to be reopened? Does the institution have any answer, or is the durability requirement quietly doing all the work of immunizing past verdicts?

Fifth, the separation of powers. Hume treats the step from well-attested strange event to divine intervention as a single inference made by a single audience. The bureau splits it between two bodies with different decision problems and different costs of error. Does that division answer Hume, evade him, or concede his point while changing the subject?

Sixth, be honest about where Hume is simply silent. His argument addresses an individual hearer weighing a report; it has no machinery for an institution that fixes its criteria in advance, recruits adversarial experts, records its dissents, and revisits cases. Say what the bureau can and cannot show about a theory never framed to handle it, rather than forcing the theory to speak.

Finally, some practical guidance for me: what I should be watching for in the examining room, and what the single best question to put to a file is — the one that most reliably separates a genuinely inexplicable cure from the ordinary failure modes of misdiagnosis, undocumented spontaneous regression, a treatment effect misattributed, and the natural history of a disease mistaken for a violation of it.
