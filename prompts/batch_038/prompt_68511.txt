I'm not a philosopher — I do battery materials — so my exposure to this is a philosophy of religion seminar I audited a few years back and an argument with my brother in law that has now run across three Thanksgivings and one very long text thread. Fine-tuning is the argument that won't let go of me, and what I've noticed is that I don't react to the cases the way the people in the seminar did, or the way he wants me to. So I sat down and wrote out my actual verdicts, and I'd like to know what principle they add up to, if any.

Lottery: my friend wins a jackpot with odds of something like one in three hundred million. Nothing to explain, as far as I'm concerned. Somebody was going to win, it happend to be her, and I feel zero pull toward believing the drawing was rigged — even when I add that she was the only person who bought a ticket that year. Firing squad: the whole squad misses me at twenty feet. I immediately believe there was a plan — a payoff, blanks, somebody aiming wide — and I hold onto that when someone points out that the particular pattern of misses that occurred was no more likely than any other pattern. Those two are the same shape on paper: a very unlikely outcome, which I was equally in a position to observe either way. I cannot see why they feel so different to me, and "one of them involves me" doesn't satisfy me, because the dial case does too. Dial case: suppose some quantity has to be set within about three positions out of a trillion for anything like life to happen. That strikes me as genuinely impressive in a way the lottery doesn't, and it isn't just the size of the number, because the lottery number is smaller. Empty universe: suppose the dials all land inside the life-permitting window but life never gets going anyway — chemistry never takes off, nothing is ever around to notice. I want to say the fine-tuning is still real in that world, but that it doesn't support a designer at all. I have no idea how that can be right, since the physics is bit for bit identical to ours. Miserable universe: dials set for life, life shows up, and it's uniformly awful — constant agony, no beauty, no curiosity, nothing that ever wonders about anything. My gut says this counts against a benevolent designer but doesn't count for design of any kind, and I'm bothered that I'll let one hypothesis get hurt by facts while refusing to let the other get helped by the same kind of facts. Value version: when the claim is not just that the constants permit life but that they permit beings who can value things, the argument feels stronger to me — and at the same time I feel like that version is smuggling in something physics has no standing to assess. Discoverability version: a guy in the seminar argued that a universe whose constants also make it legible to the creatures inside it — regular enough to do science, enough of the right stuff around to build telescopes — is better evidence for design than a universe that's merely habitable, and I half agree, and I half suspect I'm just flattered by the idea that things are arranged for people like me. Multiverse: I do think fine-tuning gives me some reason to posit other universes. But I also think a theory that predicts most observers are momentary hallucinating brains floating in the void is a bad theory and I'd chuck it, and it seems weird that I'm willing to hold a cosmology responsible for implying that I'm not a freak, since theories don't normally get in trouble for that.

My half-formed idea is that an unlikely fact needs explaining only when some alternative hypothesis would have made it a lot more likely, and only when that alternative is one I can characterize independently of the fact. But I know that's close to just restating likelihood ratios in folksy language, and it plainly doesn't separate the firing squad from the lottery, since in both cases a rigging hypothesis would have made the outcome likelier.

So: what principle actually captures this, or as much of it as can be captured? I want it stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause named, and I want to know whether it needs an observation-selection clause, a clause about which hypotheses I'm allowed to take seriously at all, a clause about how good the outcome is, or something I haven't thought of. Then tell me what it predicts about cases I didn't list — invent two or three new ones, ideally ones that make your principle look bad — and be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save. If holding onto all eight requires something baroque, I'd rather get the simpler principle plus the name of the verdict I have to give up and an argument that giving it up is correct, not just convenient. Last thing: tell me how confident you are in whatever principle you land on, and single out the point you're least sure of. Mine is the empty-universe case, which I keep flip-flopping on — though I suspect you're going to tell me a different one of my verdicts is the confused one.
