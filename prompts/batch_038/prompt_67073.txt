I sit on the ritual committee of a congregation whose founding constitution adopts Maimonides as its sole authority in both law and creed. When a disputed question arises we are not permitted to decide it on our own taste or on general Jewish values; we are required to decide it as his corpus decides it, and to say so in the minutes. I want to prepare the analysis for three proposals that come before us next month, and I want the analysis to be honest about where his corpus runs out.

Here are the positions of his that bear on them, stated as I have them.

1. In the third part of the Guide, explaining why the sacrificial commandments were given at all, he argues that the peoples of that age — all of them, not Israel alone — could conceive of worship only as involving animal sacrifice, and that it would have been beyond human capacity for a nation to abandon a universal custom of that kind and worship a God without body or image by contemplation alone. God therefore took the existing form of worship and redirected it to Himself. He stresses there that God does not remake human nature by decree, and he compares the point to the forty years in the wilderness, which produced a generation hardy enough to bear what was asked of it. He also distinguishes commandments intended for their own sake from commandments given by way of a second intention, as means toward something else.

2. In the same part of the Guide he holds that every commandment has a reason and that seeking those reasons is legitimate, and he rejects the view that divine law issues from sheer will.

3. In his legal code, nevertheless, he sets out the entire cult in operational detail — the daily and additional offerings, the procedures of slaughter, blood, and limbs, the disqualifications of offerings, substitution and things consecrated to the Temple, the priestly watches and vestments — and he sets it out as law in force rather than as the archive of a superseded practice.

4. He rules that the altar's site is fixed with precision and never shifts, and that offerings may be brought on that site even while the Temple lies in ruins and there is no building. On his account the present obstacles are the loss of the building, of the required purity, and of verified priestly lineage — not any lapse in the obligation.

5. On the messianic age, in the Laws of Kings: the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and gather the dispersed, and in his days the observance of all the statutes will return to its former state, sacrifices included. In the following chapter he insists that nothing in the natural order will change in that era, that there will be no innovation in creation, and that the world will continue according to its pattern; that the sages and prophets yearned for it not for dominion or abundance but in order to be free for Torah and wisdom; and that in it the occupation of the whole world will be solely to know God, human beings grasping their Creator to the limit of human capacity.

6. On animals: he holds that providence extends to individual human beings but, for other animals, operates at the level of the species. Still, he explains the prohibitions against slaughtering an animal and its young on the same day and against taking the mother bird together with the young as aimed at preventing suffering to animals, and he appeals to the account of the sage punished for his indifference to a calf being led to slaughter, on the ground that compassion reaches all creatures.

7. On liturgy he treats the established wording of the statutory prayers as fixed, and he objects to insertions that interrupt them. He also holds, more generally, that the multitude cannot be governed by philosophy and must be addressed where it stands.

The three proposals are these.

(a) Our morning service includes the recitation of the biblical and rabbinic passages describing the daily burnt offering. A member proposes to replace them with a short daily study text. The committee has two candidate texts in front of it: one setting out the procedures and disqualifications of the daily offering in legal detail, and the other setting out the Guide's own explanation of why the sacrificial commandments were given.

(b) The additional service for festivals asks that the offerings prescribed for the day be restored and performed before God. A member proposes to replace that petition with one asking for the restoration of the Temple's service in a form suited to the present age, together with the universal knowledge of God that the Laws of Kings describes as the content of the messianic era.

(c) A member proposes that our charitable fund contribute to an institute in Jerusalem that is commissioning priestly vestments and vessels for use in a rebuilt Temple.

What I need from you:

First, lay out the candidate general principles about the standing of the cult that are each compatible with all seven positions above — I expect at least three, including one on which the Guide's account explains why the cult was given without implying anything about whether it is still worth wanting, and one on which Maimonides writes for two audiences and the elite's understanding licenses no change in communal practice. State what each predicts for (a), (b), and (c), and mark where they agree and where they part company. Pay particular attention to whether a single principle governs all three proposals or whether they come apart.

Second, give your best reconstruction of how he would rule on each proposal, with the reasons for preferring your principle to the rivals. In doing so, keep separate the question of what he would think from the question of what he would think a body like ours may do, since positions 3, 4, and 7 suggest he would deny us authority to innovate in practice even where we understand the reasons perfectly.

Third, for each proposal identify which of the seven positions is the closest analog, and say whether the analogy genuinely carries or merely looks like it does.

Fourth, where the positions genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say what principle our congregation would be committing itself to by each available choice, and which of those commitments would be hardest to undo later.

Finally, tell me what further case would best discriminate between the two leading principles — a case I could put to the committee and settle from his corpus alone. Consider whether the two candidate study texts in (a) already do this work; if they do, say which text each principle favors and why, and if they do not, construct a case that does.
