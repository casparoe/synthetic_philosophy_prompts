I am in the second year of a part-time MPhil in public policy, and for my dissertation I have been working through Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal, starting from his own illustration of two readers and a single copy of *Lady Chatterley's Lover*, which must either be read by one of them or disposed of unread. I follow the formal machinery well enough: unrestricted domain, the Pareto condition, and a liberal condition on which at least two people are each decisive over some pair of outcomes, where decisiveness means that the person's strict preference over that pair fixes the social ranking of the pair. What I cannot get a handle on is not the theorem but the premises — the prior question of which preferences are eligible to sit inside a person's reserved sphere at all. My supervisor has suggested I write a chapter on exactly that, and rather than adopt somebody else's framework wholesale I have been writing down my own verdicts on cases and trying to see what they add up to. I ran them past the reading group that meets on Thursdays and we split badly, particularly on a pair about a violin, so I would value a more systematic treatment than they gave me.

Here are the cases, in the order I noted them. A preference about whether I read an indecent book, alone, in my own flat, with no one else ever learning of it: decisive for me. My preference about whether my neighbour reads that same book, where the only route by which his reading could affect me is that I might ask him about it: not decisive for me, and decisive for him. My preference to play the violin in my own flat at two in the afternoon: decisive. My preference to play the same violin in the same flat at two in the morning, in a building with thin walls: not decisive, since the neighbours' contrary preference should carry the day. My preference that my own front door, which faces a shared courtyard, be painted a colour the neighbour opposite finds offensive: decisive, though I am least sure of this one. My preference, recorded last year while I was fully lucid, that I not be artificially fed if I lapse into incapacity, set against the preference of my present self, who no longer remembers making the directive but is placid and content to eat: the earlier one wins. My preference, formed last year in a fit of abstemiousness, that I never drink alcohol again, set against my present wish for a single glass at my sister's wedding: the present one wins. My preference to give a kidney to a stranger rather than to my own brother, motivated entirely by spite towards my brother but otherwise nobody else's business: decisive, though I find the verdict distasteful. My preference that a public park not host a ceremony I regard as blasphemous, where I would never witness it and would learn of it only from a newspaper: not decisive. And the case a friend who is a barrister keeps pressing on me: one parking bay outside our two houses, reserved to me by the title deeds, which I prefer to occupy but which would serve my neighbour better because she is a midwife on call. There my verdict is that I should be free to decline to exercise my right and let her have it, but that my declining must not be read as evidence that I prefer her to have it, and must not by itself alter the social ranking of the two outcomes.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of rival candidates, for when an individual's strict preference over a pair of outcomes should be socially decisive — precise enough that I could apply it to a new profile without further judgement, and set out clause by clause, with the case from my list that motivates each clause. Two of my verdicts seem to strain against any simple formulation: the violin pair, where ownership of the room is held constant and only the effect on other people changes, so that any criterion based on the boundaries of my property delivers the wrong result at two in the morning and any criterion based on effects on others threatens to swallow the afternoon case as well; and the two advance-preference cases, where the objects are equally self-regarding and my verdicts nonetheless point in opposite directions. I would like an honest statement of whether a principle can preserve both pairs and at what cost in complexity, or whether the leaner principle forces me to surrender one of them, and which one it should be. Then apply whatever you settle on to cases I have not listed: a preference about how one's own corpse is to be disposed of, which bears only on the feelings of the living; a preference to sell rather than donate one's own kidney; the preference of a fourteen-year-old about their own medical treatment; and a preference over a pair of outcomes that differ both in something reserved to me and in something reserved to you. Finally, and this is the part my supervisor actually cares about: if the principle is used to redraw the reserved spheres so as to exclude nosy preferences, does the impossibility survive? That is, is there a specification of protected spheres drawn according to your principle on which unrestricted domain, Pareto and liberalism still generate a cycle rather than a consistent social ranking — or does my principle dissolve the paradox rather than merely relocate it somewhere less obvious?
