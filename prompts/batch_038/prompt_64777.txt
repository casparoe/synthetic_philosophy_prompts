> "Here's the thing about Solomonoff induction: it turns Occam's razor from a slogan into a theorem. On this picture a hypothesis just *is* a computer program — a finite string of bits which, run on a universal machine, spits out your data. You give each program a prior weight of two to the minus its length, and the probability of a hypothesis ends up being 2^-K, K being the length of the shortest program that generates the data. Complex hypotheses therefore get exponentially less weight than simple ones, not because anyone decided simplicity is a virtue, but because there are simply fewer short programs. And there's no fudging about which language you count programs in: the invariance theorem says the answer shifts by at most a fixed constant when you switch machines, so the prior is essentially unique and there's nothing arbitrary left to argue about. Yes, K is uncomputable — but that turns out to be no obstacle. In practice you compress your data with an off-the-shelf algorithm and use the compressed length as your estimate of K, which is why minimum description length works and why MDL is best thought of as Solomonoff induction restricted to a model class you can actually handle. Then comes the punchline. Solomonoff proved that his procedure converges to the truth, and converges without assuming anything about where the data came from: no principle of the uniformity of nature, no 'the future will resemble the past', nothing but the arithmetic of program lengths. Any computable predictor that can find the pattern in your data, Solomonoff induction finds too. That is what people mean when they say it solves the problem of induction. It can't be implemented, but it's uncomputable in the same way that perfect chess is uncomputable — an ideal limit you approach as fast as your hardware allows — and AIXI is what happens when someone actually sits down and builds the thing."
