I convene a third-year course in the philosophy of the social sciences, and I am setting its in-class test: ninety minutes, closed book, no texts permitted, one compulsory question worth twenty-five marks. The topic is Weber's argument in The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism, and more generally whether a body of values can be a cause of an economic arrangement.

I want a question that cannot be answered by narrating the thesis. Every cohort produces the same essay: predestination, salvation anxiety, methodical conduct in a calling, ascetic restraint on consumption, accumulation, secularisation, and then a paragraph noting that the thesis has been criticised. That essay recites a chain without ever asking what kind of causal work each link is supposed to do. My question should force precisely that issue.

To that end, here is a device I have used in lectures, and I want the question built around it. Suppose a belief or value can be load-bearing in a causal story in at least four distinguishable ways:

1. Propellant — the belief motivates the agent to perform the very acts that produce the outcome, because the outcome, or something wanted for its own sake, figures in the agent's reasoning.
2. Index — the belief supplies the agent with a sign to be read; the agent acts in order to produce or display evidence of something else, so the outcome is a by-product of evidence-seeking rather than a goal.
3. Selector — the belief creates no dispositions at all, but changes which of the dispositions already present in a population get recruited, reinforced, retained, or transmitted.
4. Legitimator — the belief lifts a prohibition or supplies a justification, so that conduct already possible becomes permissible, honourable, or institutionally channelled.

Candidates should be required to work out which of these roles the Calvinist doctrine must occupy for Weber's reconstruction to hang together, to argue that at least one of them it cannot plausibly occupy, and to say what evidence would bear on each of the roles it can occupy and what observation would count against each. The intended crux is that nobody plausibly desires capital accumulation for its own sake, which makes the propellant role look unavailable, whereas Weber's Calvinist does treat worldly success as a mark of election, which makes the indexical role essential; and it is exactly that role that exposes the circularity worry, since the anxiety is inferred from the very conduct it is invoked to explain. Candidates should also have to confront Weber's own closing insistence that he is not substituting a one-sided spiritualist account of history for a materialist one, and to say whether that is a limitation of scope or a retreat.

Please produce four things. First, the stimulus material to be printed on the paper: a numbered reconstruction of Weber's causal chain in five or six steps, in plain paraphrase. Second, the question itself, around 120 words, which deploys the four-role device exactly as set out above and states the marks and the word allowance; assume candidates can write roughly 1,200 words by hand in ninety minutes. Third, a model answer of about 1,000 words, pitched at what a strong candidate could write in that time. Fourth, a marking scheme totalling twenty-five marks which tells me what separates a first-class answer from a good second-class one by naming the moves each must contain, with marks allocated to each component or band, plus a short list of the specific mistakes that should cost marks — for instance, treating the doctrine as a straightforward motive, treating ideal types as empirical generalisations that a single counterexample refutes, or citing the later withering of the religious roots as though it undermined the original causal claim.

There must be no verbatim quotations from Weber or from any critic anywhere in the deliverables: students are closed book, and I have no way of checking quotations at short notice. Do not include a bibliography, and do not ask candidates to cite secondary literature — the marking scheme should not reward naming commentators. Where the model answer refers to Weber, refer to the arguments rather than to page numbers.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the model answer's characterisation of Weber's actual argument, and single out the one point in it where you are least sure — where you think you may be misrepresenting him, or where you are leaning on a contested reading that a specialist colleague of mine would dispute. I intend to have that one point checked before the paper is finalised, so please isolate it specifically rather than hedging across the answer as a whole.
