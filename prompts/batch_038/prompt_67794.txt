I have no university training in philosophy, mathematics, or physics. I read on my own, mostly older essays and the arguments people have made about them, and for two years now I have been turning over Wigner's piece on the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences, together with the standard deflationary replies to it. I am not looking for a survey of the debate or a map of the positions. I want a number from you, and the arithmetic that produces it.

Here is the proposition I want a credence for.

(P) The applicability of mathematics to physics is a genuine anomaly: it is not fully explicable by the combination of (i) selection effects, in that we retell the hits and forget the misses; (ii) retrospective re-description, in that mathematics usually turns out to have had a physical ancestor within a generation or two once you dig; and (iii) the flexibility of theory-building, in that physicists bend their descriptions to whatever mathematical tools are already at hand and then marvel at the fit.

Because that is vague enough to be argued about forever, I want to pin it down with a test I invented, which I have been applying badly on my own and would like you to apply better.

(Q) Suppose a future historian catalogued every mathematical theory developed between roughly 1600 and 1950 whose motivations and working materials were purely internal — no empirical puzzle, no physical model, no instrument, no patron asking an applied question — and then checked which of them later became load-bearing in physics. More than half of the catalogued theories would turn out to be load-bearing.

My own version of the exercise: I listed about forty candidates for the purely-internal column, and after tracing each one's ancestry, four or five survived. Non-Euclidean geometry, number theory (though its applications arrived only once there was a demand for ciphers, which may disqualify it), and the theory of conic sections. And even those are shakier than I would like. My best case has a physical uncle in Gauss, who as I understand it spent years doing geodetic surveying and took seriously the thought that the curvature of space might be a matter for measurement rather than for axioms. My favorite case is also my worst one, because the geometry of conics is ancient and only the astronomical use falls inside my window, which makes me unsure whether Q should be indexed to the date a theory was developed or the date it was applied. My credence in Q is around 0.05, and my credence in P is around 0.3.

Give me your own credence in P, decomposed. I want a number and one line of justification for each of at least these terms: the probability that Q is true; the probability that purely internal is a coherent category at all rather than a label we can only ever assign retrospectively; the probability that applicability and precision are the same phenomenon, since a merely workable fit and the exactness of something like the electron's magnetic moment seem to demand different explanations, and the case of Dirac's negative-energy solutions being read as a prediction of a new particle seems stronger than either; the size of the denominator, meaning how much mathematics has simply never been applied to anything; and the probability that physics's habit of inventing mathematics that mathematicians would not supply — path integrals, renormalization — counts as evidence for P or against it. Then show me how you combine them into your figure for P.

Finally: which of these terms is your number most sensitive to; where do you think my list-building method is systematically biased, given that I compiled the candidates and judged their purity myself; and what argument or piece of evidence would actually change your mind about P, or reveal the step in your own reasoning most likely to be wrong? If you think the framing is malformed, say so plainly and tell me what a well-formed version of the question would look like instead.
