I am in Year 13 and writing an EPQ on why some online groups are easy to escape from and others are not. I have been reading C. Thi Nguyen's paper on echo chambers and epistemic bubbles (Episteme, 2020), and I have taken from it the rough idea that a bubble simply leaves other voices out, so it is fixed by exposure to more of them, whereas a chamber actively teaches its members to distrust the outside, so exposure makes things worse rather than better. I want to test that idea against groups I actually know, because I suspect my own verdicts do not all fit it.

Here is my working principle, in my own words: the difference between the two is not which information is missing but what the group says about the people who have it. If the group is merely silent about outside sources, it is a bubble and the cure is information. If the group has something to say about outside sources — that they are corrupt, captured, stupid or lying — then it is a chamber, and the cure is the slow rebuilding of trust rather than the supply of facts.

Here are my verdicts on six cases.

A. My school chess club. A strong player recommends a particular opening line, everyone copies it, and nobody checks an engine. I have never heard anyone disparage engines or books. Verdict: a bubble, and a trivially curable one — an hour with a free engine bursts it. But I am uneasy, because we also simply defer to the best player, which seems like normal and sensible reliance on an expert rather than a defect at all.

B. A large group promoting a supplement said to cure exam anxiety. It does not ignore the NHS website and GPs; it explains them, claiming that both are captured by the pharmaceutical industry and that any official warning is itself evidence of the capture. Verdict: a chamber, and the clearest one I have. Reading more mainstream sources confirms the group's story rather than undermining it.

C. My extended family's WhatsApp group. Everyone votes the same way, nobody ever mentions the other side, and nobody distrusts the news — my cousin reads the broadsheets and disagrees mildly, and this is tolerated. Verdict: a bubble, but I do not think it counts as a harm. Nothing is being suppressed and nobody is being inoculated against anything.

D. A support group for people with a rare genetic condition, which my friend's mother is in. Newcomers are warned, firmly and repeatedly, not to trust pharma-funded patient forums, and not to trust a particular celebrity's wellness advice. As far as I can establish, both warnings are simply true, and following them has protected members from being exploited. Verdict: not a chamber. Yet structurally it does exactly what B does — it discredits outside sources in advance and tells members that apparent counter-evidence should be discounted.

E. A friend who deliberately left a partisan forum and started reading across the spectrum for six months. He did not become more moderate. He became more extreme, and far more contemptuous of the side he had left, and he has now joined a group that explains away all of his new reading as manufactured. Verdict: bursting the bubble made it worse, and I think the reason is that the chamber was waiting downstream. I want a principle that explains this rather than treating it as bad luck.

F. A group I found while researching which claims that every mainstream news outlet is lying, but which never offers any rival account of what is actually happening, and has no positive doctrine at all — only distrust. Verdict: neither bubble nor chamber, because on my principle a chamber must be doing something with the distrust, and this group does nothing with it. I have been calling it a vacuum, but I have no idea whether that is a real third category or a confusion.

B and D are the pair I cannot reconcile, and I expect any honest answer will have to break one of them: either the principle must be allowed to look at whether the discrediting is true, in which case it stops being a test I can apply from inside a group and becomes a test I can only apply once I already know the answer; or the principle stays structural, in which case D is a chamber after all and I have to give up a verdict I feel strongly about.

Please do the following. State a principle — or, if one will not do the work, a small set of competing candidates — precise enough that someone could apply it to a new group without consulting me. Tie every clause to the case that motivates it and say what that clause is there to exclude. Then say explicitly which of my six verdicts your principle forces me to drop, and which it can save only at the cost of complexity that I should not be willing to pay. I also want your verdict on whether ordinary deference in case A is on a continuum with bubbles, and if so where the threshold lies and what marks it. And say whether F needs a third category or is better handled as a limiting case of one of the existing two.

I have no access to journals or the internet for this, so please do not send me to sources or suggest further reading; everything has to be argued out in front of me.
