I teach a philosophy-of-medicine course that's about half pre-meds and half nursing students, and week 3 is always the naturalism-vs-normativism fight over what a disease even is. Same thing happens every year: the students show up already sorted into camps because they've absorbed a slogan somewhere ("it's just biology" / "it's all socially constructed" / "if it causes suffering it's an illness"), and then we spend two hours trading slogans instead of trading case verdicts. So this year I want to hand them a diagnostic instrument to fill in anonymously *before* we talk, and then publish the distribution and argue about it.

You're a philosopher of medicine who's taught this material to clinical students many times over — think carefully and work out the branching structure before you write anything polished.

What I want is a decision tree that sorts a student onto a named position purely from what they say about cases. Hard constraints:

- Every branch node must be a concrete case with a verdict, never a question about the theory. "Is the concept of disease value-laden?" is useless to me, because a student who can answer that already knows the labels. "A healthy 60-year-old woman can't conceive — is she diseased?" is the kind of thing I want.
- No leaf may be named or hinted at anywhere in a node. Nobody should be able to reverse-engineer where a branch goes.
- Two or three options per node. After each node, give me one line — for my eyes, not the students' — naming the underlying commitment each option encodes (value-freedom, statistical typicality vs. evolved design, population reference class vs. individual baseline, whether harm or suffering is required, etc.).
- Between 6 and 9 nodes. Deep enough to actually seperate the views, shallow enough to run in 15 minutes on paper.

Leaves that must appear: Boorse-style biostatistical naturalism; Wakefield's harmful dysfunction; a Nordenfelt-style vital-goals normativism (health as the ability to reach your vital goals in standard circumstances); a social-model / constructivist position of the kind disability studies pushes; an eliminativist or deflationary "stop using the word" position; and a pluralist position holding that we've got several concepts doing different jobs and the fight is a conflation. If the logical space has room for a seventh that nobody really defends, include it and mark it unnamed — I like those.

Cases I want used, distributed across the tree rather than clustered: postmenopausal infertility; congenital deafness in someone raised in a signing Deaf community; grief three weeks after a loss vs. grief fourteen months after an identical loss; a child who is short but otherwise entirely normal; asymptomatic high blood pressure; sickle-cell trait in a carrier who's fine at sea level; adult lactose intolerance, which is the global majority condition; and the DSM-5 decision to drop the bereavement exclusion for depressive disorders — that last one should be a node asking whether that change was a discovery about how grief works or a decision about how much suffering licenses treatment.

Two more things I care about. First, the axis the textbooks skip — population norms versus an individual's own baseline, and how you pick the reference class by age and sex — has to show up as an actual branch, not a footnote. Second, at least one node should be a case where something is statistically subnormal but nobody is any worse off, so the answer turns entirely on whether disadvantage or suffering is required at all.

Format: use exactly these sections, in this order.

1. **Scratch work** (~200 words). The axes you considered, which ones you pruned as redundant or non-discriminating, and why. Show me you tried more than you kept.
2. **How it runs** (~120 words). Instructions I could paste straight onto the handout telling students what to do with it.
3. **The nodes**. Numbered, in order, branches spelled out, one-line commitment note after each. This is the bulk — call it 700-900 words.
4. **The leaves** (~600 words total). One short paragraph each: name of the position, two sentences stating it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the question its defenders least like being asked.
5. **Cells nobody occupies** (~250 words). Combinations of answers your tree permits but no actual position fills. For each, say whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's thought of it yet. Be honest about which of those it is.
6. **Leaves the tree can't separate** (~200 words). Flag any pair your nodes genuinely fail to distinguish, and either give me a tie-breaker case or admit there isn't one. I already suspect harmful dysfunction and some normativist views are going to be hard to pull apart, so don't paper over that.
7. **Answer key** (~150 words). A compact mapping from answer strings to leaves, which single node is the most diagnostic, and which one you'd cut first if I need to shorten it for time.

Aim for around 2,000 words overall. Plain prose; a table is fine only if some three-way node gets hard to keep straight in the text.
