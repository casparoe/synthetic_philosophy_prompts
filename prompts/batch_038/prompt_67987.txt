Second-year aesthetics PhD student here, working on the paradox of painful art. I've been doing little semi-structured chats with about 30 people who say they actively seek out sad films, and one shape of report keeps coming up that I can't find a label for. They describe the pleasure as produced by *fighting* the tears: several said the good part is the holding-on, the bracing against it, and that the moment they gave up and actually sobbed it stopped being good — it went flat, like ordinary grief — and afterwards the film felt cheaper than they'd remembered, like it had cashed out too early. One guy's phrase was that he wanted to be almost beaten but not beaten. So the pleasant feeling seems to sit downstream of the resistance to the unpleasant one, and it dies exactly when the resistance does.

I don't think this is just bittersweetness or "mixed emotions," because on their account the two aren't running in parallel as separate strands — the pleasure is generated by the struggle and extinguished by its ending. And it looks like it cuts against the control-theory line I've been reading, which explains the enjoyment by our being safely in charge of where our attention goes; these people are describing an enjoyment that requires *eventually* losing that charge, but not too soon, and they rate works partly by how long the work made them hold out.

Is there an established term for a pleasure that's generated by resistance to a displeasure and destroyed by the resistance ceasing? If not, what would you call it, and how would you fence it of from catharsis, from mere relief, and from plain "tension and release"? I care way more about the fenceposts than the name — where exactly does this stop being a species of those and start being its own thing?
