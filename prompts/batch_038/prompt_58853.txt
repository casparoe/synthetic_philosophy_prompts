I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political ethics and I am writing the closed-book final. One essay question must be on the problem of dirty hands, but I do not want anything a student could answer by reciting the standard moves: a clash between consequentialist and deontological reasons, Weber's contrast between an ethic of ultimate ends and an ethic of responsibility, or the observation that the dirty-handed politician is somehow both justified and guilty. My students have all read those things and can reproduce them without understanding them. So I want the question to turn on a conceptual device of my own that they will meet for the first time on the paper and must reason about from the definition supplied.

Here is the case and the device I want the question built on.

Dr. Ilona Vaszary is medical director of a regional public health authority during a severe outbreak of a respiratory infection. The national stockpile of the only effective antiviral is exhausted, and the sole remaining supply is held by a manufacturer recently placed under sanctions; procurement from it is prohibited absent a legislative waiver that will not be granted for six weeks. Vaszary instructs her procurement office to buy through a third-party intermediary and, asked point-blank at a committee hearing whether the antivirals come from a sanctioned source, answers that they do not. The drug reaches patients within days. Over the following year some forty thousand people are treated and the outbreak is contained; eleven patients die of an adverse reaction that a properly conducted trial would likely have identified. Eighteen months later Vaszary discloses the lie to a public inquiry, unprompted and before she is compelled to. The legislature votes compensation to the families of the eleven, formally censures her, and then reappoints her, on the stated ground that the authority cannot function without her.

In the paper I will define the following for them. Call the Ledger Closure Thesis the claim that a political agent who commits a genuine moral wrong for sufficient political reasons can extinguish the moral remainder of that wrong by satisfying three conditions: full public disclosure of the wrong, material compensation to those it harmed, and submission to formal sanction. Where all three are met, the thesis holds, neither guilt nor diminished moral standing is any longer appropriate in the agent; what legitimately remains is only regret that the world was such that the wrong was required. Vaszary's case is meant to look like a paradigm instance of all three conditions satisfied.

Please write the question in the exact wording I would hand out. It should ask candidates to evaluate the thesis against the case, and it should require them to:

(a) say what the moral remainder is supposed to consist in, and why the three steps are or are not the right kind of thing to discharge it;

(b) identify the ambiguity in the thesis as to whose ledger is being closed — the agent's or the polity's — and say whether the two can come apart, using the reappointment after censure as their evidence;

(c) confront the objection that the first condition, disclosure, may itself work a further wrong on the eleven and their families by making their deaths into the material of her reputation for candour;

(d) conclude whether the thesis is false outright or true but restricted, and, if restricted, state the restriction in a form that does not collapse into "closure is available whenever we feel it is."

I need five deliverables. First, the question as it will appear, including the case and the definition, with the time allowed (fifty minutes) and the mark allocation stated for the students. Second, a model answer of about 750 words in continuous prose, written the way an excellent candidate would write it under exam conditions — not an outline, not a list of talking points. Third, a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, organised around six must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on how candidates typically botch it. Fourth, a list of five specific mark-losing moves, named concretely rather than generically; for instance, the candidate who says that the agent is both blameworthy and praiseworthy and then stops, having reported a contradiction instead of resolving one. Fifth, a single sentence telling me which one point most reliably separates a first-class answer from a merely good one, so that I can weight it accordingly.

Because the exam is closed book, the marking scheme must award nothing for citations, titles, or correct attribution of any view to any author, and the model answer should argue rather than name-drop. It may use familiar vocabulary — guilt, agent-regret, moral residue, justification, excuse, integrity — provided it explains what work each term is doing instead of assuming the reader supplies it.
