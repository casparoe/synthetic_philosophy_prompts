My sister's an ICU nurse and I've been sitting in on her hospital's ethics committee once a month as their token layperson, which has made me realise I have no coherent idea what makes a "yes" actually count. So I wrote down my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases, in the order I thought of them, and I want to know whether there's a principle underneath them or whether I'm just a pile of reactions. Deception first: a guy on a dating app tells a woman he's a doctor when he's really a rep who sells surgical staplers, and she sleeps with him, and she definitely wouldn't have if she'd known — consent valid, he's a gross person but she wasn't wronged in the relevant way. A guy tells his partner he had a vasectomy two years ago, they have sex on that understanding, she gets pregnant — consent invalid, and I'd go further and say something like assault, though different places apparently handle that differently so ignore the law. A surgeon who knows a complication rate is 5% tells her patient it's 1% because she thinks the patient will chicken out — invalid, no contest, and the fact that she's doing it for the patient's own good doesn't help her at all. Now the annoying one: same surgeon, same 5% complication, but she just doesn't bring it up — she walks through the two most likely risks and stays quiet about the third. I also want to say invalid. But if that third risk were 0.05% I'd say she's totally in the clear, and I can't tell you where in between the line sits, and it bugs me that identical conduct is fine or not depending on a number nobody can specify. Last deception case: a woman thinks the man in her bed in the dark is her boyfriend and it isn't — invalid, obviously. But a guy signs up for a study described to him as "research on memory," signs the form, and it turns out the form said plainly that it involves watching an upsetting film, he just never read it — valid, and that one's on him.

Coercion and competence, where I'm worse. A landlord tells a tenant he'll drop the eviction he's filed if she sleeps with him — invalid. The same landlord tells the same tenant he'll renew her lease at the current rent, which he is legally free to raise by 40%, if he can store his boxes in her spare room — valid, and she can complain all she wants about the boxes. I cannot articulate what's different between those two except that one involves sex, which feels like the wrong kind of reason. A man says "sleep with me or I'll kill myself" — I say invalid, even though he's not taking anything away from her and the person he's threatening is himself. A trial pays a broke undergrad $50 to be in a phase-one drug study versus $5,000 — the $50 is obviously fine, and the $5,000 makes me uneasy in a way I hear ethics boards call undue inducement, but when I actually think about it I can't see how a bigger offer makes a yes *less* valid when offers only ever add options, so I want someone to tell me whether my unease is confused or whether it's tracking something. A defendant whose overworked public defender says take the three years, because if we go to trial you're looking at twenty — I want to say that plea isn't really free, and I also think it's valid enough to lock him up, which is definately inconsistent. Then competence: a 19-year-old at a party, drunk, coherent, enthusiastic, who says the next morning she'd have said the same thing sober — valid, I want to keep that one. Same degree of looseness, but it's a dentist's midazolam and the patient signs for an extraction while floaty and agreeing with everything — invalid, the office should wait an hour. The drug isn't the difference there, it's who's doing the asking, and I don't want it to be true that a professional's presence changes what counts as valid consent. And the case I've flip-flopped on maybe five times: my uncle spent six weeks telling my grandmother that nobody in the family could cope with her at home anymore, she signed the papers for assisted living, and afterwards she said "I didn't feel like I had a choice." Nobody threatened her with anything, he may even have been right about the coping, and its not like she was confused about what she was signing.

So: is there a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, that comes out right on these? I want each one stated precisely enough that I could hand it to someone else and get the same verdicts back, and I want you to say which of my cases is doing the work for each clause — the reason the clause is there rather than the clause being decorative. Then run it on cases I haven't thought through: clicking "I agree" after scrolling past forty screens of terms of service without reading a word; a tenant who agrees to a rent increase two minutes after the super mentions in passing that the building has "some foundation stuff going on"; a man who agrees at Sunday dinner, in front of his whole family, to donate a kidney to his brother after their mother says loudly "well, I suppose we know who the selfish one is"; and a research subject who consents while privately believing, for reasons nobody gave him, that the study is a cure for his condition. Finally, and this is the part I actually care about: if the simplest principle that handles most of my list has to overturn one of my verdicts, tell me which one it overturns and argue for overturning it rather than just bolting on another clause to save it, and tell me plainly which verdicts each candidate can't accommodate. And while you're at it — does the distinction I keep hearing, between deception about the nature of the act and deception about collateral matters, actually explain anything in my list, or does it just restate my verdicts in fancier language? I keep feeling like I already believe it whenever I agree with it and only reach for it afterwards.
