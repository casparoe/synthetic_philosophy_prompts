Eleven years ago I took a job coordinating activities at a nursing home in central Illinois, and one of the things I ended up doing, which nobody assigned to me, was sitting down with residents and recording their life stories on a cheap digital recorder so their families would have them. There are something like two hundred of these on a shelf now. I have no academic training — the last credential I earned was a community-college certificate in 1994 — and what I know about old books I have taught myself by reading in the evenings. I am writing because I have run up against a genuine puzzle in that work, and I have found a passage that seems to state it better than I can. I would like help understanding the passage rather than agreement with me.

After a few dozen interviews I noticed that the big events came out flat. The war, the wedding, the year the plant closed, the move out of the farmhouse: rehearsed, smooth, interchangeable, the same shape in every mouth. What actually told me who the person was came out sideways — a phrase a woman repeated four times in an hour, a joke a man made at his own expense and then refused to explain, the particular tone in which a brother was referred to, a rule about not wasting string. I began asking for that material deliberately. Then a friend told me this was an old idea and sent me to Plutarch, and I read the opening of the Life of Alexander in a free old English translation I found online. It runs:

"It being my purpose to write the lives of Alexander the king, and of Cæsar, by whom Pompey was destroyed, the multitude of their great actions affords so large a field that I were to blame if I should not by way of apology forewarn my reader that I have chosen rather to epitomize the most celebrated parts of their story, than to insist at large on every particular circumstance of it. It must be borne in mind that my design is not to write histories, but lives. And the most glorious exploits do not always furnish us with the clearest discoveries of virtue or vice in men; sometimes a matter of less moment, an expression or a jest, informs us better of their characters and inclinations, than the most famous sieges, the greatest armaments, or the bloodiest battles whatsoever. Therefore as portrait-painters are more exact in the lines and features of the face, in which the character is seen, than in the other parts of the body, so I must be allowed to give my more particular attention to the marks and indications of the souls of men, and while I endeavor by these to portray their lives, may be free to leave more weighty matters and great battles to be treated of by others."

Later I found the preface to the paired life, that of Caesar, in a different free translation, and it seems to pull the other way. Speaking of what the reader's attention should fasten on, it says:

"Such objects are to be found in virtuous deeds; these implant in those who search them out a great and zealous eagerness which leads to imitation. In other cases, admiration of the deed is not immediately accompanied by an impulse to do it. Nay, many times, on the contrary, while we delight in the work, we despise the workman, as, for instance, in the case of perfumes and dyes; we take a delight in them, but dyers and perfumers we regard as illiberal and vulgar folk."

That preface goes on with the saying of Antisthenes about the excellent piper being a worthless man, and with Philip asking his son whether he is not ashamed to play the lyre so well — which, if I have understood it, is Plutarch using a remark as evidence about a character, in practice, a few pages after announcing the principle.

Here is what I want help with, in the order that matters to me.

1. What is the actual force of "not histories, but lives"? Is he marking a difference in subject matter (events versus character), in completeness, or in the kind of truth aimed at? And is the first sentence a real apology — a concession that he is leaving things out — or a piece of management, so that what follows reads as a defense of a choice the reader was about to object to?

2. The painter comparison looks to me like it does not quite carry the conclusion. A portrait painter does not leave out the shoulders and hands; he simply spends less care on them. Plutarch likewise does not leave out the sieges; he abridges them. So the "therefore" may be trading on a stronger claim about omission than the analogy licenses. Is that a fair objection, or is the analogy about something else — the reader's attention rather than the painter's hand, or where a likeness consists?

3. What kind of evidence is "an expression or a jest"? For a small thing to out-reveal a battle, it seems to have to be both unguarded and typical. If it is unguarded it may be an accident; if it is typical it may be a performance. How does he think the inference runs, and does it require a view of character as a settled disposition rather than something that changes with circumstance?

4. The two prefaces seem to sit badly together. Alexander's says great deeds are unreliable indicators of virtue or vice; Caesar's says virtuous deeds plant an eagerness to imitate, while also conceding that we can admire a thing and despise the man who made it. Is the underlying point that the deed and the agent come apart in both directions, so that the biographer's real job is to find the joint where they do not come apart — and that the trifles are the only reliable joint? Or is one of the two prefaces doing rhetorical work the other contradicts?

5. I have my own worry, which I have no name for except the one I invented: the anecdote filter. The only trifles that reach a biographer are the ones somebody thought worth keeping and repeating, so what survives is already shaped by a household's or a court's taste. What Plutarch offers as the soul showing itself may partly be the memory of the people around the soul showing itself. I feel this constantly with my recordings, since it is the families who keep the tapes. Does the passage have any resource against this, or am I importing a modern suspicion that has no purchase on it?

6. I have also been reading early Christian lives of saints and martyrs in free translations, and they use the identical trick — the small gesture, the memorable saying — for what looks like the opposite purpose. There the saying is handed over to be copied; in Plutarch the saying is offered as a clue to be judged. Can you help me articulate what the structural difference is, without assuming I know the vocabulary for it?

Please work through the Alexander passage move by move before answering the questions, and keep the two separate: what the sentence says, and what has to be reconstructed to make it cohere. Tell me plainly where I am overreading or where my objection in 2 simply misfires. Where something turns on a Greek word I cannot check — especially whatever stands behind "marks and indications of the souls" — say so and explain the issue rather than assuming I can look it up. I also seem to recall Plutarch saying somewhere that he will not repeat discreditable stories when he need not; if that is right, does it sit oddly with a method built on scraps of gossip, and if I have misremembered it, say that too. Roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words is plenty. I am not asking for a bibliography or a list of scholarship; I will add nothing of that kind and do not want to be handed references I cannot evaluate.
