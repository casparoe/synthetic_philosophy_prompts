So, situation. I'm about sixty percent into my third novel and it's got a structural problem: the woman who runs the institution at the center of the book is supposed to be genuinely kind and genuinely complicit at the same time, and in every draft so far she comes out as either a villain in a cardigan or a saint with a guilty secret, and both are boring. Trying to fix it I reread Never Let Me Go at two in the morning, and then, because I'm like that, I pulled down a battered Garnett Karamazov off the shelf and read the Grand Inquisitor chapter straight through, and now I cant unsee the overlap. Miss Emily is the old cardinal, or is trying very hard to be. I'd like you to work out exactly how far that goes. Warning about me: I'm a plot person, I've never taken a philosophy class, and I want the actual argument rather than the vibe, so please be concrete about the ideas on Dostoevsky's side rather than just gesturing at freedom versus happiness.

First the mapping, laid out explicitly. The three temptations in the wilderness — bread, and then miracle, mystery, authority — what in Hailsham answers to each? My own guess: bread is the physical comfort, the Cottages, the clean recovery centers; authority is the guardians and the whole apparatus of telling-and-not-telling; and mystery is the Gallery, and above all the deferral rumor, which strikes me as the perfect piece of mystery because it's unfalsifiable, it's passed hand to hand among the children rather than handed down by the people in charge, and everybody is a little embarrassed to admit they believe it. Tell me if that's right or if I've mislabeled things. Then the part I actually care about. The Inquisitor's pitch, as I read it, is that he's a reformer: he takes human weakness as a fixed fact he didn't create, and then administers mercy within it, and he claims the credit for having taken the unbearable burden of freedom onto his own shoulders so the weak don't have to carry it. Miss Emily sounds exactly like that when she explains what Hailsham was for, and the collapse of the experiment after the scandal sounds like the Inquisitor's kingdom turning out to have been temporary too. So where does the parallel hold and where does it snap? I keep coming back to one thing: the old man can actually pay for the obedience he's buying — cheap bread, no more conscience, happiness forever, and the Sevillian crowd piling up his pyre at a nod out of love. Miss Emily can only buy a postponed childhood that terminates in a donation chair. Which makes me wonder whether Hailsham isn't a version of the Inquisitor's argument but a refutation of it, the same bargain with the happiness half quietly removed so you can see what the structure was really resting on. Argue that for or against me, don't just agree.

Second, the ending. Christ never says a word to the Inquisitor, he answers the whole interrogation by kissing him, and the old man lets him go and tells him not to come back. What is that kiss doing in Ishiguro's book? The candidates I can see are Kathy standing in that flat field at the end with the rubbish blowing, imagining Tommy appearing at the edge of it; Tommy's rage in the muddy field on the drive back from the old women's house, which might be the exact opposite of a kiss, the one moment anybody in the novel refuses; and the way the tape-and-dance scene gets retold near the end when the two women describe having watched her. Pick one, defend it, and tell me whether the last pages are grace or the Inquisitor winning. And please tell me where I'm forcing the whole thing — my suspicion is that the clones' failure to run and the crowd's failure to object in Seville are being made to do the same job in my argument and they aren't the same phenomenon at all, but I can't say why properly and I'd rather you said it than flattered me. One housekeeping thing: don't bother quoting either book at me, I've got both of them on the table and I'm not checking lines, I want the reasoning and the places where it's shaky, because whatever survives this is going straight into chapter nine.
