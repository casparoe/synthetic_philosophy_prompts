I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and the piece I am working on concerns why pain is bad: whether its badness is a property of the feel itself, or a matter of what the feel does to the person having it. The obvious test case is pain asymbolia, the syndrome in which patients locate and grade a noxious stimulus accurately, describe what they are feeling as pain, and yet appear untroubled by it — they do not withdraw, do not ask for relief, sometimes smile or laugh during the examination, and say that the pain is there but does not bother them. The philosophical reading I want to test is the motivational one, associated in recent decades with work by Nikola Grahek and others: asymbolia supposedly shows that unpleasantness is not an intrinsic hedonic quality sitting inside the sensation, but consists in the sensation's standing in the right relation to the subject's motivational or evaluative systems.

Here is the proposition I would like a credence on. Call it A: the pattern of findings reported in asymbolia is best explained by the claim that a pain's unpleasantness is constituted by its motivating its subject to want it to stop, or by its representing the bodily condition as bad for her, rather than by an intrinsic badness of the sensation itself. A is false, as I understand it, if unpleasantness is a separable felt quality that happens in these patients to be disconnected from the systems that would normally make them care about it and act on it; on that picture the syndrome would show something close to the opposite of what the motivational reading claims, namely that hedonic tone can remain intact while motivation is severed.

To make the case concrete, please work with a composite I have built out of the reported pattern rather than out of any single patient, and treat its details as stipulated. A man in his sixties, left posterior insular stroke, no general cognitive impairment. On examination he localises a pinprick to the right hand, calls it sharp, rates intensity seven out of ten, and rates unpleasantness zero. He declines the paracetamol he is offered, and continues reading his newspaper while the examiner pricks him. Now the wrinkle I want you to take seriously: an hour later, unprompted, he refers to the pain in his hand, and when asked whether he would rather the doctors stopped testing him he says yes, quite readily, and explains that he would prefer not to be pricked. I am not asserting that this is how every reported patient behaves; I am asking what your explanation of A should make of a case where a calm, reflective, forward-looking aversion to the stimulus survives while the immediate, embodied one does not.

Give me a number for A, and give me the derivation. I would like a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification for each of the following, plus any term you think I have left out: the chance that the sensory-discriminative experience in genuine asymbolia is fully intact rather than attenuated or degraded by the extent of the lesion; the chance that the patients' untroubled reports reflect an absence of felt unpleasantness rather than an absence of the capacity to attend to, or to care about, an unpleasantness that is still present; the chance that, given a genuine absence, that absence is best explained by the loss of a constitutive relation to motivation rather than by the loss of a distinct affective component; the independent standing of the motivational account against its rivals, including the view that unpleasantness is an intrinsic hedonic tone and the view that it is a matter of the experience representing the body as being in a bad state; and the evidential weight of the collateral cases, which strike me as doing a lot of quiet work — opioid analgesia, cingulotomy, and the older leucotomy reports, in which patients describe the same combination of feeling the pain and not minding it.

Then tell me how those terms combine into your bottom line, which of them your number is most sensitive to, and roughly how much that term would have to shift to bring you down to even odds. Say what single new finding — a lesion pattern, a behavioural measure, a first-person report of a particular kind — would move you furthest and fastest. And since my own provisional figure is 0.7 while a neurologist I interviewed gave 0.2 and dismissed asymbolia as largely a reporting artefact, say where you think our disagreement actually lives: in the clinical picture, in what counts as evidence for constitution as opposed to correlation, or in something about the concepts themselves.

A note on form. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, nothing that looks like a slide. Put your number in the first sentence. British English throughout. I am after your own reasoning, not a survey of who has said what, so please do not include references, a reading list, or citations of any kind — and eight hundred words or so will be plenty.
