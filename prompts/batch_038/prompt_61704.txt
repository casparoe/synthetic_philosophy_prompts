I am writing the research essay for my final-year Nietzsche module on health and physiology in the late works, and I have hit a passage I cannot get a grip on. It comes from *Ecce Homo*, in the opening section of the chapter 'Why I Am So Clever' — the book was written in 1888, though not published until 1908 — in the English translation I have been working from. I have elided some of the material between the quoted stretches:

> "I am interested in quite a different way in a question upon which the 'salvation of mankind' depends far more than it does upon any kind of quaint curiosity of the theologians: the question of nutriment. One can for convenience' sake formulate it thus: 'how to nourish yourself so as to attain your maximum of strength, of virtù in the Renaissance style, of moraline-free virtue?' — My experiences here are as bad as they possibly could be; I am astonished that I heard this question so late, that I learned 'reason' from these experiences so late. […] German cookery in general — what does it not have on its conscience! […] If one adds to this the downright bestial dinner-drinking habits of the ancient and by no means only the ancient Germans one will also understand the origin of the German spirit — disturbed intestines… The German spirit is an indigestion, it can have done with nothing. […] Later, towards the middle of life, I decided, to be sure, more and more strictly against any sort of 'spirituous' drink: an opponent of vegetarianism from experience, just like Richard Wagner, who converted me, I cannot advise all more spiritual natures too seriously to abstain from alcohol absolutely. […] A couple more signposts from my morality. A big meal is easier to digest than one too small. That the stomach comes into action as a whole, first precondition of a good digestion. One has to know the size of one's stomach."

In seminar we disposed of this section in about five minutes, in one of two ways: either as a symptom of the collapse that was a few weeks away, or as so much more of the self-mythologising that pervades *Ecce Homo*, where the content is really just performance. My own working hypothesis, which my tutor thought too generous, is that neither reading engages with what is actually being argued: that nutrition here occupies the place a transcendental question would occupy, in the sense that Nietzsche is asking after the conditions under which one is capable of judging well at all, with the body simply taking over the role the mind has in the traditional version of that question. What I would like help with is the following.

First, what is the epistemic status of the single case? His declared evidence is his own experience — "I learned 'reason' from these experiences so late" — yet he proceeds, with no visible warrant, to advise "all more spiritual natures". Is that an induction from one body, a claim about a type to which he takes himself to belong and for which he is entitled to legislate, or something else again? What, if anything, licenses the move from what is right for him to what is right for them?

Second, "The German spirit is an indigestion, it can have done with nothing." I cannot settle how literally to read this. Taken causally it makes German idealism the product of German cookery, which appears to commit him to reducing thought to digestion; taken as symptom-reading it is a diagnostic claim on which the stomach discloses a way of being rather than causing one. Is there a reading on which both are meant at once without incoherence? And do the offences listed beforehand — soup before the meal, meat cooked to shreds, puddings degenerated into paperweights — function as evidence for the diagnosis or as caricature?

Third, why does he describe the dietary maxims as "signposts from my morality"? Is nutrition for him a part of ethics or merely a precondition of it, and does it matter that the guiding question is framed in terms of "maximum of strength" rather than of health, or of getting well rather than of judging rightly?

Finally, I want your honest verdict on my transcendental hypothesis: does it survive contact with the wording, or am I imposing a Kantian frame the text will not bear? If it fails, what better description of the shape of the argument would you offer?

Please work through the passage in order rather than giving me a general account of Nietzsche's philosophy of health, argue from the phrasing of the text rather than from standing theses about him, and mark clearly where you think the passage underdetermines your reading or where you are extrapolating from elsewhere in the late writings. If you conclude that the dismissive seminar reading is right after all, I would much rather have the case for it than a polite silence.
