I'm putting together a seminar handout on how Galileo actually adjudicated scripture-vs-nature conflicts, and I want to test whether his track record pins down a single rule or underdetermines it. Heres the record as I have it, all from his own rulings:

1. The letter to Castelli (1613) and the expanded one to the Grand Duchess Christina (1615), on Joshua's long day. He argues that if you read the command for the sun to stand still over Gibeon literally on the Ptolemaic system, the miracle would have *shortened* the day rather than lengthened it, since diurnal motion is passed down from the outermost sphere — so the literal reading defeats itself. On the Copernican system, halting the earth's spin lengthens the day and also explains why sun and moon stop together (the text names them separately, at Gibeon and in the valley of Ajalon). Verdict: reinterpret, and go further — use the passage as positive evidence *for* heliocentrism.

2. Same letters, on passages giving God anger, repentance, hands and feet. Verdict: non-literal, and not even controversial — accommodation to the vulgar.

3. The psalm texts about the world being established so it cannot be moved, and Ecclesiastes on the earth abiding forever, plus the sun rising and setting. He reads these as speaking to the earth's permanence in its appointed role and to universal appearance, not as a claim about diurnal motion; but he insists the literal sense shouldn't be abandoned absent necessary demonstrations from the senses, and that declaring a view heretical without such a demonstration is precisely the mistake his opponents are making.

4. After the 1616 admonition he's to hold Copernicanism only as supposition. He publicly accepts the hypothetical framing, keeps thinking it's true privately, and builds the Dialogue so that its stated aim is to show the arguments on both sides as inconclusive.

5. The Dialogue, end of the fourth day: Simplicio's omnipotence argument, that God could have produced the tides in countless ways unknowable to us, so no human reasoning can demonstrate the earth moves. Salviati calls it admirable, concedes it ends any claim to apodictic proof, says we should rest content with probability — and then goes on arguing from probability anyway.

Now the new case. Suppose in 1624 a Jesuit mathematician writes to him and says: your own telescopic work has made the solid crystalline spheres optional — the moon is rough and earthlike, the Milky Way resolves into a swarm of stars, Jupiter carries its own satellites — so the sky is plausibly open space, no solid vault, no waters above it. But Genesis describes God setting a firmament in the midst of the waters and dividing waters from waters and calling it Heaven; the Psalms have the waters above the heavens praising God; Job compares the sky to something molten and spread out. Three features matter: unlike Joshua, the literal reading here is internally coherent and doesn't defeat itself on its own assumptions; unlike the anthropomorphisms, it isn't about divine psychology but about the arrangement of the world; and unlike the tides, the evidence against it is observational and strong but not apodictic. Also the Fathers are split — Basil and Ambrose read the waters-above literally, Augustine is openly unsure whether they're corporeal at all.

What does Galileo write back? I want four things. (a) Your prediction of his verdict, plus roughly what his reply would argue. (b) The set of general rules consistent with all five precedents and exactly where they diverge on this case — I have four in mind, a demonstration-gated accommodation rule, a domain-separation rule on which scripture never teaches cosmology at all, a self-defeat test where you only reinterpret once the literal sense collapses under its own assumptions, and a two-way concordism where scripture can serve as evidence about nature too — but tell me if I'm missing one, or if two of these are really the same rule wearing different clothes. (c) Which rule you think he's actually running, and in particular whether the Joshua ruling is decisive between them or whether it's compatible with more than one. (d) One further case, from his own era or one you construct, that would discriminate between your top two candidates — I want a case that would be genuinely hard for him, not one he swats down.

And then: if he rules the way you predict, what hermeneutical principle is he thereby committed to that his 1616 opponents would have found objectionable, and would owning that principle have gotten him into more or less trouble than the tide argument did? Mostly I'm after the reasoning, and after whether the precedents are even consistent — if you think two of the five rulings are in real tension, say which and dont paper over it.
