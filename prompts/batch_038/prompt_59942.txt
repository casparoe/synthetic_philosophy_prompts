You're the person I want for this: someone who actually knows the formal epistemology of disagreement — Aumann's result and the arguments around it — but who also thinks about how real analytic institutions behave rather than how idealized agents do.

Some background on me: I spent about eight years as an analyst in a national intelligence community, most of it on the coordination side of the big all-agency estimates, so I'm describing this from inside rather than from a handbook. Here is how our product worked, concretely:

- The estimate is one coordinated document that is supposed to speak for the whole community, not to be the sum of the agencies' views. Coordination means every participating shop sees every other shop's evidence — there's no compartmentation at that stage, no "yours but not mine."
- When an agency won't sign onto a judgment, it isn't outvoted or averaged away. It gets a footnote in the text saying that Agency X dissents from this judgment, what its own judgment is, and why.
- Judgments carry a word-level confidence rating — high, moderate, low — and we were trained hard that this is not a probability and must never be converted into one or aggregated.
- Separately, some agencies fund a permanent office whose job is to argue the other side. Devil's advocacy, red teams, whatever you call them. Same evidence, good analysts, and they are required to produce a coherent minority case whether or not anyone in the office believes it.
- When the community thinks it got badly burned, the fix is more of the same, not less. After the Iraq WMD mess the reform was to institutionalize the dissent and the red teams — nobody proposed making coordination converge harder.

What puzzles me, and what I want you to think carefully about before answering: what does this whole apparatus presuppose about disagreement itself?

Please brainstorm three or four candidate theories first, out loud, before you commit to one. Then:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. What has to be true about our analysts' priors, their evidence, and what they know about each other's reasoning for a footnoted dissent to be the *correct* output rather than an admission of failure?
2. Aumann's theorem, as I understand it, says two Bayesian agents with a common prior can't agree to disagree once their posteriors are common knowledge. We claim to satisfy the everyone-sees-all-the-evidence condition, and we literally print each shop's posterior in the same document. So which condition is this practice implicitly denying? I'd guess most people reach for "no common prior," so make the case either way instead of defaulting to it.
3. Is the practice internally consistent? We insist coordination requires full evidence sharing, and we also insist that a standing, permanent, unresolvable dissent is a legitimate output. And the red team has to build an arguement nobody endorses — what evidential weight can such an argument carry, and what does obliging the mainline to answer it presuppose about what makes an argument good?
4. Seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely fits it well. I suspect the confidence words and the footnotes are doing different philosophical work and nobody upstairs has noticed.
