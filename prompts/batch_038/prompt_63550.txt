I write about science and ideas for a magazine, mostly ecology and the people who argue about it for a living. Last week I was covering a land-use conference and ended up within earshot of a hotel-bar argument between two people who'd been on the same panel earlier that day, and I've been trying to reconstruct it ever since because I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning. Neither of them knows I'm going to write it up. Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step, then say who's more right overall and what both of them are missing?

Panelist A is a landscape ecologist. Panelist B is a philosopher who works on animal welfare. The thing that set it off: whether clearing habitat is bad for the animals in it, welfare-wise.

1. B opened by saying the standard picture of habitat loss as a harm to wildlife quietly assumes the habitat is a good place to live, and mostly it isn't. Species that produce enormous numbers of offspring — most marine invertebrates, lots of fish, insects — lose the overwhelming majority of them before they ever reproduce, to starvation, parasitism, being eaten, weather. So an intact, well-functioning ecosystem is a machine for generating huge numbers of short, bad lives. Destroy it and you've subtracted suffering, not added it.

2. A said that only works if you're allowed to count lives that never happen. Nobody who was going to exist is harmed by not existing — there's no subject for the benefit to land on. What your bulldozers do produce is very concrete: animals crushed or burned outright, plus the ones who survive the clearing and then starve or get picked off over the following weeks because thier territory is gone. You're trading a bookkeeping gain about hypothetical animals for an actual catastrophe happening to real ones.

3. B said fine, use whatever population ethics you like, but you don't get to treat the status quo as neutral. Leaving habitat intact is also a choice with consequences, it just happens to have a subject-less beneficiary. And the animals who die during the clearing were mostly going to die painfully anyway, probably within a year or two; you've moved their deaths earlier, you haven't created them.

4. A said the "earlier not created" line is doing sneaky work. A cod that gets eaten as a juvenile is not the same event as a raccoon spending a month starving in a parking lot after the woods come down — intensity and duration matter and your arithmetic is treating them as zero. Also: nobody has measured any of this. People who actually watch these populations say the ones who survive the first week mostly do fine, and a lot of the mass mortality you're pointing at is eggs and larvae that may not have anything like a capacity for pain. You're summing over a trillion entities you've simply assumed are suffering.

5. B said that cuts both ways — you don't know the average wild life is net positive either, and "hands off nature" is itself a welfare bet, just an unexamined one. Granted on measurement. But on the rest: if you think my conclusion is monstrous, tell me which premise is false rather than telling me it's monstrous, because an argument that works too well is still an argument until somebody finds the flaw in it.

6. A said that's exactly the problem. A theory that licenses clear-cutting isn't a theory that discovered a flaw in common sense, it's a theory that has detached from common sense, and the detachment is itself evidence against it.

A also threw out a distinction that I think is the actual crux and neither of them developed, so I'd like you to weigh it separately. Call them harms of removal — what happens to the animals who are there when the habitat goes — versus harms of continuation — the suffering that would have occurred had it stayed. A's claim is that these two can't be traded off on one scale, because only one of the categories has a subject. B's claim is that this distinction is just the person-affecting restriction wearing a field jacket, and that it begs the question by assuming from the start that only existing beings count.

So: rate each of the six moves from clearly wrong to clearly right and justify each rating; say whether B's move-5 burden shift is legitimate or a dodge; and say whether A's removal/continuation distinction actually holds up or collapses under B's description of it. I don't want a verdict on land-use policy, I want the argument graded. And flag whatever big thing neither of them raised — I have a feeling there's an obvious third move available here and I can't see it.
