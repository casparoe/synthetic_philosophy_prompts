I'm not a philosophy person — I do UX research — but I read Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments over the winter because a friend wouldn't shut up about it, and since then I've been noticing a pattern, mostly in myself, that I dont have a word for. Two examples.

First one: a friend of mine lost her dog in March and she was a wreck for months. I remember catching myself thinking her grief was disproportionate — not "too long for a pet" as a considered position, just a felt sense of too much — and yet her father had died the year before and I'd thought that grief was completely appropriate at the same duration. It bothered me, because I don't love her less than I'd love a parent, and I'd swear I don't think a dog matters less. What seems to have changed the verdict is that I've never had a dog, so I couldn't get inside the shape of that loss, whereas I can imagine my own father dying. And here's the tell: the disapproval basically evaporated the afternoon she mentioned, in passing, that she lives alone and the dog was the only thing that greeted her at the end of the day. One concrete detail and the grief suddenly looked correctly sized. She'd lived alone the whole time, so nothing about the objective badness of the loss had been updated — I'd just been handed a bridge into it.

Second example is about judging my own conduct. Before sending something blunt I do a quick preview of how it lands, and I've realized the preview has a cast. The same email reads as refreshingly direct when the audience in my head is my two closest friends, and as gratuitously harsh when it's the recipient's manager, and both verdicts feel like real judgments about the email rather than strategic calculations. But the part I can't map onto anything I've read is this: sometimes I can't settle on an audience at all, and when that happens the self-judgment doesn't fall back on some neutral view of the matter, it just goes blank. I don't disapprove less. I fail to generate a verdict. Which makes me suspect the supposedly impartial judge is really a weighted blend of specific people I've actually known, weighted by how vividly I can picture them and by whether I can simulate the other person's circumstances, and none of those weights have anything to do with fairness.

So — does this have a name, or is it a known configuration in the Smith literature? I've tried candidate terms on people and gotten shrugs: empathy gap, imaginative resistance, moral dumbfounding, something about hermeneutical injustice. And I know Smith's spectator is supposed to be built up out of real onlookers and then idealized, and I've heard the standard complaint that the idealization never really escapes its sources. But what I'm describing feels more specific than any of that: not that the judge is biased, but that it's plural and audience-indexed, that its output depends on whether I can get inside the situation, and that there's a genuine failure mode where no simulation means no verdict rather than a default verdict. Can you tell me which of those near-misses is closest and exactly where each one stops short; whether the going-blank case has any recognized counterpart (it seems to me like the sharp version of the whole problem, since it shows the mechanism rather than just its bias); and if there's no established term, coin one and define it properly with its edges drawn in — what counts, what doesn't, and how you'd separate it from ordinary in-group favoritism, from just being bad at perspective-taking, and from the case where someone has a strong moral verdict but no reasons for it. The label is the least interesting part to me. What I want to know is whether the thing I've described is one phenomenon or two that I've been running together.
