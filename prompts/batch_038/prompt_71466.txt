i'm a fourth year in a small interdisciplinary program (history and philosophy of science-ish) and i'm one of two grad students on our admissions committee this year. six readers total, about 180 applications, six funded slots, and the ranked list goes to the graduate school friday at 5pm.

here's how we run it, and i've only just realized how strange it is. everyone scores into a shared spreadsheet as they get to files, 1-9, plus a one-line note. the sheet is live, so you see every score everyone else has entered before you type your own. there's no assigned order — it's whoever reaches a file first, which in practice means the two most senior faculty, because they read fastest and earliest in the season. thursday we meet, the chair reads out the averages, and the norm (nobody wrote it down, its just how it goes) is that whoever scored a file first explains and defends their number. we also have an explicit rule that you don't revise your score after discussion.

two things happened this week that made me want to write something up. first, one of the readers said, about a file in the borderline band, that she hadn't opened the writing sample because "the number was already settled" — she said it cheerfully, like it was efficiency. second, i went back through the sheet for the ~40 files that drew three or more scores: once the first two readers land within a point of each other, the third score is almost never outside that range, and the one-line notes start borrowing each other's wording. i know there's a proper name for this from the social epistemology stuff i took last year and i can't dig it up right now, but you'll know what i mean.

what i want the memo to do, though, is keep apart at least three things i've been lumping together. one is the sequence effect above. two is something that looks identical from the outside but might not be a failure at all: a lot of our applicants work in subfields where only one or two of the six readers can actually tell whether the work is any good, so when the medievalist's number comes in first on a file about thirteenth-century logic and the rest of us fall in behind it, that might be correct weighting of a better signal rather than surrender of judgment — and i don't know how to tell the two cases apart from inside the process. three is plain numerical anchoring: people who *did* read the file still calibrate to the visible distribution so their score is comparable rather than honest. i think the fix is different for each and i don't want a memo that treats it as one blob called bias.

so can you draft the memo i'll circulate wednesday night? audience is the other five readers and the chair is going to hate it, so it has to be good.

i want a bottom line about what we do *this cycle*, not just next year. realistically there are about 40 files in the band that decides the six slots, six readers, and maybe nine hours of committee time before friday. whatever you recommend has to fit in that. be concrete: which files get looked at again, by whom, in what order, with what visible and what hidden, and what we do with the numbers already on the sheet — i dont think were in a position to just delete them and pretend, and i'd rather the memo say why than hand-wave it. also, since what we're producing is an ordering for a fixed number of slots rather than six yes/no calls, say something about whether the ranking in the band is even the right object to be fixing, and about how much of the current ordering would have come out different if one early reader had been having a bad day.

then i want the standing rule we should adopt for future cycles, written as something a reader could apply to the file actually in front of them, not as an aspiration.

give the chair's side a real hearing, not a strawman. her position roughly: a committee is a group of people who trust each other's judgment, hiding the scores just makes everyone lazy and strategic, we've done it this way for fifteen years and our admits have turned out fine, and a number with no discussion behind it means nothing anyway — so "independence" is a statistical fantasy imported from a context where voters face the same question with the same evidence, which we don't. i think a good chunk of that is right and i'd rather concede it in the memo than have it said at me on thursday. but i also think the fifteen-years claim deserves scrutiny, since the one thing the procedure guarantees is that we never meet the applicants it filtered out early, and i want the memo to handle that carefully rather than triumphantly.

one last thing and it's the part i keep going back and forth on. the file that started all this for me is one i'd personally fight to admit, which is exactly why i don't want the memo to be a case for admitting him. build in something that cuts against me: the fix should be able to move candidates i like *down* as easily as up, and i want that said out loud in the document, because if it reads as relitigating one decision the chair will dismiss the whole thing and she'd be entitled to.

if you think i'm misreading the pattern on the spreadsheet, say so, and tell me what else would produce it — i'd rather find that out wednesday than in the meeting.

no citations, no reading list, no named results, i don't need them and half the committee would skip straight past. i just need the reasoning. aim for something like 900-1200 words, headed sections, actual prose.
