I chair a statutory advisory panel that recommends to a government department whether early-stage field deployments of certain high-consequence technologies should be permitted: small-scale solar radiation management trials, contained releases of gene-drive organisms against invasive species on islands, and large-scale ocean chemistry interventions. Our enabling instrument requires us to satisfy ourselves that the expected benefits of a proposal outweigh its expected risks, and for fifteen years panels have read that as a simple instruction — attach probabilities to outcomes, attach values to them, multiply, add, compare. In the last two years the instruction has broken down. Three applicants have submitted models in which the sum we are being required to compute does not settle on a number at all: one because the upside tail is modelled in such a way that the mean does not exist (the applicant reports a tail exponent below one and appears not to have noticed what that implies); one because benefits and harms both have unbounded tails, so that regrouping the terms of the sum changes its sign; and one in which the expectation is technically finite but is produced almost entirely by a single term whose probability nobody can defend. A board member described the first of these as the St Petersburg problem in a lab coat. I am told the second also has a name in the literature; use it if it helps, but do not make the procedure depend on my knowing it. What we lack is any way of proceeding that is not simply a polite way of ignoring the offending terms.

Please write the decision procedure this panel can run. The constraints it must survive are these. We take roughly forty proposals a year and give each a single ninety-minute meeting. Six members sit: one statistician, and otherwise an ecologist, a marine chemist, a lawyer, an economist and a lay member; only the statistician would recognise a formal definition of an expectation, and the procedure must be usable by the others without her translating every step. We cannot commission new modelling, so we must take the applicant's numbers as given, though we may require them to be restated in a form we specify. Every recommendation must be accompanied by written reasons capable of surviving scrutiny by a reviewing court, and I sign them.

The department's guidance still cites a rule left by my predecessor, which we call the truncate-and-median rule: replace every outcome value above +£500 million with +£500 million and every value below −£500 million with −£500 million, compute the expectation of the truncated variable, and recommend permission only if that figure is positive and the median outcome is also positive. Address this rule directly before replacing it. Does it repair the problem, or relocate it into the choice of the £500 million figure? Is it stable if an applicant re-slices their own scenario set? And can a truncation point be fixed non-arbitrarily — for instance by reference to the maximum loss the public purse has actually been appropriated to bear, or to the ceiling on compensable harm — rather than by round number?

The procedure you write should contain, in order: the questions to ask of a proposal; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; a workable test that distinguishes the four situations we currently conflate (the expectation does not exist; the expectation is infinite; the expectation is finite but rests on terms no one can estimate; the expectation is fine and we are merely queasy); at least two fallback criteria that can be applied when no expectation is available, each with a statement of what it assumes and what it can legitimately be used to justify; the points at which the procedure stops with a refusal to permit; and the points at which it stops because the remaining question is one of value rather than fact and must be handed to the panel, or to the minister.

Then run it on three cases. (i) A contained gene-drive release to suppress an invasive rodent across an inhabited island group: harms bounded and modest, with a stated worst case; benefits modelled by the applicant with a tail so heavy that the mean upside diverges. (ii) A stratospheric aerosol injection trial in which avoided heat deaths and disrupted regional monsoon rainfall both carry unbounded tails, and in which the applicant's spreadsheet sums scenarios in an order that happens to make the total positive. (iii) An ocean alkalinity enhancement trial whose expectation is finite and computable, with the entire net benefit supplied by one scenario assigned a one-in-ten-thousand probability and valued at several hundred times the cost of the trial, where our own experts consider that probability not merely uncertain but not estimable at all.

I expect the procedure will permit at least one of these, and I expect it will be one I think should not be permitted. If so, report that plainly rather than adjusting the procedure until it agrees with me, and tell me whether the disagreement is a defect in the procedure or a defect in my instincts. Close by stating what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire in ordinary use, and how an applicant could model their way through it in bad faith — I would rather know that now than discover it in a year.

Do not supply citations, a reading list or a history of the debate; I want the procedure, not its provenance. Set out the steps as numbered items, each with its question, its sufficiency condition, and the exit or handoff it triggers, in plain prose that assumes no more than a first-year statistics course, defining any technical term at first use. Please write throughout in British English, including spelling, and keep monetary amounts in pounds sterling.
