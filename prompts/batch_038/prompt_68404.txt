I'm an SRE on a platform team, no philosophy background whatsoever, so bear with the framing. Roughly twice a week I have to say whether a service "survives X" — AZ loss, region failover, a dependency going dark — on the basis of a chaos drill or a game day, and the results are close to useless as evidence. I can feel that they are but I cant articulate why, which makes signing off on them miserable.

Green drill, except: the autoscaler and a self-healing watchdog restarted things before anything could visibly fail; the harness pre-warmed the standby region; client retries and a circuit breaker ate the errors; someone was babysitting with the runbook open; half the dependencies were mocked. Any one of those might mean the property was never actually exercised. And the mirror image: red drills where the service was fine and the synthentic client was the problem.

My roommate did a philosophy degree, and when I complained about this he said there's a whole literature on dispositions and conditionals where my exact problem is the central puzzle — something about "finks" and "masks" and why you can't just define a disposition as *if stimulus then manifestation*. If that's real, use it: I want the reasoning and the distinctions, not the labels for their own sake. Tell me if I've got the terms backwards. No citations and no reading list — I can't look anything up and wouldnt read it anyway.

What I need is a triage procedure a junior can run on one drill result in about 30 minutes before it goes into a design review doc that a staff engineer signs. So:

- The questions in order, and for each one what counts as a sufficient answer versus a hand-wave.
- A taxonomy of everything that breaks the inference from "the drill showed M" to "the service has property D" — capability supplied by the harness, something that resets state right when the fault lands, something that swallows the manifestation while leaving the underlying defect intact, human in the loop, boundary drawn around the wrong set of components — and for each, how you'd detect it from config, logs and topology rather than from vibes.
- Where the procedure has to output "you cannot know this from this experiment" instead of a verdict, and what it tells you to do at that point.
- The nasty part: every move that would clean up a drill (freeze the watchdog, kill the retries, un-mock the dependency) changes the thing you're testing, so you end up with evidence about a system that isn't deployed. Don't paper over that. I want the procedure to handle it explicitly, including when to accept a dirty drill and say so in the doc.

Then run it on three cases: (1) an AZ-loss drill that's been green for 18 months, with pod auto-restart, a client retry budget, a circuit breaker falling back to a stale cache, and the fault injected during a scheduled traffic trough; (2) a failover drill that went red, but on-call skipped a runbook step and the load generator held ~10x normal connection counts; (3) the one I don't want the answer to — our flagship region-failover drill, which gets cited in the customer trust deck, where I suspect the honest conclusion is that it evidences nothing and that cleaning it up would mean disabling production safety nets during peak season.

Finish with a one-page version a junior could actually keep open during a game day, and a two-column glossary mapping our words (green, red, retry, fallback, mock, blast radius, "it recovered") onto the philosophical ones and back.
