I've been reading up on IP law and there's a three-way thing I can't reconcile. Patent: if I independently invent the same gadget as you, never having seen your patent, I still infringe — my own work counts for nothing — but you had to publish the whole thing and the right dies after ~20 years. Copyright: if I independently write something almost identical to your novel I'm totally fine, no matter how similar, as long as I didn't copy — but that right lasts roughly a century, attaches automatically, published or not, and demands no disclosure whatsoever. Trade secret: no disclosure, no expiry, protection can run forever, but it only reaches people who got the secret improperly, so a clean-room reverse engineer just walks.

What theory of the ownership of ideas does each one presuppose? Patent reads like the invention is a discovered object that gets staked by whoever files first; copyright reads like what's owned isn't the idea at all but a causal fact about my own act of making. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind each as charitably as you can, then tell me whether a single system can coherantly hold all three. Here's what's bugging me: on a straight incentive story trade secrets look like the disaster, because they bury knowledge that disclosure would have spread, while on a Lockean labor story patent looks like the disaster, because the second inventor mixed his labor in too and gets nothing. So neither of the two obvious theories seems to license the whole package. Is the system therefore just unprincipled — three historical accidents sharing a building — or is there some third account of what an idea can be owned as that makes all three fit? And if we forced it to pick one theory, which concrete rules would have to flip: does independent invention become a defense, do trade secrets get a term limit, does copyright shrink toward the patent's?
