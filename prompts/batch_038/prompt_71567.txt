Last night I spent two and a half hours in a diner arguing with a friend — a good friend, and a thoroughgoing consequentialist who does cost-effectiveness analysis for a living — about whether there are things one simply may not do, even when doing them would prevent a greater number of exactly those things. I kept insisting that a constraint against, say, killing one person is not the same kind of reason as the reason to prevent five killings, and that the world's containing fewer killings does not by itself convert my reason into a reason for me to add one; he kept insisting that a theory which refuses to minimize the very thing it declares terrible has not yet explained itself. We got nowhere, which I suspect is the correct result, and I have decided that what I want to send him is not another argument but a document.

Please write it as a municipal Request for Proposals: a Notice of Solicitation issued by an invented American city, from an office of your devising (something along the lines of a Department of Agent-Relative Obligations, or an Office of Moral Risk), seeking one qualified contractor to perform a Restricted Act exactly once in order to prevent its performance on five separate occasions by third parties. Roughly 800 to 1,000 words, in authentic numbered procurement format — solicitation number, scope of work, eligibility and conflicts, evaluation criteria, indemnification, term and termination, and a closing section answering written questions from prospective bidders. The Restricted Act is never to be described: it lives in an attachment available upon written request and expressly not reproduced in the notice. Several beats I would like you to hit:

- The scope of work turns on doing versus allowing. The contractor must perform the act itself, not cause it, permit it, or arrange for it, and this must be flagged as a material term.
- Subcontracting is prohibited on the ground that the constraint attaches to the performing agent; a bidder proposing to assign performance has thereby disqualified itself. In a single clause the City should acknowledge that this rule generates an unbounded chain of responsibility which it declines to adjudicate.
- Intending versus merely foreseeing appears as a compliance obligation that the City states it will not audit.
- The City publishes a numerical threshold at which the constraint is suspended, warns bidders against bidding toward it, and notes in passing that publishing the threshold alters the behavior of the third parties whose acts the procurement exists to prevent.
- Moral residue is borne entirely by the contractor; regret is not a compensable cost; the City is indemnified against it. The City also declines to state whether the expected net reduction in violations is a good, on the ground that such a statement would exceed the scope of this procurement.
- Termination for convenience is available at any point before performance and at no point after.
- Evaluation criteria award points for evidence that the bidder finds the work objectionable, and disqualify any bidder who would perform it anyway and gratis, on the stated ground that such a bidder has failed to demonstrate a constraint.
- The Q&A includes a question to the effect of whether the City ought to be doing this at all, declined as outside the scope of the solicitation, and ends on a quieter question whose answer implies that the City has, in substance, awarded the contract to itself.

The humor has to come from the moral theory being handled correctly, not from the procurement format being absurd: the format stays perfectly straight, and every clause should exaggerate a real feature of the paradox rather than a straw man built for the occasion. A deontologist should be able to tell that you understand why the constraint is supposed to be agent-relative and non-transferable, and a consequentialist should be able to tell that you understand exactly how the minimizing move is supposed to work — ideally both should feel mildly indicted. No winking, no authorial asides, no explaining of the philosophy anywhere inside the document, and no real philosophers, real cities, or living persons: everything invented except the ideas. No citations, footnotes, or reading lists of any kind. It should also stay readable for someone who has never encountered the paradox, so nothing may depend on recognizing a reference — only on following the sentences. After the document, in a short separate paragraph clearly outside the fiction, tell me which single clause carries the most weight, and whether any clause you wrote quietly resolves the paradox instead of restaging it; if one does, say so and cut it.
