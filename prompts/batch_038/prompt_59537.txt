I'm not an academic — I fix HVAC systems and my reading is mostly podcasts and whatever's on the library's shelf — so feel free to tell me I've got this wrong. My brother made me reread Station Eleven over the holidays and we got into a long argument, because in the last year I fell down a rabbit hole on collapse: the Bronze Age palaces, the Maya cities getting abandoned, Easter Island. The fight I keep running into is over whether collapse is even the right word. One side says these societies piled on complexity until it stopped paying for itself — that's an archaeologist's idea I only half follow — and then they came apart when something knocked them over. The other side says that's the story told by the people who lost: when a palace city empties out, most of the farmers don't die, they just walk somewhere else and keep their gods and their language, and what actually ended was the tax system and the king. By that account the Maya are still here, and the Easter Island story has been picked apart pretty badly. I can't referee any of that. But I do think a novel picks a side whether it means to or not, and I want to know which side this one picks.

Here's the thing I noticed on my own, and I want you to test it rather than be polite about it. Collapse stories almost always come with a museum. Somebody's keeping a room full of stuff from before — a curator, an archive, a hoarder with a handwritten label on every object. My theory is that the museum is a tell: the moment a story puts the old world's things in a case or a drawer where people come to look at them, it's already settled the question, because exhibits are finished things, and the people standing around them are inheritors instead of continuers. I've been calling it the Museum Problem, which I'm sure is a dumb name. Station Eleven is absolutely loaded with it — Clark turns the grounded airport into a Museum of Civilization and ends up curating dead phones and laptops and finally a comic book, Kirsten scavenges through empty houses for tabloids of a dead actor, and the Traveling Symphony has that motto painted on their lead caravan about how just surviving isn't enough, which I'm pretty sure is a line lifted off an old Star Trek show, though I could be wrong about that.

So: does the book commit the Museum Problem, or does it beat it? Because there's an obvious counter to my own theory and I can't decide whether it holds up — the Symphony is carrying old stuff around too, Shakespeare and Beethoven, except they're using it instead of displaying it. A play that gets performed every night for an audience that pays in food and a place to sleep isn't an artifact, it's a tool that's still in service. Maybe the real line isn't preservation versus forgetting but display versus use, and maybe Clark and the Symphony end up on opposite sides of it even though they're both conserving the same lost world. I want you to actually commit here, not hand me a both-readings-are-defensible shrug: tell me which camp on collapse the novel lands in, and pin it to the specific parts that decide it — the airport settlement, the Prophet and what his parents' story does to him, Jeevan ending up as a small-town doctor, the lights coming back on at the end. Then tell me straight where my museum idea strains the book or where I've just found a pattern that isn't really there. No reading lists or citations, I can't chase those anyway; if there's an established term for what I'm calling the Museum Problem, drop the name in one sentence and get back to the argument.
