Last month I spent most of an evening arguing with a friend about patriotism, and I have come to think we were not so much disagreeing as answering different questions in the same words. He is a naturalised citizen who has twice declined to stand for the anthem. His position is that the word names no single attitude but a bundle — affection for one's home ground, a sense of civic duty, loyalty to the people one grew up among, sentimentality about flags — which need not hold together, and that everything defensible in the bundle would survive the removal of the country as its object. He offered what struck me as a decisive test, and I could not answer it at the time: if I cannot name anything I would give up for my country that I would not equally give up for some comparably governed country I had merely happened to be born into, then I am not a patriot, only a person with local tastes. I believe he is wrong. I also believe the argument cannot be settled until each of us can say which of several distinct claims he is actually making.

I moderate a monthly discussion group — retired teachers, two journalists, a district nurse, no philosophers — and for our next session I would like to hand them an instrument that sorts them by what they believe rather than by what they call themselves. Please write a decision tree of exactly ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, together with a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto named positions.

Order the deliverable as follows.

1. First, the logical space. Lay out the axes along which the positions differ. At minimum these should include: whether the object of attachment is substitutable by a better country; whether the attitude is essentially comparative, a ranking among countries, or non-comparative; whether special concern for compatriots may outweigh impartial duties, and which duties it may never outweigh; whether the object is an inherited particular — soil, language, kin, the dead — or a political order of institutions and professed principles; whether one's own identity is implicated in the country's acts, such that pride and shame are appropriate responses to deeds one did not perform; and whether patriotism is a standing sentiment or a disposition to act, and if the latter whether free institutions depend on it. Refine, split, or extend these as you judge necessary.

2. Then a grid of the combinations those axes permit, marking which are occupied by real positions and which are empty, and saying of each empty cell whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply tried it.

3. Then the ten questions. Six must turn on the following cases; the other four are yours to choose. Shame at a massacre the country committed sixty years before the respondent was born. An offer to relocate permanently to a country that is freer, better governed and more prosperous. Disclosing evidence of one's own country's atrocity to a foreign newspaper while its soldiers are still fighting. Supporting the national team against an opponent whose government one judges more legitimate. A scrupulous, generous, law-abiding naturalised neighbour who is unmoved by every national symbol. One school place or one transplantable organ going either to a compatriot or to a stranger of equal need and equal desert.

4. Then the leaves. At least these must appear as terminals: the view that the country's own standing and glory is an end in itself; the view that shared ways of life are a precondition of anyone's having moral reasons at all; the view that free institutions rot without active attachment, so that patriotism is a duty of citizenship rather than a feeling; the view that the proper object is the country's professed principles, making criticism loyalty's highest form and shame a patriotic emotion; the view that special concern is legitimate but bounded and excludes any claim to superiority; the view that patriotism is a vice not because of what it produces but because it corrupts the patriot's judgement about his own country; the cosmopolitan view that borders are morally arbitrary and the patriot is simply mistaken; and my friend's dissolution, on which there is no single attitude left to evaluate. If the grid turns up an occupied combination that none of these captures, add it as a further leaf.

Three constraints. No question may ask what a word means, and no answer option may contain the name of a position, of a philosopher, or of an ism. Options should be written in the first person, as someone would say them over a kitchen table, and made comparably appealing, so that a reader cannot infer from the wording which leaf I favour. Give the scoring key as decisive combinations — this answer here plus that answer there — rather than an enumeration of every possible pattern, and say what the group leader should do with a respondent whose pattern lands in an empty cell. Finally, tell me honestly which pairs of leaves this instrument cannot separate. I expect the principles-based leaf and the cosmopolitan leaf will collapse into one another, since a person may attach herself to liberal principles in her own country for purely local reasons, and I want to know whether any question could pry them apart. Plain prose throughout, and no suggested reading or references, which the group would not have.
