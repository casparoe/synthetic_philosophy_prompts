I'm a statistics PhD student auditing an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on societal collapse this term — I know enough demography to follow the arguments and nothing at all about Mesoamerica or Pacific archaeology — and two weeks ago a discussion between two of the regulars, Nadia (archaeology) and Theo (history), turned into a genuine argument that the professor let run for forty minutes without resolving. The question was whether "collapse" names something real or is mostly a measure of how much complexity a society had to lose in the first place, with Rapa Nui and the Classic Maya as the test cases. They asked me to referee afterwards, and I honestly couldn't say who was ahead. Here is the exchange as best I reconstructed it.

(1) Nadia: Rapa Nui is the cleanest case of self-inflicted collapse we have. Settlers arrived on an island dominated by a tall palm, cleared the forest for canoes, fuel, and the timber needed to move the moai, and lost the palms altogether — the pollen record simply stops. Without trees they could no longer build ocean-going canoes, soils eroded, and the monument program that had organized labor for centuries ended; the standing moai were deliberately toppled and the traditions from that period are full of warfare and cannibalism. By the time Europeans arrived in 1722 the population had fallen from a pre-contact peak of perhaps 15,000 to 2,000 or 3,000. That is a collapse on any ordinary use of the word.

(2) Theo: Nearly every load-bearing element of that is contested. The Polynesian rat arrived with the first settlers, and palm-nut endocarps from the midden deposits carry rodent gnaw marks — a palm cannot regenerate if every seed is eaten, so the deforestation may be chiefly a story about an invasive commensal rather than human recklessness. Radiocarbon work increasingly favors first settlement around 1200 CE rather than 800, which compresses the timeline and removes the long slow overshoot your account needs. The moai probably did not travel on timber rollers at all: Lipo and Hunt have moved a full-scale replica upright with ropes, which is what islanders' own accounts describe, and that eliminates the main motive for felling. And the demographic catastrophe is post-contact — the Peruvian slave raids of the 1860s, smallpox, removals to Tahiti; the count stood at 111 in 1877. Folding that into a story about pre-contact deforestation is not just wrong, it converts an argument about ecology into an argument about the character of Polynesians.

(3) Nadia: Grant all of it and something still stands. The statue program stopped, the moai were thrown face-down, the birdman cult at Orongo took its place, and obsidian mataa — conventionally called spear points — litter the landscape in enormous numbers. A society that abandons the institution that had organized its labor and then topples its own monuments has lost something, and calling that "transformation" is a way of declining to name the loss.

(4) Theo: That equivocates on "lost." The working definition the field mostly accepts is Tainter's: a rapid, substantial loss of sociopolitical structure, a political process measured in decades, with the fall of the western Roman Empire as his paradigm case. Rapa Nui had no stratified state, no palace economy, no administrative apparatus whose disappearance would register as that kind of loss — it had lineages, a chiefly system, and a ritual cycle. Substituting a birdman cult for a moai cult is institutional replacement, not institutional collapse, so on the standard definition Rapa Nui is not a case of collapse at all. The mataa point the other way too: morphometric analysis of hundreds of them suggests general-purpose tools badly suited to work as spearheads.

(5) Nadia: Then the definition is doing the work, and arbitrarily. If a society can collapse only once it is complex enough to have something to lose, "collapse" measures prior complexity rather than describing what happened, and every small-scale society is collapse-proof by construction. That is an artifact, not a finding. Consider the Maya: on the order of forty dated monuments a year around 750 and none by 900; the southern lowland centers — Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán — abandoned; and yet Chichén Itzá, Uxmal, and later Mayapán flourish in the north, the highland K'iche' and Kaqchikel polities persist, the last independent Maya polity is not conquered until 1697, and millions of people speak Mayan languages today. If that is not a collapse, nothing is; if it is, then Rapa Nui is too.

(6) Theo: Those are two different claims, and you have run them together. In the Maya case the loss is real at one level of description and not at another: a specific institution — divine kingship — and a specific regional political order did come apart in the southern lowlands within a couple of generations, which satisfies something close to the Tainter criterion; but the Maya world did not end, it relocated and reorganized, so "the Maya collapsed" is simply false at the level of the civilization. The answer is level-relative, not arbitrary. Rapa Nui is the opposite case: there is no hinterland to relocate into and no successor polity, so the level-relativity move has nothing to bite on — which is exactly why the two cases should not be treated as parallel.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the six moves, give it a rating — clearly right, defensible but overstated, or clearly mistaken — with one or two sentences saying what is wrong with it, if anything, and what the other side should have said in reply. Then tell me which of them is more right on the question that started it: does "collapse" track something real, or is it largely an index of how complex a society was to begin with? Finally, say whether what is left between them after your audit is an empirical disagreement (about rats, settlement dates, population curves, and mataa morphology), a conceptual one (about what counts as a loss), or both — and whether any particular piece of evidence or distinction could actually settle it. Hard limit of 700 words for the entire response; please give me no background on the two cases beyond what you need to make your ratings intelligible.
