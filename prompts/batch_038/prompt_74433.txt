I teach an upper-level logic course and every single year I end up in the same conversation, and I've never found a label for what's actually going on in it. Let me give you two versions of it, because the shape is the same even though the subject matter isn't.

Version one. Someone challenges a rule of inference — why does that conclusion follow from those premises? I answer by handing over a further rule, or a conditional that licenses the move. They accept it cheerfully, zero resistance. And then they say: fine, but that conditional is itself a claim, and now I'm asking about *it* — and here's the twist — the question they're now asking isn't the question they started with, because the rule I just produced lives in a slightly richer vocabulary than the one we were working in. So I answer again, they accept again, and every acceptance shifts what's under discussion. Nobody ever refuses a step. Every individual demand gets met on its own terms. And after six rounds we're nowhere, but the feeling isn't the feeling of being stuck — it's more like climbing a staircase that keeps growing new landings underneath you.

Version two. Someone asks what justifies induction. I offer the uniformity of nature. They say: granted, I'll take that — but now, why should *that* license the inference? And they're right to ask, because uniformity has quietly imported a notion of resemblance that was no part of the original question, so what they're now pressing on is broader than what I set out to answer. I extend the account. They accept it. They re-ask. The target has grown again.

The nearest thing I know is Lewis Carroll's tortoise, but I'm fairly confident its not the same phenomenon and I want to be told exactly why. The tortoise swallows every premise you heap on, including the conditional, and simply declines to make the transition — the blockage is at the step itself, and nothing new enters the picture, so the regress is homogeneous, the identical demand repeated forever. In my cases it's the reverse: the demand is heterogeneous, each round is a genuinely different and slightly larger question, and my interlocutor never refuses anything. It also isn't moving the goalposts, since they're not being slippery or changing there mind — they're correct each time that my new answer covers more territory than the old question did. And it isn't a plain infinite regress of reasons either, because there's no single proposition whose justification is being demanded all the way down.

So: is there an established term for justification that's locally satisfiable at every stage but globally unsatisfiable, on account of the fact that satisfying a stage enlarges the thing being asked about? I have a hunch this is connected to certain results about formal systems where adding axioms just generates new sentences the system still can't prove, but I'd rather you made the connection than watched me guess at it. And if there's no name, coin one — definition first, then draw the boundary lines precisely against the tortoise, against a benign or non-vicious regress, and against ordinary question-begging. The label is the least interesting part; I want the carving, and I want to know where the edges are, including whether a case counts where the vocabulary grows but the underlying question really hasn't changed.

Give me your bottom line as the very first sentence — named or unnamed, and what you'd call it. Then keep the entire reply under 300 words, plain prose, no headings, and no reference list; I just want the concepts, not the bibliography.
