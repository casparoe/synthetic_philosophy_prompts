So here's the situation. I run evaluations on the negotiation agents we use at work — two autonomous systems that hammer out multi-round contracts with each other, alternating who gets to propose, hundreds of rounds long. In theory the whole thing is a giant sequential game and I could sit down and solve it backwards from the last round and tell you exactly where it should fall apart: on the very first move. That's not what happens. In almost every run, both agents keep passing, keep sweetening, keep the thing alive way past the point where the backwards reasoning says it should have died, and then something snaps around round eighty-ish and it collapses fast. I've explained this to engineers with slides about subgame perfection and about reputation effects and about the fact that if you're not *sure* your opponent is a strict backwards-reasoner then the whole induction loses its footing, and everyone nods and nobody gets it. So I want to try a different route for our internal all-hands next month: a short story. I'm not a writer and im not really a philosopher either, I just want the logic to be *felt* rather than explained.

The setup I have in mind: a mountain pass with a hundred camps strung up it. Two climbers are roped together and hauling a shared cache of supplies that gets heavier and more valuable the higher they go — at each camp, whoever is in the lead can either cut the rope, take the lion's share, and walk back down alone, or pass, which grows the cache and hands the decision to the other one. Cutting is always locally better than passing, but if they both pass all the way to the top camp, both end up absurdly well off. The narrator is first person and is a member of an order whose monks are trained from childhood to think from the end backwards — they genuinely cannot reason any other way, it's a discipline, almost a disability. The narrator knows with total confidence that the other climber is one of them, a fellow graduate of the same training. And yet at camp one the other climber passes. I want the story to live inside the narrator's head as they try to make sense of that, and I want them to reach for the inference I keep reaching for at work: he passed, therefore he can't be what I thought he was, therefore he must be a "passer" by nature — a vowed type, a fool, someone with a rule — and if that's what he is, then the smart thing is to keep passing along and milk him right up until near the summit, then cut. Show that reasoning working, and show it getting more and more expensive to hold as the camps go up. Then the turn I actually care about: near the top, the narrator gets evidence that the other climber is *not* a vowed type at all — that he did the same backwards walk the narrator did, saw exactly where it leads, and passed anyway, on purpose, with his eyes open. And the narrator cannot fit that into any model they have. That's where I want the story to sit. If it ends up being a fable about how trust is nice, I've failed — the point is that the narrator's own certainty about the other's rationality is precisely the thing that makes passing impossible to justify, and that the crack in the argument has something to do with how many layers of "he knows that I know that he knows" it takes before the induction stops biting. Please don't resolve which climber was right. Leave the narrator unable to tell whether the other one is a saint, an idiot, or something whose reasoning simply can't be represented — and make it clear that that undecidability is what the whole game has been resting on the entire time.

A few craft things. Somewhere between 1,500 and 2,500 words, give it a title. Keep the game-theory vocabulary almost entirely out of the prose — no character should say words like "equilibrium" or "dominant strategy"; the monks can have their own term for thinking-backwards-from-the-end, something plain and slightly liturgical, and use it consistently. Let the cold weather and the weight of the cache do the work that exposition would otherwise do. Don't include any real-world references, studies, or named economists — I want it self-contained so nobody in the room goes off googling. And the ending shouldn't be a speech: something physical should happen at or near the last camp, and the reader should be able to argue afterwards about what the narrator chose and whether they knew what they were doing.
