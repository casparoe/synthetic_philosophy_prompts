I'm co-teaching a grad seminar on minds and machines with someone from our logic group, and I'm stuck writing the final. It's a 48-hour take-home, 2500 words, 30 marks, closed book and closed internet, and I've told them explicitly that name-dropping earns nothing — they just have to reconstruct arguments. Background: one week on the incompleteness theorems at proof-sketch level (nobody in the class has actually done the arithmetization), one week on Lucas's 1961 paper, one on the Gödelian chapters of The Emperor's New Mind. Predictably half the seminar thinks the upshot is "Gödel proved minds aren't computers, discussion over," and the other half thinks "this argument has been refuted by people smarter than you, also discussion over." Last year's version of this question just asked them to expound and criticize Lucas, and every single answer was the same two pages. I want one essay question that neither camp can coast through.

The crux I want them to hit is the gap between *being* sound and being *knowably* sound, and I want to build the question around a thought experiment I sketched while arguing with my co-teacher about it. Feel free to sharpen it, but here's the seed: suppose some future neuroscientist hands me a tablet containing a complete and verified specification of the algorithm A that generates all of my arithmetical reasoning — not a model or an approximation, the actual thing. I study A, and after a lot of work I satisfy myself that A is sound. Now, given that I finally know which system I am and know that it's sound, does the Gödelian argument against mechanism go through in my case? I want the question to force them to explain why the answer isn't an obvious yes, and to say what would have to be true for it to be a yes. There are at least three strands I want untangled: (i) the second incompleteness theorem means that my being sound doesn't by itself put me in a position to see that I'm sound, so the honest conclusion of the argument is weaker than the one Lucas and Penrose advertise; (ii) consulting the tablet means using a resource external to A — perception — and they need to say whether that's a legitimate escape for the mechanist or a cheat, i.e. what it would take to know which system you are "wholly under your own steam"; (iii) once the anti-mechanist concedes that all he's shown is that we aren't a *knowably* sound system, the mechanist can retreat to the perfectly boring, perfectly livable position that we're a sound system we can't identify, and students should say what's left of the argument after that retreat — plus notice that the other retreat ("you're just unsound") costs the anti-mechanist the very superiority he was trying to establish.

So: please write (1) the question exactly as it would appear on the paper — it can have parts (a)/(b)/(c), but don't make them so leading that they hand over the structure of the answer; (2) a model answer of about 800 words; and (3) a marking scheme out of 30 that names the moves a first-class answer actually makes. I want the criteria phrased as things an answer *does*, not topics it mentions, each with the marks it's worth and one line on how students typically fumble it (the ones I'm bracing for: sliding between consistency, ω-consistency and soundness; thinking the second theorem says a system can't prove its own Gödel sentence; and just assuming that "the human mathematician" is a determinate formal system with a decidable set of axioms instead of arguing that anything deserving the name has one). Finally, tell me what ceiling to put on an answer that's fluent, accurate, and well-organised but never gets past restating the incompleteness theorems — I'd like to be able to defend giving that a B-minus when someone complains.
