You are a philosopher of statistics with a working command of causal inference: comfortable with potential outcomes, with directed acyclic graphs, and with the difference between a conditional association exhibited by a dataset and a claim about what an intervention would do. Think carefully, and show your work rather than only your conclusions.

I sit on the evidence review board of a regional health system. Each quarter we choose between competing post-discharge programs using observational registry data. This quarter the choice is between two care-management programs, P and Q, judged on 30-day readmission; in the pooled registry, P's readmission rate is lower than Q's. A committee member — call him V., a philosopher by training — has circulated a short argument for why the board cannot act on that comparison. Cleaned up, it runs:

(1) *Closure.* A recommendation to treat a patient is justified only if it survives conditioning on every covariate the board could in principle obtain from, or construct out of, its own data.

(2) *Ubiquity.* For essentially any dataset exhibiting a marginal association between treatment and outcome, there is a covariate — measured, or constructible by cutting existing fields into bands — relative to which the association is weaker in every stratum, or reversed in every stratum. V. presents this as a construction claim about contingency tables rather than an empirical generalization, and has not supplied the construction.

(3) *Iteration.* Whatever refinement is adopted, (2) applies again within each stratum: age band, admission quarter, distance to clinic, index diagnosis, comorbidity count, and every interaction among these are all available in our registry. So there is no last refinement.

(4) Hence no finite analysis ever satisfies (1): for any completed analysis there remains an admissible further conditioning at which the recommendation could flip.

(5) Hence the choice between P and Q is not supported by our data at all — evidentially, we are selecting at random. The same holds of every observational dataset the board will ever receive, so the board should stop evaluating programs from registry data altogether.

(6) V. adds that (5) is not self-undermining, because (1) is a principle rather than an association in a dataset, and principles are "already maximally refined": the regress runs through data, never through premises.

For orientation, the registry also records whether a patient attended the program's first scheduled contact; P books that contact within 48 hours of discharge, Q books it at two weeks. The remaining fields are those listed in (3).

I cannot accept (5), and I suspect (6) is carrying more weight than V. admits. But if I am forced to choose, I would rather abandon (2) than (1), since (1) reads like plain epistemic decency while (2) reads like an unproven conjecture. Tell me whether that ordering is right. Do not name the phenomenon for me and do not walk me through the familiar textbook exhibits; assume I know both.

Your task:

- Begin with scratch work. Design at least one small numerical case of your own — invent whatever cell counts you like — to test whether (2) holds universally, whether it fails for some tables, whether a degenerate stratum is doing any work in the cases where it appears to hold, and whether refinement is really unlimited once cell counts are finite. Say explicitly what your case does and does not establish.
- Then give your diagnosis: name the step or steps at which the argument fails. If instead you judge it valid with true premises, defend (5) and tell the board to stop.
- For each of (1), (2), (3)–(4), and (6), state what the board would lose by giving that step up, and whether the loss is fatal or merely expensive.
- Finally, say what the board should actually do with the P/Q comparison this quarter, and what would have to be true for that recommendation to change.

Be rigorous rather than diplomatic. If two steps fail independently, say so and keep the failures separate.
