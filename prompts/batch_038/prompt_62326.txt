ok, stuck on a couple of sections of Leibniz's fourth paper to Clarke (the 1716 exchange about space and God's reasons for creating). Here's the stuff that matters to me, from the 1717 collection:

"4. There is no such thing as Two Individuals indiscernible from each other. An Ingenious Gentleman of my Acquaintance, discoursing with me, in the presence of Her Electoral Highness the Princess Sophia, in the Garden of Herrenhausen; thought he could find two Leaves perfectly alike. The Princess defied him to do it, and he ran all over the Garden a long time to look for some; but it was to no purpose. Two Drops of Water, or Milk, viewed with a Microscope, will appear distinguishable from each other. This is an Argument against Atoms; which are confuted, as well as a Vacuum, by the Principles of true Metaphysicks."

"6. To suppose two things indiscernible, is to suppose the same thing under two Names. And therefore to suppose that the Universe could have had at first another position of Time and Place, than that which it actually had; and yet that all the Parts of the Universe should have had the same Situation among themselves, as that which they actually had; such a Supposition, I say, is an impossible Fiction."

and from §9 of the same paper, his claim that space is "an Order of things, and not any thing absolute."

Three things I don't get:

(a) The Herrenhausen story. Is that supposed to be evidence for the principle? It looks like a botanical survey with n = one gentleman and a very patient princess. What work is the anecdote actually doing — illustration, inductive support, or a jab at someone in particular? And how does it connect to the last sentence: why does indiscernibility threaten atomism specifically, rather than threatening Descartes or the plenum people just as much? What has to be true of atoms for "no two indiscernible individuals" to refute them?

(b) §6 opens by flatly asserting that supposing two indiscernibles is "to suppose the same thing under two Names." Is that an argument at all, or is it just the principle restated as though it were a point about language? And then what's the structure of the rest of the sentence — is the reasoning God-would-have-no-reason-to-choose-one-position-so-there-cant-be-two-positions, and does that reasoning depend on the naming point or is it independent of it?

(c) the part I actually care about. Someone walked me through a well-known mid-20th century counterexample to the principle — I think its Max Black, 1950s — roughly: imagine a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, a mile across, same temperature, same colour, same everything, and then every property of one is a property of the other, and yet there are two of them, so indiscernibility doesn't imply identity. The standard reply I've heard is that they aren't really indiscernible after all, since they're in different places, or stand in different relations to the rest of space. But wait: if space for Leibniz is nothing over and above an order of relations among bodies, then in a two-sphere universe there's no such thing as "different places" — each sphere stands in exactly the same relations to the only other object that exists. So does it follow that the two-sphere world isn't a counterexample to Leibniz at all, but only to a version of the principle that smuggles in absolute space, which he rejects for separate reasons? Or is it the other way round — does his own argument in §6 need absolute space to run, since the "another position of Time and Place" business sounds like it's about relocating the whole universe in a container?

Tell me what the main readings of these sections are, but tie them to the specific words quoted above rather than giving me a general survey of the principle. And at the very end give me one line: \answer{...} with a short phrase saying whether §6, together with his relational view of space, already rules out the two-spheres world — something like \answer{yes, on his own terms} or \answer{no, it needs absolute space} or \answer{underdetermined}.
