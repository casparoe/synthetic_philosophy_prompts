I'm a novelist and I've got a scene that two friends have now made me doubt, so I need a referee who isn't either of them.

The setup: my narrator's uncle, a dying mathematician, offers her a bet. A fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads. If the first heads arrives on flip n, she recieves 2^n/n pounds when n is odd and has to pay out 2^n/n when n is even. So heads first time wins her £2, heads second time costs her £2, heads third wins £8/3, heads fourth costs £4, and so on forever. I sent the draft to Roz, who prices insurance for a living, and Callum, who teaches philosophy of decision theory, and they fell out over it inside ninety seconds. Roughly how it went:

(1) Callum said the scene is fine as written, because the bet has a positive expected value: weight each payoff by its probability of 1/2^n and you get the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln 2, about 0.69. So a rational character takes the bet, and the tension in the scene is that the whole gamble is worth under a pound while the possible prizes are astronomical.

(2) Roz said the 0.69 isn't a fact about the bet at all. The series is only conditonally convergent, so by the rearrangement theorem you can list the very same terms in a different order and get any total you like, or no total. Callum, she said, has chosen an ordering and mistaken it for a property of the gamble.

(3) Callum said the ordering isn't free. The payoffs arrive labelled by flip number — n=1 is the first flip, n=2 the second — and you don't get to shuffle a sequence of coin tosses. With the canonical order there's a determinate value, and that's the one that goes on the page.

(4) Roz said that just shows expected value has come apart from worth-taking. Her example: add a pound to every payoff. That new offer pays strictly more than his bet in every single possible state of the world, is made out of exactly the same prizes, and decision theory still can't rank it as better — neither bet has a value to compare — and if you re-add the terms in a friendlier order the worse bet can be made to look better than the one that dominates it. So it isn't the ordering that's sacred; it's that there's no invariant number there to look for.

(5) Callum said patch the theory, don't bury it. Truncate: cap the payoffs at some ceiling, compute the ordinary finite expectation, then let the ceiling go to infinity — the limit exists and it's ln 2 again. Or use what he called the weak expectation, the value that survives across a sequence of bounded approximations. Either way the theory gets to say something, and the something is: take the bet.

(6) Roz said truncation is sensitive to how you clip, and weak expectation just re-selects one canonical sequence of approximations and calls it objective — move (3) again with a nicer coat. Her own view is that I should rewrite the scene so the narrator refuses: the honest response to a bet that can't be compared isn't a number, it's a shrug. And anyway real agents have bounded utilities, in which case the series converges absolutely and the dilemma is fake.

(7) Callum said bounded utility would indeed make it converge, but that it dodges the actual discovery, which is that you can build a gamble where every bounded approximation says take it and the gamble itself still has no value whatsoever. That's a fact about the framework, not a bug in his character's head.

Neither budged and I've edits due. So please go through moves 1 to 7 and rate each one — −2 for clearly wrong through 0 to +2 for clearly right — with a sentence or two on why it lands where it does. Then tell me who's more right overall, and where they're simply talking past each other rather than genuinely disagreeing. Then tell me what both of them left out. And finally the practical bit: can I print the scene as it stands without a numerate reader calling it a howler, and if not, what's the smallest change that fixes it?

British English please. No reading list, no citations, no named papers — I can't chase anything down and don't want to; I just want the adjudication and the reasoning behind it.
