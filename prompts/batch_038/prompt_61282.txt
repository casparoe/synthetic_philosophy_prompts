I've read my share of population ethics over the years — total versus average aggregation, the non-identity problem, the dust-speck-style tradeoff puzzles — but I've only ever met Parfit's repugnant conclusion through other people's summaries, and I want to know whether I've been carrying around a distorted version. A chapter in a recent trade book about the ethics of the far future puts it like this:

"Parfit asks us to compare two possible futures. In A, ten billion people live lives of the very highest quality. In Z, the population is astronomically larger and every one of its members has a life that is still worth living, though barely — no great loves, no accomplishments, just a faint hum of contentment. Because each of those lives is a net plus, adding them makes the world better, and since nobody in Z is worse off than anybody in A, no one is harmed by the switch; the arithmetic simply forces the verdict that Z is the better future. Parfit's point is that the conclusion only bites because Z's lives are so meager: stipulate instead that Z-people live genuinely good lives, just not quite as good as A's, and the argument loses its grip. His conclusion is that we ought therefore to aim at Z — to have as many children as we can, however thin their lives turn out to be. That refutes any moral theory that sums welfare across people, which is why total utilitarianism is now widely regarded as dead."

Please audit this sentence by sentence. For each one, say whether a specialist in population axiology would accept it, treat it as a defensible simplification, or reject it outright, and give me the accurate replacement where one is needed. Three things especially: whether the derivation really is forced by arithmetic, or whether it leans on premises that a specialist would flag as contested; whether the claim about Z's lives being meager is doing legitimate work or smuggling in an escape hatch; and whether the slide from "Z is better than A" to "we ought to bring about Z" is licensed at all. Then tell me what survives of the final sentence once the earlier claims are corrected — is the repugnant conclusion a problem for total utilitarianism specifically, for all additive axiologies, for consequentialism generally, or for something narrower still, and which of the familiar moves out of the paradox the corrected argument leaves available rather than forecloses. Finally, and please don't dissolve this into hedging: my own intuition is that Z is obviously worse than A, and I want to know whether that intuition is the sort of premise the argument is obliged to respect or precisely the sort of thing it is built to overturn.
