I am preparing a roughly 4,000-word essay for a general-interest venue with an academic-adjacent readership (no footnotes, no apparatus), on the moral and psychological argument in Keynes's Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren (1930). What follows is the draft of the first three sections — about 1,300 words — together with notes for the remaining two. I would be grateful for detailed comments, of the specific kinds listed after the draft.

**Two Debts Keynes Never Pays: The Interim Morality and the Missing Theory of Leisure**

*1.* Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren is almost always discussed as a forecast that failed. Keynes argues that the compounding of capital accumulation with technical improvement will multiply output in the advanced economies by something like four to eight times within a century; that the economic problem — the struggle for subsistence, which has been humanity's primary business for as long as there have been people — will thereby be solved; and that the working week can then fall to something in the region of fifteen hours, with a residue of work kept in the schedule only because our nature still requires some of it. He was broadly right about the multiplication and wrong about the hours, and the standard explanation of the error is that he underweighted status competition: we do not work long hours because we need the goods, but because we need to be ahead of other people.

My central claim is that this explanation cannot be the interesting one, because Keynes had already offered it. He distinguishes absolute needs, which we feel whatever the situation of our fellows, from relative needs, whose satisfaction matters chiefly insofar as it lifts us above them, and he treats the second class as insatiable: the higher the general level rises, the higher rises the level at which superiority is felt. The familiar objection is therefore not a criticism of the argument but a restatement of one of its premises. If the argument failed anyway, the failure must be located elsewhere. I want to locate it in two places — the temporary inversion of values he recommends for the transition period, and the account of leisure he assumes can be produced on demand once the economic problem is off the agenda.

*2.* Keynes is explicit that the transition requires a dispensation. For a further stretch of time, which he puts at on the order of a century, the dispositions that drive accumulation must go on being treated as legitimate; only afterwards may the older verdict be restored, namely that the love of money as a possession, as distinct from money as a means to the enjoyments of life, is a disreputable motive. The structure is a recognisable one in moral theory: an esoteric morality, in Sidgwick's sense, in which the justification of a rule is available to a few while the rule itself is lived by everyone.

Keynes's version has a defect the generic case lacks. It is dated, and the date is published. An esoteric morality of this kind is stable only insofar as the agents who live by it are not moved by its justification: the accumulation drive must operate as a first-order motive, not as a temporary sacrifice accepted in the light of a prediction about one's descendants. But the essay exists precisely to change what its readers expect of the coming century, and its persuasive force depends on the prediction being believed. To the degree that it succeeds, it weakens the disposition whose continuation it recommends. Call this the proclamation problem: the argument is a self-undermining prophecy, in that its uptake destroys a condition it identifies as necessary for the outcome it forecasts.

There is an obvious reply, and I would rather test it than wave it away. The essay is addressed to a small class of readers who already have the leisure disposition in embryo and are positioned to cultivate it, while the accumulation motive of the population at large is untouched by anything so abstract. On that reading there is no instability, because proclamation and disposition operate on different groups.

The reply is available, but it costs more than it appears to. If the moral conversion occurs in a minority while the majority goes on accumulating, the good life is not a general human prospect but a class prospect, and Keynes's claim about the dissolution of humanity's traditional purpose becomes a claim about a fraction of humanity. More awkwardly, his own material on the dangers of arrival cuts against the reply. He observes that people who suddenly acquire security often break down under it, and he reaches for the case of the housewife released from economic necessity, together with the pattern in which the illness arrives only once the money has. Those breakdowns are individual rather than class phenomena, and the best explanation of them is the loss of a structure of purposes rather than relative deprivation: they are what happens to someone whose only reason for getting up in the morning was an external one. If that is the right explanation, the disposition that survives arrival is not distributed by class, and cannot be reserved for a cultivated minority while everyone else waits out the century.

*3.* The vice Keynes wants to expel is purposiveness — the state of mind that treats every activity as instrumental to some further state, which is exactly what accumulation requires and what a long history of scarcity has trained into us. His positive picture is its converse: a life in which ends are valued above means again, in which the good is preferred to the useful, in which art, personal relations and the direct enjoyment of things are what remain, and in which the people to be honoured are those capable of taking direct enjoyment in things rather than those who have sold themselves for the means of life.

The argument requires a distinction Keynes does not draw, between purposiveness directed at goods external to an activity and purposiveness internal to a practice. External purposes are fungible: money earned by teaching can equally be earned by trading, and nothing in the activity recommends it over any other route to the same thing. Internal purposes are not: the standards that make a quartet well played or a proof sound are constitutive of the activity, cannot be met by a substitute, and cannot be traded away for whatever external goods the activity also happens to produce. The distinction is not mine — the closest familiar articulation is MacIntyre's — and section 4 will have to state what my use of it owes him. Provisionally: only the distinction, with the application to a forecast about the length of the working week doing independent work.

On this reading, Keynes's positive picture is not the absence of purposiveness but purposiveness of the internal kind, transferred from the market to practices whose standards are not prices. What he recommends is not that we stop organising our lives around ends, but that we change which authority sets them. That reframing matters for the forecast, because internal purposiveness has the property his account needs and cannot otherwise supply: it is insatiable in a non-positional way. There is always another piece to learn and another argument to get right, and the dissatisfaction this generates does not escalate with the general level of achievement in the manner of relative needs. So far as I can see it is the only candidate on Keynes's list capable of filling fifteen hours of work plus everything else without producing either boredom or the collapse he fears.

The reframing also reverses the direction of his argument, and this is where I think the essay's most serious omission lies. A disposition toward internal goods is not available on demand the moment necessity is lifted. It is accumulated, slowly, by scheduled and unglamorous effort at things one is initially bad at. Repertoire, technique, the trained ear, the habit of returning to a difficult problem: these are built by precisely the non-optional, time-blocked labour the fifteen-hour week was meant to abolish. The good life is capital-intensive in a currency Keynes never counts. He assumed that once the economic problem was solved the art of life could be picked up, as though leisure were a container to be filled rather than a competence to be acquired. That, and not the insatiability of status needs, is why the forecast failed. He was not wrong that abundance would arrive, and he was not wrong that relative needs are insatiable; he was wrong that the disposition to enjoy anything else would be waiting there when they were.

*Sections 4–5 (notes only).* S4: the MacIntyre question — whether internal purposiveness needs the full apparatus of practices and traditions, or only the fungibility contrast; and whether a purely private aesthetic practice, with no community of judgment behind it, has internal standards at all. This is where the class-restricted reply from §2 may return in modified form. S5: whether anything survives of the forecast — in particular, whether internal purposiveness is itself positional, so that the enjoyment of difficult things functions as a marker of superiority; if so, the key property in §3 collapses and the draft owes a different conclusion.

**What I would like comments on**

1. Mark every claim in §§1–3 about what Keynes says that you would not defend. I have deliberately quoted him nowhere and would prefer to be told where a paraphrase has drifted than to be handed better wording. I am least sure of §2's use of the breakdown material: tell me whether he really deploys it in the way I describe, and where in the essay the verdict on the love of money actually sits relative to the interim dispensation.

2. I use purposiveness as Keynes's own name for the vice of treating everything instrumentally. If you cannot place that term confidently in his interwar writing, say so explicitly, and tell me whether §3 can be rebuilt without it and what the argument loses if it is.

3. Test the proclamation problem against the three best replies available from inside Keynes's framework: the class-address reply I already consider; the reply that a disposition can be weakened by belief without being extinguished, so that publication merely shortens the interim period rather than contradicting the argument; and the reply that the forecast concerns aggregate capital rather than anyone's motives, so nothing Keynes's readers come to believe bears on the arithmetic. Rank them. If one succeeds, tell me what survives of §2.

4. Tell me whether the objection proves too much. If every dated esoteric morality is self-undermining, §2 is a general point about esoteric morality with Keynes as an illustration, which makes it far less interesting as a claim about Keynes. Identify any feature of his case that makes the instability specific, or concede that there is none.

5. In §3, is the fungibility contrast doing real work, or is it a relabeling of the means/ends inversion Keynes already performs? What should the draft say to a reader who insists that playing a quartet well is, for them, purely a route to standing?

6. The reversal in the penultimate paragraph of §3 — that the good life must be accumulated by the scheduled effort leisure was supposed to abolish — is what I most want to keep and what I most expect to be wrong. Press it hardest here. In particular: does it contradict §1's claim that the standard status-goods explanation is uninteresting, given that a defender of that explanation will say accumulation-of-competence is simply status competition with better manners?

7. Structural triage: if the piece had to appear at 1,500 words, which section goes, and what single paragraph would have to be added for the survivor to stand alone?

8. A blunt verdict on novelty. Is the central claim — that Keynes's failure was a missing account of how dispositions are acquired, not an underestimate of positional needs — a genuine advance on the standard criticism, or a re-description of it? If it is a re-description, say so plainly and identify the sentence at which the re-description becomes visible.

9. Point to two or three sentences where the prose is carrying weight the argument is not. I suspect the line about leisure being a container to be filled rather than a competence to be acquired is one of them, and I want to know whether it is worth keeping as rhetoric or whether it equivocates on leisure.

Two constraints on your reply. Please do not supply a bibliography, reading list, or citations: I cannot chase references and do not want to be given ones I cannot check. If you believe a passage of Keynes's says something different from my paraphrase, describe it in your own words rather than reproducing it. And where you are relying on a recollection you are not confident of, flag it as such rather than dropping it silently — a marked uncertainty is considerably more useful to me here than a confident guess.
