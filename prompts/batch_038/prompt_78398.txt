I cover science and ideas for a magazine — mostly physics and complexity, lately a run of pieces that drift into how historians think about causes — and I'm about three weeks out from a long feature on contingency, the whole for-want-of-a-nail business. Working anchor is the wrong turn in Sarajevo: the driver misses the route, the car has to stop and back up, and it ends up within arm's reach of the one conspirator whose part in the plot had already blown up earlier that morning. I talked to a cliometrician, a military historian who despises the genre, and a physicist who works on nonlinear dynamics, and honestly they mostly talked past eachother. Before I write the section where I say what I actually think, I want a check on whether my own package survives contact. Here's what I'd sign up to:

1. The macro world is deterministic for anything I'd ever write about. No historical outcome ever hinged on a radioactive decay or a quantum coin flip; when people say history was undetermined they usually just mean unpredicted.
2. History is nonetheless contingent in the nail sense — tiny accidents get amplified into enormous divergences, and the actual twentieth century was one of several that were sitting right next to it.
3. But I'm a structuralist about the big outcomes. Wars, revolutions, whether and when a country industrializes — those come out of fiscal capacity, demography, geography, technology, coalition structure. Individuals mostly fill slots that are already cut.
4. When I run counterfactuals I hold myself to a minimal-rewrite discipline I picked up from the counterfactual-history crowd: change one thing, keep everything else as close to the actual record as you honestly can, then let the mechanisms run. And I think a properly posed one — say, what if the driver had followed the route — has a determinate answer. Either the general war comes that summer or it doesn't, and careful work can establish which.
5. My notion of causation is interventionist all the way down. To say the wrong turn caused the war is just to say that if you had intervened on the turn, whether the war happened would have been different. I don't believe in causal powers or necessitation lurking behind that.
6. The physicist's objection was that you can't specify initial conditions to infinite precision, so the nearby world where the driver doesn't turn isn't one world but a smear of them. I take that as an epistemic point about us and our records, not a metaphysical point about there being no unique answer out there.
7. I'm also comfortable putting objective probabilities on one-off past events — I want to be able to write that a general European war in 1914 was likelier than not even without the assassination — and I don't want that cashed out as somebody's degree of belief. I think of it as roughly a measure over the range of starting points the situation could have had, which is definately a feature of the situation and not of my head.
8. When historians disagree ferociously about a counterfactual, I read that as evidence the question is hard, not that there's no fact of the matter.

So: audit it. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and how hard — rank them for me rather than handing back a flat list where everything is equally suspect, because I need to know what to worry about first. Which pairs only look like they conflict and are actually fine on inspection, and where does the appearance come from? For each real tension, what's the cheapest revision, and what do I give up by making it — the giving-up part matters, since a couple of these are load-bearing for how the piece reads and I don't want to end up with a feature that can't say anything confident about 1914. And then the bottom line: is there one single change that clears the most problems at once, and could I make it without turning the whole thing into a story about my own confusion? No reading list, please, and no general primer on counterfactual history — I know the genre's reputation and I know what the critics say. Just the audit.
