I teach an undergraduate seminar on religion and politics, and every year the same thing happens: within two weeks my students have started describing an established church in the language of subscription services. Defenders say the national church guarantees universal coverage — a parish in every village, a funeral for anyone who dies in one, rites of state that no voluntary market would supply — while critics say it turns citizens of other faiths and of none into involuntary customers of a product they never chose, sold by a monopoly whose cancellation procedure involves a courthouse, a weekday morning, and a fee. I think both descriptions are partly right and I want to push the framing until it snaps, so I'd like to commission a piece of satire I can hand out in week five.

The piece is an internal strategy memo — roughly 600 to 700 words, plus a short dashboard of four to six key performance indicators with plausible figures — written by the invented Director of Membership, Stewardship and Mission Effectiveness of an invented national church, addressed to that church's governing assembly ahead of a budget review. Set it in a fictional constitutional monarchy whose arrangements are a deliberate composite of the real European and American ones, and feel free to name the country and the church, but do not name any real denomination or real state inside the document. The composite should include at least the following features: the reigning monarch is the formal head of the church and bishops are appointed by the crown on the advice of the head of government, over the objections of the church's own nominating committee; the national revenue service collects a payroll levy of around eight or nine percent of income tax on the church's behalf from every registered member; cancellation requires a personal declaration before a district court or registry office during weekday business hours, on payment of a fee, with written declarations allowed only if notarised; twenty-six of its bishops sit in the upper chamber of the legislature by right; its clergy are under a statutory duty to marry and bury any resident of the parish, member or not, and to conduct the coronation, the national memorial, and the remembrance services; and the legislature retains final approval over any revision of its prayer book, a power it has used. Give the church two competitors it discusses in market terms: a neighbouring separationist republic that recognises no cult at all and treats religion as a private association, and a former colony with a constitutional prohibition on establishment that nonetheless keeps legislative chaplains and prints a religious motto on its currency. The author should also be coping with real demographic facts, rendered as churn data: a large cohort who attend only at the midwinter festival and at life-cycle events, a growing population of the believing-but-not-belonging, and an annual spike in cancellations in the first pay cycle of each new cohort of young workers, the week they notice the deduction on their payslip. Append to the memo a cancellation FAQ of 300 to 400 words, addressed to members, which must include: what happens if someone stops paying but keeps believing; whether leaving affects eligibility for the sacraments and for burial in the churchyard; how automatic enrolment of newborns works and at what age a member may cancel in their own right; and a question beginning roughly "Isn't it unfair that non-members still use your services?", whose answer inadvertently concedes the point.

The humour has to come from getting the political theory right, not from blasphemy or from mockery of believers: the author is sincere, vaguely devout, and fluent in consultancy jargon — lifetime value, reactivation campaigns, bundling the full lifecycle, à la carte pricing for weddings — and he believes he is serving the church's mission. Every joke should exaggerate a feature that actual establishment regimes really have, so that a reader who knows the English, German, French and American cases recognises each one; if you invent an institutional feature that has no real-world analogue, cut it. The memo should let the reader reconstruct, without ever stating, the case that establishment is a form of recognition the state owes rather than a subsidy it buys, the case that it demotes everyone outside it to second-class citizenship, the case that disestablishment is not itself neutral — the separationist republic's calendar, holidays and public ceremonies remain shaped by the old faith, and the author should count this as market share he does not have to pay for — and the case that plural establishment, extending recognition to other bodies, is a product-line extension that dilutes the brand. Above all, the author must never notice the central mismatch, that he is running a church like a utility and a utility like a church, and he must never make either the case for or the case against establishment explicitly; if he slips into candour, the piece fails. Keep it deadpan and legible: no winking, no authorial asides, no footnotes explaining the jokes. Total length around 1,000 to 1,100 words.
