I am writing the central chapter of a dissertation on introspection, and I want an interlocutor who will commit to numbers rather than map the debate. Please argue from the concepts themselves; I am not looking for citations or a survey of who holds what.

The proposition I want a credence on is deliberately narrow. Set aside, for now, whether qualia exist in some loose, weakened, or revisionary sense, and set aside whether consciousness is "real." The question concerns what a particular argument establishes about a *concept*. Grant, for the sake of the question, the empirical and structural premises Dennett's Chase and Sanborn case (from "Quining Qualia," 1988) leans on, as I read them: two longtime tasters of the same familiar coffee, who once agreed it was good and now agree it is not, cannot tell from the inside whether what has changed is the character of the experience or their disposition to react to an experience that is character-wise just as it always was — and there is no further fact of the matter accessible to them that would settle it; and the same structural point is meant to extend to color, where the sheer number of fine discriminations a subject can make vastly outruns anything the subject could claim to be directly acquainted with in the experience itself, so that what is advertised as a rich intrinsic property looks increasingly like a package of judgmental and reactive dispositions.

Now let me fix the three verdicts. I want your credence that the argument, granted those premises, supports:

- H1 (incoherence): the standard posit of qualia is internally inconsistent — nothing, not even in principle, could satisfy the conjunction it demands (intrinsic and non-relational, private, directly apprehensible in a way immune to certain kinds of error, ineffable), because those properties cannot be jointly instantiated or cannot be made determinate enough to compose a concept at all.
- H2 (coherent but empty): the concept is internally consistent, and there is a clear sense in which something *could* have satisfied it, but nothing in fact does — the argument establishes a negative metaphysical or psychological finding, not a conceptual one.
- H3 (revision required): the concept is neither inconsistent nor empty, but the argument shows that any property worth keeping from it must be given a relational, dispositional, or judgment-involving analysis, so that what survives is a successor concept rather than the original.

Please give me the following, in this order.

1. Your credences in H1, H2, and H3, plus any residual you think is needed, normalized to sum to 1, with one or two sentences of justification for each. If you think my trichotomy is not exhaustive, or that H1 as I have stated it is a straw man or a confusion, say so and restate it — but then still produce numbers for your restatement. I would rather have your corrected decomposition than a demurral.

2. The derivation. I want the specific sub-questions your numbers depend on, not a generic list of issues. At minimum, assign a probability to each of these, with a line of reasoning:
   (a) That the taste case actually transfers to color — i.e., that a shift in liking and a shift in discriminative capacity are close enough in kind that what goes for the coffee tasters goes for the experience of a determinate hue.
   (b) That introspection is the sort of process whose *deliverances* can establish the absence of intrinsic properties, as opposed to a process that merely fails to detect them; this is the term I suspect does the most work, and I want to know how you think a defender of H2 or H3 should treat it.
   (c) That "directly apprehensible" and "ineffable" can be held jointly without contradiction, given that anything apprehended directly seems to have to support at least some report, and anything reportable seems not to be ineffable.
   (d) Whether the argument, even if sound, has any purchase on the *phenomenology* it targets, or only on a philosophical gloss that few working introspectionists actually endorse — that is, whether the incoherence, if there is one, is in the folk concept or only in a philosopher's refinement of it.

3. One test case of my own, which I want you to treat as a fourth hypothesis-discriminator rather than as decoration. Suppose a perceptual-learning study runs subjects through extended training on hue discrimination along a narrow stretch of the spectrum. Before training they cannot reliably sort two closely spaced patches and say the patches look the same; after training they sort them reliably and say the patches now look different. But they also insist, unprompted and consistently, that nothing about the appearance of the patches has become richer, finer, or more detailed — only that they are now more sure of themselves, and that they would have made the same judgments if asked, had they been paying attention. Two readings are available: training has changed their reactive and judgmental dispositions while the intrinsic character stayed fixed, which looks like grist for H1 or H3; or the character of the experience genuinely changed while their concepts for describing it lagged behind, which would suggest introspection tracks something real but is too crude an instrument to report on it, favoring H2. Tell me the probability that a case of this shape, run and replicated with careful debriefing, would discriminate between H1 and H3 *at all*; which way you think it cuts; and whether your answer to that changes your numbers in part 1.

4. The combination. Show me how the terms in (2) and (3) actually produce the numbers in (1) — whether you are multiplying conditionals, weighting scenarios, or doing something looser, and why that is the right structure here. Then tell me which single term your final credence is most sensitive to, in the sense that a plausible 0.2 shift in it moves the bottom line the most.

5. Your least confident term, and the one argument or piece of evidence that would move you furthest, with the direction and rough magnitude of the move.

Two closing requirements. First, commit: I want your own numbers and your own view on which verdict the argument actually supports, defended with reasons, not a balanced presentation that leaves me to choose. If you are torn, say which way you are torn *to*, and by how much. Second, tell me where a competent opponent — say, a realist about phenomenal character who grants the Chase and Sanborn premises but rejects the inference — should most reasonably place their numbers, and identify the single term on which your disagreement with them is genuinely substantive rather than verbal.
