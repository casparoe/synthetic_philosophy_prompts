I'm the only non-curator on my museum's new media acquisitions panel (they wanted someone for "context," whatever that means), and Thursday we have to settle a case that's split us badly. A big virtual world — think 15-year-old MMO with a shrinking population, not a VR headset thing — is switching off its servers next March. Back in 2019 a guild of players built a memorial in-world for a member who died: a terraced garden on a cliff edge, his name carved into a stone arch, a few hundred people logged in for the dedication, and they've held a vigil there every anniversary since. The guild has written to us asking the museum to save it. Three options are on the table and they differ in kind as much as in cost. (a) Documentation only: 4K walkthroughs, screenshots, the build schematics the guild kept, chat logs from the dedication and the vigils — about $18k, six weeks. (b) Live emulation: the guild has a working private-server build made from a client dump, we'd host it and keep it joinable so the vigil can keep happening — roughly $60k a year indefinitely, plus a legal opinion nobody on the panel wants to commission, because the EULA licenses all the assets and the dump is, let's say, complicated. (c) Reconstruction: we commission the garden rebuilt in an open engine from the schematics — $240k, 18 months, zero third-party dependency.

Two details are making this harder than the price tags suggest. First, our collections policy says we acquire artifacts "made or modified by human hands" that are "capable of being conserved in a state that does not depend on the continued commercial operation of a third party." (a) and (c) clear that clause cleanly; (b) arguably clears it too, but only because after March we'd be the ones running it. Second, and nobody on the panel has clocked this: the memorial includes a scripted flower that opens once a year, tied to the game's internal anniversary clock. The emulator's clock is broken, so under (b) the flower either never opens or opens every time the server ticks. Under (c) we could script it correctly, but it'd be our script, not theirs. So the three options don't just cost different amounts, they each preserve a different candidate for what the object actually is — the geometry, the behaviour, or the recurring gathering of people in a particular place. Our head of conservation is against spending anything past (a), and honestly her argument is better than the rest of us want to admit: the grief and the friendship were real and they were in the players, not in an arrangement of a company's polygon assets; we're a museum, not a hospice for software; the same $60k a year would keep a climate-threatened photograph collection out of a leaking storage unit; and whichever way we go we set a precedent — either we become the institution that funds private servers for every dying game forever, or we start commissioning our own versions of other people's things, at which point we're makers and the word "acquisition" is a fig leaf. On the other side, my colleague keeps waving Chalmers's Reality+ around: virtual worlds are genuine realities, the garden is a real object sitting in a real space, switching the servers off really destroys it, and option (a) is the museum equivalent of photographing a cathedral before demolition and calling that preservation.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night. It needs a recommendation — one option, or a staged combination if you can defend the sequencing — with a dollar figure, a timeline, and a named trigger for revisiting the decision. It also needs a short set of criteria the panel could actually apply the next time a world shuts down, because this won't be the last one, and criteria like "case by case" will get laughed out of the room. Two constraints on how you argue: assume the panel has read no philosophy, so don't recite the realism/irrealism debate at us, and don't just referee the fight by fiat either. Take the conservation head's objection head-on — including her precedent and opportunity-cost points, which I think are the real weight of it — rather than treating "it's real" as settling the question. And if your recommendation turns on whether the object is the geometry, the behaviour, or the practice, say that out loud and explain why that's a question people with our job description and our budget can decide, instead of a metaphysics seminar we don't have time for.
