You are a literary science-fiction writer who has also spent time as a welfare assessor on aquaculture sites; think carefully about the biology and the philosophy before you draft, and let the conceptual problem drive the plot rather than the reverse.

Write a short story of 1,500–2,200 words set at an indoor shrimp farm holding a premium high-welfare certification. The certification rests on a behavioral welfare index built from things like willingness to feed in the open, use of shelter, and responsiveness to a graded noxious stimulus. What no one caught until a routine audit is that the farm's broodstock has also been selected, generation after generation, partly on that same index: the calmest, least reactive animals were kept as breeders. The index has climbed every year since. The question the story must sit inside rather than answer is whether the index measures how the animals fare or only how readily they show it.

Include: (a) a scene in which two characters watch the same animal in the same tank and read it in opposite ways, one seeing a contented creature and the other seeing one that has stopped responding because responding never worked; (b) a scene at the harvest and stunning step, where the parameters that reliably render one life stage insensible fail for another; and (c) a moment in which whoever must sign the audit sheet recognizes that the evidence available to them cannot, even in principle, settle the matter. No villains, no speechifying, and no ending that resolves the dispute by fiat.

After the story, add a brief note outside the fiction: list the empirical assumptions your story leaned on, identify the two or three places where you took liberties with the science or rested on a contested reading — for instance, whether behavioral indicators can track subjective states at all, or whether a motionless animal is distinguishably well-off or shut down — state how confident you are in the technical framing you used, and single out the point you are least sure of.
