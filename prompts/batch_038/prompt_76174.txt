A general-audience book I have been reading devotes a chapter to Maxwell's demon, and summarises the resolution as follows:

'In 1867 Maxwell imagined a small being stationed at a trapdoor between two chambers of gas. Since it can see individual molecules, it can open the door only for the swiftest ones travelling in one direction and the sluggish ones travelling in the other. Before long one chamber is hot and the other cold, and a temperature difference has been conjured out of nothing, with no work done: entropy has fallen and the second law of thermodynamics has been broken. The puzzle stood for the better part of a century, until Szilard showed in the 1920s that the demon does not get to look at molecules for nothing, because the very act of measuring a molecule's speed necessarily burns energy. Landauer and Bennett then finished the job. Whenever the demon's memory fills up it must wipe a bit clean to make room for the next measurement, and wiping one bit always releases at least kT ln 2 of heat into the surroundings, where T is the temperature of those surroundings and k is Boltzmann's constant. That is exactly as much entropy as the demon's sorting removed, so the books balance and the second law survives. The moral is that information is physical: knowing is not free, and the universe presents the bill at the moment you forget.'

I found this convincing on a first reading, but two things have stayed with me. The passage tells me both that knowing is not free and that the bill arrives when you forget, and those do not sound to me like the same claim; and I cannot tell how much of the story is settled physics and how much is one preferred way of telling it. My own training stopped at school level, so please define any term you need.

Could you take the passage sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on the thermodynamics of information would accept without qualification, what they would want reworded, and what they would flatly deny? I am especially curious whether the account of who established what is in the right order, whether the figure given for the cost of wiping a bit holds without exception or only under conditions the passage omits, and what assumption about the demon's memory the whole argument quietly depends on. Lastly: does the conclusion that the second law is safe survive these corrections, or was it leaning on the parts that are wrong?
