I serve on the model risk committee at a consumer lender, and this quarter's review of our credit-limit model ended in a stand-off. A senior colleague waved off a proposed fairness audit with a line I now hear constantly in this field: the impossibility theorem proves you can't be fair to everyone at once. He followed it immediately with the claim that the only defensible course is therefore to optimize accuracy and disclose the tradeoff. The way he delivered it, the sentence was not a premise in a weighing of options but a reason not to weigh them at all.

I am fairly confident the slogan is being made to carry more than it can bear, but I want to be precise about exactly where the excess load sits before I say so in a meeting. Could you lay out the distinct claims that this one sentence might be expressing, arranged roughly from the ones any expert in the area would grant without argument to the ones that are simply false? I would like the menu to address, in particular:

- Which formal criteria are genuinely in tension in the well-known impossibility results, and what background condition must obtain for that tension to arise — is the conflict a mathematical necessity in the abstract, or does it depend on contingent facts about the populations being scored, including the degenerate cases in which it evaporates?
- Whether an impossibility of satisfying all the candidate criteria simultaneously entails anything about which criterion to adopt, and if not, what exactly is being smuggled in when someone moves from "not all of them" to "so it makes no difference which."
- Whether the slogan my colleague invoked and the accuracy-cost claim he slid into next are even about the same thing, or whether he has fused two separate theses that have different support and different implications.
- Which of the available readings, if true, would actually license his conclusion that an audit is pointless, and which would on the contrary supply a reason to conduct one.

What I need is a disambiguation with a verdict attached to each reading, not a single recommended interpretation defended at length. Please also mark clearly which parts of this are settled among people who work on the topic and which remain live disagreements, including on the normative side; I would rather not walk into the committee repeating a consensus claim that is in fact contested, or presenting someone's contested view about what fairness requires as though a theorem had established it.
