Consider the following thought experiment. A closed economy — trading with nothing outside itself, and in which no goods are exchanged among members for money — comprises several hundred production units: mines, smelters, machine shops, farms, transport fleets, engineering teams. Each unit is run by its own staff and reports nightly to a central computing service its current stocks, its capacity limits, its input requirements per unit of output, and the set of production techniques it currently knows. The service assembles these reports into one enormous linear program whose constraints are the material balances of every intermediate good together with each unit's capacity limits, and whose objective is discounted total consumption over a five-year horizon. Each night the program is solved from scratch, and the primal solution is transmitted back to the units as their production schedules.

The designers add one feature they regard as decisive. Along with the schedule, every unit receives the full vector of dual variables — the shadow prices attached to the material-balance and capacity constraints — and is instructed to use them as the accounting prices governing every decision the linear program did not model: which maintenance to defer, which product variants to develop, how to trade quality against throughput, which small process improvements to fund, and how to allocate labour among tasks. The designers argue that this closes both prongs of the socialist calculation debate at once: because there are now genuine prices for every means of production, the objection that rational calculation is impossible in the absence of money prices for capital goods no longer bites; and because those prices are recomputed nightly from continuously refreshed data, the objection that a planning authority can never possess the dispersed, local, and rapidly changing knowledge that market prices embody no longer bites either.

Set out the most important implications of the socialist calculation debate for assessing this proposal. Treat the contributions of its participants — Mises and Hayek on one side, Lange and the other market socialists on the other — as live arguments rather than as intellectual history, and address at least the following:

1. Whether the dual variables of a centrally solved linear program are prices in the sense the debate requires, and precisely where the analogy with exchange-generated market prices breaks down.

2. What follows from the fact that the program takes its constraint set — which things count as resources, which techniques are feasible, which outputs are wanted — as given data, when a central claim of the debate's critics was that the constraint set is itself the locus of the dispersed-knowledge problem and of continuous, unforeseeable revision.

3. What Lange's parametric-pricing and trial-and-error machinery does and does not accomplish here, given that units face the shadow prices as unalterable parameters, hold no ownership stake in their results, and never compete rivalrously for the same inputs; and how the fact that the units supply the very data from which the shadow prices are derived bears on their incentives to report honestly.

4. Whether an arrangement of this kind resolves the dispute or merely relocates it — that is, what the proposal implies about whether the calculation debate was ultimately about computation, about knowledge, or about institutions and incentives, and whether a convincing engineering answer to the first two could ever settle the third.

Argue analytically and concretely, drawing on the structure of the linear program itself — duality and complementary slackness, the handling of non-convexities and indivisibilities, the choice of horizon, the aggregation of heterogeneous goods into a single scalar objective — wherever these features carry argumentative weight. Reason from your own understanding of the material; do not attempt to cite sources or reproduce quotations.
