ok so our lab is spending the summer building a small Copycat-style program, and our supervisor (I'll call her M) invented her own microdomain for it so we couldn't just crib the standard alphabet-string problems. She calls it the Ring. There are six glyphs sitting on a circle in a fixed order — a, b, c, d, e, f, and then back around to a. Every problem has the same shape: if this string changes to that string, what does this other string change to?

She hand-wrote an answer key of six problems, and these are the ones we're fitting against:

1. ab → ac
2. cd → ce
3. de → df
4. ad → ae
5. bce → bcf
6. abcd → abce

The next one she's going to rule on, Thursday, is: bee → ?

Two of us have been arguing about this all week. One says it's obviously bef, because the rule is just advance-the-rightmost-glyph and she's done exactly that six times running. The other says that's naive — that her six examples are suspiciously bland, every single one of them a string with no repeated glyphs, in increasing ring order, with the rightmost glyph also happening to be the highest one — and that the key is built in a way that deliberately doesn't let you tell which of several rules she's running.

So: what do you predict she says bee changes to, and what's the full set of candidate rules consistent with all six of her answers? I want the ones that actually fit the key, not ones that just sound plausible in the abstract — walk each of them back through the six and show they survive. For each, say what it predicts for bee. Then tell me which one you'd bet on and why, and whether six answers is even enough to settle it. If it isn't, what's the smallest addition to the key that would break the tie, and is bee the right next problem for that or should we have asked her something else first?

There's a second problem queued up behind bee, which is abf → ?. I asked M once whether f wraps around to a or whether the rule just refuses at the end of the ring, and she tapped the circle on the whiteboard and said something like "its a ring," which I can honestly read either way. Does that case discriminate between any of your candidate rules, or does it open a second and independent question that we should keep separate?

Last thing, and maybe this is the more interesting part: I dont think "the rule M is running" is necessarily the right object to be looking for. In the alphabet-string domain the nasty case is the one where the source string butts right up against the end of the alphabet and the letter you'd want to change it into simply doesn't exist, and the point there is that people diverge and the divergence is the data, not a failure. So — what spread of answers would you expect a well-tuned Copycat-style model to produce on bee, what would temperature be doing while it worked on it, and are we making a mistake by fitting a program to one gold answer per problem when the thing we actually care about is the shape of the distribution? If you think our training setup is broken, say so plainly and say what you'd change about it.
