So I'm TAing a second-year history of ethics course this term — Hutcheson and Hume early on, then Smith, then a bit of Reid — and I've been handed the essay section of the two-hour final. Students do one essay out of four, 25 marks each, and the prof wants them answerable in roughly 45 minites with no books or notes in the room. My assignment is the Adam Smith one, on sympathy and the impartial spectator.

The problem is that every year at least half the essays are the same three paragraphs: Smith thinks morality comes from imagining yourself in the other person's shoes, the impartial spectator is basically your conscience, Hume says the same thing but with more feeling. I want a question where that answer scores badly. The crux I keep coming back to, and that I argued about with my roommate for a solid hour last week, is this: on Smith's own telling, the impartial spectator seems to get built up out of our experience of how actual spectators react to us — we notice they're biased in their own favor, we correct for it, we generalize, and the man within the breast ends up being that cleaned-up, averaged version of other people's judgments. But the whole point of the spectator is that its supposed to be able to condemn what every actual spectator around you is currently applauding. If the ideal spectator is just an abstraction from real ones, where does it get the leverage to override all of them? That looks either circular or like it quietly collapses the spectator into internalized local opinion, and I want students to actually feel that pincer rather than just label it.

So: write the exam question, with a short invented vignette built into it — I'd rather the case did the work than any quotation, since they can't bring texts in anyway. If it helps, I was picturing something small-town and non-political: a community that warmly approves of some bit of treatment, say cutting off a family who refused to chip in for someone's funeral, and one member who feels sick about it even though she can't find anyone who agrees with her. Please don't put any verbatim Smith passages in the question or the model answer; if you gesture at something he says, paraphrase it and say it's a paraphrase, no page numbers, because I'm not chasing down references. Then give me a model answer of around 600 words and a marking scheme out of 25 that splits an A from a B by naming the specific moves — I want at least five of them, each with the marks it's worth and a one-liner on how students typically fumble it. I don't want a pure takedown though: if Smith has resources for a reply, the A answer should say what they are and exactly how far they get him. Also flag two or three things that sound sophisticated but should actively lose marks — confidently announcing he's a closet utilitarian, or that the spectator is just God in a wig, or that there's a social contract hiding underneath — and say which misunderstanding each one is catching. Keep it self-contained enough that a student who read the assigned selections and paid attention can do it; I'm not testing trivia. American English throughout, please.
