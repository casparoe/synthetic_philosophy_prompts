Last term I attended a seminar dinner at which two historians of early modern natural philosophy — I will call them A and B, since neither's name matters — argued at length about Johannes Kepler's astrology. The exchange was unusually well matched, and I have not been able to decide which of them I think was right. I would be grateful for an adjudication of the argument move by move.

First, the background on which both of them agreed, so that the real dispute is visible. Kepler cast horoscopes throughout his career and published annual prognostications for patrons; some eight hundred natal charts survive from his collection. In his 1601 treatise on the more certain foundations of astrology he proposed to strip the discipline of much of its inherited apparatus — denying that the figures of the zodiac have any efficacy in nature, and setting aside most of the received lore of significations and house divisions — and to rebuild it on a narrower base: the angular aspects between celestial bodies, with the list of aspects itself enlarged according to which divisions of the circle are geometrically constructible. In the Harmony of the World of 1619 he derived the power of the aspects from the harmonic proportions of the circle, the same proportions that make musical intervals consonant and that, in the final book of that work, yield his law relating the planets' periods to their distances. He explained the reception of these configurations by attributing to the Earth an animate faculty that apprehends them, as a soul apprehends harmony, and he invoked celestial influence in accounting for tides, weather, and organic rhythms. In a polemical work of 1610, and again later, he remarked that astronomy would starve if her foolish daughter astrology did not earn the bread for both. And in 1620–21 he intervened to secure his mother's release after she had been condemned to torture in a witchcraft prosecution.

The argument ran as follows.

(1) A opened by claiming that Kepler's astrology was a scientifically constrained research program, and that the proof is what he discarded: he dropped the zodiacal images, the houses, and the received significations because they showed no observable effect, and retained only what could be tied to measurable angular geometry and to reported effects on weather. That, A said, is what a researcher looks like when the data amputate part of a theory.

(2) B replied that the amputations were costless. Everything Kepler discarded had come down from authorities he had independent reasons to dislike, and everything he retained was load-bearing for the astronomy he wanted to defend. The aspects are not a residue left over after testing; they are the same harmonic proportions that generate his third law. A theory that sheds only its expendable limbs and keeps its heart is not being tested, it is being redecorated.

(3) A answered that this inverts the point. That the aspect doctrine was continuous with his physics is precisely what makes it scientific: he offered a mechanism — an animate faculty in the Earth, sensitive to angular configuration — and he offered effects to look for, in weather around great conjunctions, in tides, in organic responses. He asked that aspects be judged by repeated experience and reported his own observations. A hypothesis with a stated mechanism and a stated domain of effects is falsifiable in principle, whatever else is true of it.

(4) B said that in principle was doing all the work. The faculty is not independently detectable: nothing about the Earth's alleged apprehension of aspects can be measured apart from the very effects it was posited to explain. And when predictions failed, the failure was always chargeable to the astronomer's side of the ledger — an uncertain birth time, a chart requiring rectification, an aspect vitiated by another. Kepler wrote his own almanacs and revised them. No observation could have compelled him to abandon the aspects while retaining the harmony of the world.

(5) A replied that by that standard nothing in 1610 passes. Kepler's claim that the moon raises the tides was equally committed to an occult action at a distance and equally beyond any test of its underlying faculty; yet it was true, and it fed directly into the later gravitational synthesis. If B's criterion condemns the tidal hypothesis along with the aspects, the criterion is not tracking the difference between science and non-science but condemning an entire century.

(6) B rejoined that the tidal claim is not in the same boat, and that the difference is instructive. It specified a checkable correlation — tidal range against lunar position and phase — that anyone with a coastline could confirm, and it constrained a mechanism that later entered a theory which made novel predictions come out right. The aspect doctrine constrained nothing: it fed no successful theory, produced no novel prediction that succeeded, and its one great public test, the annual prognostications he was obliged to publish, was a trade he himself described as disreputable.

(7) A closed with the witchcraft case: when his mother was condemned to torture, Kepler went back and argued the prosecution down, insisting that the only support for it was her own intemperate speech and that the procedure was invalid. A man willing to spend years applying evidential standards to an occult accusation at real personal cost is not a man whose occult beliefs sat beyond the reach of evidence.

(8) B closed by asking what he actually argued. He contested testimony and procedure; he did not contest the existence of occult influence. He was entirely comfortable with occult influence — he merely located it in geometrical aspects and an ensouled Earth rather than in a pact with the devil. The defense shows a lawyer, not a falsificationist.

Please rate each of the eight moves individually, on a scale running from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating, and flag any move in which a speaker reaches a defensible conclusion by an unsound route. Then answer three further questions. First: on the proposition that actually opened the dispute — that Kepler's astrology was a research program constrained by evidence rather than a system insulated from refutation — which of the two is more right overall, and is the margin large or small? Second: what is the strongest consideration bearing on that proposition that neither of them raised? Third: is the word science doing the same work in both their mouths, and if it is not, would restating the dispute in a more precise vocabulary dissolve it or sharpen it?
