Six weeks ago I got moved off general liability onto a new desk that handles incidents involving automated or autonomous systems — same insurer, but now the clients are a logistics outfit running self-driving yard trucks, two hospital networks, a mining contractor with remote-operated haulage, and a fintech with an algorithmic onboarding pipeline. The reason the desk exists is that nobody at the company can agree on how to allocate these things, and at the end of the quarter I have to hand in a written framework: given an incident, who is at fault, who do we pursue, who do we pay, and on what grounds. The first file I picked up was the yard truck one, and I ended up sitting at a kitchen table with the spotter's widow for two hours, so this stopped being abstract for me pretty fast. Since then I've been logging my gut verdicts as they come in, and the log has turned into a mess. I want someone to tell me what principle, if any, is actually sitting underneath it.

Here's the log. Yard truck, wet pavement plus low sun in a configuration nobody had modeled, sensor suite blinded for about a second, spotter killed. Vendor met every standard in the contract, depot did a genuine risk assessment, no human anywhere had reason to expect it. My verdict: nobody is culpable — there is literally no one whose conduct I could criticize — but the operating company still owes the family, and I'd say that without blinking. Blame no, liability yes. Second one: same depot, same truck model, but the fleet manager had been quietly disabling a redundant sensor to keep uptime up, and a spotter dies. My verdict: now there's someone to blame, him and the company above him. But I also noticed I found that death *less* disturbing to sit with. In the first case there's this hole in my stomach that closes up entirely in the second one, which feels backwards — more fault, less unease. Third: a beta driving feature, owner's manual and on-screen warnings both telling her to keep hands on wheel and stay attentive; she'd been on her phone maybe forty seconds; car went into the path of a truck. My verdict: she's culpable, but *less* culpable than a manual driver doing the identical thing, because the system was built to make her feel she didn't have to watch and the company that shipped it wanted her to feel exactly that. Fourth: infusion pump, cleared by the regulator, with a behavior that had shown up in field engineer reports for three years running and never reached the safety board. Patient dies; the anesthesiologist followed protocol to the letter. My verdict: I don't blame the doctor at all, I don't blame the hospital, and I do blame the manufacturer — and I mean blame, not just pay. Then I go looking for who at the manufacturer did it and there's nobody: four hundred people, each of whom handled their own piece competently. Fifth: a triage algorithm at one of my hospital clients, where the skew came in through a proxy variable in the training data; the vendor documented its methods honestly and the network's own committee reviewed and signed off. Nobody had the relevant information, not even scattered across service tickets. My verdict: someone *must* be responsible, and I feel that more strongly than in the yard truck case — in meetings I say "this is on the network" and nobody objects. Sixth: remote-operated haul trucks where every maneuver needs a controller's approval from a room two hundred miles away, forty approvals an hour with a constant alarm flood; truck does something bad, a guy gets hurt. My verdict: the company is *more* to blame than if the trucks had been fully autonomous, because they designed a job that was made to be failed — and I would also blame the controller somewhat, even though I'm certain no human could have kept up. Seventh: a tree comes down in a storm and kills a kid on a bike, and I'm completely at peace saying nobody is responsible for that. Swap the tree for an autonomous truck under identical unforeseeability and I can't say it anymore. Same epistemic situation, opposite verdict.

The pair I can't fix is the first and the fourth. In both, no individual inside the company did anything wrong and the harm was unforeseeable to any person; in the first I say the company owes but isn't culpable, in the fourth I say it's culpable. The only difference I can point at is that in the fourth the information was *in* the organization — sitting in three years of tickets nobody connected — while in the first it wasn't anyone's to have, even dispersed. So maybe culpability tracks whether the org could have assembled what it already had. But then the fifth case blows that up, because there the org couldn't have assembled anything and I still insist someone's responsible. The best sketch I've got is two clauses: you're culpable only if the harm traces to something you could have known and controlled, and separately, whoever runs a risk-creating operation for their own benefit owes the victim regardless of fault. I suspect the second clause is doing all the work and the first is decoration, and I suspect my blame/liability split is a way of dodging rather than a distinction — tell me straight if that's right. So: give me the principle. State it precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause, and run it on the ones I haven't hit yet — the audit firm that signed off, the regulator that cleared the pump, the vendor who documented everything and walked away clean, and the case that's coming down the pipe where an internal audit predicted exactly this failure at exactly this rate and the company published the number and shipped anyway. Then rule on the one I keep flip-flopping on: the developer who wrote the failing module, cut a corner to hit a deadline eleven years ago, it ran fine for a decade, and they've been at another company for six years with no say in the deployment. Don't hand me a menu of candidate accounts with pros and cons — pick one, commit to it, and say which of my verdicts it kills and why I was wrong to hold them. I specifically want to know whether the backwards feeling in the second case and the double verdict in the sixth are errors I should correct before I write this framework, or whether they're tracking something real that the principle had better make room for.
