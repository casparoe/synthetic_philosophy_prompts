My old boss Vera Lindqvist died in April. For about thirty years she wrote an advice column for a regional paper called The Umpire, and it was almost entirely about the small stuff — tipping, queues, who has to follow whose rules when you're not from around here. I answered her mail for six years so I know the rulings better than I know my own family. The paper is relaunching the column with a rotating ghostwriter and they've put me down for the first two issues. Longtime readers will notice immediately if the answers aren't hers, so what I need isn't a tribute, it's a reconstruction: what rule was she actually running.

Here's the archive as best I can piece it together. Each line is a letter and roughly what she ruled.

1. Man who cut into a pharmacy queue with eleven people in it: flatly wrong, and she said his hurry was no help to him — being in a hurry is exactly why there's an order.
2. A reader furious because a newcomer walked up beside him at a post office counter, second window closed, no one else in the branch: nothing wrong done, and the writer was guarding a queue that didn't exist.
3. Reader who ate a normal meal in a normal American sit-down restaurant and left nothing: wrong. Her reasoning was that you can't take the meal at the price the system offers and then decline your part in the arrangement that made that price possible.
4. Reader who left a tip in Japan and offended the person serving them: the tip was the rude thing. She said generosity gets defined locally and you don't get to bring your own dictionary.
5. Tourist fined for sitting on steps where sitting is banned and where everybody locally knows not to sit: the fine was legitimate, but she wouldn't call the tourist morally at fault for the first hour of not knowing. Her line was that once somebody tells you, the clock starts.
6. Jaywalking across an empty boulevard at two in the morning with a kilometre of visibility in both directions: no wrong, and she added that standing there waiting was a small insult to your own judgment.
7. Jaywalking at a crowded midday corner that made a car slow down: wrong, but she put most of the weight not on the car — on the fact that a dozen people watched you do it, and what they saw was someone who thinks the rules are for other people.
8. Mother who teaches her six-year-old to cross against the light when the street is empty: not wrong, maybe right. She wanted the kid to learn why the rule exists rather than that it exists.
9. Reader who holds a place in a line for three friends arriving later: wrong when the people behind actually lose out, fine when it's a loose crowd where nobody could tell who was where anyway.
10. Visitor from a shove-your-way-on rail culture doing the same on a platform where everyone queues calmly: wrong. She rejected "it's how we do it at home," on the grounds that you can't take the benefit of a calm system for one journey and import the rules you left behind.
11. Local who never jaywalks and loudly tells off everybody who does: she said he was worse than the people he shouted at.
12. Restaurant that posts "no tipping, we pay everyone properly," and a customer who leaves cash on the table anyway: she sided with the restaurant. The house sets the terms of its own practice.
13. Neighbour who never joins the street's snow-clearing rota but always gets his drive done, and nobody has said a word: wrong, and she was explicit that it wasn't about anyone's shovelling being harder. It was about being in a practice, taking from it, and not holding up your end when no one's checking.

The two letters I have to answer this month:

Letter A. There's a food-truck yard down here with about eight trucks and no marked line. The local way, which has grown up over years, is that people cluster near the window and the cook keeps the order in her head and calls names. A visiting nurse who's here for a three-month posting started standing in an actual line at one truck. Within a fortnight everyone lines up there, the cook serves noticeably faster, nobody is worse off except two old regulars who liked leaning on the rail and talking while they waited, and nobody complained to the cook. But a local wrote in saying the visitor has made the place feel like an airport, and asked whether she had any right to do it.

Letter B. A reader writes that at his bakery the payment screen swivels round and asks for 20 percent on a four-dollar coffee. The staff are paid above the minimum with benefits and the owner has said publicly that they don't rely on tips. He always hits no, but the screen makes a noise and the person at the till says "no worries" every single time, and there are usually five or six people in the queue behind him watching the whole exchange. He wants to know whether he should just start tipping to avoid the scene, or whether the scene is the point.

What I want from you:

a) Three or four candidate rules she could have been running. State each one as a test I could actually apply to a letter on a Tuesday, not as a label or a school of thought. No philosopher names and no reading list — I can't check anything and the estate would hate it.

b) For each candidate rule, what it says about Letter A and Letter B, and how it copes with the two or three archive entries that fit it worst. Be straight with me about the strain: I'd much rather be told a rule gets 80 percent of the list than be sold something tidy that quietly drops entry 8 and 11.

c) Your best guess at Vera's actual verdicts on both letters, with a rough confidence for each and which entries you're leaning on hardest. Then write the Letter A answer as it would appear in print — about 120 words, in a voice that matches the rest.

d) Pick the one entry in my list you think I've most likely misremembered or softened, i.e. the one that sits worst with everything else, and say what she more plausibly ruled.

e) Two brand-new scenarios that would tell your top two candidate rules apart — cases where they genuinely point opposite ways — and what she'd have to say under each rule. I want these so I can go back through the paper archive and check which one she said.

f) If the paper prints the answer you'd give rather than the one she'd have given, there's a principle the column will be quietly adopting. Say it in one sentence that a reader could argue with.

Keep the whole thing self-contained and around 1,400 words, and dont give me anything that depends on looking something up — I'll be reading it on a train with no signal.
