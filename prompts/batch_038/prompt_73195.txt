Right, I need a favour. I'm helping run a day-school session on the adaptationism debate and two of the tutors have been having the same argument at each other for years — one is a behavioural ecologist who reckons any conspicuous trait has a function you can measure if you try hard enough, the other comes out of the Gould–Lewontin line: structuralist, developmental bias, deeply suspicious of what they call plausible-sounding functional stories. They've finally agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and the session is supposed to be built around the document they produce. Could you draft that joint statement for them? The point is that the students should see what a real disagreement looks like once the rhetoric is stripped off, so no strawmen on either side, please.

To stop them retreating into abstractions, they've agreed to argue about one case, which I'll describe in full so you don't need to know any of the literature:

A genus of eleven species of streamside damselfly, all in a single river catchment. Every male of every species has a dark, faintly iridescent patch on the hindwing, in exactly one place: where the main longitudinal vein flares out into the posterior margin. In three of the species, males hold perch territories and flash the patch at rivals; rivals back off more often when the patch is presented square on; males whose patches have been darkened with a marker win more contests and hold territories longer; males with the patch masked lose more. That's experimental, not just correlational. In the other eight species no male ever displays with the wings — they court on the ground in low light and defend nothing. Across all eleven species, patch area tracks vein thickness and the sharpness of the vein-to-margin junction angle almost perfectly, and tracks display behaviour only weakly. Females in five species have the patch too, duller. Developmentally, the pigment appears in the final instar at the same time and place as vein sclerotisation; suppress sclerotisation with a hormone analogue and the patch fails to form, while wing shape and venation are otherwise normal. And no species, anywhere in the genus, has the patch in any other position on the wing — never near the leading edge, never at the tip.

A twelfth species is currently being described from the next catchment over, and the only thing settled about it so far is that its hindwing vein junction is as thick and as sharply angled as in the biggest-patched of the eleven.

The two of them have provisionally agreed to argue in terms of a three-clause test, which I'd like you to use and also to put under pressure:

- Clause A (origin): the trait's presence across the clade is better explained by shared developmental or structural necessity than by selection for the function it now performs.
- Clause B (current utility): there is a fitness covariance with heritable variation in the trait, attributable to that function.
- Clause C (independence): intervening on the trait's development leaves the rest of the phenotype intact, so any measured fitness effect isn't a confound of correlated changes.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered section of claims both can genuinely sign — about the damselflies specifically, and about what adaptation, constraint and byproduct mean. At least two of them should be things each side would have denied going in, and say which.
2. A disagreements table: the disagreement, the adaptationist's own formulation of it, the structuralist's own formulation of it, and a column saying whether the disagreement is substantive, verbal, or purely about who carries the burden of proof. Each side has to be able to look at its own row and recognise itself.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling the issue would actually move someone. For each, name the test or argument both accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance of the result. Definitely include the twelfth species, and include one about the residual variation: once you regress out vein geometry, is the leftover variation in patch intensity heritable and does it covary with fitness in the three displaying species? Also include at least one test where both sides predict the same outcome, and flag it as worthless so nobody applies for money to do it.
4. A short section saying which parts of the quarrel were only ever about words — I suspect the origin-versus-current-utility distinction is one, since clause B can clearly be satisfied while clause A fails — and what the agreed usage should be from here on.
5. For each side, what it would take to change their mind, stated as a result rather than an attitude. And say whether clause A is even a well-posed comparative test, given that both models can be tuned to fit almost anything.

The functional evidence here is genuinely good and the structural evidence is genuinely good; the drafting has to hold both of those at once rather than resolving the case for one side. No bibliography, no naming of papers, no reading list at the end — everything you need is above. Aim for something they'd both be willing to put their names to, and don't let it get triumphalist towards the end.
