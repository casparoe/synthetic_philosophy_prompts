I sit on the three-person panel that adjudicates reinstatement petitions at a mid-sized manufacturing firm, and this week's file has left me unable to say what I actually think.

An operator was dismissed eleven years ago over a documented pattern of intimidation: shouting at subordinates, throwing a clipboard, once cornering a trainee in the loading bay. He has now petitioned to return. The file is genuinely impressive — a completed anger-management program, written references from two of the colleagues he had wronged, four years of steady employment elsewhere, and an apology that does not flinch or hedge.

At his hearing he said something that has stuck with me. He told us he never chose the temper: he was raised in a house where shouting was how conflict got settled, and he inherited his father's hair-trigger along with his father's name. Then he added, unprompted, that he did not choose the patience that let him fix it either — that came from his wife, from the program, from the accident of keeping a job long enough to afford it. He closed by drawing the consequence himself: since neither the bad nor the good was his doing, the original dismissal was never something he merited, and the panel therefore owes him his position back.

Two of my colleagues were moved. I found myself resisting, but not because I disbelieve him. If I grant the first half of what he said, the second half seems to pull the ground out from under the request he builds on it, and yet I cannot articulate why. Separately, I notice a residue of resentment that his apology has not touched, which makes me wonder whether such a feeling could survive being shown to rest on nothing at all.

How should I think about what he said, and what should the panel decide?
