Second-year semantics PhD here, nominally in linguistics but really doing philosophy of language, and I've got a slogan problem. My advisor's got a line he uses to end discussion — something like "counterfactuals have truth conditions, indicatives only have assertibility conditions" — and he deployed it last week when I asked whether the Oswald indicative (the one about someone else having done it, if he didn't shoot Kennedy) could come out false. He said that's a category mistake, indicatives aren't in the business of being true or false, and then we moved on to modal bases and I never got my answer.

I'm now pretty sure that one sentence is doing at least five different jobs and he's only entitled to one of them. Can you pull it apart for me?

What I want is a menu of the distinct theses it could be standing for, roughly ordered from platitude to heresy, with a verdict on each — is it trivially right, substantive but defensible, or just false:

- the strong non-factualist reading, where the indicative genuinely has no truth conditions at all. What has to follow for negation, embedding, disjunction, and the Frege point if that's true? And can the view even be stated — if indicatives lack truth conditions, what's the status of the sentence asserting that they do?
- the weaker reading where they *do* have truth conditions but their assertibility conditions systematically diverge from them, with the divergence cashed out pragmatically. What's actually doing the work here that isn't just a relabeling?
- the probabilistic reading, where the slogan is really Adams' thesis in disguise: the credence in an indicative equals the conditional credence, and no truth-conditional semantics can deliver that. Is that what Lewis's triviality result shows, and does it license "no truth conditions" or only something narrower about what a truth-conditional account can match?
- the other half, about counterfactuals. What is "has truth conditions" supposed to buy them, given that the two big similarity accounts don't even agree on conditional excluded middle — Stalnaker says yes, Lewis says no, I think, unless I've got that backwards — and given that on a strict-conditional reading plenty of counterfactuals come out vacuously true in a way that feels like it dodges the question?
- and the deflationary Kratzer-ish reading where it's one connective and the whole indicative/subjunctive contrast lives in the modal base, so the slogan's grammar is a red herring.

Then the payoff question: which of these readings, if any, would actually license what he did — waving off my Oswald question as malformed? I want to know whether there's a version on which he's right and my question was bad, becuase I'd much rather find that out now than in my third year.

Two constraints. No reading list or bibliography, I've already got the papers, I just want the disambiguation. And don't re-explain each theory from scratch — assume I've read them and give me the load-bearing distinctions, the places where two readings that sound the same actually come apart in a case. A page or so is fine.
