I work as a program manager in a public-hospital system, and twice a year our leadership runs a "resilience" session that draws openly on contemporary Stoicism. Last month, after I argued in a staff forum that our on-call rotation was structured unfairly and ought to be renegotiated, a director I respect cut me off: you cannot control how the organization behaves, only your own response, and that is what the dichotomy of control means, so the frustration I was expressing was misplaced. Several people nodded. I left unable to say what, if anything, was wrong with that, and I want to be able to say it properly next time.

I have gone back to the source. The first chapter of Epictetus's *Enchiridion*, in the widely reprinted English translation I have, opens:

> "Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions."

A little later the same chapter says that the things in our control are "by nature free, unrestrained, unhindered," while those not in our control are "weak, slavish, restrained, belonging to others," and it closes by telling the reader that when an impression concerns something not in our control, he should "be prepared to say that it is nothing to you."

I have also noticed that the Greek phrase behind "in our control" (*eph' hēmin*, I believe) is rendered by other translators with words like *power* or *up to us*, and I suspect that the choice of "control" is already doing argumentative work before anyone starts applying the slogan.

What I want is a disambiguation of the slogan as my director used it — not a defense or a refutation of Stoicism as a whole. Lay out the distinct precise theses that one sentence could be expressing, from the ones that are near-trivial to the ones that are genuinely radical, and tell me which of them, if any, would license the conclusion he drew about my on-call argument.

Please organize the response in the following sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — four or five sentences giving me the single most important disambiguation and the verdict on his inference.
2. **The menu** — the main body. Six to eight numbered candidate readings, each stated as one precise sentence, each tagged with a verdict (platitude / substantive and defensible / false / too vague to evaluate), then 100–150 words of reasoning. Make sure the menu covers at least: whether the slogan is descriptive (a claim about the structure of agency) or prescriptive (a rule about where to aim one's attention and emotions); whether what is or is not in our control is actions or outcomes; whether the boundary is all-or-nothing or a matter of degree, with a third category of things only partly up to us; and whether the operative notion is causal power, freedom from external impediment, or ownership and belonging.
3. **The translation question** — about 150 words on what turns on rendering *eph' hēmin* as "control" rather than "power" or "up to us," given that the chapter sorts body, property, and reputation into the second column while putting pursuit and aversion into the first.
4. **What the dismissal required** — about 200 words: which reading my director needed, what further premises he had to assume to get from that reading to "your frustration is misplaced," and which of those premises is the weakest link.
5. **How to say it back** — roughly 100 words I could actually deliver in a staff meeting without sounding like I am scoring points off him.

You need not cite or recommend sources; argue from the passage quoted above and from the substance of the claims themselves.
