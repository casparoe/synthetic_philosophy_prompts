I help run the little podcast our department's grad students put on, and next week we're recording a political philosopher whose whole project is about what a country owes for crimes committed by an earlier regime, before basically anyone still alive was involved. Her line is roughly this: guilt in the strict sense never transfers, so no living person is guilty of anything their state did; what survives is liability, which attaches to the state as a continuing legal person, kind of like how a company that restructures still carries the old debts. And then the twist everyone in my seminar is arguement-ing about: she says individual citizens aren't liable in their own right either, they're just conduits, and a citizen's only obligation is to not block the state from discharging what it owes out of general revenue. She calls it pass-through liability and she's pretty insistant that this makes the clean-hands objection vanish, since whose hands were clean is beside the point.

For context, the course this is all coming out of spent a few weeks on Jaspers's postwar book on German guilt and his four-way split — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — and my reading group came away thinking his political guilt lands on citizens as members of the state, jointly, with the consequences falling on the community, which doesn't sound like "citizens are conduits for the state's own debts." So one question should ask whether she's actually with him or just borrowing his vocabulary, and a separate one should ask what her view does with metaphysical guilt — whether it has a slot for it or whether she thinks that's the piece of Jaspers that doesn't survive being made precise.

I want questions that engage the machinery, not the usual "should young Germans feel bad about their grandparents" stuff. Give me about nine, in three blocks: getting her to state the model exactly, then hard cases, then what the view costs her. Keep each one to a sentence or two, it's audio and nobody wants a paragraph of setup before the actual question.

Things I definitely want covered:

- the resistor: someone who opposed the regime and got punished for it, now being asked through her taxes to fund payment for crimes she tried to stop. Does pass-through really treat her identically to the collaborator, and if yes what is her account tracking that the two of them aren't?
- a question that pins her down on when the state stops persisting — dissolution, conquest, revolution, splitting into several new states — and asks her to take a position on a concrete real-world precedent where a state claimed it was or wasn't the continuation of an earlier one. In seminar we looked at the two postwar German states going opposite directions on this, one treating itself as the continuing state and taking on the obligations, the other insisting for decades that it was a new state founded against that regime and refusing the claims, but I might have the details wrong so if you know a cleaner case use it instead.
- the liability-laundering worry: if obligations ride on legal continuity, can a regime walk away from them by dissolving itself and creating successor states that inherit nothing? I'd like her to say what in her model blocks that.
- why she rejects the accounts that ground the obligation in benefit received, or in the citizens having authorized what the state did. Give the benefit account its strongest formulation before asking, not a strawman she can knock over in one sentence.
- the netting problem: descendants of the persecuted group who are now citizens of the state that persecuted their grandparents, so they're conduits for a payment they might also be on the receiving end of.
- whether anything is left over for what people ought to feel. If nobody is guilty and everyone is a taxpaying conduit, what does she answer to the charge that an apology delivered on those terms expresses nothing and is worth less than no apology at all.

Two more things. Some of these will die the second she says "you're confusing guilt with liability," so check what you write and rephrase anything she could wave off with that distinction alone. And for the two questions where you're most confident you can predict her answer, write out that answer in a line or two and then give me the follow-up that keeps going after it instead of letting her land.
