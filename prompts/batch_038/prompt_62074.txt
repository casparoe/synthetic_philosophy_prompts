I am helping draft a gifts-and-hospitality rule for a regional land-titling office, and one of the drafters keeps insisting that a bribe is simply theft with extra steps: the official takes what belongs to the public, and since theft is wrong everywhere, no appeal to local custom can rescue gift exchange, so a bright-line rule of zero value is the only coherent option. On his telling, a village head who accepts livestock as a customary gift from a family applying for a title — in the open, reciprocated over years — has stolen from everyone who was not that family. Please audit the analogy rather than the politics behind it. First lay out the mapping explicitly: what plays the thief, the owner, the property taken, the victim, and the possibility of restitution. Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the use he is making of the comparison from those he can safely set aside, and say in particular what the theft frame quietly imports that a gift or patronage relation may lack — a determinate owner, a holding that exists prior to the transfer, a wrong that consists in the taking rather than in the distortion of a decision. Does his conclusion survive once those are on the table: does a single universal standard really follow, and are custom-based defenses merely special pleading? If theft is the wrong source domain, propose a better one and state what that alternative would license instead. Please write your answer in American English.
