I work as a software engineer on the tooling side of a small video game studio, and I have been asked to settle a dispute for which I don't have the vocabulary.

Our game opens with a short verse spoken over the title sequence: four lines, rhyming in pairs, with a pun in the last line that the whole thing turns on. The verse is timed to the animation, so each line has to land within a fixed window, and the same four lines are printed on the cover artwork, where the layout only accommodates a set number of characters per line. We are now localising into five languages, and our two freelance translators have returned incompatible solutions.

Translator A has preserved the pun throughout, in some cases by finding a different piece of wordplay that does equivalent work, but her lines run long: two of them overrun the timing, and none of the puns fit the cover. Translator B has kept the line lengths and the rhyme scheme exactly, which means the pun has gone and, in one language, the fourth line now says something the English never said. Our production lead favours A, on the grounds that the joke is what the verse is for, and that we can re-cut the animation and redesign the cover; on her account B has merely produced something tidy at the cost of the point. I keep feeling that B has not obviously failed, and that A's solution involves a real loss which re-cutting the animation does not repair — but I cannot say what the loss consists in, or why I think it is a loss rather than a cost we simply decline to pay.

There is a further wrinkle. Native speakers we have shown B's version to, in one of the five languages, describe it as more affecting than the English original is to us. If a verse works better in its new language than the thing it translates, but works differently, I don't know whether to file that under success or failure.

What I would like to know is whether there is any principled way to choose between these two solutions, or whether we are only registering taste and dressing it up. In particular: is it legitimate to hold a translation to a formal constraint such as line length when doing so forces a change of meaning, and if it is, what makes that kind of constraint worth honouring in some cases and not in others? Finally, whatever policy we adopt has to be applied by people who do not share our intuitions about the English source, so it needs to be written down. What should it say?
