I am the teaching assistant for a fourth-year seminar on the comparative history of complex societies, and I have a pile of take-home midterms to mark. Before I commit to a grade on one of them, I would like an independent read of it: an assessment I can compare with my own rather than a defence of a grade I have already formed. The essay question issued to the students was as follows.

> It has been argued that "collapse" is not the name of a kind of event but an evaluative label that later observers attach to what were in fact processes of reorganization, relocation, or decentralization. Assess this claim with reference to two of the following: the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean; the Classic Maya lowlands; Rapa Nui. Your answer must distinguish at least two accounts of why complex societies lose complexity, and must say what would count as evidence for or against collapse in the cases you discuss. (800 words maximum.)

Here is the submitted answer. It is within the word limit, and the marking scheme for the course is the standard letter scale, A through F with pluses and minuses.

> The claim is largely right, but it is right for the wrong reasons in some of these cases and does not hold at all in others; my conclusion is that "collapse" should be retained for a restricted class of losses and dropped for the rest.
>
> The Classic Maya lowlands are the strongest case for the sceptical view. What happened between the eighth and tenth centuries was the emptying of the great southern centres — Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán — the end of dated royal monuments, the end of large-scale construction, and the end of divine kingship as a political form. None of this was the end of the Maya. Maya people, Maya languages, and Maya polities continued; the north of the peninsula prospered in the centuries that followed, and Chichén Itzá went on to dominate much of the region. If the population survived, moved, and in some places grew wealthier, then to say that the society collapsed is to adopt the standpoint of the palace and to treat the fate of a dynasty as the fate of a people. On the other hand, the southern lowlands did depopulate heavily, and something was genuinely lost here rather than merely relocated, so the label cannot simply be dropped for this case.
>
> The Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean is harder for the sceptic. Within a few decades around 1200 BCE the palace systems of Mycenaean Greece, Hittite Anatolia, and the Levantine coast were destroyed or abandoned; the Hittite state did not reappear; and the Aegean lost its writing. The Linear B records were administrative — inventories, allocations, rations — and after the palaces fell nothing of the kind was written again in Greece for centuries, until the alphabet was borrowed from Phoenicia. This is subtraction rather than relocation: a record-keeping technology, the exchange networks that moved grain, copper, and tin, and the political units that maintained them all disappear together. The usual way of stating the cause is to point to the Sea Peoples, but this mistakes a symptom for a mechanism. Raiders and migrants arriving by sea and overland are themselves plausibly downstream of drought, of earthquake damage, and of the failure of an interdependent system in which a shortage in one place was transmitted to the others through the very networks that had made them wealthy. A system whose members depend on one another can be undone by the loss of any single link, and once undone it produces precisely the displaced populations that are then cited as its cause.
>
> The two accounts the question asks us to distinguish are the environmental one and the fiscal one. On the environmental or carrying-capacity account, complexity is sustained by drawing down a resource base, and when the base is exhausted the society must intensify, contract, or fail. On Tainter's account, which is the same argument put in economic terms, societies add complexity because it solves problems, but each further increment of complexity returns less than the one before it, so that a society eventually finds itself paying more for less. For Tainter, collapse is a catastrophe that befalls a society which can no longer afford its own institutions.
>
> Rapa Nui illustrates the environmental account most cleanly. The island's palm forest was cleared, for agriculture and to provide the timber on which the great statues were hauled; with the trees went the soil, and with them too went the canoe-borne nearshore fishing; and the population crashed, which is why the island held only a few thousand people when the Dutch arrived in 1722. The losses here are ecological and demographic rather than institutional, and if that sequence is right, the case for calling it a collapse is at its strongest.
>
> My conclusion, then, is that "collapse" is not one thing, and that the word should be sorted rather than abandoned. Where what disappears is an institution and its monuments while the population persists or relocates, the term imports the values of the people who built the institution, and "reorganization" or "transformation" describes the events more honestly. Where what disappears is population, literacy, and exchange, the term is doing descriptive work and should be kept. The test I would propose is whether the loss can be measured without taking up anyone's point of view: monument counts and dynastic lists are indexed to a regime, but the absence of writing, of settlement, and of people is not.
