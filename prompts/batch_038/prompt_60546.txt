2. The theory spat out exactly the right number, and by all accounts Einstein was thrilled; it was one of the main reasons physicists took relativity seriously so fast.
3. But on Bayesianism — the currently fashionable account of what confirmation is — that number should not have counted as evidence for general relativity at all, not one bit.
4. Here's why. Bayesians say you update by conditionalizing, which means your new confidence in a hypothesis is just your old confidence in it given the evidence. The Mercury anomaly wasn't new. Everyone already knew it. And anything a rational agent already knows has probability 1 for that agent.
5. So the calculation is P(H|E) = P(H) / 1 = P(H). Bayes' theorem literally divides by one. The evidence leaves your confidence exactly where it started.
6. And this isn't a quirk of Einstein's case, it's the general rule: old evidence can never confirm anything, because confirmation only ever comes from novelty. A theory that merely explains what you already knew has to be worth nothing to a Bayesian; only predictions that come true count.
7. You might think the fix is to say Einstein wasn't really 100% sure about the Mercury data — and sure enough, that's what some Bayesians say, that you should never assign probability 1 to any observation. But it's an absurd price to pay, because nobody in 1915 was sitting around unsure whether Mercury's orbit precessed the way it did.
8. The upshot is that Bayesianism can't tell the difference between a theory that genuinely explains and a lucky guess that happens to fit, so however useful the formalism is for settling betting odds, it can't be the whole story about how theories gain support.
