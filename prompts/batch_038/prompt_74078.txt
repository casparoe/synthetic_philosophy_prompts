I spent forty years characterizing detector noise and the limits of measurement before retiring, and I have only recently taken up social epistemology. I am fluent in the statistics of instrumentation and have no mathematics of social choice whatever. I would like Condorcet's jury theorem restated in the vocabulary I actually think in — repeated measurement, ensemble averaging, signal-to-noise, and the redundancy schemes by which one rejects uncorrelated noise across parallel channels. Render it as faithfully as you can: the competence condition (each juror better than a coin flip), the independence condition given the state of the world, and the asymptotic claim that the majority verdict converges on the truth as the panel grows. My working suspicion is that a jury is a repetition code and majority rule is its decoder, so that the standard objection about correlated errors is merely the observation that redundancy cannot reject common-mode noise. Tell me whether that suspicion survives scrutiny or quietly imports something the analogy cannot carry.

Then give me the residue report, item by item: which claims translate exactly, which translate only by being distorted, and which have no counterpart at all in the language of measurement. Two candidates I have already run into are the suggestion that a jury's verdict constitutes the fact it reports rather than tracking one, and the suggestion that publishing the theorem alters the behavior of the very jurors it describes. For each piece of residue, say plainly whether it exposes a genuine limit of my instrumental vocabulary or shows that the philosophers' problem was less well posed than it first appeared.

Please write the answer in American English.
