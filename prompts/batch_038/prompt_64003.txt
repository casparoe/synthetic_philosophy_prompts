I'm giving a 30-minute talk at a philosophy-of-cosmology workshop in about three weeks, and the audience is... a mixed bag that I'm honestly a little scared of. Roughly: four or five philosophers of probability (one of whom works on the Sleeping Beauty problem and considers it settled), two cosmologists who care about the measure problem and think of themselves as doing the adult version of this stuff, and a couple of Bayesian statisticians who will want to know what the likelihood function actually is. Nobody in the room is a fan of anthropic reasoning generally; the default mood is "this whole literature is a mess of badly-specified thought experiments."

Here's the thesis I'm defending: the self-sampling assumption is right and the self-indication assumption is wrong — you should reason as if you were a random observer from your reference class, *not* weight hypotheses by how many observers they contain — and the famous "presumptuous philosopher" case, where a theorist becomes near-certain of a theory just because it predicts a trillion times more observers, doesn't refute SIA at all. It's really an objection about prior sensitivity dressed up as a paradox. My argument sketch, so you have something to aim at: (1) SIA makes it possible to get massive confirmation from facts that have nothing to do with your evidence — the theorist never looked through a telescope, he just counted; (2) SSA at least ties the shift in credence to your actual location among observers, which is the only genuinely new information you ever get; (3) the reference-class relativity everyone complains about is a feature, because your total evidence narrows which class you're sampling from, and once you do that properly the presumptuous philosopher's trillion-to-one swing mostly evaporates; (4) I'll bite the bullet on the doomsday argument, but only in a restricted, sane form.

What I need is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eightish objections this particular room will actually raise — not the ones I'd get from an anthropics-nerd forum — ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased roughly the way that person would phrase it (the cosmologist will talk about measures and typicality, the Sleeping Beauty person will insist my view is committed to halving, the statistician will ask me to write down the sampling model). For each: a two-minute reply I can deliver out loud, an honest note on where the reply is soft, and what I should just concede up front so I don't waste the Q&A defending something indefensible. Also tell me which single reply has to land or the whole talk collapses, and finish with the question I'm praying nobody asks — the one I don't have a good answer to yet.
