I'm a backend engineer, not a philosophy person, but for about three years now my team has owned a library that a lot of other people's code depends on, and we're currently fighting (politely, in Slack) about whether to finally delete an API we marked deprecated in 2021. Somebody on the team, who reads more than the rest of us, dropped a link to Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation from 1874 — the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, with the monumental / antiquarian / critical split — and said our whole deprecation policy is basically that essay with worse hair. I laughed, then I read it, and now I can't unsee it. So I want you to actually work through the mapping rather than just tell me it's a fun analogy.

Here's the practice as concretely as I can state it. We follow semver: a MAJOR version bump is the one and only license to break other people's code, and everything below it promises compatibility. Marking something deprecated is officially a *minor* bump — i.e., a backward-compatible addition, filed in the same category as a new feature. Deprecated things keep working for at least one full major release, with a runtime warning, and in practice we also write migration guides, ship codemods that mechanically rewrite the user's code for them, keep LTS branches alive past their nominal end of life, and publish a blog post that reads like an apology. Under 1.0 we claimed the right to break anything at any time with no notice. And our license says the software is provided as-is, with no warranty and, as far as I can tell, no obligations to anybody. Meanwhile the ecosystem's founding horror story is "dependency hell," described as version lock on one side and version promiscuity on the other — both of which are failures of how you relate to your own past.

So: what theory of the past does all this presuppose? Sort the pieces into Nietzsche's three modes as precisely as you can, and tell me where a piece refuses to sort cleanly — I suspect 1.0.0 itself is doing something weird, like the moment a project acquires ancestors and starts having a history it's responsible for. Two puzzles I especially want pressed on. First, if semver genuinely *licenses* the break, why does every break come wrapped in warnings, grace periods, codemods, and atonement? What would a practice that treated breaking as merely permitted look like, and what would one that treated it as a wrong look like, and which is this? Second, the license disclaims every obligation, so the felt duty to people who adopted us years ago can't be contractual — what is it, then, and does the practice presuppose that the past has a claim on us that no present agreement can dissolve? I'd also like you to be honest about where the practice is underdetermined: probably several different value theories would license the same policy (churn-minimization, some kind of piety toward tradition, a straightforwardly Nietzschean "history must serve the living program"), and I want to know which features actually discriminate between them and which don't. And finally, Nietzsche's warning that the man who judges and condemns the past is himself a product of that past seems almost literal here — our code is made of the dependencies we deprecate, and you can't saw off the branch you're sitting on — so tell me whether that's a real philosophical point about self-undermining criticism or just a cute pun. If the whole analogy collapses somewhere, say so; I'd rather hear it from you than present this at a meetup and get destroyed.
