I run an evening class for adults — 14 of them, no maths beyond adding fractions, 75 minutes a week — on how to read evidence. I want an in-class activity where they run into Simpson's paradox themselves instead of being told about it: teams get a table of outcomes for two treatments, have to commit to a choice, and points ride on getting it right. Please don't use the standard textbook scenarios (university admissions, kidney stones, batting averages) — half the class has seen those — invent something mundane of your own.

What I really want them to feel isn't just that the ranking reverses when you pool the groups. It's that which table you should trust depends on what you know about the case in front of you: a clinician dealing with one patient whose severity you can see, versus an administrator buying for a whole district who can't. And ideally a second round where the same-looking numbers have a different story behind them — the groups were never comparable to begin with, rather than some third variable driving both — so they notice that the table on its own doesn't settle which reading is right. I'm nervous two rounds wont fit; tell me what to cut if it runs long.

So give me the actual numbers (small enough to check by hand on the whiteboard, and the reversal has to survive rounding), the team roles, a scoring scheme that doesn't need real money, a run sheet, and a debrief that maps the moments they'll actually have had onto the formal structure. They shouldn't hear the name of the paradox, or the word confounder, until the debrief. Also: what predictably goes wrong the first time you run this and how to handle it, and which bit of the idea the activity can't reproduce so that I have to supply it in words.
