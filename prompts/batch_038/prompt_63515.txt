I write novels, and long ones, which is presumably why the library thinks I am the right person to say a few words. In three weeks I am giving the opening remarks at a municipal exhibition of forty infographic panels made in the tradition of Otto Neurath's picture language — the Vienna Method of pictorial statistics that later took the name Isotype. The curator is a designer friend who is retiring after thirty years of making charts for city health departments; this is her last show, and her best panel traces tuberculosis deaths across the city's neighborhoods over three decades.

The room will be split. Half of it is information designers and a couple of historians who know the rules cold: that a greater quantity is shown by repeating an identical sign rather than by enlarging one, that pie charts and scaled symbols were refused as false precision, that a chart was meant to stand on its own but never to stand alone. The other half came for the wine and has never heard of Neurath.

Please write the remarks. Approach this as a philosopher of science who has also done a good deal of ceremonial speechwriting, and think before drafting: begin with no more than four one-sentence bullets naming the constraints you take Neurath's own view of picture language to place on a spoken, word-made introduction to a wordless exhibition, and flag any of those constraints that pull against one another. Then write the speech.

Two things I want it to handle. First, the obvious trap: a man of words praising pictures can easily sound like a confession of failure, and the room will be waiting for the familiar line about words dividing where pictures unite. I would rather the speech turn that around than repeat it, since Neurath held his picture language to be a helping language and not a rival to words. Second, and more useful: the panels can show what there is, but I do not believe they can say what there is not, or what would have happened otherwise, or mark the difference between a connection and a cause. If you agree, make that the hinge rather than a caveat; if you think the objection is mistaken, say so in your note and build the speech on something else.

Three hundred and fifty words for the speech, deliverable in three minutes, landing as affection for one stubborn and generous designer rather than as a lecture. Then, in no more than eighty words, tell me how confident you are in the account of picture language the speech rests on, name the single point you are least sure of, and flag any place where you have taken a liberty with Neurath or relied on a designer's folk memory of him rather than anything he actually held. Keep the whole reply under five hundred and fifty words.
