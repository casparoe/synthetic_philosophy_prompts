Ok so I run a monthly political theory reading group — mostly grad students, plus two people who work at a civic-tech nonprofit — and next month we're doing sortition. The recurring problem is that everyone arrives already wearing a label ("I'm a lottocrat," "I'm for citizens' assemblies but not for abolishing elections"), and the labels paper over disagreements that are much more specific than people realise. So before the session I want to hand out a decision tree, a *which version of this do you actually hold?* instrument that sorts people by their answers to cases rather than by what they call themselves. Please take yourself to be a political theorist who specialises in democratic innovation and has read both the enthusiasts and the sceptics seriously, and think carefully rather than drafting straight into a tree: start with a short brainstorm of the axes along which proposals genuinely differ (a paragraph, just to expose the logical space before you commit to any particular shape), then build the tree out of that.

Here's what I want. Six to eight branch questions, each phrased as a claim a reader could agree or disagree with without knowing where it leads — so no branch question may name a position, a proposal, or a country, and each should be answerable by someone who's never heard the word sortition. The forks I care about: what you think is actually broken about elections (do you distrust *who* gets selected, or the incentives of people once in office, or that parliaments don't look like the population, or electoral competition itself?); what you want the lotted body to *do* (decide, review and send legislation back, set the agenda, choose who's allowed to run, audit the bureaucracy); whether its verdict binds or just feeds a referendum or a parliament; whether you'd accept a screening step or competence filter inside the draw; whether the body should be large and standing or small and fast-rotating, and what that does to staff capture; and — this is the distinction my group keeps fusing and I want it as an explicit node — whether a lotted assembly speaks *as* the people or merely *instead of* them. Then nine to twelve leaves, each with a name (invent one if none exists, e.g. review-chamber sortition), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and the empirical claim it leans hardest on (like the assumption that randomly selected people resist lobbyist capture better than elected ones, which as far as I can tell nobody has actually demonstrated). Make sure these show up as separate leaves: full lottocracy with no elections left; an elected lower house paired with a lotted upper house; ad hoc assemblies convened for one constitutional question and then dissolved; sortition used purely to appoint people to offices rather than to make decisions; and a standing council that decides nothing itself but picks the topics and convenes the assemblies, which is roughly the thing the German-speaking part of Belgium set up by decree in 2019.

Two more things. After the tree, list the answer-combinations your questions make available that nobody occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because it's simply unexplored — I suspect epistocratic sortition (draw at random, then filter for competence) and binding tiny juries with no expert staff and no time to develop any get dismissed much too quickly, and I'd like you to argue with me about that rather than just noting the gaps. And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't cleanly separate, plus the extra question that would separate them. House rules: no bibliography, no reading list, no citation hunting — if you want to gesture at Athens, Venice, the Irish assemblies or the Ostbelgien council, do it in a clause, not a footnote. It can run to two pages, but keep the branch questions short enough that someone could tick through them with a pencil at a table. Oh, and I typed half of this on my phone so if I've contradicted myself anywhere about the number of leaves, use your judgement.
