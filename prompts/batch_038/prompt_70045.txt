I retired four years ago from a career in experimental biophysics, and I have taken up ancient philosophy with more seriousness than I expected of myself. I am working through Plutarch's Parallel Lives in the Loeb edition, and I have become thoroughly stuck on the programmatic paragraph that opens the Alexander — the one in which Plutarch says that he is writing lives rather than histories; that the most splendid deeds do not necessarily disclose virtue or vice; that a slight thing, a remark or a joke, often reveals a man more fully than battles in which thousands fall; and that just as portrait painters derive their likeness from the planes and contours of the face and give very little attention to the rest of the body, so he will attend to the signs of the soul and leave the great contests to others. I should say at once that this is my own rendering of the paragraph's sense rather than the translator's exact wording, because I do not want us arguing over a translation; please treat what I have written as faithful in content.

Immediately after this paragraph comes the story of Bucephalus: the horse nobody can handle, Alexander's perception that the animal is shying at its own shadow, the turning of it toward the sun, the successful mount, and the father's tearful remark to the effect that Macedonia is too small for his son and that he must find a kingdom equal to himself.

I want help understanding what the paragraph is actually claiming, and I want to be precise about where I am stuck. First, what sort of claim is it? Is it epistemic — the small sign carries more information about a stable disposition than the large public act — or is it compositional, a statement about how to economize in narrative and hold a reader? The painter analogy seems to me to carry most of the weight, and I cannot tell which way it cuts, since a painter who neglects the body is making a decision about where to spend his labour, not a claim about where the evidence lies. Second, if it is an epistemic claim, how does the Bucephalus episode satisfy it? Taming a horse no one else can manage does not look like a slight thing at all; unless the revelatory item is the diagnosis of the shadow, or the father's remark at the end, in which case I want to know what makes those items signs of soul rather than merely episodes in a story. Third — and this is what my former colleagues press on me whenever I describe the project — the whole procedure looks unfalsifiable. A trait is posited, material is then selected that displays it, and whatever cuts the other way is absorbed as youth, or fortune, or the corruption that attends great power. That is, so far as I can see, exactly how edifying lives of the saints have always worked. Does the paragraph contain any internal check on this, any way in which a reported deed could count against the character it is offered to reveal? Fourth, in the opening of the Aemilius Paulus Plutarch appears to say something that sits awkwardly with all of the above: that he took up the writing of these lives for the sake of others, but now continues it for his own sake, using virtue as a mirror in which to set his own life in order. Who, then, is the mirror for? What is the mechanism by which writing or reading such a life improves a person — imitation, admiration, self-comparison — and is that mechanism the same for the writer and for the reader? If it is not, in what does the reader's benefit consist, and can it survive the possibility that the episodes are traditional rather than documented?

Please take these four in order and work on the concepts rather than the philology. I have no need of references or suggestions for further reading; what I want is a patient reconstruction of the reasoning inside the paragraph. And if my way of putting the questions — in terms of information, evidence, and falsifiability — is itself distorting the text, tell me so plainly, and say what the questions ought to have been.
