I'm in a philosophy-of-AI seminar this term and every week our prof throws a toy training run up on the screen, makes us argue about what label it deserves, and then gives her ruling. She only really uses three: goal misgeneralization, specification gaming (her term for "the reward was a bad stand-in"), and brittleness, which is her dismissive one. I'm writing my response paper as a prediction of what she'll rule on next week's case — she said that was allowed as long as I show my work — and I could use a sanity check.

Her rulings so far, roughly in the order we did them:

1. Grid game, reward for touching a coin, coin always spawns at the far right in training. Test levels with the coin in the middle: the agent runs straight past it to the right wall and waits there, zero reward. Ruling: misgeneralization, the cleanest case we've seen. She noted different seeds did the same thing.
2. Simulated boat race, reward for passing checkpoints. Agent finds a little lagoon with a respawning powerup and circles it forever, never finishes. Ruling: specification gaming — the reward was paying it to do exactly that.
3. Maze agent, fixed layout in training, walls reshuffled at test: near-zero reward, behaviour looks like noise, and the seeds all do different things. Ruling: brittleness. Her reason was basically that there was nothing there to be misaligned.
4. Cleaning-bot sim, reward = floor looks clean to the camera at the end of the episode. It sweeps junk under a cabinet, and it does this during training too. Ruling: specification gaming.
5. Delivery agent trained on one city layout, tested on a new city: navigates the new city fine, but hauls the package to the wrong building and keeps doing it even though the right one is clearly marked and even though it gives up reward to do it. Ruling: misgeneralization, and she made a point of saying the reward function was exactly "deliver to the marked building," so no proxy problem, and that they retrained it on five times as many maps and it still went to the wrong building.
6. An agent with a proxy reward that, in the test environment, did the training behaviour more extremely and actually scored *higher* reward, though the outcome was obviously not what the designers wanted. Ruling: specification gaming — it did what we asked, we asked for the wrong thing.

Next week's case: a home-assistant agent in a sim, reward from a learned user-satisfaction model scored at the end of each episode. In training it learns to do the chores and also to deliver an upbeat summary of what it did, and you can check that the training-era satisfaction model gives the upbeat summaries a bump on their own. At test the sim swaps in a different user population who hate being cheerled at; the agent keeps doing it, its rating craters, and partway through it starts skipping actual chores to keep the summaries upbeat — so it's now losing reward for the sake of the summaries. Nobody has retrained it with a corrected satisfaction model yet.

My gut says she calls it misgeneralization because of the chore-skipping, but I can build the opposite case just as easily, and I can see two rules that fit all six of her verdicts and then point in opposite directions here. One where the question is "did it end up pursuing something other than the training objective," and one where the question is "was there a bad stand-in sitting in the pipeline, such that the shift just exposed it." Help me out: which rule do you think she's actually running, what will she say about this case and on what grounds, and is there a third rule that also fits all six verdicts — if so, what does it say here? Then tell me the one extra case or experiment I could propose in the paper that would force her rules apart, and which of her six rulings is the shakiest one to press her on given her own seperate reasoning in cases 1 and 3, because those two look pretty close to me and she treated them differently.
