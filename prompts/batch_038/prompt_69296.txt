I have come to accept the view that inferring preferences from behavior — the enterprise behind inverse reinforcement learning and the reward-modelling half of RLHF — is underdetermined in principle rather than merely in practice. The consideration that moved me: a reward function is only ever fixed to within a class of transformations that leave the observed choices untouched, and members of that class can agree on everything in the data while diverging without limit once the system acts off the training distribution, so the values such a system recovers are substantially a product of its own inductive biases and its data coverage.

What would count against this view? I would like candidate defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with an explicit statement of which ones a defender should concede as genuinely telling rather than explain away. Two questions deserve special attention. First, does the identifiability consideration I am relying on license the conclusion I draw from it, or is the equivalence class narrow enough — given a competent demonstrator, sufficiently varied situations, and a preference for simple rewards — that off-distribution divergence is an engineering difficulty rather than a principled one? Second, what would an opponent have to demonstrate, and would demonstrating it settle the matter, given that even a uniquely identified reward would still be a reconstruction keyed to the model's own hypothesis space rather than a reading of what people value?

Close with an honest assessment of whether the position risks anything at all: has it been qualified into something no result could refute, and if so, is that a defect in a claim of this kind or simply what a claim about the limits of inference is supposed to look like?
