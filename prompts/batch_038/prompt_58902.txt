I am a first-year doctoral student in economics, and our methodology seminar has just spent two sessions on the old dispute over whether a theory's assumptions need to be realistic — the position the seminar calls the F-twist, after Milton Friedman's methodological essay on positive economics. The discussion became concrete when a classmate objected to an empirical model of labor supply we had been assigned. The model's agents choose hours in response to after-tax wages and are represented as knowing the tax schedule that applies to them and as facing no friction in adjusting their hours; her objection was that a large share of actual workers do not know their own marginal rates, so the assumption is not merely coarse but false of the population being studied. The professor running the seminar replied that an economic model is a map, not a survey of the territory: a map has to leave things out to be usable, a map drawn at one-to-one scale would be of no use to anyone, and you judge a map by whether it gets you where you are going rather than by whether every alley appears on it. On that basis he declared the objection beside the point and moved on. He reaches for the same image in the opposite direction as well — when asked what his own models imply about how people actually deliberate, he says that a map is not a description of the country and should not be read as one. I left unsure whether the analogy had answered her or simply ended the conversation, and I do not yet have the vocabulary to say which.

Could you audit the comparison for me? In particular:

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly — territory, map, scale, the features deliberately left off, the destination, the user of the map, and the standard by which a map counts as accurate — saying for each what it is supposed to be on the modeling side, and flagging the pairings that are already contestable rather than agreed by both parties.

2. Identify the disanalogies, and separate those that are fatal to the use he made of the image from those that are merely local inconveniences. One suspicion I cannot shake: the analogy seems to work by treating an unrealistic assumption as an omission, whereas her objection was that the assumption asserts something untrue about the agents in question. Does that difference matter for a map, and if so, what would the corresponding map defect be?

3. Say what the analogy quietly imports that may not hold of models — for instance, that the omissions are known to and recoverable by the person using the map, that there is a nested family of more and less detailed maps of the same territory at different resolutions, that the destination is fixed independently of the mapmaker, and that a map remains answerable for the accuracy of what it does depict even when it is praised for getting someone somewhere.

4. Give a verdict on the inference he needed. Can the image support only the modest claim that idealization is unavoidable and that models must be assessed relative to a purpose, or does it also carry the strong claim he used to close the discussion — that the descriptive realism of assumptions is irrelevant to a theory's standing so long as its predictions are accurate? If the inference fails, locate precisely where the analogy stops bearing the weight.

5. Say whether one image can perform both jobs he asks of it: deflecting a criticism of the assumptions while simultaneously disclaiming any commitment about the agents' psychology. If not, state what each use separately commits him to.

6. If the map is the wrong picture for this purpose, propose a better one and say what it would license that the map does not; if no picture survives, say so plainly.

I am not asking for a defense or a refutation of Friedman's position in general. What I need is an account of what this particular comparison can and cannot carry, since that is what decides whether my classmate's objection was in order or whether it was, as he said, beside the point.
