I am in Year 13 and I run my school's philosophy society, which meets fortnightly for forty-five minutes. Next week's session is the problem of future contingents: the argument, running back to Aristotle's sea-battle passage, that a statement about a contingent future event must already be true or already be false, and that this seems to leave the future fixed. The audience is about fifteen students, nearly all of whom take Maths, Physics or Chemistry at A-level; none studies Philosophy or Religious Studies, and none has met the topic before. I have a whiteboard, no handouts, and a standing society rule that no session may turn into an argument about a live political or religious question.

I am not asking for the explanation. I want a plan for delivering it, addressing the following.

- The order in which the components must be introduced, and what depends on what. My suspicion is that the three-way distinction between 'we cannot yet know it', 'it is not causally determined', and 'there is not yet any fact of the matter' has to be secured before anything else can be said, but I do not know where bivalence and the idea of a truth-value gap sit in that sequence, or whether either can be reached without the other.
- One example to carry the whole session. I had thought of a coin I shall flip in the car park at break tomorrow, but I expect a physicist to ask at once whether the outcome is fixed by initial conditions, whereupon the session becomes a debate about chaos rather than about truth. Tell me what the example must have and what it must lack, and propose one. It must additionally resolve itself before our next meeting, since I intend to open that meeting by revisiting it and asking whether anyone's answer has changed now that the outcome is known.
- The misconceptions this audience will arrive with or form along the way, each paired with the precise point in the sequence at which it should be headed off. I anticipate at least: that the question is really about free will; that Aristotle is a fatalist of some kind; that the issue is merely epistemic; and that denying bivalence amounts to holding both that the event will not occur and that it will not fail to occur.
- A single question to put at the end that would separate a student who has grasped the problem from one who has simply been agreeable.
- What I should refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud. I assume the rival logical systems and the historical responses to the argument are all beyond reach in forty-five minutes, but I would like that judgement argued rather than taken for granted.
