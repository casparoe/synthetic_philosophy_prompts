For the past year I have been serving on an institutional panel that decides, case by case, whether a novel biological technology falls under containment and oversight rules whose preamble applies them to living organisms and their products. My colleagues are mostly molecular biologists plus two lawyers; the biologists insist the answer is obvious in every instance while being unable to say what makes it obvious, and the lawyers want a criterion they can apply without a seminar. We have deadlocked often enough that our report, due in the spring, currently reads as a list of exceptions to nothing. So I have gone back to first principles and written down my own verdicts on a wide range of cases — mostly sensible ones, as far as I can tell — to see whether they add up to anything at all. Here they are.

A mule is alive, unproblematically. A human red blood cell, which has no nucleus, cannot divide, and will be gone within a few months, is alive. A HeLa cell proliferating in a flask, descended from a cervical tumor biopsied from a woman who died more than seventy years ago, is alive, and I do not think the death of the woman it came from bears on the question. A mitochondrion I have purified out of a cell and that is still consuming oxygen in a buffer is not alive, even though it is doing about the most characteristically lifelike thing there is. A heart removed from a body and beating for hours in a perfusion rig is alive; a body declared dead by neurological criteria and kept warm and ventilated, with kidneys still filtering, is not alive as a whole, though everything in it is. Tobacco mosaic virus in a vial is not alive, and it does not become alive once it gets inside a leaf, though the leaf does. Viroids — naked circles of RNA that replicate in plants and code for no protein whatsoever — are not alive, and neither are prions. A tardigrade dried into a tun, metabolically undetectable for years and revivable on being wetted, is alive the whole time; so is an embryo parked in liquid nitrogen in a clinic freezer; so are freeze-dried bacteria that wake up on a plate. A flame is not alive, but this is the verdict I trust least, because a flame grows, consumes fuel, holds its shape against the wind, throws off sparks that start new flames, and dies when deprived of what it needs — and every reason I give for calling a bacterium alive seems to have an answer here. A crystal growing in a supersaturated solution, seeding new crystals of its own habit and passing heritable dislocations from parent to offspring, is not alive. A robot built from commercial parts that assembles copies of itself out of a bin of spares is not alive, and neither is a computer virus, nor a self-sustaining chemical oscillation maintained in a stirred tank. A synthetic minimal cell with a genome designed on a workstation and fewer than five hundred genes, which cannot synthesize many of the molecules it needs and must be fed a rich laboratory medium by a technician, is alive — and so, obviously, is an obligate intracellular bacterium that has shed most of its biosynthetic machinery and can be kept going only inside a host. Now the pair I cannot reconcile. A population of self-replicating RNA enzymes in a machine-fed tube, which mutates and competes across successive transfers until one lineage takes over, is, I want to say, alive — or at least the closest thing to alive that contains no cells. But it is a parasite on its apparatus in exactly the way the intracellular bacterium is a parasite on its host: neither makes its own building blocks, and both persist only because something else keeps supplying them. If I say the bacterium counts and the RNA does not because the bacterium's supplier is itself alive, that looks like a distinction without a difference, and I can feel it dissolving every time I try to defend it to the panel.

What I want is the principle, or the small number of candidate principles, that best accounts for this pattern — stated precisely enough that one of our lawyers could apply it, and with each clause tied back to the case that motivated it, since I suspect the clauses will turn out to be doing very different kinds of work. Then run it over my list and tell me where it comes apart. Tell me what it delivers on cases I have not thought to include: a biofilm; a slime mold that is one continuous mass of cytoplasm with thousands of nuclei and no cell boundaries; a forest whose trees are joined by fungal networks; an ant colony or a hive; a population of digital organisms evolving inside a simulator, instantiated in silicon rather than chemistry; a bacterium whose genome has been rewritten in a six-letter alphabet; a person whose organs have been replaced one at a time by functioning artificial substitutes, so that no single day marks the transition. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and if the only way to save all of them is a baroque disjunction, say so and tell me which verdicts I should abandon rather than pay that price. If your answer is that life is a cluster concept with no sharp boundary, I still need to know where you would draw the regulatory line and why that line is not arbitrary. Finally, situate your principle against the two approaches our panel keeps gesturing at without naming: the strategy of listing a handful of properties — metabolism, reproduction, heredity, adaptation, compartmentalization — and requiring most or all of them, and the claim that we should not expect a definition of life at all until we have encountered a second, independently originated instance of it. Say which of those your account rescues, refines, or rejects.
