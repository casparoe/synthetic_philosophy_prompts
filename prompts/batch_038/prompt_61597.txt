For a seminar paper on supererogation I have set down the view I actually hold, and I would like an audit of it before I defend it in public. My commitments are these:

1. Whether an act is wrong is settled entirely by its consequences: omitting an act is wrong just when performing it would have made things better overall, and there are no agent-relative permissions to prefer one's own projects.
2. There are nonetheless genuinely supererogatory acts — better performed than omitted, yet not wrong to omit. Giving away most of one's income to save distant strangers is my paradigm case.
3. A moral theory is disqualified, whatever else it gets right, if it requires serious sacrifice of the agent; there must be a cost threshold beyond which obligation lapses.
4. Praise and blame attach only to what lies within the agent's control; how things turn out through luck is irrelevant to assessing the agent.
5. Performing a supererogatory act merits more praise than doing what is merely obligatory.
6. When someone acts supererogatorily toward me, I come to owe them gratitude — and yet no one can place me under an obligation by their own unilateral act.
7. When I perform a supererogatory act, I do what I ought, all things considered, to do.

I have been told that (1) and (2) cannot both be held. What I want to know is whether that is the worst of it or whether something less obvious is doing more damage. Which of these pairs genuinely conflict, and which only look as though they do? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, say for each what I would have to give up and what that would cost the rest of the view, and then tell me which single revision would repair the most.
