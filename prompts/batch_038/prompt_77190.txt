I am working through the literature on collective intentionality and I want to test it against an episode rather than against the standard two-person thought experiments. The episode is the tacit non-aggression that developed along parts of the Western Front in the First World War, of which the Christmas Truce is the most famous instance.

Let me set out the facts I am relying on, so that nothing in your answer turns on material you would have to look up. From roughly November 1914, once the war of movement had ended and the two sides were dug in within shouting distance, infantry in quiet sectors began to abstain from firing on men who were visibly working, resting, or collecting rations. Over the Christmas period this escalated in a number of sectors — mainly where British and German units faced one another, with comparable episodes reported between Austro-Hungarian and Russian troops on the Eastern Front — into shouted greetings, carols answered from the opposite trench, and then men coming out unarmed into no man's land to exchange food, tobacco and small souvenirs, and in places to bury their dead together. Many of the German soldiers had lived or worked in Britain and the two sides could often converse directly, so this was not purely a matter of signals. Accounts also describe football, though its prevalence is disputed; treat it as a matter of degree rather than a settled fact. The truce was never general — fighting continued in other sectors. Afterwards commanders on both sides issued emphatic orders prohibiting fraternisation, and by 1916 the practice had largely died away, for reasons including those orders, the transfer of units known to have fraternised, and the hardening of attitudes after the losses of 1915. The quieter, longer-lived version persisted in places for much of the war: rifle fire deliberately aimed off, shells dropped into known empty ground at known times, raids telegraphed in advance.

The question I want answered is whether any of this was joint action in the technical sense, and if so, between whom.

First, the disciplined mapping. Take the three canonical accounts — the analysis of shared intention in terms of interlocking intentions with meshing subplans, mutual responsiveness and mutual knowledge; the plural-subject account, on which a joint commitment arises from mutually expressed readiness and carries obligations, standing to rebuke, and no unilateral rescission; and the view on which we-intentions sit in individual heads and require no mutual belief at all. For each, say what attitudes and relations it demands of the men in the trenches, which features of the episode supply them, which features it cannot find, and where the account simply goes silent rather than delivering a verdict. Be specific about the parts of the episode that pull in different directions: the singing answered from across no man's land before anything had been agreed; the joint burials, which look like genuine coordinated activity with roles and timing; the deliberate miss, which works only if both sides believe the other is doing it; and the resumption of fire under orders.

Second, the verdicts. On the plural-subject account, if a joint commitment existed, then the man who shot at the enemy he had shared tobacco with wronged that man personally, and the man who resumed firing because he was ordered to breached a commitment he could not rescind on his own. Work out what each of the three accounts says about the resumption of hostilities, and tell me whether those verdicts are a credit to the theory or a cost it is paying.

Third, the grain problem. Nobody supposed that the British and German armies intended anything together. The plausible candidates are much smaller and much less stable: a company here, a platoon there, an ad hoc gathering in no man's land whose composition shifted minute by minute. Do these accounts have any resources for saying which group, if any, was the plural subject in a given sector, or does each one simply build in a different default scale? I would like to know whether the truce sits inside all three accounts' intended range, outside all three, or on the boundary of each in a different place.

Fourth, persistence. If the local arrangements were inherited by newly arrived units — apparently through informal handover from the units they relieved, though you should flag this if you regard it as uncertain — then the practice outlived every individual party to it. What are the persistence conditions of such a thing on an account where the commitment is held by particular persons? Does the episode instead favour the line of thought on which a group can be an agent in its own right and survive wholesale turnover through procedures and roles, except that here there is no procedure, only a routine and a handover? If the bearer of the commitment turns out to be the trench or the company rather than the men, say what is lost by that redescription.

Fifth, competition. There is a well-known game-theoretic treatment of the same practices as tacit cooperation in an iterated prisoner's dilemma, where mutual benefit plus the shadow of the future does all the explanatory work and no pair-level intention is needed. Are that reading and the intentional readings rivals, or descriptions of the same thing at different levels? Identify whatever in the episode would discriminate between them — I suspect the felt obligation not to fire on men one had met, the warnings passed across about coming raids, and the sense of betrayal when firing resumed are data for one and idle wheels for the other, but they might equally be after-the-fact rationalisation, and I want your judgment on that rather than my hunch.

Finally, step back. The canonical illustrations of this literature are two people painting a house, rowing a boat, or taking a walk: small, consensual, non-hierarchical, with a real option to decline. What does a case assembled from carols across no man's land, between people whose standing orders were to kill one another, in defiance of a larger collective they belonged to and could not leave, do to those assumptions? Is the episode evidence that the accounts are too demanding, too permissive, or simply not addressed to cases like this — and did any of them anticipate a case like it?

My own inclination, which I expect is too neat, is that the shared-intention account handles the burial parties and fails at everything larger; that the plural-subject account can claim the whole episode but only by making joint commitment cheap enough that a crowd chanting at a match would qualify too; and that the we-intention account is the only one that can take the first ten minutes seriously, before anyone had agreed to anything. Tell me where that is wrong.

Two housekeeping requests. Where you are inferring the participants' psychology from thin evidence, say so explicitly rather than writing as though the diaries settled it; and end by naming the two or three claims in your own analysis that you would most want checked, plus any detail of the episode you think I have got wrong. I am not asking for a reading list or for citations — I want the analysis, and an honest account of what the theories cannot say about this case.
