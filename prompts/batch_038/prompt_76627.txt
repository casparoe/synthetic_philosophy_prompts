Background, briefly: I spent thirty-five years in isotope geochronology — dating zircons, fighting about decay constants, the usual — and since retiring I've fallen headlong into philosophy, mostly philosophy of time, which felt like a natural move for someone who spent a career turning ages into depth columns.

Our reading group (six of us, two with actual philosophy degrees) has been working through persistence, endurantism versus perdurantism. The member who picks the readings leans hard on one analogy and last week he used it to just end an argument. The analogy: an object spread out in time is exactly like an object spread out in space. Your hand is only a spatial part of you and isn't where your foot is; your 1980 self is only a temporal part of you and isn't there alongside your present self. The whole you is the four-dimensional worm, and insisting the entire object is wholly present at each moment is like insisting your whole body is located in your left hand. Then he added that physics has already settled the matter — special relativity fused space and time into a single manifold, so treating time as a privileged dimension that objects sit fully inside of is pre-1905 thinking. His conclusion was that the debate is basically over and endurantists are refusing to look through the telescope.

I'm half sympathetic. A drill core is a real object you can hold in your hands that lays a time sequence out along a spatial axis, so the worm picture feels native to me. But I'm also the one in the room who actually used the metric for a living, and I keep wanting to say that the signature has a minus sign in it, that you cannot rotate a timelike axis into a spacelike one, and that a light cone is not a sphere. Which makes me suspect the analogy is carrying more weight than it can hold.

So can you audit it for me? Please take this seriously: you're a professional metaphysician who works on persistence and who knows enough relativity not to get laughed out of a physics department seminar. Think carefully, and before the real answer do a short free-brainstorm about which features of the space/time comparison are load-bearing and which are just decoration. Then give me:

- the correspondence table, item by item: what in the spatial case plays the role of the temporal part, of parthood itself, of being co-present, of change, and of the object as a whole;
- the disanalogies, sorted into two piles — the ones that genuinely threaten his conclusion and the intersting but harmless ones — with a line each on why they land where they land;
- what the analogy quietly imports from space that time lacks (symmetry of directions? our ability to survey the whole thing at once? an assumption about how parthood behaves?), and whether his relativity move helps or hurts: does the frame-dependence of simultaneity actually support the worm, or is he reading a block-universe picture into formalism that doesn't contain one?
- whether the Lewis-style temporary intrinsics problem, the thing everyone says is the real cost of endurantism, still bites if the analogy fails;
- and if the hand/foot comparison is bad, what a better analogy for a four-dimensional object would be that doesn't smuggle the answer in. Maybe the drill core is the honest one, since nobody mistakes the core for the formation. Maybe there isn't a better one.

Finish with a plain verdict on how much of his conclusion survives the disanalogies.
