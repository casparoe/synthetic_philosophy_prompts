ok so this went down in my civics class and I honestly still don't know who won. our teacher made up this thing she calls the mandate ratio — it's just the share of the *whole* eligible group that voted for whatever won, so you multiply turnout by the winner's share. she had us run it on brexit: 52% of the 72% who voted = about 37% of everyone who could of voted. then our student council ran an actual school-wide vote last month on the schedule and the same math came out ugly, and two people in my class (noah and ana) got into it for like three days.

the vote had three options: keep the current 5-day week, switch to a 4-day week with longer days, or 4-day week with friday online. results: keep it 38%, longer days 33%, online friday 29%. turnout 61%. the council also did an exit poll where people picked between pairs, and in those longer days beat keep-it 55–45 and longer days beat online friday 52–48. so longer days would've won against either other option one on one, but it lost the real vote.

here's the argument, roughly in order:

1. noah: the vote didn't express the majority's will, it expressed the menu. the option that beats everything head to head lost, and the only reason it lost is two similar options split the vote. whoever wrote the ballot decided the outcome, not us. so "the people have spoken" is a slogan, not a fact.
2. ana: that's an argument about a badly designed ballot, not about referendums. our student president elections regularly have four candidates and the exact same thing happens — if vote splitting is disqualifying then no election ever expresses anything, which proves too much.
3. noah: elections are different because losers can go back and amend stuff, and reps negotiate between the options afterwards. a referendum answer is locked in: you get a yes/no nobody can fine tune, and changing it means running a whole other campaign.
4. ana: switzerland already solved your problem. parliament can put its own counter-proposal on the ballot next to an initiative, and if voters pass both there's a tie-break question about which one wins. if the fix already exists inside direct democracy then your objection is just "design our ballot better," which I agree with.
5. noah: no, that proves my point. the second anything gets complicated you hand the decision back to whoever writes the wording, chooses the tie-break question, and picks what goes on the ballot. and run the mandate ratio on our vote: 61% turnout, winner got 38%, so 23% of the whole school picked the schedule everybody else is now stuck with. either you demand more than half of *everyone* — in which case basically no referendum in history counts — or you don't, in which case "the people decided" isn't doing any work at all.
6. ana: sure, but that's true of every government that's ever been elected, so you've argued against democracy in general and haven't said anything specific about referendums. also 23% is definately a lot when the alternative is 100% of a twelve person student council deciding it for you.
