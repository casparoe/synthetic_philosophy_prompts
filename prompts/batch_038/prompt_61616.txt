Write a satirical document of roughly 800 to 1,000 words, in American English, presented as the third draft of an official state apology together with the marginal comments left on it by two rival government offices.

The setting is entirely invented, and you should invent freely rather than reaching for a real case. In 1874, the Republic of Vess authorized the Alder Diversion Works, which channelized the Alder River and drained the seasonal wetlands on which the valley communities had depended for fishing and irrigation; the same statute transferred title to the riverbed to a chartered company, and the valley's elected assembly was dissolved the following year. A century and a half later, after nine years of negotiations, the Vessian government has agreed to issue a formal apology on the anniversary. The document below is that apology in draft.

Present it as numbered paragraphs of draft text, each followed by bracketed annotations from two reviewers. The Office of Legal Counsel has a mandate to eliminate any formulation that could ground a claim, set a precedent, or be read as an admission; the Office of Civic Reconciliation has a mandate to ensure the text is actually received as an apology by the people it is addressed to. The two offices should contradict each other directly and in specific places, and the anonymous drafter should try, with visibly diminishing success, to satisfy both.

The humor has to come from the conceptual machinery of apology, not from slapstick, anachronism, or absurd-sounding nouns. Without ever naming a philosophical problem as such, the draft should be worked over on the following pressure points: who the apologizing agent is, given that the current ministers did nothing and the state that did it no longer exists under that name; whether the drafter's "we" quietly includes the annotators' own forebears; the recipient problem, since the assembly that could have accepted was dissolved and two descendant organizations now dispute which of them speaks for the valley; the steady downgrading of acknowledgment as counsel substitutes softer verbs and nouns for harder ones; the requirement that an apology cost the apologizer something, which the reconciliation office keeps pressing and counsel keeps noting has not been funded; the commitment to non-repetition, which is awkward because the diversion infrastructure still supplies the capital and there is a live proposal to raise the weir; whether the apology can be entrenched against a future government that might withdraw it, and counsel's compromise retraction clause; and the question of what the anniversary ceremony is for if the substantive bill has already failed.

Close with a section headed "As adopted": the text that survives both offices, four or five sentences long, in the same flat register, which a reader should recognize as having shed some condition that made it an apology at all. The piece must never say this outright.

Keep the tone deadpan throughout, with no winking at the reader and no authorial aside. Both offices should be competent and their concerns legitimate within their own mandates, so that the satire falls on the practice of drafting rather than on a strawman. Use no real countries, no real historical atrocities, and no real living persons, and include no citations, footnotes, or references to scholarship; the piece must stand on its own.
