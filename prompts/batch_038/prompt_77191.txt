I am the second reader for the midterm in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on archaeological inference — specifically, how far one can get from excavated objects to claims about belief, ritual, and political institutions. I have not yet formed a view of this script and want an independent assessment before marks are standardised across readers.

**The question as set (25 marks, 900 words):**

> Site Q is a low mound in a dry-farming zone, excavated over three seasons. The published report records: (i) a rectangular mudbrick building of about 120 m² at the highest point of the mound, thick-walled, with a continuous bench around its interior, and with no hearths, ovens, or food-preparation debris on any of its three successive floors; (ii) a pit immediately outside its doorway containing more than 400 cattle and sheep mandibles, all from animals that died at roughly two to four years of age; (iii) twenty-two clay sealings recovered from a later levelling fill inside the building, every one bearing the same geometric impression, nine of them carrying on the reverse the negative impression of a twisted cord and of a door-post; (iv) the building was rebuilt twice on exactly the same footprint and orientation; (v) there are no human burials anywhere inside it; (vi) the surrounding settlement consists of some thirty houses of comparable plan and floor area, and its few burials show no consistent differences in grave goods.
>
> What can be inferred about the ritual and social organisation of this community, and with what confidence? Distinguish what the evidence licenses from what it merely permits, and state what further evidence would most change your conclusions.

**The marking scheme I have been given:** 8 marks for handling of depositional context and site-formation processes; 8 marks for the inference chain from finds to institutions, including the licensed/permitted distinction; 5 marks for the treatment of absences of evidence; 4 marks for the final section on what evidence would change the conclusions. Bands: A = 21–25, B = 16–20, C = 11–15, D = 6–10, F = 0–5.

**The candidate's answer, with paragraph numbers as written:**

> 1. The evidence permits a reasonably specific reconstruction: a special-purpose building used for communal ritual meals, controlled by an office that also held surplus, in a community with little visible differentiation of wealth. My confidence differs across these elements and I will say where.
>
> 2. The building is not a house. Its plan resembles those of the ordinary houses at Q, so what distinguishes it is not its form but what is missing from it: no hearths, no ovens, no food-preparation debris. That absence appears on all three successive floors, and this matters. Routine domestic life in a dry-farming village generates processing debris that is trampled into floor surfaces; when the same absence recurs three times in a building that was deliberately rebuilt on the same footprint, we are not dealing with an argument from silence but with a positive property of the deposits. Cooking did not take place here, and the debris of routine meals was not deposited here.
>
> 3. Since the building was not domestic, and since a deposit of animal jawbones was placed at its doorway, the most economical reading is that it was a temple: a building set apart for dealings with powers rather than for daily life. The absence of burials inside it removes the main alternative, a mortuary shrine. What the three rebuilds on the identical footprint license is continuity of use across generations; that one family or line held custody of the building is only permitted by this evidence, not required by it.
>
> 4. The pit tells us about the meals. The age profile is decisive on one point: these animals died at two to four years, prime meat age, not the age at which one disposes of diseased or spent stock. Mandibles alone, more than four hundred of them, do not look like ordinary butchery waste either, which would include vertebrae, ribs, and long-bone shafts in something like natural proportion. So the pit is the residue of consumption, and since it was filled as a single deposit at the doorway, it records a single occasion. Four hundred prime cattle and sheep is far more meat than any one household, or a dozen, could consume before it spoiled; the feast must have drawn in the whole settlement and probably people from beyond it. This is therefore one of the rare cases in which we recover a dated ritual event rather than a general practice.
>
> 5. For the meaning of the jawbones I rely on an ethnographic account I have read in which cattle jawbones were deposited after communal feasts by pastoralists in East Africa. Two methodological points make the comparison more than decorative. First, the direct historical approach allows the ethnographic case to be used here, because both it and Q concern herding peoples in a dry environment, so the relevant conditions of life are shared. Second, middle-range theory exists precisely to convert analogies of this kind into reliable inferences: once a general link between an activity and its material result has been established, its application to Q is not merely suggestive but demonstrative. I therefore treat the ritual reading of the pit as established rather than conjectural.
>
> 6. The sealings give us the institution. All twenty-two bear the same impression, and nine carry cord and door-post negatives on the reverse. A sealing with a cord impression is made when a door or container is fastened and the closure sealed; a single impression across all of them means one seal, hence one seal-holder, hence one person with authority to open and close the storeroom. Control of stored surplus by an office is redistribution, and redistribution is what separates a chiefdom from a village of equal households. Q was therefore a small chiefdom, with the temple at its centre.
>
> 7. There is an obvious objection. Item (vi) says the houses are of comparable size and the graves show no consistent differences in goods, and I agree this counts against any strong claim of wealth inequality. But the objection does not touch the argument. Redistribution is administered by an office, and an office need not enrich its holder: the surplus sits in the temple's storeroom, not in the chief's house. Wealth-based ranking and office-based control of surplus come apart, and it is the second that the sealings document. The absence of differentiation in houses and graves is therefore compatible with a chiefdom, indeed expected under one, and the sealings settle the matter.
>
> 8. Confidence: high for the identification of the building as a temple; moderate for the reconstruction of the feast, since a single event is harder to pin down than a practice; lowest for the political reconstruction, which rests on twenty-two objects. What would change my mind: domestic debris on any of the floors would remove the basis for the temple identification; a second and different seal impression in the same fill would remove the single-office inference; a stratified sequence inside the pit, showing separate fills, would turn the feast from an event into a practice. Nothing in the report so far suggests any of these.
