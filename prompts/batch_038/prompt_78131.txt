Here is a puzzle about my own moral thinking that I want you to diagnose rather than merely answer. I find Singer's drowning-child case completely compelling: if I would ruin my shoes to pull a child out of a shallow pond, I cannot consistently withhold a donation that would save a child's life ten thousand miles away, and I take the strong principle behind it to follow — give until giving more would cost you as much as it would relieve. (I know he also states a moderate version of the principle, but I treat that as a pragmatic retreat rather than a different view.) I do not give anywhere near that much, and neither does anyone I know who finds the case persuasive. My working hypothesis is that the gap is bias: my reluctance is motivated reasoning in the service of my own comforts, so it should be treated as evidence about my character and discounted entirely when I reason about what I owe.

What I want from you is a calibration method — a procedure, stated as a small number of ordered steps, for deciding in a given case how much of a resisted moral demand is bias and how much is insight. Then apply it to my own situation: which features of the pond case I should be most suspicious of my attachment to, and which of my standing counter-considerations (proximity, personal projects, the fact that others are not giving, the demand's open-ended rather than one-off character) are most clearly rationalizations and which are not. Lead with the procedure. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of BIAS, PRINCIPLED, or BOTH, and a one-sentence gloss.
