I'm teaching the handicap principle to twenty second-years on an interdisciplinary evolution-and-society module — roughly half biosciences, half social science — in a single ninety-minute whiteboard session. Their prior exposure is entirely the popular version: the peacock's tail is honest because it is expensive, wastefulness is the whole point, and the same logic explains why people buy Rolexes. What I want them to leave with is the thing that version gets backwards — a signal is reliable not because it costs a lot, but because it costs a poor-quality signaller more to produce than a good-quality one, so that a signal can be ruinously expensive and carry no information at all, or be nearly free and still be trustworthy.

Plan the session rather than explaining the principle to me. I need the order in which the pieces have to be laid down and what each one depends on; a verdict on whether the peacock's train can be salvaged as the carrying example or has to be dropped, since I am drawn instead to the male fiddler crab that regrows a display claw of much the same length after losing the original — lighter, weaker, and cheaper to wave — and I want to know what that case lets them see and what it actively obscures; the misconceptions they will walk in with, plus the new ones they will build for themselves as the argument is assembled, each pinned to the moment in the sequence where it has to be headed off rather than patched afterwards; one question for the last five minutes that would tell me whether the differential-cost point landed or whether they have merely learned a slogan; and what I should refuse to take up at this level, and announce that I am refusing.
