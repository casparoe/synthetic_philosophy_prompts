WORKING TITLE: "You Are Not One Thing"

Open on a petri dish. The slime mold Physarum polycephalum creeps across agar as a lacy yellow sheet. It navigates mazes, allocates its own resources, and anticipates a cold snap it has been trained to expect. Cut it in two and each half grows back into a complete, functioning slime mold. Biologists call it an organism, but under the microscope it is one vast cell studded with many nuclei, with no clean inside and outside. It is unclear what the "it" is.

That is a biological puzzle, but it is also an old metaphysical one, and metaphysicians have a name for it. They distinguish the general composition question — when do some things compose a further thing? — from the special composition question, which asks what composition itself consists in. The first is a request for a list of cases; the second is a request for a definition. Most of the interesting arguments are about the second.

The most famous answer to the first is Peter van Inwagen's, in Material Beings (1990). Some things compose a further thing, he says, only when those things are physically bonded to one another and jointly maintain themselves against their environment. That is why, on his account, a chair does exist after all: it is a swarm of particles bonded into a self-maintaining arrangement. What does not exist, he argues, is anything arbitrary — no mereological sums of a chair in Ohio and the Eiffel Tower.

The picture is four-dimensionalist. An organism is a spacetime worm, a long thin object stretched through time, and its persistence through the total replacement of its matter is just a matter of how its temporal parts hang together. Your hand, on this view, is one of the many objects you are made of, and so is your body.

At the extremes sit mereological nihilism, which denies that anything at all exists — a position with no serious defenders outside thought experiments — and mereological universalism, which says that any things whatever compose something, so that there really is an object that is your left shoe plus the Moon.

Between them lie the puzzles. Peter Unger's problem of the many points out that for any candidate cloud, cat, or person, there are countless overlapping aggregates that match the description equally well. Metaphysicians mostly accept the resulting fuzziness and move on: ordinary objects have vague borders, and that is simply how things are. And because vagueness is acceptable, a restricted answer like van Inwagen's is perfectly safe from the objection that no principled line can be drawn.

A different move, associated with David Lewis, is the slogan that composition is identity: the many just are the one. This is sometimes offered as the answer to the special composition question, since if the parts are identical to the whole, then wherever there are many things there is one thing they compose.

Now bring in the biology. Evolutionary theory has, in effect, already answered the metaphysician's question. The criterion is a reproductive bottleneck plus a division of labor between a germ line and a soma: when a group of organisms has those, it is one individual and selection acts on it as a unit; when it does not, it is many. Van Inwagen's talk of a self-maintaining bonded whole is just a philosopher's name for that criterion, which is why his account counts an ant supercolony as a single organism and denies that a swarm of bees is one — a swarm has no germ-soma split.

That has consequences for the technology section. A modern AI system runs across thousands of processors in different buildings, with no bottleneck, no germ line, and no metabolism. On van Inwagen's criterion it is a swarm, not a life, and therefore it cannot be one thing — which is why, strictly speaking, it cannot be one mind either. Whatever such a system does, it is many things doing it.
