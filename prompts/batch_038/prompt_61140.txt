I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the last several weeks on both sides of the argument over procreative beneficence — the claim that when prospective parents can choose among the children they might have, they should choose the one expected to have the best life. My editor is not convinced that a four-thousand-word reported piece can carry that argument on its own, and he has suggested the piece open with something a reader can feel rather than parse. So I would like to commission a short work of fiction dressed as a real magazine feature: one complete issue of an advice column that appears in the in-house publication of a large private fertility group which has adopted the principle as official guidance while insisting, with perfect sincerity, that nothing about it is compulsory.

The column can be called Ask Dr. Wren; write about 650 words, three letters and three answers, in the warm, unhurried, faintly condescending register of a veteran agony aunt who has heard everything and is offended by nothing. The first letter is from a mother who was shown a ranking of her three viable embryos at her selection appointment and chose the second-ranked one because she felt a pull toward it; she wants to know whether she has wronged her son. Dr. Wren must explain that she has wronged no one at all — the son she has would not otherwise have existed, and the embryo she passed over is not a person who has been deprived of anything — while also explaining, without flinching and with genuine kindness, that the choice was nevertheless the wrong one. That gap between no victim and a real wrong is the joke, and it should be delivered straight. The second letter is from a father asking whether, since he and his wife got it wrong the first time, they should simply have another child and get it right, and whether the second can be ranked so as to make up for the first; Dr. Wren should treat this as a sensible question with a sensible answer, noting only in passing that a better addition does not undo anything, though it does improve the total. The third is from a woman who declined the ranking entirely and wants reassurance that not choosing is also a choice; Dr. Wren agrees warmly, and then hands her the responsibility anyway.

A few things I want handled correctly, because bioethicists read my column and write in. The obligation is impersonal and comparative rather than harm-based, and the column should be explicit that this is why it feels so strange. The clinic's ranking instrument should be candidly described as an expected-value calculation over how well a life goes, built on polygenic scores whose effect sizes are minuscule, whose variants push in opposite directions depending on the trait, and whose confidence intervals swallow most of the differences being ranked — with the guidance saying best regardless. The distinction the clinic draws between screening against disadvantage and selecting for advantage should appear, and should visibly dissolve in practice. Include one nod to the consent objection — the worry that a person whose genetic makeup was chosen by someone else stands in a different relation to their own life than the rest of us do — and let Dr. Wren handle it as a lovely sentiment that changes nothing, since no alternative arrangement was ever on offer either. And keep the liberal reflex audible throughout: nothing is mandated, everything remains your free choice, and therefore none of this is eugenics. Stay entirely in voice, with no winking, no author's note, and no paragraph that turns around to tell the reader what to think; name no real philosopher and cite nothing. The target is the doctrine and the institution's manner, never disabled people and never the parents, who should come across as ordinary, sympathetic, and entirely reasonable throughout — people asking ordinary questions and receiving answers that are technically correct and quietly monstrous.
