A friend of mine and I argued for most of last night about Newton's bucket, and we finished the evening in precisely the place we started it, so I have decided to send her a poem rather than a seventh message. She is a relativist by training and a relationist by temperament: her position is that the water climbing the sides of the pail is a fact about the water and the distant matter of the universe and nothing further, and that anyone who posits something further is paying for structure that can never be used. Mine is the older instinct — that the concavity is there in the dark, that it does not require witnesses, that the surface keeps its shape even when the vessel has stopped mattering. I want the poem to put her case at full strength and mine at full strength and then to decline to arbitrate, because that is honestly where we ended.

Form: a sestina, in the voice of the water. Use exactly these six end-words, and use them in this order in the first sestet: apart, curve, cord, turn, same, space. Thereafter follow the standard rotation — the previous stanza's last end-word becomes the next stanza's first, and you continue working backward through that stanza's order, outside in — which, for these six words, comes out as:

apart curve cord turn same space / space apart same curve turn cord / cord space turn apart curve same / same cord curve space apart turn / turn same apart cord space curve / curve turn space same cord apart

Close with a three-line envoi containing all six words, two per line, one inside the line and one at the end. Unrhymed, and roughly pentameter if it comes naturally, but accuracy of thought outranks the metre here.

Content requirements — please treat these as checks rather than suggestions:

1. The first three sestets must run Newton's own experiment correctly. The vessel is hung by a twisted cord and released; it spins while the water lags behind it, so that the relative motion between water and pail is at its greatest and the surface stays flat; then the water is gradually carried along until it no longer moves against the vessel — and it is precisely then, when there is nothing left to be relative to, that the surface climbs and goes concave. One stanza must turn on that inversion, and the reader should feel the trap it springs.

2. Let the cord do double duty as Newton's other device: two globes tied together with nothing else in the universe, where the tension in the cord is the whole of the evidence.

3. One of the later sestets should be a rearrangement in substance of an earlier one — the same bodies, the same distances, the same relations among them, the words merely reordered — so that the reader is made to feel the question the form keeps asking: is a rearranged arrangement a new world, or the same world wearing a different chart? This is where the argument we were actually having should live. Do not name it, and do not reach for the specialist vocabulary of the debate; the rotation is the argument.

4. The end-word "same" must earn its place in the envoi. If rearranging never makes a difference, then the "space" of the first stanza has bought nothing; if it does make a difference, then the future is underdetermined by everything that could ever be measured. End on the curve, with the words still turning and no stated reason for them to stop.

5. Proper names: at most one appearance each for Newton and Mach, and only where a name carries weight the line could not carry alone. Everything else should be legible to a reader who has never heard of the bucket, and recognizable step by step to someone who has taught it.

Please give me the poem first, with nothing before it, and then a single section titled "Notes on the permutation," about 250 words, in which you say what each sestet is doing in the argument, which rotation carries the rearrangement puzzle, which of the six words you treated as the invariant that the others turn around, and what in the debate the form resisted or could not hold. No other sections, no reading list, no bibliography.
