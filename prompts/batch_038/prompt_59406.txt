You're going to be a forecasting methodologist with a philosophy-of-science background — someone who's thought hard about base rates, reference classes, and the inside/outside view — but I want you to treat this as a live puzzle, not a lecture. I already know the standard story. The standard story doesn't fit my own head, and I want you to think carefully about why.

Context, so you know where this is coming from: six months ago I quit a salaried job to start a small business with two friends, I got weirdly addicted to a prediction tournament site around the same time, my mom got a diagnosis in the spring, and my city is about to break ground on a light rail line that I've been loudly complaining about. So I've been living inside this question from four directions at once.

Here are my verdicts. I'm confident about some of them and at least two are obviously in tension and I can't tell which to give up.

1. The rail line. The people running it have a detailed bottom-up cost estimate. I think that estimate should be close to ignored in favor of the base rate for projects like it — most come in way over and way behind. Outside view wins, no contest. When I said this at a council meeting someone called me a fatalist and I didn't think that was a real objection.
2. My business. Base rate for new small businesses: a lot of them are gone within five years. I think that's the right starting point for me too, and that my reasons for feeling different (two paying customers, twelve years in the industry) license an adjustment, not an exemption. I also think I'm probably rationalizing, so the adjustment should be smaller than it feels like it should be.
3. My mom. Her oncologist said something to the effect of "you're not a statistic, we treat the patient in front of us." I think the numbers DO apply to her, that the line is comfort and not argument, and that ignoring them would be a mistake.
4. My marriage. If you asked me for a number based on the divorce base rate I'd say that's insulting and also just wrong — that rate doesn't apply to us. I have no good account of why this differs from 3. I know how bad that sounds.
5. Empires. I don't think base rates work here. There have been a handful of great powers, they aren't interchangeable, and "the average empire lasts N years" isn't measuring anything, it's picking a number. And yet I do think the story of Roman overextension tells me something real about my own country over the next twenty years. Not a probability. Something.
6. My cousin, first in her family to apply to college. I think it would be wrong — wrong epistemically, not just unkind — to tell her the rate at which kids from her high school finish a degree. Because if she takes the number seriously it helps make the number true, and if she ignores it the number stops being about her. It's Lucas-critique shaped, I think: the forecast changes what's forecast. I don't think this is a special plea. I think it names a feature some base rates have and others don't.
7. Things with no class at all, like a date for some technology milestone. I claim I wouldn't forecast without a reference class. In practice I'll happily give a confident number. I think that number is unjustified and I should either find a stretched analogy or say I don't know.

My tentative principle: take the outside view when (a) there's a class with enough members that the rate means something, (b) the members are similiar enough to be exchangeable with my case, (c) my private information isn't stuff the base rate already reflects — plus (d) don't take the outside view when acting on the forecast would change the forecast.
