I put together a small graduate-student zine for our department's open house, and this spring we want to run a send-up of the theory of descriptions in the form of an advice column — think of an agony aunt who happens to be an unrepentant Russellian and who will not offer a single correspondent any counsel until their referring expressions have been parsed. Please write it as a complete column of roughly 900 to 1,100 words: a two- or three-sentence masthead blurb establishing the columnist's credentials, her standing policy on letters, and the fact that she has been answering correspondence for some years in a provincial paper; then five or six letters from readers together with her replies; then a sign-off. Each letter should read as ordinary, slightly sad domestic correspondence — a flatmate, an estranged brother, an ex, a neighbour's dog, a workplace grievance — in about 60 to 90 words, written by a person who is not stupid and who has a real problem. The comedy must come exclusively from the analysis she brings to bear, never from the correspondent being foolish, and the column should feel like something clipped from a regional newspaper rather than a philosophy blog.

Here is what I want the column to demonstrate rather than explain. At least one letter should involve a description that denotes nothing at all, and the columnist should get the verdict right: the correspondent's claim is false, not meaningless or truth-valueless, and she should identify which conjunct fails and insist that this is a virtue of her analysis rather than a scandal, to the correspondent's bewilderment. One letter should turn on scope — something in the neighbourhood of a reader reporting that the person she is seeing might not be the person she is seeing, or that the winner of the raffle might not have won — and the columnist should separate the two readings cleanly and give genuinely different practical advice for each, which should strike the reader as both rigorous and insane. One letter should exercise the uniqueness clause: plural descriptions, or a correspondent who turns out to be one of two people who jointly satisfy the description, and she should handle it with whatever machinery a Russellian needs. One letter should be a straightforward referential use, where the reader is plainly picking out a particular individual — the man who poisoned her aunt's cat, say — and the columnist insists on the attributive reading and pronounces on the wrong person; I want that reply to be mistaken in a way that anyone who knows the literature spots immediately, while remaining perfectly coherent from inside her commitments, and I want the correspondent's one-line rebuttal printed at the foot of the column without any comment from the editor. The final letter should be about a name: a reader who has discovered that the man her family has been calling by a certain name is not the person everyone's descriptions of him pick out. Here she should fall back on names being abbreviated descriptions, notice that this fails to preserve her advice, and then retreat to the only expressions she thinks are genuine names — the ones that can be used truly only by her, here, at the moment of writing — so that the column ends with her cheerfully explaining that she can answer questions about nothing but what is immediately present to her, and that this is a satisfactory result.

A few constraints. Deadpan throughout, no winking, no anachronistic internet humour, and no character who is a philosopher by name or a lightly disguised portrait of one: the columnist should be your own invention with a name, a town, and a reputation for being impossible. Do not put any real text in quotation marks, and do not supply footnotes, dates, a bibliography, or a list of further reading — the piece has to stand on its own for a reader who has never met the distinction it is satirizing, and everything technical must be carried by the letters rather than by apparatus. Most importantly, make the satire fair: a specialist reading it should be able to say that every move she makes is one her theory actually licenses, that the objections the correspondents raise are the real ones, and that the piece gets funnier the more correct it is. Where the theory is genuinely strong — negative existentials, the informativeness or un-informativeness of identities, sentences that seem to be about no one — let her score the point and let the reader feel the force of it before the costs accumulate.
