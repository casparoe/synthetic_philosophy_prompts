Been chewing on this and I want a tight answer, not an essay.

The Dutch book arguement — the Ramsey/de Finetti line that if your degrees of belief dont obey the probability axioms, some clever bookie can sell you a package of bets that loses you money however the world turns out — is supposed to show that incoherence is pragmatically self-defeating. I keep thinking it has a real-world instance in the Knight Capital meltdown, and I want you to tell me if I'm forcing it.

Facts I'm working from: on August 1, 2012 Knight pushed new market-making software to eight servers and one of them never got the update, so a retired flag still sitting on that box got read by the new code as an instruction to start trading. It fired off roughly four million executions across 154 stocks in about 45 minutes, took a pre-tax loss around $440M, lost about three quarters of its equity value in a day, scraped together a rescue from outside investors within the week, and agreed to be bought by Getco a few months later. The counterparties on the other end of those orders mostly did fine. Regulators later sanctioned the firm for having no pre-trade risk controls.

So run the Dutch book machinery over it. Who is the bettor, and what are the bets? My worry is that a garbage price isn't an axiom violation at all — only a crossed market is the trading analogue of incoherence, and those get arbitraged away in microseconds — so I want you to say plainly which part of the theorem is actually doing work here, if any. Handle the no-retracting assumption too: the theorem assumes an agent stands behind the odds its credences imply, but Knight's machine had no credences, was retracting nothing, and nobody offered it a package — they just took whatever was on the screen. And keep coherence and accuracy separate; Knight could have been perfectly coherent and still lost everything.

Answer as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion, and between them they've got to cover the mapping, the axiom question, the retractability business, the coherence-vs-accuracy distinction, and a bottom line on whether this episode confirms the argument, embarrasses it, or is a case the argument never anticipated.
