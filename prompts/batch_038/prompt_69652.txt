Right, so I'm about three-quarters of the way through my third novel and I've got myself into a hole that I'm increasingly sure is philosphical rather than structural. It's set in a town like the one I grew up in, somewhere between Doncaster and the coast, and there are two strands: Nessa, a housing options officer who spends her days reading other people's files, and Priya, the girlfriend of Nessa's teenage son, who ends up in a forty-second video that goes round the whole county in about ninety minutes. Neither of them is a victim in the tidy way the plot needs, and that's the trouble — I keep writing scenes where they say they've been violated and then can't say why, and I've started to worry that I'm only leaning on the word privacy because it sounds weighty in dialogue.

So before I draft part two I want the specification rather than the arguments. Treat it as though I'm commissioning a theory of why privacy is worth anything at all, adequate to the purpose of letting a character of mine speak from it without sounding like she's read a paper: what does such a theory have to deliver? Give me the requirements list — the phenomena it has to explain (why the watcher nobody ever catches still seems to do something to the person watched; why being described accurately can sting worse than being lied about; why I don't mind my GP knowing things I'd never tell my sister; why I've nothing to hide reads as a mistake rather than a defence), the cases it has to come out right on, the distinctions it mustn't run together (privacy against secrecy, confidentiality, anonymity, solitude; the right to privacy against the value of it; controlling access against restricting access against getting the norms of a situation right), and the facts it can't contradict — including that people say one thing and do another with their data, and that some invasions really do protect people, safeguarding being the obvious one. For each requirement, give me the case or consideration behind it in a sentence or two, and mark it non-negotiable or else a pet preference of a particular camp. I want you to be blunt about which of the things demanded here are genuine desiderata and which are just an anti-reductionist reflex: is it actually a requirement that a theory show privacy is good in itself rather than borrowed from property, bodily security or autonomy, or is that a thumb on the scale? Same question for the demand that a theory condemn all observation the subject hasn't agreed to. Then tell me whether some subset of the requirements simply can't be met together — I'd love an impossible triangle, honestly, because that's a novel — and which single requirement knocks out the most candidates.

Some seeding, since these are the scenes I've already got and they're where I suspect the theory will either hold or break:

- Nessa has entirely lawful access to a tenant's whole history, including her refuge addresses, reads it all, is kind about it, leaks nothing, and the reader is still meant to feel something has been done to that woman.
- Priya's video is shot at a bus shelter, contains nothing illegal and nothing anyone at school didn't already know about her, and ruins her anyway.
- A widow finds a second phone in her late husband's jacket and doesn't unlock it; I want the not-looking to matter and the looking to be something other than a betrayal of him.
- A councillor who's pledged total transparency livestreams his own life and publishes his inbox and loves it, then gets an early dementia diagnosis, and his recordings become a record his family can't get out from under — consent given by an earlier self about a later one who can't give it.

After the requirements, briefly and no more than a paragraph each, how do the main contenders do against the list: the control account, the restricted-access one, the one built on context-relative norms, the one that ties privacy to intimacy and friendship, and the reductionists who think there's no such thing over and above other interests. Flag which of my four scenes is pulling the most weight and which one I could probably cut without losing a requirement. No reading list, no citations — I'll find my own — and don't quote at me; I just want criteria sharp enough that I can hold a scene up against them and see if it stands.
