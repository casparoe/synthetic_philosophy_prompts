I'm writing a paper with four cases in it — Mytilene, Sicily, Arginusae (the one where they tried all the generals in a single vote and Socrates refused to put the motion from the presiding board), and Socrates himself — arguing that Athenian democracy failed in a specific way, not just that it lost a war. A guy in my workshop pushed back with the following, and I have been unable to answer him for a week.

(1) A decision is legitimate just in case it was made by the procedure the polity authorizes. There is nothing else for legitimacy to be.
(2) In Athens the demos was sovereign. No council, court, or body of law stood above it; it could decree, reverse, and decree again. So whatever the assembly or the people's courts decided was, automatically, an authorized decision.
(3) Socrates' conviction was one of those. Jury of five hundred-odd drawn by lot, a public charge of impiety, a penalty phase in which he got to propose his own counter-penalty (and proposed free meals for life instead). And in the Crito he concedes the frame: persuade the city or obey it, he agreed to the laws by staying, and emigration was open to him the whole seventy years.
(4) So executing Socrates was legitimate.
(5) Same for Mytilene. The decree to kill every adult male was authorized, and so was the reversal the next day and the second trireme. And notice that the man who talked them out of it didn't argue it was unjust — he argued it was inexpedient, that it would make every future rebel city fight to the last man. If the reversal wasn't correcting an illegitimacy, there was no illegitimacy to correct.
(6) Same for Sicily: authorized decision to sail, authorized change of heart afterwards when the demos turned on the speakers and the diviners who'd pushed it. No illegitimacy at either end.
(7) Therefore nothing the Athenian demos did was illegitimate, and my four cases are failures of luck and of available information at best — not failures of the democracy. Which leaves me choosing: either Athens' failures aren't its democracy's failures, or legitimacy is something a democracy can lose precisely by being one, and I cant see how to say that.

Every step looks true to me and the conclusion is one I can't accept. So: name the one step you'd reject — one, not a shrug at the whole chain — and tell me why it looked so solid to both of us. Then price it. What do I lose in the Socrates chapter, and do I get to keep Mytilene and Sicily or do they go too? I'd rather not touch (1), because if legitimacy isn't procedural I don't know what makes any government legitimate at all, and I'd rather keep (3) since the Crito is load-bearing for me. If the honest answer is that one of those two has to go anyway, say which and why.

400 words max, please. Lead with the step you're rejecting in one sentence, then the diagnosis and the costs.
