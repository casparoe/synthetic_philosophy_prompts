**The question (25 points, 50 minutes, closed book):**

*3(a), 8 points.* An evil demon announces that he will inflict horrific suffering on someone you love unless you admire him. You find him despicable. Explain why this case is taken to generate a problem for the fitting-attitude analysis of value, and why the problem is thought to be sharper for the buck-passing version of that analysis in particular.

*3(b), 10 points.* Set out two different responses that have been offered to this problem. For each, say what it claims about the demon's threat.

*3(c), 7 points.* Say which response you find more convincing and why. Make explicit what you take a successful response to have to show, and say whether you think the problem generalizes to other attitudes.

**The student's essay:**

The fitting-attitude analysis, FA for short, says that what it is for something to be admirable is for admiration of it to be fitting, and in the buck-passing version associated with Scanlon, to call something good is not to attribute a substantive property to it but to say that there are reasons to respond to it in favourable ways. Goodness passes the buck to reasons. The demon case is meant to break this. The demon's threat gives me an extremely strong reason to admire him, since if I don't, someone I love is tortured. But there is nothing admirable about the demon. So we have a case in which there is overwhelming reason to take up an attitude toward an object while the object lacks the very property the analysis says consists in there being such a reason. The case comes from Parfit, and Scanlon discusses something like it when he sets up the buck-passing account.

The problem gets its name from the fact that FA cannot distinguish right-kind reasons, which bear on whether the attitude fits its object, from wrong-kind reasons, which bear only on whether it would be good or prudent to have the attitude. Parfit's distinction between object-given and state-given reasons is meant to mark exactly this difference, and the WKR problem is the problem of marking it without simply restating the analysis.

This strikes me as a fatal objection rather than an awkward one. The analysis says: admirable = such that there is reason to admire. But the demon case shows something stronger, which is that what one has most reason to feel and what actually fits the object can come apart, and FA is supposed to be the view that these are the same thing. More generally it shows the flaw in any analysis of an evaluative property in terms of reasons, because reasons are responsive to the whole situation, threats included, whereas admirability is responsive only to the object. So FA's one available move is to deny that the demon's threat is really a reason to admire him. That is the move both of the responses I want to consider make.

The first is Scanlon's own. His formulation is careful: he speaks of reasons to favour, not of reasons to want or to bring about. The demon gives me a reason to want to admire him, or a reason to get myself to admire him, but not a reason to favour him, since favouring is a matter of being moved by features of the object. On this line the alleged counterexample never gets going, because the reason the demon supplies was never a reason of the kind the analysans mentions. I think the objection to this is correct, though. Unless we already have an independent grip on what favouring is, saying that wrong-kind reasons are not reasons to favour is just another way of saying they are not the right kind of reason, which was the thing that needed explaining. The response risks restating the distinction it was supposed to ground.

The second is Hieronymi's. On her account reasons always bear on questions, and a reason for an attitude has to bear on the question that the attitude itself is a way of answering — for admiration, roughly, is this person admirable. The demon's threat bears on a different question entirely, namely whether it would be good for me to come to admire him, and so it is not in the running as a reason for the attitude. As far as I can tell this is the same response as the first one with a general theory of reasons sitting behind it: both amount to saying that so-called wrong-kind reasons are not really reasons for the attitude at all. I find it more satisfying than Scanlon's version only because it says why, and so is less obviously circular, but it inherits the same burden, which is to characterise the question an attitude answers without helping itself to fittingness.

A third consideration seems to me to settle the matter, and to settle it against FA. In the case of belief the point is not even arguable. Someone offered a million dollars to believe they can fly cannot thereby come to believe it, and Shah and Velleman's argument about doxastic deliberation is that this shows belief is governed by truth alone. Admiration and desire are like belief in that they are attitudes which present their objects as being a certain way, so the same must hold for them, and the WKR problem turns out to be a merely verbal problem produced by sloppily describing reasons to bring about an attitude as though they were reasons to have it. But notice what this costs. The denial FA needs is available only if you buy a substantive and controversial thesis about the nature of the attitudes, which is not itself an analysis of any evaluative property. So FA either collapses or becomes hostage to a theory of attitudes, and neither is what its defenders were selling.

The problem also generalises. The same trouble arises for fear, where a threat can give me reason to fear something that is in no way fearsome, and for blame, where an incentive could be offered for blaming someone who has done nothing blameworthy. Any FA analysis of a response-dependent evaluative property will face the same choice.
