ok so slightly weird request — I need help writing a speech and I've been staring at a blank doc for two days.

I'm a third-year philosophy major, and for the last two summers I've crewed on the Petrel, a 42-foot wooden research schooner our university's marine station has owned since 1965. She's being decommissioned in the spring. There's a ceremony on the dock Saturday: about eighty people, the boatyard crew who've kept her going, marine bio faculty and grad students, alumni donors, the dean, and Captain Reyes's whole family. Reyes has skippered her for 41 years and is retiring the same day. Somebody found out I study ideas for a living and I got volunteered for the student remarks. Four minutes, spoken out loud, so the rhythm matters.

Here's the part that's eating me. Almost none of the wood is original. The yard's rule was you only replace a plank when it fails, never preemptively, and the shipwright signs and dates the inside face of every plank he hangs — so her interior is a running log of repairs going back decades. Meanwhile the shed where the original lines were lofted full size burned down in 1998, which means the boat is now the only surviving record of her own shape. And an alum wants to 3D-scan her before she's hauled out and build an exact replica. That proposal is getting announced at the ceremony and half the room is already emotional about it one way or the other.

I've spent the whole term on the replace-it-one-neuron-at-a-time arguments and I can feel the trap closing. The easy moves are (a) she's the same boat because she's the same shape — but the source of the shape is gone and the replica would have the shape better than she does now, and (b) she lives on in everyone who sailed her — which is exactly the sort of line Reyes has said out loud that he hates, and which is also just a different claim wearing her clothes. Third problem, and this is the one I can't tell anyone about: she was holed in a grounding in 1994 and the yard swapped the keel and never told Reyes. Everybody in the room believes the keel is original oak from 1965. I've decided it stays out of my speech, I'm not going to be the guy who says it — but I don't want to give a speech that turns into a lie the day somebody else does. So nothing in it can depend on there being any original material left, anywhere.

What I want is the speech itself. Plain dockyard English, zero philosophy vocabulary — nobody should be able to tell it's secretly an identity paper, and if the two people in the audience who are in my seminar catch the structure, that's a bonus for them and not the point. It has to be warm toward Reyes without being sentimental. There's a gift in it: he's got a titanium hip, a pacemaker, one new knee and both lenses replaced, and he's been making the joke for years that at least the yard signs its work on him. I'd like that to land as a real moment and not a lecture, ideally with him as the one who laughed first. And it should leave the room able to think clearly about the replica without telling them what to decide — the guy offering to pay for it is sitting in the second row.

Start with the first line of the speech. No preface, no title, no notes afterward about what you were going for. If you want a pause, leave a blank line. Around 550 words.
