Compose a flat-pack assembly manual — the brisk, numbered, faintly corporate booklet that arrives in the box with a wardrobe — for a product called the Universal Hinge Set. The customer is fitting the hinges on which the doors of their picture of the world turn. Around 650 words, with a tools-required note, a numbered parts list, numbered steps, a troubleshooting table, and a warranty page. British English throughout, including spellings.

The humour should come from the philosophy being right rather than from puns about doors, so please build in the following:

- The parts list ranks hinges by load and makes plain that a hinge is not part of the door: it is not discovered, not fitted on the strength of evidence, and not inspectable while the door is hanging on it.
- One step conveys that the customer cannot test whether a hinge is correctly seated by operating the door it holds, since any such test presupposes other hinges — which the manual instructs them not to test at the same time.
- The troubleshooting table covers a hinge that has worked loose in somebody else's house, two incompatible fitting diagrams both of which yield doors that open, and the customer who proposes to remove every hinge at once and reassemble from nothing.
- The warranty page commits to the view that fitting a hinge is not a matter of holding it to be true but of acting from it, and voids cover for anyone who tries to justify a hinge by appeal to the hinges holding it in place.
- One boxed testimonial reports how much time the customer has saved since she stopped checking whether the Earth existed before her birth.

Do not name Wittgenstein, do not cite or quote *On Certainty* or any secondary literature, and invent all the text yourself. The target is hinge epistemology's quietism — its answer to the sceptic amounting to the concession that nothing can be said on the hinges' behalf — delivered in the confident register of DIY prose. Keep it deadpan and legible: a specialist reader should judge the satire fair rather than contemptuous, should be able to see what is being ironised without being told, and should find no glossary, afterword, or explanation of the jokes.
