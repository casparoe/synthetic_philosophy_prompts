Every January I have to split the costs of a shared imaging suite between two departments — radiology and cardiology — and this year it's uglier than usual beacuse the service contract jumped and there's a shortfall of about $400k on top of the usual pot. My job is to walk into the meeting with a proposed split that both chairs can be shown is fair, and the CFO signs whatever I bring her. I get maybe an hour with finance beforehand and I have no economist on staff.

I've been reading around and landed on the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution as the thing I want: draw the line from the disagreement point to the point where each side gets the best it could possibly hope for, and take where that line hits the frontier. That's roughly my whole understanding, so please correct me where its wrong or too glib.

What I actually need is a procedure I can run in an afternoon, every year, not a lecture. So:

- what I have to have in hand before I start, and where each of those numbers comes from inside a hospital finance system (the feasible set, the two aspiration points, the disagreement point) — including which of them I can pull from a spreadsheet and which ones I have to construct out of judgment;
- the steps in order, with the arithmetic spelled out so a smart admin could redo it without me;
- a rule for what to do when the frontier isn't a nice smooth curve — lumpy staffing, a machine that only runs in whole shifts, stretches where nobody is indifferent between adjacent options;
- how to tell whether I'm allowed to just use dollars instead of utilities, and how I'd check before trusting the output;
- what to do when one chair's stated best-case number is obviously inflated, and whether the procedure rewards him for inflating it — this is the part I'm most nervous about;
- where the procedure stops and hands the question to a human because it's about values rather than facts. I want those marked explicitly, not buried.

Then run it on three cases. (1) Our actual situation: radiology's outside option is shipping cases to a private imaging center across town at a known per-scan price, cardiology's is deferring volume into the following year, shortfall $400k, pick whatever plausible numbers you need and show the work. (2) A version where cardiology lands a grant that covers part of its own scanning, so its best case improves and nothing at all changes for radiology — and tell me honestly whether the split moves, in whose favor, and whether that's going to look insane to radiology when I present it. (3) A case where the two chairs have identical numbers but very different outside options, so I can see what the disagreement point is actually doing in the machinery.

Also, someone told me the back-and-forth offer protocol people actually use in negotiation converges on the Nash solution rather than this one. If that's right, say what it means for me walking into a room where the chairs are going to counteroffer regardless, and whether there's anything I can do about it.

Finally, tell me straight what this procedure cannot do — where it'll produce a number that looks defensible but is hollow, and what a good-faith user should refuse to use it for.
