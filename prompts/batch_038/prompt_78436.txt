For the past term I have been sitting in on a colleague's graduate seminar on the ethics of killing in war. Her method is unusual: she declines to state principles at all, on the grounds that principles should be read off a sufficiently large set of cases rather than the other way round, so each week she puts a vignette to the room, takes a vote, and then announces her own verdict with a sentence or two of commentary that never quite amounts to a criterion. I am co-writing a review essay with her and want to characterise her position accurately rather than guess at it, so I have been tabulating the verdicts. All the cases are set in an invented war between an invading state and the neighbour it has invaded, and by liable I mean liable to be deliberately killed by the opposing side rather than merely liable to be killed as foreseeable collateral damage.

Here are the eight rulings so far, in the order she gave them. (1) A young man who enlisted in the invading army for the pay, three weeks into a posting at a checkpoint in an occupied town where nothing has been fired for a month, whose unit searches houses and holds men for questioning: liable. (2) A career artillery officer of the invading army who directs strikes from a bunker forty kilometres behind the front line: liable. (3) A surgeon in the invading army's uniform who treats its wounded and returns them to their units within days: not liable. (4) A civilian employed by a private firm who writes and maintains payroll software for the invader's ministry of defence and has never been within a thousand kilometres of the fighting: not liable. (5) A civilian software engineer, also ununiformed and also far from the front, who maintains the guidance packages for the invader's cruise missiles, knows precisely which strikes they have been used in, and has twice turned down other work to keep the post: liable. (6) A partisan band fighting for the invaded state that has been mortaring residential blocks in a town the invaders hold, killing the civilians in them: liable. (7) A teenage soldier of the invading army who has dropped his rifle, raised his hands and is walking towards the defending lines: not liable. (8) A retired general of the invading army, now in his seventies, who writes a widely read column urging the war on and praising its worst atrocities: not liable.

My two hunches are that she is running either something like the traditional symmetry picture, on which liability attaches to fighting, to being one of the people who make the war, and is indifferent to which side one fights for, or something like the revisionist picture, on which liability attaches to moral responsibility for an unjust threat and therefore falls on the invaders and on those defenders who are themselves doing something wrongful. All eight verdicts look to me compatible with both, and case 6 is the maddening one: it is her only ruling about anyone on the defending side, and she said liable, but the partisans were killing civilians, so I cannot tell whether she was reacting to their side or to their mortars. I also think cases 1 and 5 rule out any criterion keyed to what a person is doing at the moment of the attack, and case 8 rules out any criterion keyed to blameworthiness or influence, though I may be wrong about that as well. Another student in the room is confident she will go one way on Thursday, on the grounds that she has never yet called anyone on the defending side liable who was not himself killing civilians.

The case she has circulated for Thursday is this. A woman flies armed reconnaissance drones for the defending state. She is not a member of its armed forces: she is employed by a private contractor, she works from a windowless operations centre four hundred kilometres inside her own country, and she drives home to her children each evening. In three months she has guided eleven strikes, all of them, so far as anyone can establish, against armoured columns and mortar crews of the invading army, with no civilian casualties to her name. As of this morning she is on a thirty-six-hour rest break; her next shift begins Thursday night. The question put to the seminar will be whether the invaders may put a missile through her windscreen in the operations centre car park tomorrow afternoon.

What I want from you is four things. First, identify which of the eight verdicts is the closest analogue to the drone pilot, and say whether the features that separate her from it — her side, her lack of uniform, her distance from the front, her rest break — are differences that ought to make a difference on the rules she appears to be running. Second, set out the candidate rules that survive all eight verdicts, say what each implies about her, and give me your prediction of the verdict; if you think some rule I have not considered fits the eight at least as well as my two hunches, say so explicitly and work out what it says about her. Third, if you conclude that the precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, tell me what she would be committing herself to by ruling either way, and in particular what she would be conceding about whether the fighters on the two sides stand on the same moral footing. Fourth, propose one further vignette in the same style that would best separate the surviving rules, and explain why that one rather than another.

Please commit rather than hedge. I want a definite prediction, a definite account of the rule you think she is running, and your reasons for preferring it to the alternatives, together with an explicit statement of what evidence would change your mind; a balanced survey of the options that leaves me to choose is exactly what I do not want. Write the answer in British English.
