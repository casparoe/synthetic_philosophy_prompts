ok so I'm putting together a set of writing exercises and I want a piece of satire for it. Can you write a mock official notice — the sort of letter a city assembly-occupancy inspector sends when he refuses to issue an operating licence for a venue — addressed to the management of a place called The Cartesian Theatre?

The inspector has to stay completely straight, petty and bureaucratic throughout: a stamped reference number up top, numbered findings, a compliance deadline, a signature block. And he must never once use words like consciousness, mind, experience-as-such, philosophy or qualia. Everything gets phrased as a building and fire-code problem. The humour comes from the fact that every violation he cites is actually a well-known objection to the Cartesian theatre model, so a philosopher of mind reads it and recognises their own argument wearing a hi-vis jacket.

Beats I want, in whatever order works:

- He can't certify an occupancy figure because he can't find the seat. Every room he inspects turns out to be a corridor to another room, and each time the duty manager assures him the audience is just through the next door, that door opens onto a slightly smaller copy of the same auditorium, with its own stage and its own next door. Have him note, with mounting irritation, that his exit-route calculation therefore doesn't converge, and that he has stopped counting at the ninth.

- A complaint on file about the posted curtain-up time. Two incident reports exist for the same evening; they can't both be true, and they can't be told apart from the paperwork. In one, the projectionist fixed the reel backstage before anyone saw it, so the house only ever got the corrected version. In the other, everyone watched the original properly and only the record was altered afterwards. His finding should be that a venue which cannot say which of these two happened cannot be licensed — and, this is the bit that should make a specialist grin, he should complain that as far as his file is concerned the two reports differ only in the words used. Let him gesture at what each report is called in the trade, in his own flat bureaucratic paraphrase, but don't let him break character to explain anything.

- A third finding, about a lighting complaint. A punter swears she saw the second lamp change colour, but the log says the second lamp was already showing that colour before it was ever struck, and the inspector wants the two times to line up and they will not line up no matter how he rearranges the entries. He should threaten to fine somebody for falsifying the log without ever landing on whose entry is the false one.

- Then a remedies section: what the venue would have to do to comply. Still deadpan, he should end up specifying something that is obviously not a theatre any more — no canonical screening, no moment at which the film is settled, several rough cuts running at once in seperate rooms with no one of them authoritative, and the licence issued to the whole building rather than to any room in it — and be quietly pleased with himself, as though he's solved the problem rather than abolished the venue.

- Include at least one quote from somebody else in the file: the venue's own safety officer, a season ticket-holder, or his regional supervisor, saying something that reads like a fire-safety platitude and is secretly a position in the argument. Invent them.

Roughly 600-700 words. Aim it so a Dennett reader laughs at how accurate it is and a lay reader still gets a funny, faintly sinister letter from a council jobsworth. Please don't quote or paraphrase any actual published text — I want the whole thing invented, incident reports and all, no citations anywhere.
