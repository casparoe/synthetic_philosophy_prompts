I'm in the second half of a medieval political thought survey — we've moved from Augustine and John of Salisbury into the thirteenth century, and this week's reading is Aquinas's *On Kingship* alongside his treatment of sedition in the second part of the *Summa Theologiae*. I have an essay due Thursday on whether Aquinas should be read as a theorist of resistance or as a quietist about established authority, and on the bus to seminar I listened to an episode of a history podcast whose host is a professional medievalist and who offered a fairly confident précis of Aquinas on tyrannicide. I wrote down his summary as faithfully as I could:

> "Aquinas is the most important medieval theorist of tyrannicide, and his position is far more radical than people usually assume. His starting point is that kingship exists for the sake of the common good; a ruler who governs for his own profit and glory is a tyrant, and tyranny is not merely an inferior regime but a corrupt one, because it severs the bond that makes authority legitimate at all. Once that bond is severed, Aquinas holds, the ruler forfeits any claim to obedience, and he is quite prepared to countenance his removal by force — in *On Kingship*, the treatise he composed for the king of Cyprus, he argues that a man who has seized power over a reluctant people has no title to rule, so that whoever gets at him first commits no injustice. He adds a single qualification, and it's an instructive one: where the tyrant came to the throne lawfully, by inheritance rather than by usurpation, no human being may depose him, because a rightful king acknowledges no superior on earth and God alone is his judge. So in practice Aquinas permits tyrannicide against usurpers and forbids it against hereditary monarchs, which is why his politics are monarchist in everything but name. He does go on, in the second book, to sketch the machinery by which the leading men of the realm are to judge and remove a king who has turned tyrant. And in the *Summa* he insists that subjects who rise against a tyrant are not guilty of sedition, since sedition is properly the tyrant's own crime: he foments discord among his subjects so as to dominate them more securely. The Jesuits later took this doctrine and made it an instrument against princes, which is why the papacy eventually had to disown it and why official Catholic teaching became quietist for three centuries. Historically, Aquinas is the man who gave Europeans the idea of a right of resistance four centuries before Locke."

Several things about this sit badly with what I remember from lecture, but I can't yet tell whether my memory or the host is at fault, and I'd rather not build an essay on a podcast.
