I'm a retired comparative physiologist — thirty-five years on fish sensory systems, mostly lateral line and nociception work — and I only started reading the philosophy stuff after I stopped. Next month I'm giving a talk at an interdisciplinary sentience workshop and I want a proper Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view.

My thesis, roughly: the encephalization quotient has no business showing up in arguments about moral status and should be retired from them. Its a two-factor view instead. What matters is (1) total neuron count in whatever structure does integration — pallium, cerebrum, functional equivalent — and (2) evidence that the animal can globally modulate its own nociception, so that pain is had rather than just reflexively handled. Absolute brain size only enters as a weak correlate of (1). Consequences I'm willing to live with: corvids and parrots move way up, cetaceans and elephants stay high, most insects and probably all bivalves fall out entirely, and octopus comes out ambiguous because so much of the processing is in the arms.

The room: mostly comparative psychologists and animal welfare scientists who care about behavioral assays and measurement validity; two philosophers of mind who are functionalists and allergic to neuron counting; a lawyer who does animal welfare policy and wants to know how you'd ever operationalize this in a statute; and a grad student from an invertebrate welfare NGO who is going to be unhappy about the insects.

Act as a philosopher of mind and animal ethics who has sat through a hundred of these seminars and knows how the question period actually goes. Then give me eight or nine objections ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way the person most likely to raise it would phrase it, with a two-minute reply I could actually deliver standing up, and a blunt note on where each reply is softest. Finish with the single question I'm hoping nobody asks — the one that goes at the deepest problem with my own view.
