I am assisting with the consent and eligibility documents for a proposed clinical programme in which synthetic units replicating the input–output profile of biological neurons would be substituted, one at a time, into the brains of patients with a slowly progressive neurodegenerative disease. Before I sign off on anything, I need to know whether my own survival intuitions about such a procedure hang together. What follows are my verdicts on a battery of variants I have been working through, together with a statement of what I want out of you. In each case, assume the substrate, the clinical setting, and everything not explicitly varied are held fixed.

1. One neuron at a time, over twenty years, each unit constructed from a scan of the neuron it displaces and installed in situ with the same connections. Verdict: the patient survives, and is plainly the same person at the end.

2. The identical end state reached in a single overnight operation from a full scan, under anaesthesia. Verdict: the patient does not survive. Nothing distinguishes this from case 1 except the path by which the end state was reached, and I cannot make that difference carry the weight I am putting on it.

3. The twenty-year procedure carried out as twenty annual operations, each replacing one whole region in a burst rather than one neuron at a time. Verdict: I waver. The overall rate of change is the same as in case 1, so whatever I am tracking there is not simply how slowly the total transformation happens.

4. Each replacement runs in parallel with the original neuron for a week, both discharging the same function, the biological one then being switched off. Verdict: survives, and I am more confident here than in case 1, though I cannot see why an interval of redundancy should make survival more secure rather than less.

5. Substitution proceeds from occipital to prefrontal. Verdict: survives. From prefrontal to occipital: survives, but with noticeably less conviction. I suspect the asymmetry is an artifact of my intuitions and would like to be told so.

6. The units reproduce the cellular function but not the internal dynamics: no action potentials, a different physical realization of an equivalent transfer function. Verdict: survives. By contrast, a synthetic brain reproducing whole-organism behaviour exactly, with an internal state-space bearing no systematic relation to the patient's own, and no neuron-level correspondence at all. Verdict: does not survive, despite organism-level functional equivalence. My intuitions are therefore keyed to something sub-personal, and I cannot say what.

7. Replacement stops at thirty percent. Verdict: obviously survives. Stops at seventy percent: survives, but I hesitate. I can name no threshold.

8. Case 1, except that each new unit is marginally better than the one it replaces, faster and less noisy, so that the patient ends measurably sharper with no discontinuity anywhere along the way. Verdict: survives, and improved. Case 1, except that each new unit is marginally different in temperament rather than better, no gain and no loss, ending in someone the family describes as recognizably him but not quite the same man. Verdict: I want to say he survives less, or not at all. I recognize that these two verdicts cannot both stand unless improvement is doing work that identity has no business doing.

9. Each installed unit is permitted to self-modify; ten years on, the patient's tastes and temper have changed substantially and the architecture resembles nothing the original brain contained. Verdict: survives. This suggests my principle concerns the history of the change rather than the preservation of any particular structure, but then the drift in case 8 and even the overnight swap in case 2 ought to be survivable, since in case 2 the body, the social world, and every causal relation the patient stands in change continuously.

10. After full replacement, the original biological brain is kept perfused and viable, continuing to have experiences for a year before destruction. Verdict: the synthetic patient survives, and I am unwilling to say the perfusion year retroactively undoes the replacement, yet I also feel the perfused brain has some competing claim.

11. Scan and instantiate with no replacement: the original is destroyed at the instant the copy is activated. Verdict: the original does not survive. Original kept alive alongside the copy: also does not survive. It is this pair, rather than any of the replacement cases, that makes me doubt case 1.

12. The patient loses one particular memory en route, the smell of his grandmother's kitchen, retaining every associated fact and every other memory. Verdict: survives, diminished. I find this loss far less troubling than the swap in case 2, which sits badly with any account on which what matters is psychological continuity.

What I would like back:

(a) The principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that generates these verdicts, stated precisely and clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause and the verdicts each clause is needed to secure.

(b) Where no principle fits the whole set, the minimal collection of verdicts I must surrender, ranked by how much the resulting account costs me.

(c) What the surviving account predicts for two cases I have not listed: a patient whose replacement halts at forty percent and who then dies of an unrelated cause within the week; and a patient replaced gradually while an exact functional duplicate is maintained in lockstep and resynchronized against the original every night.

(d) Whether the asymmetry in case 8 can be made principled at all, or whether I have allowed an evaluative consideration to masquerade as a metaphysical one.
