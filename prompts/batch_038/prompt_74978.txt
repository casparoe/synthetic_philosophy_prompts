Third-year jurisprudence seminar, 22 students, half law and half philosophy, two hours on a Thursday afternoon in a room with fixed tables I can't move, one whiteboard, and no budget for anything. We're finishing Devlin and Hart on whether the law should enforce morality, and I've now taught this session twice and both times it collapsed into the exact thing I was trying to get away from: everyone restating their intuitions about whether the state ought to ban stuff, somebody saying harm principle as though that settles it, and Devlin getting written off as a bigot rather than read as making an argument. Afterwards a friend and I were going back and forth in the pub and he said something I haven't been able to shake — that Devlin's test, whatever ordinary people actually feel about a practice, the revulsion and the indignation, isn't a criterion so much as a thermometer, and whoever controls the description controls the reading. I want that tested in the room instead of asserted by me.

So: build me a two-hour in-class deliberation where the students sit as a legislative committee in a polity you invent. Give the polity a name and two sentences of history so it reads as somewhere rather than a placeholder, and give me five or six consensual practices that its citizens variously find revolting, tolerable, or beneath notice. Invented ones, please — not stand-ins for abortion or drugs or sex between consenting adults, partly because I don't want anyone in the room voting to criminalise something that's live for them or someone they love, and partly because the allegory always gets spotted in minute two and then we're arguing about the real world again. For register, here's the only one I've got so far: a household practice of eating the evening meal in total silence, youngest served first, everyone wearing gloves. Harmless, gross to some, fine by others. Match that. The committee's voting rule has to be Devlin's — they vote on the strength and character of popular feeling, not on their own considered judgements — and I want role cards that hand each student a slice of the population so the composition of the committee actually bites, plus a limited pot of enforcement tokens so they can't ban everything and have to choose what goes unpoliced. The twist should live in the mechanics, not in my mouth: at least one practice gets voted on twice, once under a loaded description and once under a flat neutral one; one practice is universally disgusting and yet nobody in the room thinks the law should touch it; and one provokes no feeling at all but turns out, on reflection, to be the gravest wrong on the list. Then a ground-truth sheet they never saw during deliberation, giving the actual social costs of each practice in that society, so they can check for themselves whether feeling tracked harm. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the names of any of these moves until the debrief.

Give me the running order minute by minute, what I need to print, the role cards, the token rules, what I'm allowed to say at each stage (as little as possible), the debrief questions in the order I should ask them, and a mapping from moments in the room onto parts of the argument — the gap between a society's positive morality and its critical morality, the treason comparison and why Hart thought it a bad one, whether Devlin's claim about societies coming apart is an empirical hypothesis anyone has ever tested, and where the offence principle turns up as the compromise somebody will reach for to end the discomfort. Then tell me what will definately go wrong the first time I run it — the two students who've read the moral dumbfounding literature guessing the reveal immediately, the token economy getting gamed by one very competitive kid, somebody flatly refusing to vote in role against their conscience, the loaded description being too transparent to work — and how I head each one off. And be straight with me about what the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply: I suspect classroom disgust isn't disgust, and I suspect a ledger that turns someone's life into a social-cost figure is its own kind of distortion, but you tell me where I'm wrong. No reading list or citations, I know the texts, I need the mechanics. Please write the whole thing in American English — my co-facilitator is running the same session at a US school next term and this is going straight into a shared handout.
