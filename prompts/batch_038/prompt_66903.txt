I spent thirty-five years in experimental physics, where gauge freedom was something we were trained to divide out without comment, and I have only lately taken up the philosophy of spacetime. I should like you to draft the joint statement that two parties to the hole-argument debate could sign at the conclusion of a serious adversarial collaboration: a sophisticated substantivalist, who holds that the manifold and its points exist and that the metric is a field upon them, but who accepts Leibniz equivalence and therefore denies that diffeomorphic models represent distinct physical possibilities; and a structuralist, who holds that there is nothing to the manifold beyond the fields it carries, so that its points are individuated only by the values those fields take there.

Please supply:

1. A numbered list of the claims both can sign — about the construction itself (the drag-along, the diffeomorphism that is the identity outside the hole, whether it yields a second solution of the same field equations), about what is and is not empirically detectable in the pair of models, and about what the argument does not establish.

2. A table of the residual disagreements, each rendered in wording its own proponent would accept and its opponent would not protest as unfair, with a note on whether either side regards the dispute as merely verbal, and if so which part.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions whose resolution would genuinely move at least one party. I expect one to concern whether two diffeomorphic models could differ only in which point bears which field values, and whether that question is answerable or empty; another to concern whether the argument's force depends on general relativity specifically, given that any theory whatsoever can be recast in generally covariant form.

4. For each crux, the argument, formal result, or worked example both would agree bears on it, and each side's judgment recorded in advance about where that line of inquiry leads.

Finally, be explicit about what is settled among those who work on the problem and what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested resolution — for instance, that Leibniz equivalence simply dissolves the difficulty — as though it were the consensus.
