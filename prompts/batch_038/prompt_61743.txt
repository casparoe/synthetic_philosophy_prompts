I teach a grad seminar on cooperation and conflict, and the committment-races week is the one that always goes flat. We do Schelling on threats and promises and burning your bridges, the chicken business, plus a couple of newer pieces about machine learning systems racing to lock in bargaining positions before their rivals can react. The students follow the logic fine. What they don't get is the *feel* of it — why anyone would destroy their own options on purpose, and why the race is ruinous for the whole town even when each individual entry into it is perfectly sensible. So this year I want to open that session with a short story instead of yet another excerpt. Can you write me one?

I don't want to hand them anything they could plausibly have read before, so here's the world I'd like you to build it in. Change names and surface details as you like, keep the machinery:

- A harbor town whose economy runs through a single institution, the Ledger. Anyone may file an oath there: a dated, public promise about your own future conduct, together with a forfeiture deposited with the Ledger-keeper, which you lose if you're seen to break it. Entries are read aloud at market on the first of the month, in the order they were filed, and once read they cannot be amended or withdrawn.
- The Ledger polices nothing else. It publishes and it holds. The oaths bite only because everyone knows what everyone else has staked and can't take back.
- Two parties are racing to file — I'd make them the two fleets that each want the sole contract to carry the mines' output down the channel through the winter. Filing first is an advantage, because your terms become the fixed thing everyone else has to route around. Filing first is also a trap, because once you've been read aloud you can't answer whatever the other side does next.
- Your protagonist is a young clerk in the Ledger office. She sees both sides' filings as they come across her desk, so she can tell exactly where the two oaths collide, and that the collision costs both fleets a good deal more than the contract is worth.
- Her way out is a counter-oath, filed by both sides at once, releasing them to renegotiate. Which obviously doesn't work: filings are read in order, so whoever files second gets to see the first and defect. She has to solve a race by running a race, one level up, and I want the story to be honest about that instead of letting her find a clever loophole.
- Where I'd like it to land: the only thing that makes simultaneity believable is that somebody *else* stakes something real on holding both sides to identical treatment — the procedure itself gets committed to, at a cost. Let that cost fall on the clerk, or the Ledger-keeper, or someone the reader has come to like. Don't let it be cheap, and don't put a moral in the last line.

Craft constraints, which matter more than they sound:

- 1,300 to 1,700 words, third person limited on the clerk, with a title.
- Nobody is allowed to explain the logic. The words "commitment," "credible," "leverage," and "bargaining power" cannot appear in dialogue at all, and at most one of them may appear once in the narration. A student should be able to point at a scene and say "there's the first-mover advantage" without anyone having named it.
- Make the forfeitures physical and particular — a boat, a deed, a name painted on a door, whatever you choose — and stage the read-aloud ceremony at least twice with different contents, so the reader feels the gap between a filed oath and a published one.
- No AI, no research labs, no corporate boardroom, no legal-thriller framing, no narrator addressing the reader, and no character who turns out to be right about everything on account of being smarter than everyone else.

Above all it has to work as a story. If my students are just reading a puzzle in costume, I've failed.

Please start your response with the story itself — title on the first line, then straight in. No preface, no note about the choices you made, no afterword. If I want to hear how you'd revise it, I'll ask.
