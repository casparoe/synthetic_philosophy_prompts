ok so, context dump: I'm a third-year PPE student and this term I'm in a seminar on social choice and collective judgment — we've done List and Pettit on aggregating sets of judgments, the doctrinal paradox (that law-and-economics one, Kornhauser and Sager, with the three judges and the contract case where the panel splits on whether there was a valid contract and whether it was breached), and a bit of the impossibility-result material on why you can't just vote on everything consistently. Separately — and this is why I'm asking — I'm the managing editor of our department's undergraduate philosophy journal, which turns out to be a running live demo of everything the seminar says goes wrong. So I'd like to get something genuinely usable out of the overlap rather than just a paper I hand in and forget.

Here's how we actually run things. Roughly seven editors sit on the panel per round, submissions come in twice a year (~40 per round, we take 8), and every paper gets scored on a four-part rubric: (i) is the argument sound and does it engage the literature seriously, (ii) is it original, (iii) is it clearly written, and (iv) does it deserve a slot given what else we've already taken this round — that last one is comparative and basically quota-ish, it depends on other papers rather than just on this one. We vote yes/no by simple majority on each of the four, and then separately on accept/reject overall, and the decision letter to the author gives "the panel's reasons," which is a sentence assembled out of whichever criteria got majority yeses. Nobody has ever asked whether any of that hangs together, and last round it visibly didn't. One paper got majority yes on i, ii and iii, majority no on iv, and majority no on overall accept — and the author (who is, awkwardly, a friend of one of the editors) got a rejection letter praising exactly the things she'd passed on. Another paper went the opposite way: majority no on soundness but majority yes on overall accept, because two editors who thought it was unsound also thought it was too good to lose and voted strategically on the overall question rather than on the criterion. On top of that, membership shifts mid-round for conflicts of interest — if someone recuses themselves on (ii) they're still voting on (iii) — so "the majority" isn't even the same set of people across criteria.

What I want: act as a philosopher who works on judgment aggregation but who has also actually sat on editorial boards and knows that a procedure nobody can run in a 90-minute meeting is worth nothing. Start by brainstorming out loud, a few bullets, on which distinct failure modes our setup actually contains (I suspect the strategic-voting thing and the rotating-membership thing are separate problems and not versions of the doctrinal paradox, but tell me if I'm wrong) — then write me a decision procedure the panel can run top to bottom at the meeting: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which of our four criteria are "premises" that should determine the conclusion and which aren't, what to do about (iv) where there's no neat logical connection to accept/reject so the premise-based trick doesn't obviously apply, when to vote criterion-by-criterion vs going straight to the overall verdict, how to binarize the ones we currently score 1–5 (and whether the threshold itself is something we vote on, which feels circular), how to handle abstentions and recusal so the changing constituency doesn't quietly decide the outcome, and where the procedure should just stop and hand the call to our faculty advisor because it's a question of values rather than a question of fact. Then run it on three cases: the friend's paper above, the strategically-voted unsound-but-brilliant one, and one you invent where the procedure gives an answer I'm going to hate — I'd rather be embarrased now than in November. Also be explicit about which distinctions from the literature have to survive into something non-philosophy editors can follow and which ones I can safely drop, where you expect the procedure to misfire in practice, and give me wording for the decision letter that states an aggregate reason without attributing to any individual reviewer a judgment they didn't actually hold. End with a one-page version I can print and put on the table.
