You are a philosopher of causation who has taught metaphysics to quantitatively trained non-philosophers before, and I want you to think hard about the pedagogical structure of what follows rather than about the metaphysics per se. I have a philosophy doctorate and I run a monthly internal seminar at a health-analytics company; next month's session is ninety minutes with a whiteboard, for fourteen people who build causal models for a living. They are fluent in directed acyclic graphs, potential outcomes, the do-operator, confounding, mediation, and they have all taken some version of the same causal-inference course. None of them has read a page of metaphysics. Their topic request, submitted enthusiastically, is whether an absence can cause anything, and what our models commit us to when we code the control arm as zero and then talk about the effect of not treating. What makes this delicate is that they already believe they hold a counterfactual account of causation, and they believe it is the philosophers' account too, so they will experience the session as a formality unless I unsettle that.

Please do not explain the problem to me; I know it well enough. Plan how I should explain it. Specifically: give me the order in which the pieces have to be introduced and what depends on what — I am assuming I cannot get to the proliferation of absent causes until they have conceded that counterfactual dependence and causation are not obviously the same relation, but tell me if that is backwards; identify the single running example that should carry the whole session and say what it must contain and what it must lack, and tell me whether the one I have drafted will do the work or whether it lets them straight back into engineering mode (my draft is a real internal incident review in which a monitoring alert never fired and an outage ran for four hours, with escalating variants: nobody was rostered on call, the on-call engineer's phone was on silent, the upstream vendor three time zones away was never paged, and the chief executive did not happen to glance at a dashboard); map the misconceptions they will arrive with or form mid-session and tell me the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than argued against later — I expect at least that absence is just a zero in a column and therefore already handled, that the graph itself settles which absences are in the model and so there is no proliferation problem, that Lewis and his followers already absorbed the cost by letting context and salience do the selecting, and that Beebee-style insistence that this is dependence without genuine causation is a merely verbal retreat; give me one closing question whose answers would tell me the idea landed rather than merely entertained; and tell me what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and announce as refused, including how to say that without sounding evasive.

Begin with a short diagnosis before the plan: what in their existing toolkit will fight this idea, what in it can be recruited as a bridge, and where the honest seams are. Then commit. I want one defended sequence with reasons for the ordering, not a menu of options and not a hedged survey of the dialectic; if a step is load-bearing because the next one collapses without it, say so explicitly. I would much rather be told the session as I have framed it will not work than be handed a polished plan for a session that quietly fails.
