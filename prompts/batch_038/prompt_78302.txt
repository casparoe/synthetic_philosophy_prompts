Two participants in a graduate reading group I sit in on argued past the end of a session on Quine's gavagai thought experiment, and the rest of us failed to referee them. I am reporting the exchange as neutrally as I can: neither conceded anything, and I do not know which of them is closer to right. Mara works mainly in philosophy of language; Seth works mainly in metaphysics.

(1) Mara opened by claiming the gavagai argument is routinely understated. Pointing at a rabbit while hearing the native's utterance does not fix whether the term applies to the whole animal, to an undetached part of it, to a momentary stage of it, or to the universal. Since the field linguist's evidence is limited in principle to observable behavior, for every manual compatible with that behavior there is a rival manual, differently assigning referents, equally compatible. Her conclusion: in the radical case there is no fact of the matter what the native's words refer to — not ignorance of a fact, but absence of one.

(2) Seth said this runs two distinct things together. Underdetermination of a hypothesis by all possible evidence is one thing; the nonexistence of a determinate fact is another, and nothing in the argument gets you from the first to the second. The step needs the premise that behavioral evidence is all the evidence there could be, and that is a substantive thesis about meaning, not a result about translation. Grant him access to the speaker's inner life, or to the causal-historical chain linking the word to the animals in the world, and the puzzle degrades into an ordinary skeptical worry: reference is determinate and merely unknowable.

(3) Mara answered that the premise is not optional, because meaning must be learnable: whatever it is for someone to mean something by a term has to be something a learner could in principle recover from observable use, or it is not a candidate for meaning at all. She added that retreating to the mental buys nothing, since an image of a rabbit is exactly as indeterminate as the pointing gesture — an image of the whole animal is equally an image of undetached rabbit parts. The appeal to inner determinacy, she said, relocates the puzzle inside the skull rather than solving it.

(4) Seth said that second half is a reductio of her position rather than a support for it. If the argument applies to mental representation as well, then nothing could ever have determinate content, including the content of Mara's own claim. She is either self-undermining, or she has proved so much that the argument should be rejected as unsound instead of embraced.

(5) Mara responded with the compensation point. The rival manuals are not arbitrary. Translate the native's term as applying to rabbit stages throughout, then repair the resulting friction by re-tuning the collateral sentences: where the native counts three rabbits in the yard, the rival ontology counts three stages present together; where the native says the same rabbit came back, the rival manual adjusts its identity criteria. Every mismatch is offset elsewhere, which is precisely why no behavior discriminates. She then extended the claim homeward: nothing about English speakers supplies evidence beyond behavior, since we have no transparent access to one another's meanings and our access to our own is itself interpretive, so reference is indeterminate domestically too.

(6) Seth pressed on the empirical stability of that compensation. To make the rival manual fit, Mara must revise the semantics of counting, identity, and quantification — but at that point it is unclear whether she has described a rival translation of the same language or simply a different language with a different logical grammar. And speakers reveal the difference in practice: consider their reactions to an animal whose parts are gradually replaced, or their readiness to say, in an appropriate situation, that there are now more parts of rabbits here than there are rabbits. Wherever the two manuals make different predictions, he said, they are not empirically equivalent; wherever they do not, they are not genuine rivals.

(7) Mara said Seth owes a discriminating test and has produced none, and that any behavior he nominates can be absorbed by further compensation — that is just the lesson of holism. Seth said that is exactly the problem: a conclusion no evidence could ever dislodge is not a discovery about reference but an unfalsifiable stipulation, and the burden sits with Mara, who is asserting the absence of a fact. Mara replied that his demand for a discriminating test already presupposes the picture under dispute, since reference is only ever determinate relative to a background language, and the question what our terms refer to, asked from nowhere at all, simply has no answer. Seth said that is either a triviality — of course translation is relative to a translation scheme — or a change of subject.

Please adjudicate move by move rather than announcing a winner first. For each of the seven moves, assign a rating on a scale you define explicitly, running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, justify each rating, and say where a move is partly right — for instance, sound in its diagnosis but wrong in the conclusion it draws. Then answer three questions directly. Does the compensation strategy in (5) really produce an empirically equivalent rival manual, or does (6) show that it either fails to fit or fits only by describing a different language? Is the mental-image rejoinder in (3) an independent argument, or does it backfire in the way (4) claims? Does the extension to the domestic case in (5) succeed, and if it fails, does its failure restore anything to Seth?

Finally, both of them treated the options as exhaustive: determinate reference, or no fact of the matter. Consider the alternative Mara raised at the end of (7), on which reference is fully determinate relative to a background language and there is no such thing as determinacy relative to none. Say whether that position is coherent and substantive, trivially true, or a change of subject, and whether it dissolves their disagreement or merely relocates it. Then state what, if anything, both of them are missing — including whether either side has a principled account of what a discriminating test would have to look like, if one existed.
