For four years our project has argued about a small class of finds without making any headway, and I want a diagnosis of the argument itself rather than another contribution to it.

The finds: palm-sized fired-clay plaques, unperforated, one face carrying a deeply incised motif that varies very little across the corpus. They occur at some sixty sites in our survey region over roughly four centuries, in domestic floor deposits and in burials in approximately equal numbers, with no correlation to burial wealth or to site size. They are standardized in outline and motif but not in fabric or finishing, so nothing points to a single workshop. A minority — perhaps one in fifteen — carries a short row of notches along an edge; these notched examples are the pivot of the dispute, and they cluster at a handful of the largest sites rather than spreading evenly. The motif's distribution tracks that of one regional pottery style but not that of any exotic raw material. Not one plaque has come from a context that shows it in use: no hoard, no cache, no in situ deposit.

Three readings are on the table, held by competent people working from the same data. The first holds that the plaques are ritual objects, and that their wide, socially level spread evidences a shared cosmology across politically independent communities, with the notched minority a priestly or calendrical subset. The second holds that they are administrative devices — ownership markers, tallies, tokens — whose spread tracks exchange and record-keeping, with the notched minority the functioning subset and the plain majority imitations or blanks. The third holds that they are a popular commodity form: a fashion, copied because it was recognizable and cheap, bearing neither shared belief nor bookkeeping, with the notches as potters' marks.

The argument has not stalled for want of data. We ran the obvious discriminating analysis — mapping where the notched subset, the pottery style, and the largest-site distribution coincide and where they come apart — and the result made things worse: each camp absorbed it with an auxiliary (the notched ones were the elite subset; the plain ones were domestic copies; the meaning shifted across the four centuries; the fashion radiated from those same centers). Since then no one has proposed an observation the others would concede as decisive, and the discussion has reverted to restating priors about how common shared cosmologies, bureaucracies, and fashions respectively are.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the three positions are not rival answers to one question. They may be pitched at different levels of the explanandum — mental content, recurrent practice, institutional arrangement — and the plaques may be capable of constraining only the middle one, which would make the exchange partly verbal and partly a standoff over priors that no assemblage can settle. But I could be wrong. It may instead be a genuine empirical standoff that our sampling and preservation render unwinnable in principle rather than merely in practice, or a case where belief-attribution has no determinate truth value once the practitioners are dead.

So: diagnose the disagreement. At what point do the three readings stop being rivals, and where do they remain genuinely incompatible? Is any part of it verbal, and if so which term is doing the work and what are the candidate senses it could bear? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question that all three camps would accept as settling the matter — and if there is, state it precisely. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction to be drawn, a class of observation to seek (use-wear, breakage patterns, production debris, the contrast between use-contexts and deposition-contexts), a reformulation of the competing claims, or an honest verdict that none is available and why not. Argue from the pattern as I have described it. I do not want a tour of the methodological literature or any list of sources, and please do not offer the usual processual-versus-interpretive vocabulary as a diagnosis: we have tried that, and it did not help.
