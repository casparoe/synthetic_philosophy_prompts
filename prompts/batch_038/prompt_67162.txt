**Tools that take things off the table**

Dennett sorts minds into four kinds, stacked like rungs. Darwinian creatures are wired by selection: they come pre-equipped to avoid some things and seek others, and there's no way to argue them out of it. Skinnerian creatures can be reshaped by their own history of reward and punishment, so the same wiring gets tuned to wherever the creature happens to live. Popperian creatures carry an internal model of the situation, which means they can try a course of action in imagination and let the bad guesses die there instead of dying themselves. And Gregorian creatures — the rung named for Richard Gregory, who was interested in tools as things that let a mind do more than the mind alone could — import design from outside their own skulls: writing, counting, maps, calendars, diagrams, recipes. The point of that last rung is that the design work is no longer done by genes or by trial and error but by other people, and it arrives already built.

My complaint is about the shape of that last rung. It's almost always told as purely additive. You get a tool, and now you can do something you couldn't. Every artifact is an amplifier. But there is a huge family of cultural artifacts whose entire function is to *remove* options, and the ladder has no place to put them. Checklists. Standardised forms. Diagnostic criteria. Filing systems. Shift rotas. The seven-day week. Double-entry bookkeeping. A house style guide. Spelling autocorrect. Satnav. None of these make you able to do something new so much as they make a whole class of things no longer available to you — not forbidden, just not generated.

So I want to say the Gregorian rung splits in two. Amplifiers widen the option space; what I'll call pruners narrow it. And they narrow it in a specific place, which is what makes this interesting rather than trivial: they narrow the hypothesis space. That's precisely the thing that defines the Popperian rung — the ability to generate and discard candidate actions internally. A pruner is an artifact that does the discarding for you, before you ever had the candidates.

Here's the test I've been playing with. An artifact counts as a pruner rather than an amplifier if adopting it makes the creature *worse* at producing hypotheses in the domain the artifact governs, once you take the artifact away. A chess player who checks an engine evaluation after every game stops forming evaluations of their own, and after a few years can't tell you why a position is good without asking. A clinician who codes to a manual stops noticing symptom combinations the manual doesn't name, and can't recover them afterwards. A cook who only ever follows recipes loses the ability to look in a fridge and see a meal. What's gone in each case isn't skill in the ordinary sense — they're all still competent, arguably more competent than before. What's gone is the generative step.

Two consequences, and these are what I think the paper is really about.

First, pruners are how groups get smart without anybody in them getting smart. If everyone has been pruned the same way, everybody rules out the same bad moves, so the cost of coordinating collapses — you don't have to negotiate what counts as a completed shift report, or what a "3" means on the scale, or where the file goes. Shared pruning is cheap agreement. Which means the Gregorian rung, as it's usually described, is quietly about *systems* rather than creatures. One person plus one manual plus one rota can be Gregorian in a way none of the three is separately, and the ladder doesn't have a slot for that because it's a list of kinds of individual.

Second, the ladder stops being monotone. If pruners exist, it's possible to move up the Gregorian rung and down the Popperian one at the same time, and to look from the outside like you're just getting better. More reliable, more consistent, easier to audit, less able to cope with a case the artifact didn't anticipate. And there's an asymmetry that makes it worse: genes don't get withdrawn but artifacts do. A creature whose hypothesis space has been pruned by something that can break, be discontinued, or be bought by someone with different intentions is in a different predicament from a creature whose limits were set by selection.

I think the live version of this is assistants that generate language on demand. Such a system is an amplifier in the abstract — in principle it can produce anything. But in use it's a pruner, and an unusually aggressive one, because it hands you the first good candidate. That candidate is exactly the one you'd otherwise have had to think of yourself, and the pruning is invisible since the option space never gets displayed. You experience it as help and you can't see what didn't happen.

Tentative punchline: either the Gregorian rung needs to be split into amplifiers and pruners, or there's a fifth kind of creature here — one whose design space is mostly fixed by artifacts made by other creatures, and who can't step outside it even in principle.
