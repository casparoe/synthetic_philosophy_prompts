I write novels about scarcity, and for several years now I have been building an argument I want put under strain before I commit it to a book. My thesis: the triage position in lifeboat ethics — that when capacity is fixed and demand exceeds it, the choice is not whether to ration but only by what rule, so that a refusal to ration is simply a rule that rations by chance — has not been refuted but displaced. The institutional critique, which holds that the lifeboat's fixed capacity is itself a political artifact rather than a natural limit, moved the profession's attention to the question of why the boat is small, and triage was left standing as the unlovely fallback nobody needed to argue against. On my reading, that silence is not a verdict but an avoidance, and the structural claim survives: any allocation regime operating under hard, non-negotiable capacity constraints will exhibit triage logic, and the only live variable is whether the rule is explicit and reviewable or hidden in queues, prices, and administrative discretion.

Tell me what would count against this. Not the rival positions as such, but the particular observations, cases, and arguments that should worry me: evidence that a real allocation regime has operated under genuinely fixed capacity without producing triage; evidence that the institutional critique did engage triage directly and answered it, so that its eclipse is a settled result rather than a change of fashion; and any sense in which my framing of capacity as either natural or constructed is a false choice. Rank these from fatal to merely awkward, and say which of them a defender of the thesis would concede as counting against it and which he would dismiss as beside the point — I want the exposure of the view, not a survey of the field. Then judge frankly whether the thesis has been qualified into safety, made compatible with every possible history of the debate until it risks nothing at all, and whether that is a defect in an argument of this kind or simply what a structural claim about constraint looks like.

I would rather have a committed answer than a balanced one. Take a side on whether the thesis is genuinely exposed, give your reasons for it, and state what it risks that its rivals do not. End with a single last line in exactly this form: Verdict: [the one consideration most damaging to the thesis] — [exposed | insulated].
