So I somehow ended up on my city's charter revision commission — 11 of us, and I'm the "ordinary resident" seat they added after I talked for like ten minutes at a council meeting last spring when the housing package fell apart. I'm a physical therapist, not a lawyer, and half the time I have no idea what anyone's talking about, but we vote Thursday on what actually goes on the November ballot and I've been asked to circulate something in writing by Wednesday night. The item I need help with is the sortition proposal, and I want to hand my colleagues a real memo, not a vibe.

Here's the proposal as it currently stands, from a councilmember and a good-government group that's been pushing it. The city (about 120k people, nine council districts) would add a standing Citizens' Panel of 45 residents drawn by lot from a merged list of voter registrations and municipal utility accounts, stratified by district, age band, and renter-versus-owner. One 18-month term, no re-draws. Service is voluntary — you can decline with no penalty — and people who serve get a $4,000 stipend plus childcare and mileage. If fewer than 30 of the 45 accept, the empty seats get filled from a standing list of residents who volunteered to be on it. The panel gets two powers: it can place any measure directly on the ballot without gathering signatures, and if it votes 60% against a council ordinance, that ordinance goes back to council and can only be re-passed by two-thirds. Then there's the part that's got us fighting: the "double draw." Any measure the panel wants to send to the ballot gets reviewed six months later by a second, freshly drawn panel, and it only goes forward if that second panel also approves it by simple majority. Supporters say it filters out flukes and captured outcomes; two of us think it's insulting to the people who did the actual work, since the second group didn't hear the testimony. Separately, one commissioner wants service made compulsory like jury duty with a fine for no-shows, to kill the volunteer bias, and another says that's coercion that fills the room with resentful people. And someone floated a five-year ban on panel members running for city office afterward so it doesn't become a farm team, which raises the obvious question of whether that just scares off participants.

Draft the memo I'll send around Wednesday night — call it two or three pages worth. It has to land on one clear recommendation with a timeline: put the thing on the ballot as drafted, put it on with specific amendments you spell out one by one, or keep it off and run something smaller first. Then give the reasoning a skeptic on the commission could actually argue with, and treat the strongest case against this design honestly rather than as a strawman — the accountability problem, what it means that 45 unelected people can bind 120,000, whether a body that can be intensively lobbied and whose agenda is set by city staff is really any less capturable than a council, and whether the double draw helps or hurts legitimacy rather than just sounding prudent. I also want your take on the compulsory-service question and on the volunteer-list backfill, since those two seem to decide whether the panel is a sample of the city at all. Please reason from the institutional design itself — don't pad it with a list of other cities or countries that have done this, I can't check any of that and neither can my colleagues. And if you think the whole thing should die, say that plainly, but then tell me what we should put in its place, because "do nothing" is not going to survive Thursday. One more thing: I lean toward yes but my stomach's been in knots about the backfill for a week, so if you think I'm wrong, argue with me directly in the memo rather than diplomatically working around it.
