I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long piece about the philosophy that is quietly built into how frontier AI labs decide whether a model is dangerous. I want to check my reading of that philosophy with someone who can state it more rigorously than I can, and I would rather you engage with the practice as I describe it below than with any public document; I have deliberately left out names, dates, and version numbers so that the question does not turn on getting those right.

The practice, in outline. Several of the largest labs have published safety frameworks in which the operative commitments are expressed as thresholds of demonstrated capability: a model that can, under evaluation, do some specified thing — acquire resources on its own, replicate itself given compute and network access, produce a specified kind of technical uplift for a weapon, or conceal its aims from its operators — triggers a specified escalation of mitigations, or a halt. The thresholds are framed throughout as what a model can do, not what it wants to do. Because a model that does not attempt a task can fail for the wrong reason, the evaluations are paired with an elicitation ladder: before a low score is accepted as evidence of absence, evaluators escalate through better scaffolding, worked examples, tool access, task decomposition, and sometimes fine-tuning or explicit incentive to try, and the figure that gets reported is the best performance anywhere along that ladder rather than the first-pass or the typical performance. Sitting alongside this, in the same frameworks, are measures that assume an opposite posture toward the same system: monitors that read the model's own reasoning traces and outputs for signs of concealment, tests in which the model is told one goal and observed to see whether it pursues another, and tripwires that fire on behavior rather than on self-report.

Two things about this puzzle me, and I suspect they are connected. The first is the word elicitation. There is an older use of it in statistics and decision theory, where an elicitation procedure is a scoring rule or a betting arrangement designed so that an agent's stated belief matches the agent's actual belief precisely because honesty is in the agent's interest; the mechanism and the cooperative subject come as a package. The evaluation use seems to inherit the goal — getting the inside to show on the outside — while discarding the mechanism, and perhaps discarding the assumption of a cooperative subject that made the mechanism work, since monitors and concealment tests assume the subject may be hiding things. The second is that a maximum over an open-ended ladder of elicitation attempts is never a finished quantity: presence of a capability can be established by exhibiting it once, but absence cannot be established at all, since a better rung might always be built. If that is right, the frameworks can be crossed but never definitively not crossed, and yet the same labs routinely report negative evaluation results as reassurance.

What I would like from you:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory. What account of capability — of what it is for a system to be able to do something — does this architecture require? Something dispositional and modal, it seems, but say exactly how it differs from a view on which capability just is typical performance, and say what it makes of the fact that what is measured is always a model together with its scaffolding while what the threshold is asserted about is the model alone.
2. Separate strict presupposition from good fit. Which parts of the practice would be licensed equally well by rival accounts — operationalist, behaviorist, or frankly precautionary — and where does the practice commit to something stronger than it needs to? I want to know which of my claims are underdetermined, because a piece that asserts a commitment the practice does not actually require is a bad piece.
3. Diagnose the internal consistency. Can cooperative elicitation and adversarial monitoring be held together in one framework, and on what picture of the system's agency? Can the reassurance use of negative results coexist with the unfalsifiability of absence? Which of these tensions are substantive and which are merely verbal?
4. Tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced — specifically, what a threshold framework would look like if the monitored variable were orientation or intent rather than capability, and what would have to be given up, epistemically and institutionally, to run it.
5. Finally, be blunt about my own framing: which of the two puzzles above is load-bearing, which is rhetorical, and where have I described the practice in a way that misstates what it is doing.

I should add two constraints. Please do not give me citations, a reading list, or names of papers; I will do my own reporting and I would rather have your reasoning than your references. And please define any technical term you need in a sentence of plain English, on the assumption that my readers are intelligent and know nothing about this literature.
