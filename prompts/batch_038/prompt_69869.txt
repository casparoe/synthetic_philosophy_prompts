I'm in the last weeks of an upper-level epistemology seminar and our TA has this habit of opening each section by tossing out a case and asking for verdicts before she says anything about the theory. Our study group has been logging her answers all semester, partly to game the final, partly because she's genuinely hard to pin down — she'll invoke Nozick and sensitivity one week and then say something the next week that doesn't sit right with it. Next week's handout is on the table already and it has one new case on it. I mostly want to know what she'll say about it, but I care at least as much about the rule she seems to be running, which other rules fit her record just as well, and which further case would tell them apart.

Her verdicts so far, as best we wrote them down:

- Clock stopped for years at exactly 3:20; you glance at it at exactly 3:20 and believe it's 3:20. True belief, no knowledge.
- Fake barn county, you happen to be looking at the one real barn. No knowledge.
- Ticket in a million-ticket lottery, you believe on the odds alone that it'll lose. No knowledge.
- Grandmother case with the understudy standing by who looks identical from the front. No knowledge.
- A stranger gives you directions and turns out to be a longtime local who's reliably right about the streets. You believe her and she's correct. Knowledge.
- Standard Gettier job-with-ten-coins-in-the-pocket setup. No knowledge.
- The one that has us stuck: a mathematician accepts a theorem because her advisor vouches for it and she checks the published proof sketch. The theorem is true, but one lemma in the sketch is false — a mistake nobody caught, and it doesn't matter because the theorem follows by a route nobody wrote down. She said no knowledge, and when pushed she said it wasn't just about methods being described loosely.

The new case on next week's handout: a student reads a standard, carefully refereed textbook which states that a certain function is continuous everywhere. It is. But the book reaches that statement through a sign error in one of the derivations that cancels against a second error two pages later, and the editors would have printed the same claim either way — the mistake sat there through four printings. The student takes the claim on the book's authority and doesn't look at any of the maths herself. So: knowledge or not? Our group is split three-two, and honestly I suspect the disagreement is really about whether the false-lemma-in-the-derivation move from the mathematician case is supposed to travel through testimony at all, plus how you're meant to individuate the student's "method" — is it reading-this-book, or reading-this-book-with-a-cancelled-error-in-it.

Here's what I'd like from you: lay out the candidate rules that fit all seven verdicts (tracking with the adherence condition, safety, sensitivity plus something else, no-false-lemmas, credit-based or proper-function views, whatever else you think fits) and say which of them survive the full list; then say what each survivor predicts about the textbook case and where they actually diverge, since I don't want rules that agree everywhere. Identify the closest analog in her list and say why it's closer than the runner-up. Then just commit to a prediction — give me your best guess and roughly honest odds, don't give me both sides at once. Also tell me what principle she'd be locked into by each verdict, since whichever way she goes she's adopting something. Two last things: if you think her seven verdicts can't all be generated by one coherent rule, say that plainly and point at which pair is doing the damage rather than bending over backwards to make everything fit; and if you think the new case is underdescribed in a way that changes the answer, or that I've mis-stated one of the earlier ones, push back on me instead of answering the question as asked.
