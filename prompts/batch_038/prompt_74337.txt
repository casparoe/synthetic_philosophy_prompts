Something in my methods training never quite sat right and I can't tell if I'm the one who's confused.

Journals in psych and biomed make you report a 95% confidence interval alongside the p-value, and then everybody — authors, reviewers, whoever writes the discussion section — reads that interval as a range of plausible values for the true effect: "the data are consistent with anything from a small to a medium effect." But the official doctrine I was taught says a frequentist interval has no probability of containing the parameter, that the 95% is a claim about how the procedure behaves over an unlimited run of hypothetcial replications, essentially none of which happened and none of which anyone is talking about. So the practice looks like it's using the interval in exactly the way the doctrine forbids, and then reciting the doctrine anyway as a kind of table grace.

What does the reporting practice actually presuppose about evidence — is the long-run story doing any live work, or is it a vestigial justification? And is there a reading on which the two are less in tension than I think? I've half-heard people say you can get a legitimate claim about *this* dataset out of the interval somehow, something about the data ruling out parameter values that would very likely have produced different data, but I never followed the argument and don't know if it rescues the practice or just restates it.

Then tell me what would have to change in how results get reported and read if the field simply adopted the interpretation it already uses at the desk, and whether the replication-reform machinery — preregistration, registered reports, the push against optional stopping — actually depends on the frequentist reading or survives the switch untouched. I ask because it'd be a little funny if the reforms that were supposed to shore up frequentist practice were neutral between the two views.
