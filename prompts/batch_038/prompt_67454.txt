I am preparing a session for a graduate seminar on well-being that is cross-listed with our health policy program, and I want to put a real institutional practice under philosophical pressure rather than re-running the usual thought experiments. Last week a colleague from health economics walked the students through how his field decides which treatments a public payer will fund. As he described it, the appraisal works in quality-adjusted life years: every health state gets a weight between zero, assigned to being dead, and one, assigned to full health, so that a year lived in a state weighted at one half counts the same as half a year in full health. The weights are elicited by trade-off exercises — ask someone whether they would rather live ten years in a given state of ill health or a shorter period in full health; ask whether they would take a treatment with some probability of curing them and some probability of killing them — and those exercises are administered to representative samples of the general public rather than to people actually living in the states being valued. The states themselves are described by a standard instrument built on five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain and discomfort, and anxiety and depression. The resulting table of weights is then treated as the social value of those states, and cost per unit of benefit, measured against a threshold, decides what gets funded. My colleague was candid that this is a deliberate choice of perspective: the payer is society, so society's values should set the weights, and he noted the robust empirical finding that people living with a serious disability rate their own quality of life considerably higher than members of the public rate that same life for them — a divergence the practice resolves in favor of the public.

What puzzles me is that the very same institution behaves as though it holds the opposite view at the bedside. Consent doctrine requires that a patient be told what a reasonable person in *this* patient's position would attach significance to, and a competent refusal must be honored even when everyone else regards the refused state as unbearable; a directive written by an earlier self binds a later self who, by all reports, is contented and does not want it enforced. So the system says, in allocation, that the value of a life in a given health state is fixed by what outsiders would trade to avoid it, and, in clinical ethics, that the value of that same life is fixed by the person living it. I would like you to write, in the voice of a philosopher who has genuinely worked through the health-economics literature rather than a generalist summarizing it, an account of what the allocation practice presupposes. Specifically: which theory of well-being does a system of population-elicited health-state weights require — a hedonist one, a desire-satisfaction one, an objective-list one, or something else? Please keep two questions apart, because I suspect the practice blurs them: what well-being *consists in*, and *whose* judgment about it is authoritative. Say what the practice strictly entails as opposed to what merely sits comfortably with it, and identify the points at which it is underdetermined, so that rival theories would license the same table of weights. Then test it against the classic objection that a life of pleasant illusion is worse than a life in contact with reality: does that objection get any purchase on a health-state weight whose descriptive dimensions are all states of the subject's own functioning and feeling, or does the practice already concede everything the objection wants? Finally, tell me which of the two practices — the population-weighted appraisal or the bedside deference — would have to be revised or abandoned if the appraisal body adopted a hedonist theory of well-being, and which if it adopted an objective-list theory instead, and say whether the inconsistency I have described is a genuine contradiction or two practices answering different questions.

Before you write, work privately through the candidate theories and the two axes I have separated, and check your reconstruction against the details of the practice I have given — but do not show me that working. Think carefully rather than quickly. Lead with your bottom line in three or four sentences, then give the reconstruction. Cite nothing and list nothing to read; argue entirely from the description above. Keep the whole response to no more than 700 words.
