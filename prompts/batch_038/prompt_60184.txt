I teach a second-year seminar that runs Greek and Chinese moral psychology side by side, and last night I argued until nearly two in the morning with a friend who works on classical Chinese philosophy about whether Golding's Lord of the Flies can be used at all as a test case between Mencius and Xunzi. We are doing the novel next week and I wanted it to earn its place. My claim was that the island is essentially Mencius's hillside: the beautiful woods on a mountain near a large state, hewn down season after season until the slope looks bare and passersby conclude the place was never timbered. On that reading the boys are not exhibiting a nature so much as a nature that has been logged, and what the book tracks is the day-by-day hacking that does it — the commiseration and the shame are still detectable precisely in the places where the characters cannot bear to look at what they have done. My friend's counter was sharper and I could not answer it. The boys never arrive as raw material: they bring a whole institutional kit with them — a choir, a prefect system, a shell that functions as a token of speaking rights, an assembly procedure, the very idea of a signal fire. So the island is a Xunzian scene from the first page, an artificial order imposed on a nature that tends toward contention, and the plot is simply what happens when the teachers, standards, and positions of authority that Xunzi treats as indispensable go missing. Ritual decays and the wood springs back crooked because it was never anything else.

I would like a full critical reading rather than a survey of the two positions. Set the accounts of xing against each other properly — Mencius's four sprouts and the child about to fall into the well, his analogy of the tendency to goodness with water tending downward, his insistence that what is required is nourishment and extension rather than remodelling; and Xunzi's opening claim in the chapter on human nature being bad, that whatever is good in people is the product of deliberate activity, his image of crooked wood needing a pressing frame and steaming before it can be straight, and his argument that if nature were already good then sage-kings, teachers, models, and ritual would have nothing to do. Then work the novel through both at the points that actually decide the question. There are five I care about: the assembly under the conch as ritual performing exactly Xunzi's function, and its failure under a leader who has neither a teacher nor any coercive position; Simon's killing, which occurs inside a ceremony the boys improvise themselves in the storm, circling and chanting, so that a collective ritual amplifies violence rather than refining it — which Xunzi's account seems unable to handle unless one simply declares that self-generated ritual counts for nothing without transmission; the aftermath, in which Ralph and Piggy both took part and both subsequently insist it was an accident and call it dancing, so that shame and euphemism survive the worst thing on the island, which Mencius will read as the sprout of shame-and-dislike refusing to die and Xunzi will read as evidence that the artifice is still running; the fact that the deliberately maintained signal fire, which keeps going out, is not what rescues anyone, since rescue comes from the blaze the hunters set to smoke Ralph out, the most unqualified act of the hunt accomplishing what ritual had failed to do; and the officer's arrival, which restores order at once and is backed by a warship, so that the supposed state of nature is enclosed on both sides by an adult world already at war. I want a definite position on each, and I want the reading to be honest about where the novel refuses to be a test case at all — including my friend's best objection, that because the boys arrive fully enculturated, Golding never supplies the control condition either theory requires.

Toward the end of the argument I proposed what I half-jokingly called greenhouse Mencianism: the claim that these are not rival theories but different layers, that Mencius supplies the botany — there really are tendencies that need only nourishing, and their presence is what makes the loss tragic rather than merely mechanical — while Xunzi supplies the greenhouse, since nothing whatever happens without an artificial frame of teachers, standards, and ritual, and the sprouts die within weeks without one. My friend dismissed this as Xunzi with the serial numbers filed off, on the grounds that Xunzi's own argument is precisely that if the tendency were native the frame would be superfluous, and that once the frame is doing all the work the botany is decoration. Take it seriously as a third position and tell me whether it survives that objection; if it does, say whether it is better supported by the hillside passage, which already concedes that the sprouts are lost by repeated hacking and preserved by something external, or whether it is a modern hybrid that neither text would recognize. A few constraints. No bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus — this is for seminar discussion, not a paper. Work from what you know of both texts, and flag any place where you are unsure of the details of a particular passage rather than filling the gap; do not put anything in quotation marks that you are not certain of verbatim, and do not ask me for quotations from Golding either. Note also that my recollection of an essay he wrote in the early sixties — the one in which he reportedly says that the form a society takes follows from the nature of the people in it rather than from any political arrangement — is secondhand and should carry no weight in your argument. Above all, do not turn the novel into an illustration: I want a reading that is faithful in both directions, that says plainly which of the two accounts the book better supports and why, and that ends by telling me what Lord of the Flies contributes to the dispute between Mencius and Xunzi that argument alone, in either direction, cannot supply.
