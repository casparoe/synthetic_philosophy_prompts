I am preparing a seminar on two-dimensional semantics that uses Borges's "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" as its text, and I want a reading that takes both the story and the framework seriously rather than using one as decoration for the other. The story supplies an inventory of odd semantic material: noun-free languages that build the world out of impersonal verbs and monosyllabic affixes, a dissident whose scandalous materialism amounts to claiming that lost coins go on existing unperceived, and the hrönir, objects that come into being because someone expects to find them. Then, in the postscript, a compass bearing characters from one of Tlön's alphabets turns up in our own world, and reality begins to be rewritten along Tlönite lines.

My question concerns the reference of Tlön's vocabulary. Do its terms have primary intensions that are satisfied here, so that the postscript is a case of Tlön's turning out to have been actual all along, and thus a vindication of the thesis that a complete description of a world considered as actual a priori entails its truths? Or is the intrusion instead a shift of secondary intension — a Kripkean change of reference that no reasoning from what we knew beforehand could have predicted, making the story a counterexample to scrutability rather than an illustration of it? Which does the story itself seem to endorse, and is there a diagonal proposition worth naming in the narrator's own account of all this?

Please flag any detail of the story you are unsure of rather than filling the gap plausibly; and if you think this pairing drags a contemporary apparatus onto Borges at the expense of what the story is actually doing, say so plainly instead of humouring me.
