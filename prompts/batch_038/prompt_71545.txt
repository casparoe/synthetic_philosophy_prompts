Okay so at my school there's this Friday thing where a bunch of us argue about ethics with a teacher supervising, and last week we did David Benatar. We got the four-claim setup from Better Never to Have Been (2006): presence of pain is bad, presence of pleasure is good, absence of pain is good even if that good isn't enjoyed by anybody, and absence of pleasure isn't bad unless there's somebody for whom it's a deprivation. Plus the deserted island example, where the fact that nobody ever came into existence on some island and suffered there counts as good, but the fact that nobody ever came into existence there and was happy doesn't count as bad. My friend Theo really does not like antinatalism, so he spent the weekend building this argument to show the asymmetry eats itself, and he walked me through it and I could not find the exit:

(1) Something is good or bad only if it is good or bad *for* somebody. Value has to have an owner.
(2) Benatar's third claim says the absence of pain is good even when nobody is around to enjoy that good — that's the whole point of the island.
(3) So to keep the asymmetry, Benatar has to reject (1) and accept that states of affairs can be good or bad without being good or bad for anyone.
(4) Grant him that. But then the fourth claim has to be handled the same way. The never-born islander also never gets any pleasure, and if value doesn't need an owner, that missing pleasure is a bad-making feature of the world in exactly the same shape as the missing pain being a good-making one.
(5) The obvious escape is "absence of pleasure isn't bad because nobody is deprived," but deprivation is just the owner-requiring idea that (3) already made him give up. You can't ban subjects at step (3) and then reintroduce one at step (5) to save the asymmetry.
(6) So either both absences count or neither does. If neither counts, there's no asymmetry and no reason to think never existing beats existing. If both count, then every happy person you fail to create makes the world worse, and morality says have as many happy kids as you possibly can.
(7) Either way the asymmetry is dead, and the case against procreation goes with it.

I dont want to buy either horn honestly. The second one turns Benatar into someone arguing for maximum population, which is obviously not what the island intuition was supposed to support, and the first one makes him look like he's just asserting the thing he was trying to explain.
