I am a fourth-year PhD student in psychometrics. My dissertation concerns latent-variable measurement in the behavioral sciences, and I am fluent in classical test theory, item response theory, measurement invariance testing, and the construct-validation literature. Last year I took a seminar on classical Indian epistemology, so I have a working but secondhand acquaintance with the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas. I am drafting a chapter that puts the two in conversation, and before I write it I want the translation performed as rigorously as possible, followed by an honest accounting of what refuses to translate.

The target vocabulary is the one I actually use: latent traits and their indicators; item response functions and item parameters; reliability and validity construed as properties of scores-in-context rather than of instruments; calibration, anchoring, and linking across forms; informant reports and other collateral sources; measurement invariance, differential item functioning, and construct contamination; systematic versus random error; and the standing worry that a low score is not evidence for the absence of a trait.

Three parts to the task.

First, restate the Nyāya list of four pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison or analogy, and testimony — in that vocabulary. For each, say what in my framework plays the role it plays, what its conditions of success become, and which failures of that source my framework can represent and which it cannot. My own working glosses are these: perception as the direct indicator; inference as model-based scoring from indicators to a latent trait via an assumed invariant relation, which I take to be what vyāpti does in the Nyāya account; upamāna as linking an unfamiliar instrument to a familiar anchor; and testimony as an informant report whose evidential weight is a function of the informant's reliability. I expect several of these are wrong or shallow. Correct them rather than accommodating them.

Second, four harder cases. (a) Vyāpti appears to require an exceptionless relation between the reason and what is to be proved, whereas every relation I work with is stochastic and known to break down in subpopulations. Is Nyāya inference simply inexpressible in a probabilistic vocabulary, or does Nyāya have resources — I believe something concerning defective or inconclusive reasons — that make the contrast less sharp than it looks from where I sit? (b) The Nyāya requirement that a genuine perception be determinate seems to exclude a non-conceptual stage, yet I understand commentators still posit an indeterminate phase prior to it. Tell me what, if anything, corresponds to that phase in a framework where no datum reaches me uninterpreted, and whether the missing counterpart marks a limitation of my framework or shows that the distinction is doing no work. (c) Whether upamāna survives as a distinct source or collapses into testimony plus perception, which is what I suspect — and whether that suspicion is a finding about Nyāya or an artifact of my habit of individuating sources by evidential role rather than by causal history. (d) The Nyāya treatment of error as a species of misapprehension, on which what is cognized is real but is attributed to the wrong locus. Is that a version of construct contamination or differential item functioning, where an item response is caused by something real that is not the target construct, or does the mapping break once error is treated as a property of a cognition rather than of a score?

Third, the residue report. Sort every claim you have translated into three tiers: those that carry over cleanly, those that carry over only with a distortion you should then name, and those with no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. Then commit to a verdict on the question I actually care about. Does the residue indict psychometrics — is there a normative notion of a source's warrant, or of the conditions under which a cognition succeeds, that my framework has no means of expressing? Or does it deflate the Nyāya side, showing that the quarrel over how many pramāṇas there are, which across the schools ran from one or two up to six, tracks a classificatory choice rather than a substantive disagreement? Give me one answer, not a balance of both.

Two constraints on how you proceed. Do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and present them as translation: if you find yourself rendering a pramāṇa as a reliable belief-forming process, say so explicitly and mark it as a reliabilist reading imposed from outside rather than as what Nyāya holds. And flag any point where you suspect the analogy is being sustained by equivocation on words like information, validity, or source.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Identify the consideration, argument, or piece of Nyāya doctrine most likely to overturn your verdict in the residue report, and say where your reasoning above is most likely to fail — which of the four cases you think you will have handled least defensibly, and why. Please write in American English.
