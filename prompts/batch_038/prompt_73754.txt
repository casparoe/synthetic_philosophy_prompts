Got into a three hour argument with a friend last night (he's an environmental historian, I'm not) about whether you can say a drought caused a famine. His take: weather is just backdrop, only people and institutions cause anything, and every "the climate did it" story is determinism in a nicer font. My take: then explain why the same drought flattened one state and barely registered in its neighbour. Nobody budged. Which is annoying because it's literally my job.

I'm a story editor on a history docuseries. Most episode pitches reach me with a non-human driver bolted on — a volcano, a plague, a multi-decade drought, a monsoon shift. Roughly a dozen a season, maybe 40 minutes each before notes go back to the writer and the showrunner (showrunner has a PhD, writers generally don't). I need a procedure I can actually run on a script's causal claim, not an essay about causation. So: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a passing answer at each one, where the procedure is allowed to kill a claim outright, and where it has to stop and hand off to a human because what's left is a question about which story we're telling rather than what happened. Use whatever is genuinely settled about causes versus background conditions, about attributing anything from proxy data in a single case, and about the political damage the determinist tradition did — but write it for someone who's never read any of that.

Then run it on three pitches: an episode arguing a long drought brought down a Bronze Age palace system; one arguing epidemic disease rather than military skill explains a conquest, where the writer really wants the disease up front; and one arguing a famine was caused by harvest failure. Let the procedure say no on that third one if it wants to. And tell me straight where it'll misfire — which claims it wrongly passes and which it wrongly kills.
