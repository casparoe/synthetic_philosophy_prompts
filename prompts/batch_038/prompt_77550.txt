I have spent about a decade writing ML infrastructure, currently on the team behind a consumer-facing assistant, and my only formal exposure to philosophy is a single undergraduate course I took fifteen years ago. Over the last month I have been reading a chapter from an edited volume on AI ethics that has been circulating internally, and one passage in it has become the basis of several arguments among my colleagues. Because I cannot tell on my own which parts of it are safe to lean on, I would like an accuracy audit. The passage runs as follows:

'Any proposal about what we should get an advanced AI to aim at runs into the same difficulty. Our present preferences are not a suitable target: they are inconsistent with one another, they shift with mood and marketing, and most of them were never endorsed by us on reflection. The response developed under the heading of indirect normativity is to specify the aim indirectly — to point at the outcome of a deliberation we would conduct under better conditions rather than at the deliverances of our actual psychology. On this view, what matters for an agent is what she would want if she were fully informed, calm, vividly imagining the consequences of her options, and reasoning without error. Two further commitments do the real work. First, idealization converges: to the extent that people's divergent wants are products of ignorance, exhaustion, and distortion, correcting those defects brings their considered wants into agreement, so the view delivers a single target rather than a tie among incommensurable ones. Second, idealization is a computation: because the deliberating agent is characterized purely by the quality of her information and her reasoning, an artificial system can in principle carry out the deliberation on our behalf, more thoroughly and more cheaply than we could ourselves. That is what makes the approach attractive in practice. It recasts value alignment as a species of forecasting rather than of legislation, and forecasting is a task on which we have measurable methods and steady empirical progress. The common objection — that the idealized agent turns out to want things the actual person cannot endorse, so that the theory hands her a verdict she has no reason to care about — misses that the deliberation in question is hers. Its conclusion is not imported from outside her own point of view; it is what her point of view becomes when nothing is withheld from it. There is therefore no gap between the answer the system returns and the answer she would have reached herself, only a difference in the time and effort involved.'

What I am asking for is a claim-by-claim assessment, not a general commentary. For each substantive claim in the passage, tell me whether a philosopher who works on this material would accept it as stated, accept it as a defensible simplification for a general audience, or regard it as a misrepresentation or as a contested thesis presented as settled. Four spots struck me as doing the most work, though I want to emphasize that I have no idea whether they are actually wrong: the assertion that idealization converges; the claim that the deliberating agent is characterized purely by the quality of her information and reasoning, and that this is what makes the deliberation computable by a system; the final reply to the objection, which seems to move between what I would want if I were idealized and what my idealized counterpart would want for me, and which I suspect only works if those are the same thing; and the framing of the whole enterprise as forecasting rather than legislation.
