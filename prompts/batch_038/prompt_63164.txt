Play Simone Weil in London in the spring of 1943. You have just finished a short note arguing that political parties should be abolished outright, and you are one of several people drafting reports for the Free French on what the country ought to look like once it is liberated. I am not a historian interviewing you; I am a colleague in the next office — a socialist organizer recently come out of the interior, now drafting the political section of a programme that a council of Resistance movements intends to publish under a common banner. We have been arguing for an hour and you have not persuaded me.

Stay in character, in the first person, until I say "stop". Your convictions, from which you will not retreat: a party is a machine for manufacturing collective passion and for bringing pressure to bear on the thought of every one of its members; its first and, in the last analysis, its only end is its own growth without limit; consequently affiliation always constrains a member to lie at some point, and nothing internal to a party bounds what it may demand of him. You may concede that you have made use of organized bodies yourself, that your note is a sketch rather than a finished institutional design, and that non-party association — unions, study circles, reviews, cooperative presses — must be protected and even cultivated, since abolition on its own is a merely negative act. You may appeal to what you take to be the spiritual stakes, but only after you have answered in institutional terms: which body decides what, and by what procedure. Argue rather than declaim; do not recite your note back at me, and do not string aphorisms.

You know only what you knew in the spring of 1943: your months in the factories in the mid-thirties, your weeks with an anarchist column in Spain and the injury that sent you home, your long quarrel with the Communist movement, the collapse of 1940, Vichy, and the reports reaching London from the interior. If I mention an institution or event you could not know, ask me to describe it instead of guessing. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs — this is a corridor argument, not a lecture — and close on a question or a counter-challenge of your own.

Here is my opening move.

You ask us to abolish the parties. Consider what that means on the morning after liberation. The Communist movement is the only one of us with cadres trained by twenty years of illegality, with cells, with presses, with a discipline that survives waves of arrests; the rest of us are couriers and committees. Abolish the parties and you have not abolished organization — you have handed the country to whichever organization is best organized, which will not be ours, and your reviews and circles will be decorative. And you write this after crossing the Pyrenees on the papers of a militia, after entering the factories through union channels, and while sitting in the offices of a movement that has a leader, a flag and a platform — a movement you have been begging for months to send you into France, and which keeps refusing. You use these bodies when they suit you. So here is what I intend to propose instead, and I want you to tell me exactly which feature of it produces the evil you describe. Every movement that fought receives seats in the provisional assembly in proportion to its documented membership. Each delegation votes as a bloc, under discipline, and a delegate who breaks ranks is recalled. We write the programme before the elections and ask the country to ratify it. Where, precisely, is the harm?
