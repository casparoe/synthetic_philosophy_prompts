Third-year comp pol PhD student here, working on a forecasting side-project rather than anything from my actual dissertation, and I've hit a wall on reference-class selection that I'd like an outside opinion on.

Target question: probability that France's Fifth Republic gets replaced by a new constitutional order within the next 20 years — meaning a new constitution actually adopted, not just a new government or a new prime minister. Background I'll assume you have: it dates from 1958, it was built around de Gaulle, and unless I've got my arithmetic wrong it passes the Third Republic's 70-year run somewhere in late 2028, which people keep mentioning in op-eds as if it settles something.

My problem is that I can generate about seven candidate reference classes for it and they imply wildly different base rates, and I don't have a principled way to chose between them. Roughly as they sit in my notes:

A. French regimes since 1792 — First through Fifth Republic plus the Second Empire. Maximally relevant, n around 6, but most of the members died by foreign invasion or coup, which is not the failure mode I'm forecasting.

B. Post-1945 Western European constitutions: Italy '48, Germany '49, France '58, Greece '75, Portugal '76, Spain '78. Small n and essentially none of them have died, so the durations are right-censored and the naive base rate is "0 out of 6."

C. Every semi-presidential democracy anywhere since 1945. Bigger n, but it puts Finland and Sri Lanka in the same bucket as France.

D. Every wealthy long-standing democracy with a written constitution older than 50 years. Biggest and most stable sample, but so broad that France is a rounding error inside it and the base rate is close to zero by construction.

E. A mechanism-defined class instead of a type-defined one: polities that saw a drop of more than 15 points in a survey measure of trust in institutions over a decade, whatever their regime type. This is my favourite because the membership rule is a covariate rather than a label, but n is only about 40 and I'll admit I picked the threshold after looking at the distribution.

F. Countries that have held a binding referendum on their own constitutional order in the last 40 years.

G. The one my advisor keeps pushing: the long-cycle hegemon list — Portugal, the Dutch, Britain twice, the US — on the grounds that all state-level forecasting should start from the outside view of empires rather than from anything as parochial as constitutions.

Put these into tiers by how much you'd trust each one to anchor a real base-rate forecast, one or two sentences each on why it lands where it does. I especially want to see how you're trading relevance against sample size, what you're doing about censoring and survivorship, and how you handle the fact that these classes overlap heavily so they aren't independent draws from anything — averaging them feels like double-counting but I don't know what to do instead.
