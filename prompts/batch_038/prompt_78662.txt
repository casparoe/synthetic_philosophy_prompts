I'm a junior in high school, and I'm trying to make a decision that I've now explained badly four different times, so I want to write it out carefully. Last month I was accepted into a year-long immersion program in a country whose language I've never studied. If I go, I leave in August, live with a host family, attend school there for eleven months, and come back speaking the language reasonably well — but I would have to re-enter my school as a junior again, a year behind everyone I know now. I've played second clarinet in a regional youth orchestra for three years, and the entire point of that has been the first-chair audition in the fall of senior year, which I'd miss completely and which my teacher says I can't realistically prepare for while living abroad. I'm also in the middle of a friend group that has been the best part of high school for me. My parents are supportive but they keep saying some version of "if you hate it, you can come home in January," which strikes me as not quite a real answer, because the first semester is the intensive language block and leaving in January means coming home with almost nothing to show and no seat left anywhere. There is also a deferral option — I could finish senior year here and go the year after — which everyone tells me "buys time," though I can't see what it buys.

Three people have given me three arguments and they don't line up. My friend Mara says I'll regret not going for the rest of my life, and that the way to decide is to imagine myself at forty looking back on the version who stayed. My older brother, who's a sophomore in college and reads a lot of philosophy, thinks that move is exactly wrong. He says the person who comes back won't be me in the sense the question requires — eleven months of being unable to speak, then slowly being able to, in a place where nobody knows my history, makes a different person with different tastes — so I can't ask what she'd want, and I also can't promise anything on her behalf from here. He says imagining myself at forty doesn't help either, because all I can actually picture is my current self dropped into a forty-year-old's circumstances, which is precisely the thing that wouldn't happen. When I brought this to my friend Dev, who is very quantitative, he said I should treat the year as a purchase of information: even if it changes me, I'd be gaining something I cannot get any other way, and if I value discovery enough the arithmetic still favors going, especially since fluency has measurable career value. My brother's reply was that the information would only be available to the person who gets it, that my current self could never use it, and that the alumni blogs Dev cited are written by people who already went through the change and are reporting the preferences it produced — so they can tell me how it turned out for them and nothing at all about what I'd want.

There's one more thing I'm embarrassed about, which is that part of why I want to go is that it would change me. I've spent my whole life being the careful one — the one who checks the syllabus twice and never drops a class — and I find that I'd rather be someone else than keep being her. Mara says that's just a complicated way of saying I want to go. My brother says it's worse than that, that it's incoherent: a desire to become someone who no longer cares about the things I care about can't be grounded in my caring about them, so wanting to be transformed isn't a reason, it's a mood. So here is what I'd actually like an answer to, and I don't want to be told to follow my heart or to make a pros-and-cons list, because I've done the list and it's useless. First: is my brother right that the ordinary way of choosing — picture each outcome, judge how good it would be, pick the better one — genuinely can't be run on this decision, or is he overstating it? Second: if it can't be run, what am I supposed to do instead, and is there any procedure here that isn't a coin flip wearing a disguise — something that could still count as reasoning rather than just picking? Third: is wanting to be changed a real reason to change, or is he right that it collapses? And fourth: does deferring a year actually help with any of this, or does it just postpone the same problem while costing me the audition either way?
