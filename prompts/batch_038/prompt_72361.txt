My flatmate's doing a philosophy minor and lent me her epistemology reader, which has one of those boxed summaries on Hempel's ravens. It goes roughly: "Hempel wanted a purely formal account of when a report counts as evidence. He assumed two principles — Nicod's criterion, that a report of something which is both F and G confirms 'all Fs are G', and the equivalence condition, that whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms any statement logically equivalent to it. Since 'all ravens are black' is equivalent to 'all non-black things are non-ravens', the green apple in your fruit bowl confirms the raven hypothesis just as strongly as a black raven perched outside would. Hempel knew this looked absurd but refused to budge: the paradox stands, and the only way to escape it is to deny that a conditional and its contrapositive say the same thing, which means giving up classical logic. The upshot is that confirmation depends on logical form alone — how surprising or informative the evidence feels has nothing to do with whether it supports the hypothesis."

Two bits bug me. "Just as strongly" feels wrong for a piece of fruit, and I thought Hempel was the guy who dissolved the paradox rather than the one who declared logic bankrupty. So: which sentences would a confirmation theorist sign off on, which are the reader's own spin, and does the classical-logic claim follow from anything actually true in there? And if Hempel's own way out isn't the one the box gives him, what is it?
