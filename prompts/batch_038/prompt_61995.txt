So, background: I'm not an academic, I do IT for a hospital, and I've spent the last few years going way too deep on Kepler by myself — the actual books where I can get them in translation, plus the biographies, plus a lot of stuff about his astrology specifically. Our local amateur astronomy society has asked me to give the main talk at the November meeting, about 30–40 minutes plus questions. I've talked to them before about eyepieces and collimation and they liked me fine, but this one is going to ruffle feathers and I want to be ready.

The thesis I'm defending: Kepler's astrology is not an embarassing leftover he hadn't managed to shake off, and it's not just a side hustle he did to keep the imperial payroll happy. It's load-bearing. The same instinct that made him insist the planets had to be explained by something physical coming off the sun, and the same obsession with which geometrical figures can actually be constructed with compass and straightedge, drives both the ellipse and his attempted reform of astrology — the 1601 piece where he tries to rebuild astrology on more certain foundations, the astrology book buried inside the Harmony of the World, the extra aspects he adds on the strength of polygons you can construct. He threw out a huge amount of the traditional apparatus (the equal twelvefold division, the sign rulerships, predicting particular events) and kept essentially one idea: that the earth, or the soul in it, notices the angles that rays of light make with each other. So the usual "keep the astronomy, bin the astrology" move cuts him up in a way that doesn't match how he actually reasoned. And it forgets that the astrology he wanted to keep would be unrecognisable to anyone who reads a horoscope column — on his own view he'd be thier enemy too, arguably a harsher one than the debunkers in the room.

Audience, precisely: mostly retirees who have been doing visual observing for decades and like Kepler as a hero, the guy who ground through the numbers and got the ellipse. One retired high-school physics teacher who functions as the society's in-house expert and is loud about never mixing science with woo. Two or three members who quietly read their own horoscopes and get defensive when the topic comes up. And one guy who runs a skeptical blog in town who will very much enjoy himself. Nobody in the room is a historian of anything. They'll know the popular layer: the Rudolphine Tables, the three laws, Tycho's data, maybe that his mother was tried for witchcraft.

Can you build me a prep sheet for the question period? I want the eight to ten questions this particular room will actually ask — in the order I should expect them, and phrased the way they'd phrase them, not the way a professional historian would. Rank them by how much damage each one does to my case. For each give me a two-minute reply I could realistically say out loud, what I should concede up front so I don't look like I'm ducking, and an honest note about where the reply is soft — specifically, which of these expose a real weakness in the thesis rather than just a misunderstanding of it. Then tell me the one question I'm praying nobody asks, the one where I genuinely don't have a good answer and my only options are to bluff or to fold. No reading lists and no citations please, I just don't want to get taken apart in front of forty people who own telescopes.
