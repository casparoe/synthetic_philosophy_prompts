My mom died in March, 84, and I'm giving the eulogy at her service on Saturday. Her church calls it a celebration of life but it's a funeral. I'm not a writer — I run a hardware store — and I've been sitting on this for three weeks.

Here's the problem. For the last twenty-five years of her life my mother fought development in our neighborhood, and she was not the kind you see on the news with a handmade sign. She was worse than that, she was good at it. She read the traffic studies. She knew how to file a comment and when the deadline actually was. She had a vocabulary: parking, school capacity, stormwater, "the scale of the street," the shadow the building would throw on the community garden at 4pm in October. She never once said out loud what she meant, which was that she did not want the kind of people who live in apartments living on her street.

She won in 2011 and killed a four-story building on the old car wash lot. She lost in 2019, and that lot is now 28 units over a bakery. My nephew rents one of them — not because he's especially into the place, because it's the only thing in the neighborhood he was born in that he can afford. And her home aide Rosalba drove in for nine years from two zoning districts over, an hour and a half each way, because there was nothing she could afford within walking distance of the woman she was caring for. My mother adored Rosalba and never once connected those two facts. Or she did, and never said.

So the room Saturday is maybe ninety people and it's split right down the middle. Half are her committee people, who think she saved the street and will be offended if I imply otherwise. The other half includes my brother, my nephew, Rosalba (she asked to come), and six or seven people from the new building who genuinely loved her because she brought them tomatoes in August and knew all their kids names. Nobody is there for a lecture. But I'm not going to stand up and say she fought to preserve the character of her neighborhood like that's an unambigous good, and I'm equally not going to use her funeral to announce she was on the wrong side of history. I don't want a euphemism either. I want something that's actually true, said kindly.

One more thing. Years ago at a tenant meeting I saw a banner with the phrase "the right to the city" on it and I never fully found out what it's supposed to mean — some French guy, I think, but I don't know and I don't want to pretend I do. I keep coming back to it though because I suspect it's the one idea that lets me be honest about her without either canonizing her or trashing her: something about who gets to decide what a place turns into, and who already counts as belonging to it, and whether forty years of residency buys you a vote that a person's first year doesn't. If you think that's the wrong tool, or the right idea but the wrong tool for a eulogy specifically, say so and use something better. But whatever it is has to be doing the load-bearing work of the speech, not getting name-dropped for flavor.

Practical stuff. I'm reading it standing up at a lectern in a church, off paper, no microphone practice. Five or six minutes. Two or three funny moments are allowed and probably necessary — she would have hated being solemn about. Real details you can use: she kept a jar of pennies by the door for neighborhood kids' lost teeth; she could identify any bird in the state by its call but refused for thirty years to learn more than four words of Spanish; she missed exactly one planning commission meeting in twenty-two years, the afternoon my father was buried. Also please don't use the words gentrification, NIMBY, or affordable housing anywhere in the speech — half that room hears those as fighting words and the whole thing collapses into a hearing.

Write it in three sections, in this order:

1. "What I'm not going to say" — around 200 words. The three or four sentences a eulogy like this normally reaches for, and for each one, whether it's false, a dodge, or just cruel to the room, and what it's standing in for.

2. "The eulogy" — the thing I actually read. 700 to 800 words so it lands around five and a half minutes. No stage directions, no bracketed notes, nothing I'd have to delete before I print it.

3. "Swaps" — around 200 words. Two or three alternative passages I can drop in, especially one for the moment Rosalba's commute comes up, because I genuinely don't know how that's going to land and I may need a version that doesn't make my brother cry in front of the committee people.

Don't give me sources or a reading list or tell me to look anything up. Just make it good.
