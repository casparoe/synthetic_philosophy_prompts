I am revising a novel whose central figure is a self-taught bridge builder in a river town: no schooling in the subject, unable to state a single principle behind her judgments, yet every structure she has raised in forty years is still standing, and most of those her trained rivals raised are not. In the middle of the book a young structural engineer newly arrived from the city tells her that she does not know what she is doing — that she has merely been right so far, and that the town has been sleeping on a debt that will come due the first time her luck turns.

I showed the chapter to a friend who teaches philosophy, and he said the exchange was better than I knew, because the young man's charge is an old one and her answer, if I give her one, should come from what he called the swamping argument. He put it to me with an analogy he says he took from a paper by Linda Zagzebski. A good cup of coffee brewed by a machine you bought because a magazine rated it reliable is no better for having come from that machine: once the cup is in your hand, the reliability of what produced it adds nothing to it, being swamped by the goodness already in the cup. So too, he said, with a true belief arrived at reliably — once the belief is true, the reliability of its source adds no further value, and the whole insistence that knowledge is worth more than true opinion collapses. Which means, he concluded, that my bridge builder loses nothing by not knowing: her standing bridges are worth exactly what the engineer's would be, his charge is empty, and I may keep the scene only if I let her say so.

I would rather write the scene honestly than win it cheaply. So please lay out the analogy's correspondences for me — what in the coffee case is meant to play the role of her judgment, of her bridges, of the truth of her beliefs, of the magazine's rating, of the drinker — and then tell me where the mapping gives out. Some breaks will be idle (a machine takes no interest in its cups) and some will cut to the intended use, and I need to know which are which; in particular, whether a craft exercised across forty years and many bridges can be run through an analogy built around a single cup. Tell me also whether the conclusion he drew follows from the premise he borrowed. His young engineer's charge was about the future, and I have some sense that what Socrates himself says in the Meno about true opinion — the business about images that run away unless they are tied down — is about the future too, so that the swamping point may never touch it. Say whether that instinct is sound or whether I have muddled two different questions, and if there is a distinction here that my friend has run together, name it plainly enough that I could defend the scene against a reader who knows the material. Finally, if the analogy cannot carry the weight he puts on it, propose a better one for the same purpose, since I would sooner hand my builder an image she could actually use than a slogan she could not.

Please do not give me a balanced survey and leave me to choose among it. Take a position: is the analogy sound as he has deployed it, is the scene defensible as it stands, and what should she say back to him? Give your reasons, and flag any place where you think I have described the analogy or the Meno in a way a real reader of either would correct.
