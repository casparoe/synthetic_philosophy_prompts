I built this for a reading group talk on Wigner's 1960 essay and now I think it proves way too much, but I can't find the leak.

(1) We only notice the mathematics that works. The mathematics that fails to describe anything gets quietly dropped or repurposed, so the surviving sample is biased toward success.

(2) Take any formal apparatus rich enough and any domain messy enough, and some fragment of the apparatus will carve that domain up usefully. There are vastly more fragments than there are domains, so a fit is combinatorially guaranteed rather than lucky.

(3) So it isn't surprising that some piece of mathematics describes some piece of nature.

(4) But that's all Wigner's puzzle ever amounted to — that some piece of mathematics describes nature.

(5) So the puzzle dissolves: applicability is an artifact of which fragments we happen to keep around to look at.

(6) And the reasoning generalizes. For any body of data, some hypothesis or other in the space of hypotheses accommodates it, so no theory's empirical success is ever surprising either.

(7) If no empirical success is surprising, success isn't evidence: Leverrier's perturbation calculation pointing at Neptune, or Dirac's equation turning up a positron, carry no more weight than a horoscope that happened to come out right.

(8) But (7) is insane — it wipes out the whole of confirmation, including the no-miracles style inference that the success of science is best explained by its theories being roughly true.

(9) Therefore (1) is false, selection effects don't explain applicability, and Wigner's puzzle goes right back where it started.
