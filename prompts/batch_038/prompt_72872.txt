Last weekend I had a three-hour argument with a friend — she is an ancient historian, I work on ethics — about Plutarch's *Parallel Lives*, and it turned entirely on a single sentence that she kept repeating and I kept failing to pin down.

The occasion was our reading group's discussion of biography as a vehicle for moral education. I objected that the *Lives* are a poor vehicle precisely because so much of their most vivid material is unreliable. I offered the familiar case: Solon's celebrated meeting with Croesus, which cannot be squared with the chronology, since Croesus came to the throne long after Solon's years of travel would have ended; and Plutarch, when he reaches that story in the *Life of Solon*, says in substance that he will not throw out something so renowned and so consonant with Solon's character merely on account of chronological tables that men keep revising without ever bringing them into agreement. My point was that a work willing to retain an incident it knows to be chronologically impossible because the incident is edifying cannot be asked to carry the moral weight we want to put on it.

Her reply, delivered as though it closed the matter, was the opening of the *Life of Alexander*: "For it is not Histories that I am writing, but Lives" — followed, in the same breath, by his remark that the most illustrious deeds do not always display virtue or vice most clearly, and that some slight thing, a phrase or a jest, often reveals character better than battles with thousands slain. Her position was that I was faulting a biography by the standards of a different genre, which is a category mistake: the *Lives* aim at character, and character is exactly what the small anecdotes deliver. I said that disclaiming an aim cannot by itself lower the standard for retaining material, and that if truth is irrelevant to the moral use, the use collapses anyway, since admiration and imitation seem to require believing that the thing happened. She answered with hagiography: saints' lives stuffed with impossibilities formed moral sensibilities for a thousand years, so belief in the particular incident evidently is not what does the work. I said hagiography is the degenerate case, not the vindication. Neither of us budged.

I now suspect that the sentence she kept quoting is doing several distinct jobs, and that we were arguing past each other. What I want is therefore a careful disambiguation rather than a verdict on who won. Please lay out, as a menu, the different claims that "lives, not histories" could be making, and for each say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. In particular:

1. Separate readings about the classification of the work (what kind of thing it is) from readings about the standards it must meet, from readings about what its author was attempting, and from readings about what a reader is entitled to demand of it. I have been calling these the taxonomy, standards, aim, and immunity readings; replace my labels if you have better ones, but preserve the distinctions, and say which of the four her move actually required.

2. Distinguish a reading on which the difference between a history and a life is one of scope — the biographer admits private, domestic, and trivial material the historian excludes — from one on which it is a difference of warrant — the biographer may retain an anecdote on evidence a historian would reject. My objection needs the second; her reply, I think, supplies only the first. Is that diagnosis correct?

3. Is there a reading on which the slogan is true and my objection nevertheless survives? Can a life be faulted *as a life*, and not merely as a failed history, for keeping an edifying impossibility, if the moral work assigned to it depends on the incidents being real?

4. What does Plutarch's own practice suggest about which reading he held: his handling of the Solon story, his habit of reporting rival versions of an anecdote and sometimes declining to choose between them, and the formal comparison closing most of the pairs, in which he pronounces on which of the two men was the better?

5. Does her hagiography reply establish that belief in particular incidents is inessential to moral learning from a life, or only something weaker — for instance, that such lives functioned inside communities that already supplied the beliefs, so that the text transmitted rather than generated them? If the weaker reading, what follows for her use of it against me?

6. Is there a middle position we both missed — say, that a fabricated or impossible incident can still serve as evidence, not of the subject's character, but of what the author and his audience took such a character to consist in, which may be all a moral learner needs? If so, what has to be true for that to work, and does it survive the pairing structure, in which each life is under pressure to supply a counterpart to its mate?

Please write in American English. I am not after references, sources, or a reading list — only the readings themselves, cleanly distinguished, with your assessment of each and of the inferences my friend and I were separately drawing from them.
