I retired five years ago from thirty-eight years in population ecology, and I have been working my way, rather late, through philosophy of explanation and the historiography of the Holocaust. Since last autumn a handful of us — mostly former scientists, one ex-magistrate — have met monthly to read in this area, and we took Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men* and Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* in consecutive sittings. The two accounts are familiar enough: Browning's Reserve Police Battalion 101, middle-aged conscripts from Hamburg found unfit for field service, whose commander in the summer of 1942 offered his men the chance to step out of the shooting at Józefów, an offer taken up by a dozen or so out of roughly five hundred; Browning's explanation is built from obedience to authority, conformity with one's comrades, careerism and the routinisation of atrocity, and he draws openly on the Milgram material. Goldhagen, who had already attacked Browning's reading in a review some years before the book appeared, argues instead that a specifically German eliminationist antisemitism made these men willing participants, and that a single cultural-cognitive factor of that kind suffices to explain the killing. The scholarly reception of Goldhagen was, as everyone in our group knows, close to unanimously hostile.

What puzzles me is that this near-unanimity does not seem to have produced agreement about what was actually being disagreed about, and our own sessions have gone round in circles for three months. Two features of the material strike me as important. First, both authors work substantially from the same postwar prosecution records concerning the same battalion, so the raw evidence is not in dispute; and both accept a datum that looks decisive in opposite directions — that no German appears to have been seriously punished for refusing to kill. Browning absorbs this into a story about peer pressure and career incentives, where the sanction is social rather than legal; Goldhagen reads it as showing the absence of any coercion to explain away. Second, the books may not share an explanandum at all. In my old field we were taught to keep mechanism apart from distribution and history, and to resist treating Tinbergen's separate questions as competitors. On that model, Goldhagen's thesis looks like a claim about an enabling condition — a cultural state of affairs without which the machinery of mass killing could not have been set in motion at that scale — while Browning's is a claim about the sufficient local conditions operating on particular men on particular days. Two such claims can both be true, and much of the temperature in this controversy may come from each side hearing the other as denying something it never asserted. I also suspect a hidden empirical question is doing work that neither book answers, namely the base rate: what fraction of Germans, and of order police in other battalions, complied, refused, or exceeded what was asked of them, given that both authors are reasoning from what is in effect a convenience sample.

So I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is this a substantive disagreement about causes, a partly verbal one — for instance about what 'willing' is being contrasted with, coerced or unideological — or two answers to two different questions lodged under a single title? Where exactly do the sides differ about standards of evidence: does the same postwar testimony function as a record of behaviour for one author and as a record of motive for the other, and is that difference defensible? Is there a genuinely empirical component that better comparative work on other police battalions, on the Wehrmacht, and on the non-German auxiliaries could in principle settle? And on the basis of your diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a dataset, a reframing of the explanandum, or nothing at all — and also where my proximate-versus-historical analogy from biology breaks down, since I expect it transfers less cleanly than I would like. Finally, please keep separated throughout what is settled among people who work on this and what remains genuinely contested; I would rather not have the scholarly reception of Goldhagen's book presented as though it had answered the underlying question, and if you think a contested position is the right one, say so and say that it is contested.
