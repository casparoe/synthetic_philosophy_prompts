So I've been slowly grinding through Plutarch's Parallel Lives for about four months now, mostly off a throwaway recommendation in a comment thread somewhere, and it has done something to my head that I can't quite name. The thing that gets me is how deliberate he is about method. The preface to the Alexander/Caesar pair basically announces that he isn't writing history at all — he doesn't much care about the sieges or the battle dispositions or the casualty figures, he cares about the small thing, the offhand remark, how a man takes an insult, what he does with his body when no one important is watching, because that's supposedly where character shows through. And then he operationalizes it: a Greek paired with a Roman, and a formal comparison at the end of the pair — the synkrisis — that forces you to actually rank them rather than just enjoy the stories. That is an argument, whether or not he meant it as one. It's the claim that you can extract general moral knowledge out of carefully cross-examined individual lives, and it's almost exactly the claim Zagzebski makes in Exemplarist Moral Theory two thousand years later, where admiration picks out the exemplars and the concepts — virtue, the good life, right action — get their content from the exemplars rather than the other way round.

And then there's the other camp, which I find at least as compelling and which I can't get out of. The Harman/Doris situationist line: the experimental record doesn't support global character traits, behavior tracks situations far more than dispositions, and what Plutarch is actually running is a trait-attribution machine on a hopelessly censored sample. On that reading the Lives aren't moral epistemology, they're hagiography by construction — survivors only, written by the literate party, in a narrative form that smears coherence backwards onto a life that probably never had it, and with the "small revealing detail" doing exactly the work that the fundamental attribution error does in the lab. I keep thinking the Church took this seriously enough to institutionalize it: the devil's advocate's whole job was to attack the character case for a candidate, to hunt for the misread evidence, and that office got gutted in the 80s, which is its own weird data point. I tried this whole argument on a friend who does personality psych for a living and she waved it off with the usual counter-literature — aggregate rather than single-act consistency, predictive validity, the fact that Mischel's famous .30 "personality coefficient" is roughly the size of a lot of correlations everybody in medicine is happy with — and I couldn't tell if that was a good answer or a motte-and-bailey retreat, which annoyed me.

What I want is a dialogue between the two positions, both held by people who are genuinely good at their jobs — a Plutarchian exemplarist and a situationist moral psychologist — and I want to seed it with something concrete so it doesn't collapse into a lit review. Say they've both been asked to weigh in on a proposed study, a sort of pre-registered Plutarch protocol: nominate a couple hundred people widely taken to be moral exemplars and a couple hundred matched controls, have adversarial biographers write up each life to a fixed evidentiary standard with an actual devil's advocate assigned to every file, and then test whether the character attributions that come out predict behavior in situations the biographers never saw. Have them fight over whether that design tests anything at all. The exemplarist should be able to argue that it's roughly the right test and that she welcomes it, because her whole view is that the concepts come from the exemplars and there's no prior definition of courage to check the nominations against; the psychologist should be able to argue that the contamination happens upstream of the protocol — who gets nominated, who is legible, whose life leaves a record, the fact that admiration may track the *narratability* of virtue rather than virtue — and that no amount of adversarial editing fixes a sample selected on the dependent variable. I want real back-and-forth: each one responding to what the other just said, conceding real ground where it's owed, and neither one allowed to be a strawman or to win on a technicality. Somewhere in the middle I'd like them to hit the question of whether this is even a substantive disagreement or partly verbal — whether "character" means a cross-situational disposition for one of them and a local if-then profile for the other, and whether anything is left to argue about once you fix the terminology. And please don't have them cite specific papers with authors and years; describe the paradigms instead (the seminary hurry, the obedience setups, the Good Samaritan-style helping studies, whatever), because I can't check them and I don't want invented ones. End with each of them stating, in their own voice, what evidence would actually make them give the thing up — not a polite non-answer — and if they end in agreement, make it an agreement that costs at least one of them something real. Around 1500 to 2000 words, plain prose, no grandstanding.
