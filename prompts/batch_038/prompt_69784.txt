So last night I got into a two-hour arguement with a friend of mine — she's a medievalist, I teach history of science — and I've come away thinking my students write exactly the argument we were having, only worse. It was about whether the Scientific Revolution is a thing at all, and secondarily whether the Middle Ages and the Renaissance are things at all either.

Her position is basically that period labels are retrospective narrative furniture: their content gets fixed by the story you tell afterwards, so asking whether the Scientific Revolution happened is like asking whether the third act happened. Category mistake. My counter was that this is way too quick, because a retrospective label can still be descriptively wrong — if the mechanical machinery that supposedly arrives in the seventeenth century was already worked out at Merton College in the fourteenth and drawn as a diagram by Oresme, then the label isn't just flattering us, it's misreporting a break that wasn't there. And then she said my counter begs the question, because "already worked out" and "precursor" are themselves hindsight words: an impetus that spends itself down is not momentum, and calling the first a precursor of the second just smuggles back in the narrative I claimed to be testing. I think she's half right and I can't work out which half, which is precisely why I want it on the exam.

Context: second-year survey, about 60 students, two-hour paper. They've done the medieval material (Oxford calculators, the mean speed theorem, Oresme's diagrammatic treatment of it, Buridan on impetus) and the seventeenth-century material (Copernicus, Galileo on falling bodies, mechanical philosophy, the Royal Society, Newton). They have not done any historiography-of-historiography and I don't want to pretend they have.

Write me ONE essay question, 30 marks, answerable in about 45 minutes, that cannot be passed by reciting "some historians say it was constructed, others say it was real." It has to make them pull apart three claims that everyone fuses:

1. the descriptive discontinuity claim — did content, method and self-understanding arrive as a package in the seventeenth century, or was the package assembled piecemeal over four centuries;
2. the honorific claim — that "revolution" is doing self-congratulatory work for a tradition that wants a founding moment, independently of whether the discontinuity is real;
3. the meta question about what a period concept has to be in order to be legitimate — whether it needs to carve nature, or whether it can be perfectly fine as bookkeeping, and what would make a piece of bookkeeping a *bad* one.

And two further things that came straight out of the argument, which I really want tested:

- The unit problem. "There was a revolution" can be true of one unit of analysis and false of another: content (laws of motion), method (mathematization plus controlled experiment), institutions (societies, the paid investigator role), and the actors' own self-understanding (the seventeenth-century people genuinely believed they were doing something unprecedented — and note that this last one is evidence about attitudes, not evidence about discontinuity, which is a distinction students never make). A good answer picks a unit, says why, and notices that different units can give different verdicts on the same dates.
- The precursor trap. Showing that X existed earlier doesn't by itself refute a discontinuity claim, since literally everything has precursors; and "precursor" may itself re-import the narrative under test. I want them to say what more you'd need — was the earlier work load-bearing for the later, did it survive institutionally, was it available where it mattered, did it do the same explanatory job — and to handle the fact that impetus and inertia are similar-but-not-the-same without concluding either "so it's the same" or "so the medieval stuff is irrelevant."

Part (b) of the question, worth about a third of the marks: does the same criticism transfer to "the Middle Ages" and "the Renaissance"? I want them to see that these labels fail, if they fail, for structurally different reasons — one is honorific-and-positive, one is residual and defined entirely by what it's between, and one borrows a polemic that the period's own actors were running against the period before it. Saying "they're all just constructed" should get nothing.

Deliverables, please:

- the question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the mark split shown;
- a model answer of around 700 words, written at the level of a strong second-year, that actually takes a position and defends it rather than shrugging both ways, and that uses the medieval material concretely rather than gesturing at it;
- a marking scheme out of 30, broken into five or six components, and for each component: what a first-class answer does, what a competent-but-B answer does, and the specific mistake that costs marks. I want those mistakes named explicitly, including: treating "constructed" as though it meant "didn't happen"; answering the discontinuity question by listing seventeenth-century achievements; using "it depends what you mean by revolution" as a conclusion instead of a starting move; and, in part (b), lumping all three period labels together.

No bibliography, no reading list, no suggestions of what to assign them — and don't let the model answer lean on name-checking historians as a substitute for argument. A name or two is fine where a student would naturally drop it, but I want the reasoning to run on the historical material itself.
