I've got a 90 minute slot in our history-of-thought module and the audience is first-year PhD econ students who've just done a semester of monetary theory, so they walk in fully convinced they already know what Hume's monetary essays say. The two assigned pieces are Of Money and Of the Balance of Trade, from the Political Discourses of 1752.

The thing I actually want to land isn't the textbook summary. The summary they arrive with is "Hume stated the quantity theory and discovered the price-specie-flow mechanism." I want them leaving with close to the opposite emphasis: Hume's money is emphatically not neutral on the timescale he cares about, and that non-neutrality is the whole engine of his policy argument — a slow, continual inflow is exactly what he wants for a trading nation because industry and employment respond before prices do, whereas a one-off windfall just bids the price level up and leaves you where you started. And there's a tension at the end I want them sitting with rather than resolving: the flow mechanism says the inflow stops once prices equalise, but the good-for-industry effect lives entirely in the inflow not having stopped yet.

Plan the 90 minutes for me, don't write the lecture. What I need: the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what — especially what has to be in place before "prices rise gradually, not all at once" can be heard as an argument instead of a period detail. The single running example that carries it (I'm toying with a small port town receiving bullion, but tell me what that example has to contain, what it must not contain, and whether a numeric version helps or just gets them doing arithmetic instead of thinking). And the misconceptions, each with the exact point in the sequence where it gets headed off. I'm expecting at least these four: "so it's basically sticky prices, a nominal rigidity story" (he has no such theory — the effect is about who gets the money first and how long prices take to catch up); "the specie flow is an automatic stabiliser that restores equilibrium" (it's a tendency, and it's doing something other than stabilising); "he wants money flowing in so he's a mercantilist after all"; and "he distrusts paper credit so he's a hard money conservative" — that last one needs care, since the distrust is real and the question is what it's actually an objection to.

Also tell me what to cut, and to say out loud that I'm cutting it. I'm genuinely torn on whether to mention the argument that he wasn't first to the flow mechanism and probably picked bits of it up from earlier pamphleteers: useful deflation of the myth, or noise at this level?

Finally, one question I can ask in the last ten minutes that would show whether the non-neutrality point landed, rather than them nodding politely. No need to suggest further reading, they've got the two essays.
