Okay, context: I'm not a philosophy person, I work in public health policy, but my partner is in an informal reading group that's been slogging through Wittgenstein's last notebook — the one on certainty — and a few weeks ago someone circulated a summary of it that has since turned into the group's shared vocab. I think its steering them somewhere bad, but I'm the outsider here so I don't trust my own read. The summary, roughly as it was written:

"Wittgenstein's final notes are a refutation of skepticism. The trick is that some propositions — that I have two hands, that the earth existed long before I was born, that a chair doesn't quietly stop existing when nobody is looking at it — aren't held fast by evidence but by the whole system of beliefs around them; they're the hinges on which our questions and doubts turn. Note that this is not a pragmatic point, not that doubting them would be too costly or pointless: doubting them would leave you with nothing left to doubt against, so the skeptic's doubt isn't false, it's literally meaningless. Notice what follows. We don't know the hinges, we're certain of them, and certainty here isn't a knowledge state at all but a kind of animal trust. Different communities have different hinges, and since there's no neutral ground above them, two people whose hinges clash can't actually argue — each is simply right inside their own framework. Wittgenstein was therefore a relativist, which is also why philosophers of religion find him so useful: faith becomes a framework with its own hinges, and nothing you say from outside can touch it."

Someone in the group then used the last couple of sentences to argue that you basically can't reason with a climate skeptic or an anti-vaxxer, you can only hope they get converted into your framework, which struck me as both morally grim and suspiciously convenient. I'm inclined to think that conclusion doesn't follow, but I could easily be the one who's confused, and I don't want to walk into the next session with a confident wrong opinion.
