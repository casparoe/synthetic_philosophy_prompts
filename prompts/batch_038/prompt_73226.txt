I direct a small graduate seminar on diachronic norms of belief, ten students, meeting weekly. Two of my doctoral students — I will call them R. and M. — argued for the better part of an hour after our session on van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I have asked each of them to set out their case in writing. Before I read those, I want an independent adjudication of the exchange as it actually went, reconstructed below.

For reference, both accept the following as the target: if you are now certain that at some later time your credence in A will be p, rationality requires your present credence in A to be p; in its general form, your present credence in A conditional on your future credence in A being p is p. Neither disputes the formal statement; the dispute is over its normative standing and scope.

(1) R. opened with the diachronic Dutch book. If your present credence in A is q while you are certain your later credence will be p ≠ q, a bookie can sell you a bet now and a further bet later that together guarantee a net loss whatever happens, and the construction is formally parallel to the Dutch book for conditionalization. R. added that Reflection is what licenses the ordinary practice of deferring to one's better-informed future self, and that the expected-accuracy argument for conditionalization extends to it: setting your present credence to your expected future credence maximizes expected accuracy provided you expect to conditionalize.

(2) M. replied that the bookability argument proves nothing here. The book exploits the dispositions of two different time-slices with different information, and the guaranteed loss is never visible from the vantage point of any single slice; the disanalogy with conditionalization is exact, since there the sure loss is registered by one agent assessing one hypothesis against her own evidence at one time. At most, M. said, Reflection is a norm of prudence for agents who care about not being exploited across time — not a requirement of epistemic rationality.

(3) R. conceded the pragmatic framing and narrowed the claim: Reflection is a theorem, not a primitive. Given (i) that you expect your later credences to result from conditionalizing on your total evidence and (ii) that your evidence sets are nested, so that each later set contains the earlier, your present credence must equal your expectation of your future credence, and under certainty about that future value Reflection falls out. Its epistemic standing, R. said, is therefore no shakier than that of conditionalization itself.

(4) M. answered that this is a hollow victory. A principle that is derivable only under assumptions that already secure deference has nothing to say about the cases that generated the debate — anticipated interference with one's belief-forming processes, anticipated loss of evidence. If Reflection is silent exactly where the questions are live, restricting it to cases where it is automatically satisfied is not defending it.

(5) R. pressed the burden back: departing from Reflection requires positive reason to distrust one's future self, and that reason is itself a present opinion about what is true, so the agent who departs is not thereby escaping the demand for coherence — she is meeting it with additional premises. Absent such premises, deference is the only non-arbitrary default; the alternative policy of inverting one's expected future credence, R. said, has no rationale at all once the future self is judged unreliable, since unreliability licenses no specific direction of error.

(6) M. produced two cases. First: you know that at four o'clock you will be given a compound that produces an irresistible conviction that the capital of Australia is Sydney, so your later credence will be near one; plainly your present credence should not match it. Second, and stronger: you now know the exact time to the minute, but you also know that at four o'clock you will have forgotten it and will then be reasoning perfectly rationally — conditionalizing impeccably on a total evidence set that is a strict subset of your present one — and will form a credence in a time-sensitive proposition that differs from yours. In the second case M. grants R. everything about the future self's rationality, so the failure cannot be blamed on irrationality or interference; it is nesting that does the work, and nesting routinely fails. Conclusion: Reflection describes what happens when certain conditions obtain; it is not a norm.

(7) R.'s rejoinder: M.'s argument, if sound, proves too much. Conditionalization likewise fails when evidence is lost, yet nobody infers that conditionalization is not a norm; we say it is a default norm whose applicability depends on the agent's evidential situation. Reflection stands or falls with conditionalization, so M. has either shown that neither is normative or that both are normative with exceptions — and the second is R.'s position.

Please adjudicate move by move. Rate each of the seven moves on a five-point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with two or three sentences of justification per rating, and flag any move where you think the error is substantive rather than one of emphasis — including any place where a participant's argument depends on sliding between the certainty form of the principle and the general conditional form. Then state who is more right overall, and identify what both are missing: in particular, whether there is a version of the dispute on which they are not disagreeing, and whether move (6)'s second case really has the force M. claims. Hard limit: 700 words for the whole response.
