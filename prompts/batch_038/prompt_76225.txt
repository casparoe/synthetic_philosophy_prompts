Been putting together where I actually stand on expected value reasoning and I want to know where it breaks before I say any of this out loud in a seminar. The list:

(1) Expected utility maximization is the whole of right choice for me. No weighting of probabilities, no principled rule about ignoring the tiny ones.
(2) Utility has to be bounded. That's how I handle St Petersburg -- no unbounded payoff functions, so the game just has a finite value.
(3) The longtermist argument basically works. A small chance of shaping an astromically large future can swamp the near-term considerations, and I think thats a feature not a bug.
(4) I wouldnt pay up in a Pascal's mugging -- not because I'm giving up on EV, but because the probability the mugger is claiming can't be the real one.
(5) Dust specks over torture. If the number of people is big enough, tiny harms add up past one genuinely awful thing.
(6) But I reject the Repugnant Conclusion. A zillion lives barely worth living is not better than a smaller population of excellent ones.
(7) And I still feel like there's something wrong with any theory on which some gigantic-but-finite sum would be worth paying to play St Petersburg.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank them by how bad they are, give me the cheapest repair for each and what that repair costs me elsewhere, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I dont have a favorite here -- don't assume I'm trying to protect any one of them, and if the honest answer is that the whole package is fine and I'm worrying over nothing, say so.
