QUESTION 3 (25 points)

(a) State the Formula of Universal Law. Explain what Kant means by a maxim, and why his test is applied to maxims rather than to actions or to outcomes. (6)

(b) Kant holds that a maxim can fail the test in two distinct ways, involving a contradiction in conception and a contradiction in the will respectively. Explain the difference between them. (6)

(c) Apply the distinction to the maxim of making a false promise in order to obtain money one needs, and to the maxim of neglecting one's natural talents. Say which failure each incurs and why. (8)

(d) What kind of duty issues from each failure, and what does the difference imply about the latitude an agent has in discharging the duty? (5)

STUDENT'S ANSWER

The Formula of Universal Law tells us to act only on that maxim through which we can at the same time will that it become a universal law of nature. A maxim is the agent's subjective principle of volition: a general policy specifying what she will do, in what circumstances, and for what end. Kant tests maxims rather than actions because the same action-type can be performed on very different principles, and he tests maxims rather than outcomes because he is not asking whether the world the action produces would be a good one; he is asking whether the agent's own principle can coherently be willed as law.

A maxim fails in the first way when it cannot even be thought as a universal law without contradiction — the universalized maxim is self-defeating or self-contradictory. It fails in the second way when it can be thought without contradiction but cannot be willed, because a rational agent who willed it would find herself willing something at odds with what she must will.

False promising is a contradiction in conception. If everyone in financial difficulty promised whatever he liked with a secret reservation not to perform, no one would believe such avowals, and the practice the liar is counting on would disappear; his maxim destroys itself as soon as it is made a universal law. This yields a perfect duty, which binds strictly and admits of no exception. Neglecting one's talents is a contradiction in the will. We can perfectly well conceive a world in which everyone lets their gifts rust, as one might conceive a world of idle South Sea Islanders; but no rational being would choose to live in such a world, since it would leave each of them worse off than a world in which talents were cultivated. This yields an imperfect duty, which binds as to the end but leaves latitude about when, how often, and how much to do.

Someone might object that an agent can always redescribe her maxim narrowly enough to make it universalizable. In this case Kant has a reply available: the maxim's description is fixed by the end the agent actually sets herself, and the false promiser cannot describe himself as acting on a policy of promising without thereby describing the very institution he is exploiting. Whether this reply generalizes is a contested question, but it is not obviously question-begging.

What the contradiction in conception ultimately shows is that the lender could not consent to the maxim under which he is being used, and that impossibility of consent is just what the failure of universalizability consists in.
