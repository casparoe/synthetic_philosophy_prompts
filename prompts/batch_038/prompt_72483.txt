I work on measurement-and-incentives stuff and I keep hitting the same wall with pay for performance in primary care, so I want a philosophical read rather than another econometrics take. Here's the practice, described fully so you don't need to know it: a health system pays GP practices against a long published list of clinical indicators — blood pressure controlled in the hypertensive register, HbA1c under some threshold for diabetics, that sort of thing. The score is public, and the money follows the score. But there's an escape hatch: practices can "except" individual patients out of an indicator, with a logged reason — patient refuses, just registered, died mid-year, dementia, or clinical judgement that the target isn't right for this person. Excepted patients come out of the denominator, so the practice's reported percentage goes up, and the exceptions are audited rather than hidden.

Standard Goodhart talk says this is a disaster: a public, paid-on, gameable metric. But the design clearly doesn't see the escape hatch as a disaster, it sees it as load-bearing. What theory of measurement does that clause presuppose?

I've been playing with a three-way split — use it, or attack it if you think it's the wrong cut. A number can work as evidence about a target, in which case you want it to track and you worry about noise; as an instrument for producing the target, in which case you want it to motivate and you worry about the motivation landing on the wrong behaviours; or as a term in a contract between parties who each partly know what they want, in which case you want it legible and renegotiable and you worry about the renegotiation never ending. Which of those is the exception clause doing its work under? It looks like a measurement correction and behaves like a contract amendment, and I can't tell whether that's a confusion in the design or the whole point of it.

Then: does one number have to be able to play more than one of those roles for the practice to hang together, or is there a quiet assumption that a single score can be evidence and incentive at once without either job corrupting the other? Tell me which rival views the clause rules out — my hunch is that anything like "the metric's job is to represent the patient's health accurately" is flatly incompatible with the design, but I want to know if I'm overreading that. Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and flag the spots where it's underdetermined, since I suspect several theories license it equally well and the interesting part is which one you'd pick and why.

Finish with the counterfactual: if we insisted these scores are evidence first and incentives only derivatively, what exactly has to go — the exceptions, the publicity, the payment, or something about how the denominator gets defined in the first place? I want a concrete redesign and its costs, not "measure better." No references needed, I'll fill those in myself.
