ok so context: i'm in a master's seminar on uncertainty this term, and on the side I run a reading group of about 14 people, half of whom have never seen a single measure-theoretic proof. Every session degenerates because someone says "I don't believe in imprecise probabilities" meaning something completely different from what the next person means by it. One guy thinks it's about Ellsberg urns, another thinks it's about not being able to pin down utilities, a third thinks the world's chances are literally vague. So I want to hand out an intake thing in week two: a decision tree that sorts people into camps by what they'd actually say about cases, not by which label they've picked up.

Here's what I need.

**Shape:** 9–12 nodes, 7–9 leaves. Every node is a concrete case or a yes/no question about one, phrased so someone with zero formal background can answer it. No node may use technical vocabulary — no convexity, no dilation, no E-admissability, no talk of "credal sets." That vocabulary lives on the leaves, where you name the position each route lands on. Format as a plain-text tree with yes → node 7 / no → node 9 style arrows, plus a scoring key that maps answer strings onto leaves.

**Build it from the logical space, not from a list of names.** First lay out the axes you're branching on and how many cells the combinations generate, then place the positions in them. I want a separate table of unoccupied cells with a one-line diagnosis each: is nobody there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody's just bothered yet?

The axes I actually care about — make these real branch points, not decoration:
1. where the fuzziness lives: beliefs, utilities/desirabilities, or the chances themselves
2. what you do once you have a set: maximin over it, a permissive rule like E-admissibility, Γ-maximin, minimax regret over outcomes, or a sharp rule after all
3. whether getting more evidence can legitimately make you less precise
4. whether a set of priors is genuinely a different thing from a second-order distribution over priors — i.e. is imprecision primitive or just a compressed report of disagreement
5. what motivates the whole business: money-pump/Dutch-book style arguments, scoring rules and accuracy, or plain "that single number is a lie"

**Cases you must use** (these are the ones that cause fights in our group):
- *The spread bookie.* There's a public betting office that will sell you a ticket on "rain tomorrow" for $60 and will buy the identical ticket back for $40, indefinitely, and nobody in town thinks this is a scam. Ask what the respondent's reaction is and what they'd do.
- *The committee interval.* Eleven forecasters, each with a perfectly sharp number, spread from 0.15 to 0.7; the institution publishes only "somewhere in [0.15, 0.7]." Is that published interval somebody's belief state, or a statement about eleven other people's belief states?
- *The urn.* 30 red balls, and 60 that are either black or yellow in an unknown split — but with my twist: you get to draw one ball, look at it, and put it back. Then ask whether seeing yellow should narrow or widen your range for "the next draw is red," and whether that answer is a bug or an honest report.

**Leaves that have to appear somewhere:** the precise Bayesian who explains urn behaviour via ambiguity aversion in the utilities rather than fuzziness in the beliefs; the multiple-priors person who then chooses by maximin; the imprecisionist who thinks maximin is cowardly and uses something more permissive; someone with sharp beliefs but genuinely vague utilities; someone who thinks chances themselves are imprecise; the total-ignorance position with no priors at all; and the deflationist who says sets are only notation for a higher-order distribution.

Two things people always skip, so I'm asking up front: tell me which pairs of leaves your tree **cannot** separate. I'm pretty confident the maximin-over-priors person and the second-order-ambiguity-averse person behave identically in any one-shot choice, so if your nodes can't split them, say that plainly instead of faking a question that pretends to. And flag at least one unoccupied cell that you think is unfairly empty and worth arguing for.

Please don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — I'll dig that up myself and I don't want the tree padded with names. Also, no node should be answerable just by recognising a famous thought experiment and reciting the standard reaction to it; I want the questions to bite somewhere the standard reactions don't already go.
