Here's the core section, roughly as it stands:

The definition Spinoza gives of a free thing in the Ethics is a thing that exists and acts from the necessity of its own nature alone, and that is determined to act by itself alone. Whatever is determined to exist and to operate by something else is not free on that definition, it's compelled. Now in the Theological-Political Treatise he describes democracy as the one arrangement in which each person hands his right over not to some other individual but to the whole society of which he himself forms a part, with the result, he thinks, that everyone stays equal, just as they were in the state of nature. In the Political Treatise he adds that the right of the commonwealth is nothing but the power of the multitude, and that democracy is the most absolute form of dominion and the one that comes closest to the freedom nature gives everybody. My claim is that these are not two separate doctrines sitting side by side in two different books but a single doctrine, and that the hinge between them is the definition of freedom.

Call it the composition thesis. In a democracy the power of the sovereign is not something over and above the powers of the citizens conjoined; the sovereign just is those citizens under a certain description. So a decree of the sovereign is an effect of which the citizens, taken together, are the adequate cause. But if the citizen who obeys that decree is one of the things that caused it, then he is not being determined from outside by another thing. He is being determined by a whole he belongs to, and being determined by a whole you belong to is being determined partly by yourself. That is why obedience and freedom stop pulling against each other in a democracy and only in a democracy. The structure is the same as my raising my own arm: there is a commander and a thing commanded, but nobody would describe that as coercion, because the commander isn't other than the thing commanded.

Section 2 does the damage control on the dissenter, the citizen who voted against a decree and is still bound by it. My move is that he authored the standing rule under which majorities bind, so he remains a partial adequate cause of everything decreed under that rule even on the occasions when he loses. Section 3 handles the contrast cases: in monarchy and aristocracy the right goes to a person or a council that really is other than the subjects, so obedience there is a passion and not an action from one's own nature, which is why he can call democracy the most natural state without saying the same about the others. Section 4 is supposed to block the obvious objection, which is that I've just dressed Rousseau up in Spinoza's clothes; I argue there that Spinoza has no general will, no corporate person, and no self-legislating will of any kind, and that the composition thesis doesn't need any of those.
