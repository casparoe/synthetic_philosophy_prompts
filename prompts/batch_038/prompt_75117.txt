Below are the question as set, the marking scheme I was handed, and the candidate's script. I would like a mark, a justification for that mark item by item, and the marginal comments I would actually write in the margin.

The question, worth 25 marks: *Are genuine moral dilemmas possible? Distinguish carefully between a mere conflict of moral requirements and a genuine dilemma, and explain what would have to be true of a situation for it to be an instance of the latter. Assess at least one argument that has been offered on either side of the question, and say what your assessment implies about how moral theories should be constructed or evaluated.* The marking scheme allocates: (a) 6 marks for the distinction between conflict and genuine dilemma, including the no-overriding condition; (b) 6 marks for stating and critically assessing at least one argument bearing on the possibility of genuine dilemmas; (c) 5 marks for accurate treatment of at least two named positions or theorists; (d) 4 marks for the role of moral residue — regret, remorse, compensation, apology — as evidence on either side; (e) 4 marks for a concluding judgement that acknowledges what remains contested.

The candidate's script runs as follows.

(A) A conflict of moral requirements is any situation in which two or more moral considerations point toward incompatible courses of action. A genuine dilemma is narrower: it is a conflict in which the agent is required, all things considered, to do each of the incompatible acts, and neither requirement is overridden by the other. The distinction matters because most of the cases that get described as dilemmas in ordinary speech are only conflicts. If I promise to meet one friend at noon and, forgetting, promise another the same, then at noon I cannot do both; but that does not mean I am obliged to do both. Ought implies can, and since I cannot keep both promises I am not strictly obliged to keep either; at most one survives as an actual obligation, and once we have identified which, the appearance of dilemma dissolves and what is left is a practical annoyance plus, perhaps, an obligation to apologise.

(B) There is also a formal route to the same conclusion, and it is stronger than the appeal to ordinary cases. If an agent is obliged to do A and obliged to do B, then, since obligation distributes over conjunction, the agent is obliged to do both A and B; but by hypothesis the agent cannot do both, and what cannot be done cannot be obligatory. So there is no situation in which an agent is obliged to do each of two incompatible acts. Some object that this simply builds the desired answer into the logic of obligation and is therefore question-begging, but that objection misses the point, because the inference just given uses only principles that anyone reasoning about obligation already accepts.

(C) It follows that a well-constructed moral theory cannot admit genuine dilemmas, and the major theories agree on this, though for slightly different reasons. Consequentialism rules them out because there is always a uniquely best option, so anything else is simply forbidden; Kantian ethics rules them out because a conflict of duties is not a conflict of real duties at all, since only one of two conflicting grounds of obligation can be a genuine duty. Ross is often presented as the exception, since his plurality of prima facie duties looks tailor-made for unresolvable conflict, but he supplies a ranking of the prima facie duties by stringency, so on his account the more stringent duty simply overrides the less stringent and the residue of conflict is only apparent.

(D) The strongest evidence for genuine dilemmas is the moral residue that survives a resolved conflict: people who have done, on balance, the right thing often continue to feel that something is amiss, and they seek to apologise or make it up to the person they let down. If the conflict had merely been overridden, we would expect nothing of the sort. But this residue is best explained as agent-regret, which is remorse under another name; the agent regrets a bad outcome they were caught up in producing, and calling that remorse is just a way of insisting that the conflict was real when it was not.

(E) Two cases test the account. In Sartre's example of the student torn between staying with his mother, who depends on him, and leaving to join the fight against the occupying forces, the student faces a genuine dilemma in the strict sense: no theory supplies an overriding ranking, the values at stake are incomparable, and this is the paradigm case of dirty hands. In the tragic choices of literature and history — a parent required to sacrifice one child so that others may live — the same structure appears, and here I think even a theory that forbids genuine dilemmas by construction is simply refusing to describe the situation honestly. So genuine dilemmas are impossible in principle and unavoidable in the cases that matter, which shows that the real question is what moral theories are for: whether they aim to describe our moral experience or to discipline and reform it.
