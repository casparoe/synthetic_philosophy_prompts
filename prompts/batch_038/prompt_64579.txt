I'm a retired geophysicist — 35 years doing seismic hazard work, mostly return periods and tail probabilities for events that never happen on your watch. I only got seriously into philosophy about four years ago and came at it through decision theory, which for me meant starting with the St. Petersburg game. I keep going back to it because it looks to me like the same shape as something I watched happen in real time, and I want somebody to push back.

The episode is AIG Financial Products in the run-up to 2008. Rough facts as I have them — correct me if I've got them wrong, but please don't let the analysis hinge on the exact numbers: they wrote credit default swaps on something over $500bn of notional, of which around $78bn sat on multi-sector CDOs; the contracts only called for collateral past a threshold or if AIG's own rating slipped; the position was one-way, with no offsetting hedges that made money when the swaps lost. On 15 September 2008 the agencies downgraded them, collateral calls jumped to roughly $32bn against a shortfall around $12bn, and the next day the New York Fed went in with $85bn for 79.9% of the company.

So: take the St. Petersburg paradox — flip until heads, win 2 to the n, expectation infinite, and yet nobody will pay much to play — and run the AIG story through it as a case study. Not the usual finance telling (greed, bad models, captured ratings agencies). I want the paradox doing the work.

1. Do the mapping explicitly and be strict about it. Who is the player and who is the house? What's the coin, and what were the outcomes and their probabilities as AIG's own models saw them versus as they turned out? What plays the role of the bankroll? That last one is where I think it gets interesting, because there seem to be at least three candidate banks in this story — AIG's own capital, the collateral arrangements, and finally the taxpayer — and the old Buffon-style point that a finite house simply cannot pay an infinite prize behaves quite differently depending on which one you mean. Say which you're using and what changes if you swap it.

2. Then sort the standard resolutions by how much explanatory work they actually do here. Diminishing marginal utility of money (the Cramer/Bernoulli move): does it even get a grip, given that AIG's utility stopped being the relevant one once the payoff was big enough to touch the whole system? The finite-bankroll resolution? The you-can't-actually-flip-forever line? Risk aversion or variance aversion as something seperate from bounded utility? I want a ranked list with reasons, not a survey, and I want you to say plainly which of the famous ones did no work at all in this case — I suspect at least one is a red herring here.

3. Here's my own half-baked idea, and I'd like it treated as an idea rather than flattered. In physics we're careful about the difference between averaging over an ensemble of copies and averaging one system over time, and those two come apart when the dynamics aren't ergodic. Averaged across many parallel banks each holding a small slice of tail risk, selling the swaps looks fine — that's just the expectation calculation. Averaged over one bank doing it repeatedly and with leverage, it looks like a road to ruin, because the bad outcomes are correlated with each other in time and ruin is absorbing. If thats right, the St. Petersburg literature's obsession with the shape of the utility function may be looking in the wrong place: the paradox might dissolve once you insist the player has to survive in order to keep playing. Tell me whether this is already out there under a name I don't know (I assume it is), whether it adds anything, or whether it's just risk aversion with a physicist's accent.

4. Where does the analogy break? Be honest and specific rather than rescuing the theory. The St. Petersburg payoff is a prize with a probability structure fixed by a coin; AIG's exposure was to a distribution nobody knew, and — this is the part that bugs me — AIG's own behaviour helped make that distribution. It also collected premiums rather than paying an entry fee, so the sign is flipped. Does flipping the sign give you a genuinely seperate problem, a house-side St. Petersburg, or is that just the same paradox viewed from the other end of the table?

5. Last thing, and the one I actually care about: which single resolution, taken seriously by whoever was supervising AIGFP in 2006, would have changed what they did? Give me one, with the mechanism — what limit, rule, or question it implies — and an argument for why the others wouldn't have moved anybody. If your honest answer is none, say so, and tell me what that implies about the paradox as philosophy: was it ever really about human psychology, or was it always about institutional capacity, with the eighteenth century simply lacking institutions large enough to expose the difference?

Write it as an argument, not a literature review, and don't bother with references — I can't chase them and I don't want a bibliography. As long as it needs to be. I'd rather you disagree with me somewhere than agree with everything.
