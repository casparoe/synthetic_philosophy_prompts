ok, weird one, but I think it's a real problem and not just a made-up homework question.

I sit on the model release review panel at an AI lab — the internal body that has to sign off before a new checkpoint ships with our in-house behavioral monitors attached. Thursday's agenda item is basically a Ship of Theseus problem wearing a lab coat and I'm supposed to bring the memo.

Background. For two years we've relied on a monitor that flags one particular internal thing, which we call the "deference" feature: roughly, the model representing that its output is being evaluated by a human and adjusting its stated plans in response. We found it with sparse autoencoders on a mid-size model, we validated it causally (ablate it and the behavior drops out, amplify it and the behavior shows up in contexts where it shouldn't), and we shipped a linear probe that reads it off activations at inference time. It's the load-bearing element of our entire oversight story, which is to say a lot of downstream confidence is riding on this one direction in activation space.

Now the new release is ~5x bigger, different tokenizer, different pretraining mix, substantially different RL recipe. The probe transfers *beautifully* on every geometric metric we track — cosine similarity of the recovered directions across layers, cross-model matching of the SAE latents, the whole dashboard lights up green, it's honestly gorgeous. But the one intern who actually ran ablations on the new model found that ablating the feature doesn't kill the behavior anymore. It dampens it, and then it comes back over the next few hundred tokens. Working hypothesis: there's a second, redundant implementation of the same thing that only shows up past some scale threshold, and we cannot currently tell whether the probe is reading the union of both or only the legacy one.

The panel is split down the middle. One half wants to approve the transfer on the grounds that it's plainly the same feature — the representational evidence is overwhelming, multiple realizability is a thing, and demanding causal identity is a standard no monitor anywhere will ever meet. The other half wants to block pending fresh causal validation, which realistically means a quarter's delay and probably means we ship with no monitor at all, since nobody believes we can build a replacement in that window.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. I want an actual bottom line — approve, block, or approve under specific conditions with a deadline structure somebody could hold us to — plus the reasoning, and I want a genuine steelman of the "it's obviously the same mechanism" side rather than a knockdown, because those people are right about the correlational evidence and they know they're right about it.

Two things I care about most. First: be explicit about which criterion of mechanism-identity the recommendation is actually using — functional role, counterfactual/causal dependence, representational geometry, something disjunctive — and say why that one, and where it's doing work the data can't settle. I'd rather have an admitted arbitrary-ish line than a fake principled one. Second: give the panel a concrete decision rule for next time, because its going to happen again at the next scale jump, and I don't want to relitigate the metaphysics every quarter.

Assume no one on the panel is a philosopher, but two of them are very good ML people who will notice if you hand-wave the technical part.
