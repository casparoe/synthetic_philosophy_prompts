I am a second-year graduate student in a public-policy program, currently enrolled in a cross-listed seminar on statistical evidence and criminal-justice decision-making. Our assignment is a short critical response to a piece of journalism rather than to a paper, and I have chosen a widely shared long-form feature from a general-interest magazine. I would like an accuracy audit of the passage below before I commit to a position in my essay. I have left the author and outlet unnamed on purpose: I want the assessment to turn on the content, not on who wrote it.

Here is the relevant section of the feature:

"Consider a tool that scores defendants for pretrial risk. Two demands on such a tool look unarguable. The first is calibration: a score of 0.7 should mean a 70 percent chance of reoffending, and — what amounts to the same thing in the binary examples these papers use — the same share of the defendants the tool flags as high-risk should go on to reoffend in each group. The second is balance: the tool should err at the same rate for Black and white defendants, flagging the same share of those who do reoffend and the same share of those who do not, in both groups. Each demand is a matter of arithmetic, and neither is ideologically loaded. Yet a series of proofs published around 2016 shows that, except in two degenerate cases — a population in which the two groups have identical base rates, or a tool that predicts perfectly — no scoring rule can meet both. Neither exception is an escape. No tool predicts perfectly, and the base-rate gap is a fact about the American criminal justice system, not a modelling artifact. So these theorems are not a warning about badly built algorithms. They are a proof that a risk tool which works at all must distribute its errors unequally by race.

The authors of the proofs are careful to say that choosing between calibration and balance is a value judgment rather than a technical question, and they are right, but the point is normally stated far too mildly. What the proofs actually establish is that the search for a mathematically fair algorithm is a category mistake. Because 'fair' has no single technical meaning, there is no fact of the matter about whether a given tool is biased; there is only a choice of which definition to wave at your opponents. Two practical corollaries follow, and the technical literature tends to bury them. First, the standard repair on offer — setting separate risk thresholds for separate groups — can restore balance without giving up calibration, but only by treating identically situated defendants differently, which the law forbids. Second, since the impossibility arises only when scores are evaluated group by group, the cleaner engineering response is to stop using race as an input altogether. A tool that never sees race cannot treat people differently by race, and whatever disparities remain are then disparities in the world rather than in the algorithm.

The theorem in miniature. Take two hundred defendants, one hundred in group A and one hundred in group B, standing in for the two racial groups. In group A, fifty go on to be charged with a new offence; in group B, twenty do. A tool flags thirty people in group A, twenty-five of whom reoffend, and eighteen in group B, ten of whom reoffend. Its false positive rate is therefore 10 percent in each group and its true positive rate 50 percent in each group: balance is satisfied exactly, and the tool makes the same mistakes at the same rates on both sides. But it is not calibrated. Of the defendants it flags in group A, five in six reoffend; of those it flags in group B, only five in nine do. Sceptics have offered populations like this one as counterexamples to the impossibility results, and the theorem's authors reply that such examples simply fail to meet one of the two conditions. They are right to say so — which is precisely why this quarrel has to be settled politically rather than statistically."
