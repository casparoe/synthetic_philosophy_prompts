So — I cover science and ideas for a couple of general-interest magazines, two or three pieces a year where the whole evidentiary base ends up sitting on one side of a fight that the other side lost. Every time I've found this out at fact-check, not while reporting, which is way too late. I want a reusable triage procedure I can run on a story in a single afternoon, before I commission anything or start drafting, that tells me how lopsided my sources are and what that lopsidedness actually licenses me to say.

Ground it in whatever the historiography genuinely says about how silences get into the record. I know Michel-Rolph Trouillot's Silencing the Past has that argument about silences entering at four separate moments — the making of sources, the making of archives, the making of narratives, and whatever gets treated as significant later on — and I want the procedure to keep those four apart instead of mushing them into one hand-wavy "the victors wrote it" gesture. But write it for someone who hasn't read the book and won't.

Three things I specifically need, because I keep seeing them collapsed into each other:
- separate "the losing side never wrote anything down" from "they wrote it down and it didn't survive" from "it survived, but only inside the winners' files." Those have completely different evidential weight and license completely different conclusions, and my drafts routinely slide from the first to the third.
- a step that counts mediation layers between an event and my screen — transcription, translation, a court clerk, a missionary, a press office, a digitization grant — and says what each layer costs me.
- something that stops me treating absence of counter-evidence as evidence of suppression. Give me a way to think about base rates: what fraction of records normally survives anyway, for anyone.

Shape: numbered steps in the order you'd actually run them, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, where the procedure terminates in "you cannot run this story as pitched," where it terminates in "you can run it, but only with disclosure X," and where it hands the call up to me or my editor because it's a values question rather than a facts question — editor signs off on anything that survives with a disclosure attached. Flag explicitly which steps are judgment calls wearing a lab coat. I'd rather know.

Also include: a three-sentence disclosure template for the top of the piece; the five questions I should put to a historian on the record rather than paraphrase loosely; and hard kill criteria.

Then run the whole thing on these three:

1. A nineteenth-century public-health quarrel at a port city, quarantine versus sanitation, where the quarantine side's mortality numbers survive only inside the report of the commision that rejected them.
2. A new paper "confirming" an indigenous account of a volcanic eruption, where the account reaches us via an 1890s missionary transcription plus interviews done by grad students at the same university that ran the confirming study.
3. My pet story, and be honest with me: a claim that a discipline's founder buried a rival's priority claim, where the rival's letters are the main evidence — and those letters survive at all only because the founder's own institution collected and kept them.

No bibliography and no reading list, I'm not chasing anything down and neither are you. And please don't reach for the wartime planes-and-armour-plating story — I've written that analogy twice myself and I'm sick of it.
