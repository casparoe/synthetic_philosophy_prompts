I'm putting together a little sorting instrument for a reading group I help run — half philosophy grad students, half ML engineers, and the two halves talk past each other constantly about what a language model *is*. When a model does something that looks goal-directed, one half says "it's just simulating a helpful character" and the other says "sure, and what do you think simulating is." Neither side can say what would settle it.

So: a decision tree that takes someone from "here's a transcript of a model doing something" to a named position on the agent / simulator / neither question. Nine to eleven questions, every one a short concrete vignette the reader reacts to — a model refuses a request and then volunteers a safer alternative it wasn't asked for; a model fabricates a citation but catches itself when it's made to reread its own answer; a model spontaneously mentions it'll be cut off at the token limit and asks to be resumed; the same prompt in two framings gives a sincere answer and an evasive one; a model says it's simulating a character who doesn't want to be switched off. That sort of thing, invented by you. Crucially, no node may just ask "do you think the model has beliefs / goals" — the tree has to sort people by what they say about the cases, not by which label they'd pick in a survey.

The branches should turn on distinctions I actually care about, and I want them represented:

- **where the agency is supposed to live** — inside a single generation, across the whole conversation, in the lab-and-deployment loop the model is one part of, or nowhere at all
- **whether it persists or gets wiped** — is there something that survives from turn one to turn nine
- **whether the goal-directedness, if there is any, is fully explained by what training selected for**, with nothing left over
- **whether "the model wants X" is a claim about what's there or a useful way of predicting output**
- **if the right description is "simulator," what exactly the simulator is said to be doing** — and whether *that* thing gets to be an agent even if the character it renders doesn't

Then map the actual positions onto the space: the parrot/form-statistics skepticism, the intentional-stance instrumentalism, character simulationism, the view that goal-directedness is real but only shows up as an artifact of selection pressure, at least one "this whole question is malformed" deflationist leaf, and whatever else belongs there. Build the space from the questions first and locate the positions in it second — I don't want a list of names with a questionnaire draped over it.

Two honest bits at the end, please: name any pair of leaves your tree can't separate on behavioral grounds alone (I'm pretty sure the simulationist and the emergent-agent person land in nearly the same place), and flag at least three logically available cells that come out empty, saying for each whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because nobody wants it, or because nobody's looked. One of them should genuinely be incoherent if you can find one.

Oh and an appendix of broken routes: three to five answer patterns that sound reasonable at every individual step but can't be held all at once, with a note on which real position each one *looks* like from the outside. People will recognize themselves in those, that's kind of the point.
