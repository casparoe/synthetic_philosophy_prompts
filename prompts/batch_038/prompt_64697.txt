I'm writing up the methodological commitments behind my health-services research for a departmental methods seminar, and I would like a consistency audit of the package before I present it.

(a) Probability talk is nothing but talk about long-run frequencies in a reference class. I use no priors, propensities, or degrees of belief anywhere in my analyses.

(b) When several reference classes are available, I use the most specific one that is internally homogeneous with respect to the outcome; a broader class is admissible only when nothing relevant distinguishes its parts.

(c) I accept Savage's sure-thing principle: if I would prefer X to Y on the assumption that E obtains, and also prefer X to Y on the assumption that E does not obtain, then I should prefer X to Y.

(d) To act rationally is to choose the act that maximizes the probability of the desired outcome conditional on everything the agent knows.

(e) None of this requires causal vocabulary. Which table one should act on is settled by the frequencies together with (b); "confounder," "mediator," and "back-door path" are, as far as my method is concerned, decoration.

(f) Case verdict: two treatments for kidney stones. A does better among patients with small stones and better among patients with large stones, yet worse in the pooled data, because the harder cases were disproportionately assigned to A. A patient awaiting her scan, and so not yet knowing which subgroup she is in, should on my account take A.

(g) Reversals of this kind are a pathology of observational data. In a properly randomized trial, the pooled and the stratified figures cannot point in opposite directions.

Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which pairs only look as though they do? Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what has to go and what the loss costs. Finally, tell me what my package delivers in a trial where the variable I would stratify by is itself affected by the treatment.
