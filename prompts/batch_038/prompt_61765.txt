Context: I built this for a reading group, originally because I wanted to argue that dynamic consistency is overrated as a criterion of rational choice. Now I think it proves way too much and I can't locate the leak. Here are the steps.

(1) An embedded agent's decision procedure is a physical thing: it's the algorithm that runs at each choice node, so its outputs are the kind of thing we can inspect in the world, and the kind of thing an adversary can model and design around.

(2) A decision theory is rationally acceptable only if, when the agent runs it at every node of a sequential problem, the resulting path isn't strictly dominated — in the theory's own currency — by some other path that was available to the agent at those same nodes. If it is dominated, the agent has left free money on the table and can be pumped.

(3) Call a theory dynamically inconsistent if, at some node it can reach, and with no relevant new information, it tells the agent to do something other than what the theory's own earlier plan prescribed.

(4) At that node, then, the agent deviates. String the deviations together across the nodes of a problem designed for the purpose, and the agent lands on a strictly dominated path: that's a money pump.

(5) So every dynamically inconsistent theory is pumpable, and by (2) rationally unacceptable.

(6) Updateless-style theories are dynamically inconsistent. They evaluate whole policies from an earlier vantage point and then, at the later node, instruct the agent to carry out a step that is strictly worse for the agent as it sits there — think hitchhiker cases, where you pay the fare after you've already been driven into town, or a mugging case where the coin has already been flipped.

(7) So updateless-style theories are rationally unacceptable.

(8) And the reasoning generalizes further. Any theory that evaluates plans rather than individual acts — any theory under which a choice now is justified by what it buys for the agent's later choices — is dynamically inconsistent in the sense of (3). At the later node the justification has already been spent, so the ex-post best act is to defect: skip the training session, raid the savings, decline to carry out the threat.

(9) So by (2), (5), and (8), an agent who saves for retirement, who trains for a marathon, or who follows through on a costly threat is running an irrational decision procedure.

(10) That can't be right. Those agents visibly end up better off, and (1) says the decision procedure is just an algorithm whose results we can go look at.

(11) Therefore (2) is false: leaving money on the table at a node isn't a mark of irrationality.

Two things I'd really like to keep if at all possible: (1), the thought that the procedure is a physical algorithm we can inspect, and (10). I'm also pretty attached to (2), because dropping it looks like dropping the money-pump argument for expected utility, which may be the best argument anyone has for those axioms. But tell me straight if one of them has to go, and tell me what I'm paying.
