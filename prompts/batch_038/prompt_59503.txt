I am in a comparative constitutional law seminar, and last week's session on public funding of religion degenerated into a shouting match conducted entirely in one phrase. A visiting American student kept invoking "separation of church and state" as a pass/fail criterion for four regimes, and the results were, to my ear, incoherent. Here is roughly how it went.

On Germany, he said the church tax is a flat-out violation: the state collects a levy for the benefit of churches through its own tax authorities, and leaving a church requires a formal declaration before a state office, so the state is doing the churches' bookkeeping and policing their membership rolls. A German participant replied that the Basic Law incorporates the Weimar rule that there is no state church, that the levy is a contribution from members rather than a transfer out of general revenue, that collection is a service provided for a fee, and that the alternative to that arrangement would be state interference with religious self-governance — so Germany satisfies his criterion better than the United States does.

On England, he said establishment is the paradigm case of non-separation: bishops sitting in the upper chamber, the sovereign as head of the church. Someone answered that establishment is English, not British — that Wales was disestablished a century ago and that Scotland's national church is not established — so that whether "this state separates church and state" is true of the United Kingdom depends entirely on which sub-unit and which sense one has in mind.

On France, he held up laïcité as the strictest separation there is. A French participant noted that in Alsace-Moselle the surviving Napoleonic arrangement has the state paying Catholic priests and Protestant pastors and putting religious instruction on the public-school curriculum, which would make France's own flagship regime non-compliant on his criterion.

On the United States, he then claimed American separation is the most rigorous of the four. A classmate pointed out that the Supreme Court has required a state to include a church-run preschool in a generally available grant program for resurfacing playgrounds with recycled tires (Trinity Lutheran v. Comer), and has protected a public-school football coach's midfield prayer after games (Kennedy v. Bremerton), with the same phrase invoked on both sides of both cases. The session closed with every participant confident that separation of church and state was true of their preferred regime and false of everyone else's, which suggested to me that the phrase was doing no determinate work at all.

I am writing this up as a seminar memo, so I would like the conceptual work done properly rather than the phrase explained. What are the distinct precise claims that "separation of church and state" could be making? I want the full spread, from the ones that are near-tautologies to the ones that would be radical if asserted, with a verdict on each. Please do not tell me that the phrase is context-dependent, or that it means whatever the speaker intends; I want the readings pinned down finely enough that two people who disagree about a church tax or about bishops in a legislature can be located on different readings rather than merely disagreeing about a slogan.

Please organize the response in the following sections, in this order, at approximately these lengths:

1. **What the phrase was doing in the room** (about 120 words). Diagnose the four moves above and identify at least two distinct ways in which the disputants were talking past one another, before any of the readings are laid out.

2. **The readings** (about 900–1,100 words; the core of the memo). A numbered list, each entry giving: the reading stated as a single testable proposition; what arrangement would violate it and what would satisfy it; which category of claim it is — institutional structure, money, the kinds of reasons that may justify law, public symbolism, or jurisdictional division of labor between two authorities; whether it is descriptive (a claim about what the law in fact is) or normative (a claim about what the law ought to be); and a verdict tag — platitude, substantive but defensible, false as stated, or too vague to have a truth value. For each reading, also say which direction the wall runs: does it protect the state from the churches, the churches from the state, or both, and can it be satisfied by cooperation or accommodation, or does it require mutual exclusion?

3. **The four regimes under each reading** (a table, plus about 250 words of commentary). Rows are the readings; columns are England, Germany, metropolitan France, Alsace-Moselle, and the United States; each cell says compliant, non-compliant, or depends, with a half-line of reason. In the commentary, flag the readings on which the table comes out absurdly — the ones that condemn nearly everything or acquit nearly everything — and say whether that is a sign the reading is misformulated or a genuine substantive result.

4. **Who needed which reading** (about 250 words). For each of the disputants above, identify the reading that would license their move, and say whether that reading is one of the defensible ones. State plainly which moves fail under every reading that survives scrutiny, and which succeed but only at a cost the speaker did not acknowledge.

5. **Is there a reading that condemns all four?** (about 150 words). If some formulation makes England, Germany, France, and the United States all non-compliant, give it, and say whether its universality is a virtue — a criterion with real teeth — or a disqualifying defect, a criterion that no possible state could satisfy and therefore that no legislature could ever act on.

6. **Bottom line** (about 100 words). The two or three readings worth keeping, and the sentence I should substitute for the slogan when I write about these arrangements myself.

Two constraints on the response: treat the facts about the four regimes as I have stated them, and if one of them is wrong in a way that changes the analysis, correct it in a single line rather than rebuilding the memo around it; and do not append a bibliography, a reading list, or case citations beyond the two named above — I want the disambiguation, not the sources for it.
