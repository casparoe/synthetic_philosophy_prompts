I run a reading group that's a weird mix — two entomologists, a couple of cognitive science PhD students, one philosopher, and a lawyer who mostly comes for the biscuits. Next session we're on Hofstadter's Ant Fugue dialogue from GEB, the one where the anthill Aunt Hillary is supposed to have a mind of her own, and I can already tell it'll go nowhere: half the room will say "obviously a colony has a mind", half will say "obviously not, it's just ants", and nobody will notice they're answering different questions. So I want a sorting instrument rather than a summary.

Build me a decision tree that takes someone from "I've just watched a collective do something clever" to a named position on whether, and in what sense, a collective has a mind. Rules I care about:

- Five or six nodes maximum, one question per node, and it has to fit on one side of A4 as a handout, so keep the branch labels short.
- Every node must be answerable by someone who has never read any of this literature. No node may be a disguised "do you think a colony has a mind?", and no node may require knowing a term of art to answer. If my entomologists couldn't answer it from field experience alone, rewrite it.
- Build the tree out of the logical space, not out of a list of authors. Work out the questions and the answer-combinations they permit first; only then say which position sits at each leaf. Names and technical vocabulary belong at the leaves, never at the branches. And don't give me a reading list or any citations — if you're not confident a label is a standard one, just coin a short label of your own and say that's what you've done.
- It has to sort these six probes, because I'll be asking people to place each one separately and the tree should seperate them rather than dumping them all on the same leaf: (1) an ant colony rerouting its foraging effort to a better food source after scouts come back; (2) a slime mould plasmodium that ends up linking food sources by roughly the shortest route; (3) a bee swarm settling on one nest site as scout dances build to a quorum; (4) a long-married couple who each remember different things and offload the rest onto the kitchen and a shared notebook — who to ring, where the passports are; (5) a research consortium with a management committee that can outvote any individual member and in fact commits the group to things no single member believes; (6) the thought experiment where a billion people each simulate one neuron, linked by radio, jointly running a mind that speaks a language none of them speaks.
- At least two nodes should cut on distinctions my group constantly mashes together. I'm thinking along the lines of: whether the collective's goals can conflict with its parts' goals, versus whether you'd need to ascribe beliefs to the whole in order to predict it at all; and whether the parts must be able to represent the whole for the whole to count. Use your judgement, but make the mash-ups explicit somewhere so I can point at them in the room.
- Leaves I insist on seeing in some form: the flat no (nothing over and above the individual animals is cognitive at all); the "one system, but no extra mind" answer, where the colony is a single cognitive system only because the ants already are; the group-agent-without-experience view; the view that grants experience as well; the instrumentalist view on which "has a mind" reports a predictive strategy rather than a fact; and a deflationary degrees-all-the-way-down option. If any of these genuinely collapse into one another on your tree, say so and collapse them rather than padding.

After the tree, give me a table of the whole logical space your questions generate: mark which cells are occupied by a recognisable position, and for every empty cell give one line on why nobody is standing there — incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored (and if unexplored, whether it's worth someone's paper). Then flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really separate, and say what further question would separate them, or admit plainly that none would.

Finally, for the record, here's where I come out on the probes, given before I've seen your tree: (1) yes, but only as a redescription of the ants; (2) no, that's a single cell and calling it clever is just us projecting; (3) yes, there's something there; (4) yes, and I'd happily extend it to the kitchen and the notebook; (5) yes, and this one is the clearest subject of the lot; (6) no — I don't care how well organised it is, there's nothing it's like to be it. Sort me along with everyone else. If my pattern straddles more than one leaf, say which leaves and why, then pick the one that fits most of my answers.

British English throughout, please. End with a single line in exactly this form, naming the leaf I land on in three to six words: Verdict: ...
