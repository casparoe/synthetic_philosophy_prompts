I am building a seminar session on the relationship between rationality and the self, and I would like to anchor it in Homer's *Odyssey* rather than in the usual set of thought experiments. Before I commit to that plan, I want a genuine piece of criticism, and I want it to resist becoming either a summary of the poem or a summary of the philosophy.

The philosophical frame is Parfit's hitchhiker and the doctrine of rational irrationality that it is meant to support. As I understand the case: a traveller is stranded in the desert and a driver will take him to town only if he promises to pay a fee on arrival; the driver is a good judge of character and will not pick up anyone he takes to be transparently self-interested, since such a person would find it against his interest to pay later and would therefore not be believed; it follows that the traveller does better if he has, or can credibly display, a disposition to keep a promise that it would be contrary to his interest to keep at the moment of keeping it. The upshot is that it can be rational to cultivate a disposition whose exercise is, judged act by act, irrational. I take the neighbouring cases to be Kavka's toxin, where the reward attaches to the intention rather than the act, and Schelling's treatment of commitment as the deliberate destruction of one's own options; I want the hitchhiker's distinctive feature kept distinct from both, because that feature is what interests me here. The hitchhiker's problem is not weakness of will but *readability*: what ruins him is that another agent can see through him.

Now the poem. In Book 12, on Circe's instructions, Odysseus stops his men's ears with wax and has them lash him upright to the mast, with the further instruction that if he begs them to release him they are to bind him faster still. He hears the song, signals to be loosed, and the men — who cannot hear him and cannot hear the Sirens — row on and tie him tighter. In Book 10, Aeolus gives Odysseus a bag holding the adverse winds; the fleet sails unmolested for nine days with Ithaca in sight, Odysseus falls asleep at the sheet from exhaustion, and the men, reasoning that their captain is coming home loaded with gifts while they have nothing to show for the same labour, open the bag; the winds escape and drive them back. In Book 12 again, Odysseus extracts an oath from the crew not to touch the cattle of the Sun before they land on Thrinacia; a month of contrary winds and dwindling stores follow, and while Odysseus is once more asleep Eurylochus argues to the starving men that a quick death at sea is preferable to a slow one by starvation. The oath is broken and all but Odysseus die. Two smaller details matter to me as well: Odysseus passes on Circe's warning about the Sirens in full but deliberately conceals the danger of Scylla, on the ground that knowing about her would paralyse the rowers; and so far as I recall the Sirens' pitch to Odysseus is largely epistemic rather than hedonic — they boast of knowing all that the Argives and Trojans endured and of knowing everything that comes to pass on the fruitful earth. Please correct me if you think the emphasis in that episode lies elsewhere.

With that laid out, I would like the following.

1. The mast is nearly always read as an illustration of precommitment, and often as an illustration of *successful* precommitment. Is the hitchhiker actually the right model for it? The two cases differ in who the audience is: the hitchhiker must be believed by a stranger, whereas Odysseus must be disbelieved — or at least overridden — by his own men. Tell me whether the transparency condition does any real work in the Sirens episode or whether importing it is a category mistake, and if it is a category mistake, say what the episode is about instead.

2. Attend to the division of labour between the wax and the ropes. The men's insulation is perceptual rather than moral: they do not resist the song, they cannot hear it, and only that fact makes their willingness to tie their captain tighter a cheap disposition to have. Does the poem thereby suggest that precommitment is not an individual virtue at all but a piece of social technology whose feasibility depends on who bears the cost of enforcement? Press this against the cattle of the Sun, where the enforcement problem is inverted because the enforcer is asleep and the enforcers are the tempted party.

3. Both catastrophes in Books 10 and 12 happen while Odysseus is asleep, and both turn on a defect in what the crew knows or believes about their captain's intentions. The men who open the bag are not stupid; their inference about hoarded treasure is, on the evidence available to them, a reasonable one, and Odysseus has a settled habit of concealment that makes it more reasonable still. Is this the hitchhiker's transparency problem turned inside out — a disaster caused not by being readable but by being permanently unreadable? If so, does the poem endorse the conclusion that the traits which make Odysseus individually effective make credible commitment impossible for him, and does it distribute the costs of that in a way that counts as a verdict?

4. Weigh the two information policies against each other: full disclosure about the Sirens, suppression about Scylla. One is followed by survival and one by the loss of six men, but I do not want the crude inference that honesty pays. What does the poem actually seem to think determines when an agent should be transparent about a coming temptation?

5. Finally, say where the reading fails. I have deliberately stacked the deck by choosing episodes that look like commitment problems; if you think the *Odyssey* resists being read as a treatise on rational irrationality — that its explanations are theological, that the men are doomed regardless of what they choose, that I am flattening a poem about nostos into a decision-theoretic diagram — argue that, and tell me which of the episodes survives the objection and which does not. I also want to know what the poem contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot: something about the phenomenology of being bound while still wanting, perhaps, or about the difference between a temptation that is heard and one that is inferred.

Two practical constraints. I have the text in front of me, so please do not quote any translation at length and do not offer me citations, references, or a reading list. I would also rather you set aside the two readings this episode always receives — self-mastery as the origin of instrumental reason, and the wax-and-ropes pairing as an allegory of the division of labour — unless you intend to argue that one of them is right and I have missed why.
