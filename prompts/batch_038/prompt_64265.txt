I am drafting guidance for a public regulator on when a statistical score, or a bare base rate, may be relied upon in a decision about an identified person. Before I can write anything defensible I need to know what I actually think, so I have been collecting my own verdicts on cases rather than reading round the subject. They do not line up. I am not after references or the names of positions; I want the principle itself.

1. A civil claim against a bus operator. A pedestrian is struck at a certain junction by a bus that did not stop. The only evidence is a complete traffic record, accepted by both sides, showing that 82 per cent of the buses using that junction in that hour belong to the defendant. My verdict: the claim should fail.

2. A stadium with 1,000 seats and 999 tickets sold. The operator sues one attendee, chosen at random from those present, for the price of a ticket, on the ground that someone present almost certainly did not pay. My verdict: it should fail, and I would say the same however large the crowd, so long as the only evidence is the arithmetic.

3. A burglary with no suspect. A DNA profile from the scene is run against a national database and matches exactly one person, with a match probability of the order of one in a billion. Nothing else connects him to the offence: no motive, no opportunity, no witness, and he says he has never visited the town. My verdict: proved, or near enough that a court could act on it. I cannot explain how this differs from 2, since in both the sole link between the individual and the event is a fact about how many people in a population share a property.

4. A driver prosecuted on the reading of an evidential breath-testing device whose error rate is known from testing on a large sample, with no other evidence of impairment. My verdict: entirely proper to rely on the reading.

5. My general practitioner tells me that people with my age, sex, blood pressure, cholesterol and smoking history carry a 20 per cent ten-year risk of a cardiac event, and prescribes accordingly. My verdict: exactly right, and I should be cross with her if she declined to use it.

6. A different clinician, having read my file, tells me that left-handed second-born patients who broke an arm before the age of twelve do markedly worse — a group of forty he identified by going back through his unit's records once he had seen me. My verdict: worthless. Yet 5 is also a narrow class, and a model with four hundred covariates carves the world far more finely than he does. I cannot say what separates them beyond a suspicion of his method.

7. An insurer charges young men more than young women for motor cover, and women more than men for annuities. I know the first practice has been prohibited in some jurisdictions; I am reporting my verdict, not the law. My verdict: acceptable in both directions.

8. The same insurer prices motor cover partly by postcode, and the postcode ranking correlates strongly with the ethnic composition of neighbourhoods, although no ethnic variable enters the model. My verdict: not acceptable.

9. An insurer proposes to price life cover using a genetic test for a variant that substantially raises the risk of a fatal condition: the most accurate single predictor available, validated in large cohorts, and as narrow a class as one could wish for. My verdict: not acceptable — and here I am genuinely at a loss, because everything I valued in 5 (narrowness, calibration, a causal story behind the number) is present to a greater degree.

10. A recidivism score built from age at first conviction, offence type, number of prior convictions and postcode, validated out of sample, offered to a parole board as one consideration among several, disclosed to the prisoner, who may contest its inputs. My verdict: acceptable. The same score, undisclosed, operating as the sole determinant: not acceptable. The evidential content is identical in the two situations, so the difference cannot be evidential.

11. I am happy to say that the probability that this borehole, drilled at this site, strikes water is 0.3, and to bet on it. I am uneasy saying that the probability that this defendant committed this burglary is 0.9, even though I have just said it in 3. I suspect the difference is that boreholes are draws from a repeatable process and past crimes are not, but I cannot see why that should matter to a degree of belief.

12. One case on which I have no verdict at all. A police force deploys officers to the streets a model selects, the model being trained on recorded offences by street. It is well validated, and recorded crime falls wherever it is deployed. But recorded crime is itself partly a product of where officers were previously sent, so the class from which the model estimates its rates is constituted in part by the practice the model governs.

My own attempt at a principle is this: statistics about a class may be relied upon for an identified case provided that (a) the class was fixed before anything was known about that case, (b) the case is a random member of it, in that nothing further known about it raises or lowers its chances relative to the class average, and (c) it is the narrowest class still homogeneous with respect to the outcome, no further known fact dividing the frequency within it. I can already see that (a) and (c) pull against each other, since narrowing is usually achieved by learning more about the case, and that (b) may be empty, since whether a member counts as random seems to depend on how the class is described.

What I should like is a principle, or a shortlist of competing ones, stated precisely and clause by clause, with the items above that motivate each clause; a demonstration of exactly where my provisional version breaks and what must be added or abandoned to repair it; and, if no single principle can carry all twelve items, an argument for which of my verdicts I should surrender rather than which clause I should add, since I would rather lose a verdict than multiply conditions. Give me your principle's verdicts on four or five cases of your own construction, at least one of which should come out against my intuitions so that I can see where it bites. Then answer plainly whether 7 to 10 are evidential verdicts at all or fairness verdicts wearing evidential clothes: if the latter, split the principle accordingly and tell me what the evidential half, standing alone, permits in 9 and 10. Finally, say whether the whole difficulty dissolves once the classes in question are understood as encoding causal mechanisms rather than frequencies, or whether that merely restates it.
