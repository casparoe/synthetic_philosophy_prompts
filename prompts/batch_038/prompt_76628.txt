You are a professional philosopher of mind — an expert on consciousness studies, on the epistemology of philosophical argument, and on debunking-style arguments of the sort used in metaethics and the philosophy of mathematics — and you are unusually good at taking an argument apart step by step rather than responding with a general orientation. Here is the situation. The meta-problem of consciousness, as I understand it, is the problem of explaining why we are puzzled by consciousness in the particular ways that we are: why we report that there is something it is like to see red, why a world physically just like ours but without experience seems conceivable, why knowing all the physical facts about a person seems to leave something out. Grant, purely for the sake of argument, that the meta-problem has been solved — that we possess a complete functional and cognitive account of the mechanisms producing those reports and intuitions (attentional amplification of some representations and not others, the absence of any introspective access to the machinery that supplies that access, report generation running off a coarse-grained self-model, the way a system describes its own processing when it has only the outputs to go on), an account that leaves nothing about why we are puzzled still unexplained.

My paper's central argument is this:

(1) If that account is complete, then the production of our phenomenal reports and gap-intuitions is fully explained without any phenomenal property figuring in the explanation; for if phenomenal properties were needed to explain why the reports occur, the account would not be complete and the puzzle would still be standing.

(2) Where the facts a class of judgments purports to be about play no part in the best explanation of why we make those judgments, those judgments do not track those facts.

(3) Judgments that do not track the facts they purport to be about are not evidence for them.

(4) Therefore our phenomenal reports and gap-intuitions are not evidence for phenomenal properties.

(5) Nor is anything else evidence for them: every argument that has been offered for phenomenal properties — conceivability, the knowledge case, the explanatory gap, the claim that a physical description omits the intrinsic nature of things — terminates in how matters seem from the inside, which is to say in the judgments of (4).

(6) Therefore there is no evidence for phenomenal properties beyond what the complete functional account already explains; the hypothesis is evidentially empty.

(7) By the same (2) and (3), the same goes for any domain whose facts do not figure causally in the explanation of our beliefs about them.

(8) So if (1) through (6) are sound, mathematical truths, moral truths, modal truths, and the epistemic principles expressed by (2) and (3) themselves are all equally unevidenced.

My colleague's objection is that (8) is not a cost I am entitled to pay but a sign that the argument proves too much. She says she can see no principled stopping point between (6) and (8), and that (8) sweeps in the argument's own premises, so the argument, if sound, removes the standing to assert it. I find this unsatisfying and I cannot locate the fault; I would rather keep (2) and (3), since debunking arguments of that shape are ones I want to use elsewhere, and I am happy with (6) as a conclusion. What I want from you:

(a) Map the argument first, before you diagnose anything: a few sentences on what each of (1), (2), (3), and (5) is being asked to carry, and where the load actually falls.

(b) Say whether the argument is valid as it stands. If it is not, mark the step at which validity fails and state what suppressed premise would be needed to repair it, and whether that premise is one I can independently motivate.

(c) Identify the step you would reject, and explain in detail why it looked innocent — what plausible principle it resembles, what has to be added to it to get the next step, and whether the addition is one a physicalist, a property dualist, and someone who thinks phenomenal properties just are functional properties can each accept.

(d) Price the alternatives. For each of (1), (2), (3), and (5), say what it costs to give that one up instead, and who in this debate can most cheaply afford the cost.

(e) Tell me what survives. If the argument fails, does the solved meta-problem bear on the hard problem at all — is there a weaker claim about phenomenal properties that the completeness assumption genuinely licenses, or does solving the meta-problem turn out to be beside the point? Give me the strongest version of the debunking argument you can repair, and state precisely what it no longer proves.

(f) Finally: is (8) a real over-generalization, or could a defender bite the bullet and accept it? If it can be accepted, what does the argument then show about the status of (2) and (3), and about whether they were ever doing philosophical work rather than expressing a preference for a certain kind of explanation?
