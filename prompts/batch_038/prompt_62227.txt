Went to a talk at our local philosophy society last Thursday. The speaker writes those trade books on world philosophy — he said himself he's not an Indologist — and he did about ten minutes on the Nyāya school's theory of knowledge, which went roughly like this:

"Where the Greeks separated knowing from being saved, the Nyāya never did: their epistemology exists to tell you which beliefs can carry the weight of liberation, and the answer is four sources of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison and testimony. They argued at length that you can't get by with fewer than four, and that the extra sources other Indian schools posit can be folded into these. Perception is the foundation and can't go wrong, because it's the bare, wordless registering of an object by the senses before the mind sorts it into concepts and names; error only creeps in once you start judging. Inference is Aristotle's syllogism with two steps added for the sake of explicitness: the mountain has fire because it has smoke, wherever there's smoke there's fire as in a kitchen, and so on through five moves a Greek would compress into three. Comparison is just inference from resemblance — told that a gavaya is like a cow, you later reason from the likeness that the beast in front of you is a gavaya. Testimony is the one source that needs no checking, because the Vedas have no human author, so what they say is true by definition. Which is why Nyāya, for all its logic, is at bottom a religious philosophy: four routes to certainty, and certainty is what brings release."

I've read enough around the edges to suspect the testimony bit and the can't-go-wrong-perception bit are off, but I can't tell whats actually wrong versus what's just a simplification a non-specialist is allowed. Could you go through it claim by claim and sort it into three piles — what a Nyāya scholar would nod at, what's a fair simplification that loses something load-bearing, and what's straightforwardly mistaken? For the mistaken ones I'd like to know what the school actually held, and who he's probably conflating it with, since I get the feeling at least one of those four glosses belongs to a different school entirely. Also tell me if there's anything in there he gets right that people usually get wrong, because I'd hate to throw that out with the rest.

And then the bigger question: does his bottom line survive the corrections? If you fix the errors, is "four routes to certainty, therefore really a religious philosophy" still a defensible reading of Nyāya, or does the conclusion fall over with the premises?

No references or reading lists please — I can't chase anything up and don't want to be handed titles I can't get hold of. One thing I do want: where the specialists genuinely disagree among themselves, like how to read that "not dependent on words" clause in the definition of perception, or whether comparison really earns its place as a source in its own right, say so plainly and don't hand me one side of a live dispute as if it were the settled view.
