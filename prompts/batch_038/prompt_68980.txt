For the past two years I have been one of nine lay members of a county panel that advises the sheriff's office on conditions inside our jail, and this spring the county gave us something it has never given us before: roughly twenty-five thousand dollars and one calendar year, to be spent on a single piece of work of our choosing. We may commission a study, hire a facilitator to run a public deliberation, send a small delegation to visit institutions elsewhere, or write a report of our own — but the money stretches to exactly one serious effort, and at the end we have to put something in front of the commissioners that they can actually vote on. I have no academic training whatsoever. I have spent those two years reading around the punishment-versus-rehabilitation argument on my own, and I keep finding that the sharpest exchanges on both sides are aimed at questions which, for a county of our size, feel either already settled or entirely beyond our reach. I would like your judgment on where our year and our money should go.

The candidates I have been circling are these. First, the exportability question: everyone who tours Halden, the Norwegian prison that keeps coming up in our meetings, returns saying we should copy it, but the stays there are a fraction of ours and the society surrounding the prison looks nothing like ours, so I suspect the design only holds up when both of those things are true — is it worth our year to work out which features of such a place travel and which are load-bearing on preconditions we cannot supply? Second, something I have no proper name for and so call the audience problem: a facility can be doing everything right by the person inside and still leave the people outside — the victim's family, the neighbors who called the police — with the settled sense that nothing was done at all, and I cannot tell whether the honest answer is that we need better rituals of accounting for what happened during a sentence, or whether the demand to see something borne is itself legitimate and cannot be designed away. Third, the sequence question: if humane conditions are politically stable only where sentences are short, should a county like ours spend its effort shortening stays rather than improving cells — and would improving conditions first make shortening stays harder, by making the place more comfortable to tolerate? Fourth, the measurement question, since the only number anyone will judge us by is the reoffense rate, which strikes me as a metric that rewards us for working on the people least likely to come back and says nothing at all about the years spent inside. Fifth, whether rehabilitation offered to someone whose parole turns on participating is rehabilitation in any sense I should be willing to defend, or a bargain struck under pressure.

Pick, and defend the picking. I want an ordering of these — plus anything I have overlooked that ought to displace one of them — with the reasoning made explicit rather than a survey of the debate: for each item, how much else depends on getting it right; whether nine non-specialists with a modest budget can actually move it, or whether it needs criminologists and statisticians we cannot afford; whether an answer would change what this county does or only what we say about it; and which of them I should drop outright, because the question is not ours to settle or because its answer would change nothing downstream. Tell me where these criteria pull against each other and how you are trading them off, and tell me what I might learn in the first three months — a figure from our own jail, an outcome from a place we visit, a reaction from the commissioners — that should move an item up or down the list. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question back to me. Please do not include citations, a bibliography, or a reading list: I cannot chase references, and I would rather have your judgment about the questions than a list of books about them.
