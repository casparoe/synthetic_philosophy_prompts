I am a high school senior doing an independent study in decision theory with my economics teacher, and last week we got stuck on the two-urn problem that Daniel Ellsberg made famous. In the version we have been working with, one urn holds fifty red balls and fifty black balls, and the other holds a hundred balls that are red and black in a proportion nobody knows. People reliably prefer to bet on a red ball drawn from the first urn rather than the second, they also prefer to bet on a black ball from the first urn rather than the second, and yet they are perfectly indifferent between betting on red and betting on black within the second urn. My friend, who plays a lot of poker, insists that this pattern does not require any special aversion to ambiguity at all: he thinks it is always ordinary attitudes toward money doing the work, once the situation is described honestly. My teacher suggested I stop arguing about it and try to settle it by building a case instead, which is what I am asking you to help with, because I have not been able to construct one myself.

So: build me a decision problem that reproduces all three features of that pattern — the preference for the known-probability option in the red direction, the same preference in the black direction, and indifference between the two directions when the probability is unknown — for a decision maker who is a plain expected-utility maximizer. She must hold one definite probability for every unknown quantity in your case, with no ranges, no sets of possible probability distributions, and no element of her preferences that is sensitive to how vague her information is; and she must nowhere violate the Sure-Thing Principle, meaning roughly that her preference between two options cannot turn on how they behave in situations where they pay off identically. The constraints I care about are these. First, the setting must be an ordinary one — a small business, a household, a school, a farm, a clinic — with no urns, no colored balls, no lottery tickets, and nothing science-fictional. Second, the ambiguity must be genuine: she really does not know the relevant probability, and there is no way for her to find it out before she commits. Third, the four options must have exactly the same expected monetary payoff on her own probabilities, and the money at stake must be the same in each, so that nothing but the probability structure distinguishes them. Fourth, she can take only one of the four, so the case cannot dissolve by letting her hedge across both directions. Fifth, keep the arithmetic checkable on paper: at most three possible values for whatever unknown quantity is at issue, each with an explicit probability, her utility over money given as a formula or a short table, and stakes large enough that the money genuinely matters to her. The scenario itself should be no more than two paragraphs, and I need the four options to line up cleanly with the four bets, so please tell me which real-world feature of your case is playing the role of red and which is playing the role of black.

After the scenario, show me that the design actually works rather than asserting that it does. State her probabilities and her utility function, compute the expected utility of each of the four options — an explicit table of the possible totals and their probabilities is fine if the calculation gets tedious — and confirm that the ordering comes out exactly as specified. Then tell me which single structural feature of the case is doing the work, and run the removal test on it: say precisely what you would change, and whether the pattern survives, weakens, or collapses into outright indifference once that feature is gone. If it turns out that the feature you identified is one the original two-urn problem simply does not have, I want you to say so plainly instead of glossing over it. Finally, give me the strongest objection available to someone who thinks aversion to ambiguity is a real and irreducible feature of preferences rather than a stand-in for something else — the version that would actually worry me, not a straw man — and end with your honest verdict on whether your case proves my friend right, or only shows that some things which look like ambiguity aversion turn out to be something else.
