I run a masters seminar in philosophy of maths where half the room comes from the maths department and half from ours, and week one's discussion of constructivism was a total shambles: everyone announced they were a constructivist of sorts and then disagreed about literally everything. For week three I want a decision tree that sorts them properly instead.

Ten nodes or thereabouts, and every node should be a concrete mathematical situation rather than a slogan — the √2^√2 style proof by cases where neither case has ever been settled; the bisection argument for the intermediate value theorem, which hands you for each n a point within 2^-n of a root but no root; whether a Turing machine that provably cannot fail to halt must therefore halt; whether there are objects whose values get settled by free choices as we go along; whether truth can outrun anything we could prove. Phrase each one so a student can answer it without knowing where the answer leads. In particular no node may ask whether they accept excluded middle — that's precisely the thing they all think they're agreeing about.

Leaves get names: Brouwer's intuitionism, Markov's school, Bishop-style constructive maths, Dummettian semantic anti-realism, plain classical platonism, plus any others you think deserve one that I've left out. Then I want the empty cells: which combinations of answers the tree makes available that nobody actually holds, and for each, whether that's because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any pair of leaves your nodes can't really seperate.

Keep it to a couple of sides so I can hand it out in class.
