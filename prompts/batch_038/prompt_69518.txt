I'm auditing a jurisprudence seminar this term, and last night three of us stayed in the library until the building closed, arguing about the postwar German informer case that both Hart and Fuller discuss: the woman who, wanting to be rid of her husband, reported to the authorities the disparaging remarks he had made about Hitler while home on leave from the army, after which a military tribunal condemned him under a Nazi statute criminalizing statements detrimental to the Reich's welfare. He served time and was then sent to the front; after the collapse, she and the judge who had sentenced him were charged under a provision of the old 1871 Criminal Code prohibiting the unlawful deprivation of another's liberty. The judge was acquitted and she was convicted, on the footing that she had freely made use of a statute so contrary to the conscience and sense of justice of decent people that it forfeited the status of law. The friend I was arguing with is a positivist of Hart's stripe, and he spent the evening building the following chain, which he takes to be Hart's own way of handling the case pushed to its conclusion. A third person in the room — a natural lawyer — supplied the last two steps as a reductio, and my friend was happy to accept them. I have spent the morning unable to say where the pressure actually falls, and I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict about who won.

(1) Whether a norm is legally valid is settled entirely by its source: by whether it was made in the manner, and by the institutions, that the system's criteria of validity specify. Moral merit plays no part in making a norm valid or invalid. (2) It follows that "is this law?" and "ought this law be obeyed?" are distinct questions, and no answer to the first settles the second. (3) The Nazi statute was enacted by the authority recognized in Germany at the time, promulgated, and applied by courts whose jurisdiction nobody then disputed; so by (1) it was legally valid, and valid in 1944, when the wife acted. (4) A court may punish only conduct that contravened legally valid law at the time it was performed. (5) Informing under that statute contravened no legally valid law in 1944. (6) Therefore the postwar court could convict her only by refusing to treat the statute as valid then — which, given (1), can only mean applying to 1944 a standard of validity that did not exist in 1944. (7) Between the two things a court can then do — leave a grave wrong unpunished, or punish it under a standard adopted afterwards — the second is the more honest and the lesser evil, because the first buys its result by redescribing a wicked rule as though it had never been law, and so corrupts the concept of validity in order to spare the court an unpleasant judgment. (8) Nothing in that comparison turns on the Nazis, on the extremity of the injustice, or on the fact of a defeated regime: any source-valid norm of gross immorality generates exactly the same two options and exactly the same reasons for choosing the second. (9) So the general rule: where a legal system contains source-valid norms of gross immorality, they are to be dealt with by retroactively declaring that they were never valid — legality is to be revised backward to line up with justice wherever the gap is large enough to matter. (10) And by (2) that is the only route available, since no moral defect can deprive a norm of validity at the time of its application; hence no court may ever decline to apply a rule on the ground that applying it would be unjust. (11) But (9) and (10) cannot both be accepted: (9) licenses wholesale retroactive correction of settled legality and destroys the very feature that makes law capable of guiding conduct, and (10) is inconsistent with what courts uncontroversially do — including in this case, where the court convicted her without enacting anything. (12) Therefore (1) is false, and moral merit must figure among the conditions of legal validity, at least in extreme cases.

Which premise or inference should I reject, and why did it look unobjectionable at two in the morning? I am especially interested in the cost of each candidate exit, priced separately for the two sides. If the positivist keeps (1), which of (6), (7), (8), or (10) does she have to sacrifice, and what does giving up (7) do to Hart's claim that the retroactive statute is the more honest of two evils — does the honesty argument survive if there turn out to have been more than two options on the menu? If instead she lets (1) go, what does she lose by conceding a threshold beyond which injustice defeats validity: how is the threshold to be stated, and can she still say the plain thing that the Nazi statute was law and too iniquitous to obey? And I want the natural lawyer's bill too, since (12) is supposed to be his victory: does (11) really entitle him to it, or does it show only that my friend's dilemma was misdescribed? For what it is worth, I would rather keep (3), (4), and (5) than any of the later steps, because the first three seem to me to be about the world and not about a theory.
