**DRAFT — Standing in Line: When Conventions Bind, and Whom They Bind**

Three small cases organize this paper. Someone steps into a queue ahead of me, having waited none of the time I have waited. Someone crosses against the light on an empty street at three in the morning, in a town she is only visiting. Someone pays a twenty percent gratuity in a country where the service wage is set on the assumption that no gratuity will be paid. In each case there is a convention, and in each case ordinary people feel the pull of a question that is not merely prudential: was that wrong? My aim is to explain why the question has a different shape in each case, and to argue that the standard answers are too blunt to register the difference.

*1. The standard answers.* The harm-based answer fails first. Cutting into a queue seems to harm someone in particular; jaywalking across an empty street harms no one at all, and tipping over-conventionally harms no one either. If harm were the whole story, the first case would be a moral matter and the other two would be matters of taste, which does not match the phenomenology: people are more indignant about queue-jumping than about anything else on the list, but they also feel a residue of unease about the empty street.

The promise-based answer fails next. Nobody undertook to queue. Conventions bind, if they bind, without the consent of those bound.

The fairness or free-riding answer does better, but only for a restricted class of cases. A queue is a distributive scheme: a scarce good — a place at the front — is allocated by a currency, waiting time, that each participant pays. To jump is to take the good without paying the price, and to take it from identifiable others who did pay. That is a genuine wrong, and it is owed to specific people in a specific order, namely everyone behind the point of entry. But the red light is not a distributive scheme. It is a signalling device whose value is entirely statistical: it works because almost everyone almost always complies, and it delivers no divisible good to me for which I could be said to have free-ridden.

The two most sophisticated accounts of convention inherit this unevenness without explaining it. A Lewis-style analysis tells us what a convention is — a regularity sustained by common expectations in a coordination problem in which the participants are largely indifferent among the available equilibria — and it explains beautifully why driving on the right persists. But it also explains why the reason to conform is conditional on others conforming, and therefore why it evaporates on the empty street at three in the morning. Bicchieri's account of social norms, as I understand it, treats norm-following as a conditional preference: I prefer to comply given that I believe enough others comply, and that enough others expect me to comply and would sanction me if I did not. That is descriptively powerful, and it makes the force of a norm indexical to a network of beliefs. But it leaves the normative question exactly where it was: why should the fact that I hold such beliefs, and that others hold them about me, generate a duty rather than merely a prediction? Hume is the ancestor of both moves — the rules of justice are artificial, their content fixed by convention and their authority by the utility of the whole scheme rather than of each act — and his account has the same gap in the same place.

*2. A graded account.* I propose that conventions generate obligations only when three conditions obtain, and that the strength of the obligation tracks how many of them obtain. Call them Reliance, Legibility, and Reciprocity. Reliance: others actually use the convention to form expectations about my conduct, and my compliance is among the things that keep that expectation-forming capacity intact. Legibility: my act is recognizable as compliance or as deviation by someone, or at least by me in a way that could be recognized. Reciprocity: I am a participant in the scheme, drawing its benefits and occupying a standing from which its terms could in principle be contested.

This yields a distinction I think does most of the work in the paper. Some deviations are *scheme-defecting*: they extract from a distributive arrangement a good that others paid for, and they wrong particular people. Others are *signal-degrading*: they weaken a device whose entire value is aggregate, and they wrong no one in particular. Queue-jumping is the first kind. Jaywalking on a busy street is the second kind, and its wrongness, such as it is, is a wrong against the practice rather than against a person — which is why it feels both real and strangely impersonal, and why it feels much weaker when the street is empty and Legibility has dropped out.

*3. The visitor.* Visitors are philosophically interesting because they come apart from residents on Reciprocity alone. A visitor relies on the convention without having participated in making or sustaining it; she is legible, since her conduct is observed; but she is not a participant. And locals characteristically interpret visitor conduct through what I shall call an *exemption frame*: a deviation by an obvious visitor is read as ignorance rather than as a claim about the scheme, which is why it provokes a shrug rather than indignation.

Two consequences follow, and the second is the one I want to defend. First, a visitor's deviation does not corrode the expectation-forming function of the convention, because the frame preserves the local meaning of the rule; the signal remains intact precisely because the deviation is not counted as a challenge to it. On this ground the visitor does no wrong.

Second, the frame is self-undermining. Its protective effect depends on deviations by visitors remaining exceptional and recognizable as such. If exemption became generally expected, locals would have to read every deviation as possibly exempt, which would degrade exactly the function the frame protected. So the visitor may be bound to conform — not because this instance of deviation is wrong, but because the general publicity of a permission to deviate would be wrong-making. The obligation here has an unusual shape: it is a duty concerning the dissemination of a doctrine, not a duty concerning the act. One can hold that the permission is real and that it ought not to be advertised.

*4. Unjust conventions and the asymmetry of abstention.* Tipping is a transfer convention with distributive consequences: where it exists, it may function to relieve an employer of the cost of paying a living wage. Compliance then does two things at once — it transfers money to a person who may need it, and it reinforces an arrangement that arguably exploits her. Here the analysis inverts. Reciprocity holds for the resident, so the exemption frame is unavailable to her, and conformity may be worse than deviation. But the burden of justification is asymmetric: the resident who deviates must be able to say that her deviation aims at the injustice rather than merely exploiting a scheme whose benefits she continues to accept. The visitor, by contrast, can abstain without that burden, because abstention does not require her to mount a critique of a community she has just arrived in. Non-participation, usually a defect of standing, here becomes an advantage.

*5. Conclusion.* The moral weight of an everyday convention is not a single quantity. It is conditional on who is relying on it, on whether the act is visible as a deviation, and on whether the agent is inside the scheme; and it varies in kind between conventions that allocate goods and conventions that transmit signals. The interesting questions are not whether conventions bind but who is standing where when they do.

**What I want from you**

1. Accuracy. Test my characterizations of the Lewis-style analysis of convention, of Bicchieri's conditional-preference account of social norms, and of Hume on the artificiality of the rules of justice. For each, say whether I have stated it correctly enough for the argument to lean on it, and whether any misstatement is load-bearing. I am especially worried that I have conflated a claim about what conventions *are* with a claim about what reasons they *give*.

2. The central vulnerability. Does my Legibility condition survive the empty-street case, or does it collapse into a rule-utilitarian calculation — conform because general conformity is useful? Tell me what, if anything, distinguishes my account from that, and if the distinction is merely verbal, say so plainly and tell me what I would have to add to make it substantive.

3. Overreach. Does the self-undermining exemption frame argument in section 3 prove too much? Specifically: if the reason to conform is that the permission must not become publicly known, does the same reasoning oblige a visitor to conform to a local convention she judges unjust, on the grounds that advertising a conscience-based exemption would erode the frame for everyone? If so, what repair is available that does not simply abandon the argument?

4. Originality assessment. Rank the three claims I take to be most load-bearing — the scheme-defecting/signal-degrading distinction, the idea that a convention can generate a duty about the publicity of a permission, and the claim that non-participants have a distinctive standing to abstain from unjust transfer conventions — from most likely to be already standard in this literature to most likely to be novel. Be candid; I would rather cut a claim that turns out to be folklore.

5. Objections. Give me the two strongest objections a fair but hostile reader would press, and identify the point at which I should concede rather than argue.

6. Structure. This is a compressed skeleton of a paper intended to reach roughly seven thousand words. Tell me which section to expand into the core, which to reduce to a paragraph or footnote, and whether section 4 belongs in this paper at all or should be split off into a second one.

Please give me a report addressed to the argument, not a rewritten draft in your own voice, and no suggestions of further reading.
