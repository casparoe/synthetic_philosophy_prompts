I'm on the board of a 14-unit housing co-op — we're all volunteers, nobody's a lawyer, and our monthly meetings usually wrap in about forty minutes because nothing is ever controversial. Last month's took three hours and I still don't know how I feel about it. The situation: there's a ground-floor storage room that hasn't been used for anything since before I moved in, its got some broken shelving and four boxes of old meeting minutes in it. A member named Dan wants to convert it into a small workshop at his own expense — wiring, bench, dust extraction, he quoted something like eight or nine thousand out of pocket. Our bylaws say anything like that needs a two-thirds board vote.

Two board members, Marta and Owen, ended up in a genuine argument, and I'm reporting it as a bystander because I have no stake either way (I wouldn't want a workshop, I don't have room for one, I'd never use it). Here's roughly how it went.

Owen opens: the room is dead weight. Dan pays for everything himself, the minutes get moved to a shelf in the basement, and every single one of us keeps exactly what we have today — our apartments, our parking spots, our share of the garden, our vote. Nobody loses anything. So this is the easy case, and three hours of deliberation is obstruction with a thoughtful face on it. If you object, you have to point at something being taken away from you, and there isn't anything.

Marta: nothing-being-taken only works if you measure against what we happen to hold right now. But we all currently hold an equal say over that room, and the say is a real thing, not a technicality. Once it's a workshop the say is gone, permanently, for that room. The loss is in the option, not in the shelving.

Owen: options nobody has exercised in twelve years aren't assets. If we count every use somebody might someday want, nothing can ever get approved, because you can always imagine a future person wanting the room for something else. That standard turns an empty room into a monument.

Marta: then follow the standard forward. Say yes now, and next year Priya wants the back half of the laundry room for a kiln, and the year after someone wants the far end of the bike room. Each one is individually the identical argument: it's unused, they'll pay for it, nobody's losing anything they currently have. Three years out, three members have private converted space and eleven of us have none, and not one vote along the way was against anybody. The rule isn't neutral about where we land, it just gets us there one free step at a time.

Owen: that's an argument about Priya's application, and we should hear it when it arrives. Judge each on its own facts. Turning down a good proposal now because a bad one might come later is punishing Dan for other people's hypotheticals.

Marta: except we won't actually see it then. When Priya's application lands, Owen's first argument gets made again word for word and it'll sound just as clean, because it'll be measured against a baseline that this vote has already shifted. The only moment anyone can think about the sequence is now, while the first step is still free.

Then someone floated a compromise near the end, and that's where I got most confused. Dan offered to pay the co-op a one-time fee of, I think, $1500, and to sign something making the conversion formally reversible: if the board ever votes two-thirds to take the room back, he clears out and restores it at his own cost. Owen said that closes the case completely, because now the co-op is compensated *and* nothing is actually permanent, so there's no state of the world where any of us ends up worse off than today. Marta said the reversibility clause is theatre — after three years of a built-out, wired, insured workshop that a neighbour paid nine grand for, the board is never mustering two-thirds to rip it out, so the promise changes the politics of the future decision without changing anyone's real options — and the fee just attaches a price to the thing that was supposedly unlosable to begin with.

Can you go through this move by move and tell me who's right at each of the six, and then seperately who's right about the compromise, rather than just naming an overall winner? Two things I especially want: whether Owen's third move (unused options aren't assets) is actually as strong as it felt in the room, and whether Marta's fourth is a legitimate argument or a slippery slope that just happens to be plausible here. Also, both of them used worse off as though it meant one determinate thing, and by the end I wasn't convinced it did — if some of the disagreement is that ambiguity rather than a real clash, say what the competing readings are and which one each of them was actually using. Finally, tell me how confident you are in these verdicts and single out the one you're least sure about.
