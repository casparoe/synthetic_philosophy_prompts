I was elected to the school board of a mid-sized Texas district eighteen months ago, and last spring I asked our superintendent a question I expected to have a number attached to it: how many children in our jurisdiction are being educated at home? The answer was that nobody knows. We can count the withdrawal letters that arrive, but nothing ever comes back, and so the district's picture of the matter consists entirely of exits. Texas, as it happens, has written this ignorance into policy. Its Supreme Court has held, and the state education agency has confirmed in a 2019 letter to district administrators, that homeschooled students are "exempt from the compulsory attendance requirement to the same extent as students enrolled in private schools"; that "it is not necessary for the parents to make a personal appearance with school officials, present curriculum for review, or comply with any other requirements in order to successfully withdraw their student"; and that the most a district may do is ask for a "letter of assurance" from the parent that the home curriculum is "designed to meet basic education goals including reading, spelling, grammar, mathematics, and a study of good citizenship" — with the added note that "a letter of this type is not required each year." All of this sits underneath a state constitution that opens its education article with the sentence: "A general diffusion of knowledge being essential to the preservation of the liberties and rights of the people, it shall be the duty of the Legislature of the State to establish and make suitable provision for the support and maintenance of an efficient system of public free schools." Meanwhile, the same legal order treats a child's unexcused absence from an approved school as an offense: parents in my state can be fined, hauled before a court, and in some places referred for educational neglect. Pennsylvania, by contrast, requires an annual written evaluation of the child's progress prepared by a certified evaluator who reviews a portfolio of work, plus standardized testing in grades three, five, and eight. Two American states, two very different epistemic demands, on the same activity, in the name of the same public interest.

I want you to reconstruct the theory this pattern presupposes rather than to argue for or against homeschooling. Begin with the enforcement asymmetry, which is the part I cannot get past: a state that will compel a child's physical presence in a recognized setting, on pain of penalties against the parent, but that will not ask a single question about what the child learned while there. What does that tell me the state actually takes itself to be owed — attendance, custody, a setting of the right type, or knowledge? Notice that the private school occupies the same position: it must exist as a registered, inspected, record-keeping institution, but the state does not test its pupils or review what is taught inside it. So accountability seems to attach to the arrangement rather than to the outcome, and the homeschool is exempted precisely by being classified as a private school. What conception of education makes classification rather than demonstration the relevant fact? And what conception of the family does the "letter of assurance" require — under what theory of testimony is a promise from the interested party, not renewed annually and never checked, a sufficient warrant for the state to consider its constitutional duty discharged?

Please separate carefully what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit. I can think of at least four readings that would license the same statutes: that accountability follows the money, so the state verifies only what it pays for; that parents hold a prior and presumptively legitimate authority over their children's formation, of which the state's interest is a residue; that the curricular language about good citizenship is expressive rather than epistemic, doing symbolic work with no expectation of verification; and the flatly deflationary reading that the state simply cannot monitor households and has dressed its incapacity in the language of trust. Tell me which of these the practice actually cannot do without, which are compatible with it but optional, and where the parts of it are in genuine tension — in particular whether a state can coherently claim that a general diffusion of knowledge is essential to the preservation of liberty while structurally declining to know whether knowledge is diffusing. Then tell me what would have to change, institutionally and not merely rhetorically, if the presupposed view were replaced by one on which the child's own interest in an education is the primary object of the state's concern, and separately by one on which schooling is chiefly the production of citizens competent to sustain a republic. Finally: state how confident you are in the main claims you make, single out the one point you are least sure of, and flag any place where my description of the practice may itself be inaccurate or unusually favorable to one of the readings — since I cannot check the law from where I am sitting, I would rather you tell me which of my premises the reconstruction depends on most heavily.
