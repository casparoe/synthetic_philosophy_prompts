The exam question, worth 20 points, ~600 words expected:

"Mencius says human nature is good; Xunzi says it is bad. Explain each position as accurately as you can, then say whether you think the two are genuinely disagreeing about the same thing, and why."

Here's the student's answer, verbatim:

---

Mencius and Xunzi are both Confucians — both think the point of a life is to become a junzi, both revere ritual and the old texts, both hold up sagehood as the ideal — but they start from opposite pictures of what we arrive with.

For Mencius, human nature, xing, is good. His famous thought experiment: anyone who suddenly saw a child about to fall into a well would feel alarm and compassion, and not because they wanted credit with the child's parents or a reputation among the neighbors; the heart just reacts that way. From this he argues we all have four sprouts — compassion, shame, deference, and a sense of approval and disapproval — and these are the beginnings of ren, yi, li and zhi. Importantly he doesn't think we come out already good in the finished sense; he thinks we come out with tendencies that either get fed or get chopped down, like the trees on Ox Mountain, which everyone assumed had never grown anything because the axes kept coming back. He also argues against Gaozi, who said human nature is like whirling water: open a channel east and it goes east, open one west and it goes west, so nature is indifferent to good and bad. Mencius answers that water may not distinguish east from west, but it certainly distinguishes up from down, and human nature inclines to goodness the way water inclines downward.

Xunzi takes the other side. Human nature is bad, he says, and whatever goodness shows up in people is the product of wei — deliberate, cultivated activity. We're born wanting profit, born envious, born with an appetite for sound and color, and if we just follow those we get strife, then violence, then the collapse of any order at all. So the sages made ritual and righteousness to straighten us, the way a carpenter steams and presses warped wood straight. And for Xunzi this is available to everyone: he says the person in the street could become a Yu. Beyond that there's nothing in the mind to work with — the senses deliver raw material, and the teachers install the moral content. He's basically an empiricist with a blank slate, which is why he's so insistent on masters and models.

So: are they actually disagreeing? I'd say the disagreement is largely verbal. They agree on the empirical facts — people have desires, people can become virtuous, ritual and teachers are necessary, most people don't in fact make it. What differs is what they're willing to count as nature. Mencius identifies xing with what's distinctively human, the moral sprouts, and doesn't really count the appetites we share with animals as our nature. Xunzi identifies xing with everything unlearned and inborn, which is mostly the appetites, and calls the moral stuff wei precisely because it isn't nature. Once you see that, the two positions are compatible; they're labeling the same thing differently, and they'd agree about how to raise a child.

There's also a background difference. Mencius treats Heaven as fundamentally on the side of morality — cultivate your heart and you know your nature, know your nature and you know Heaven. Xunzi strips Heaven of that job: its course is constant, it doesn't persist because of a good ruler or perish because of a bad one, and organizing ourselves is up to us. So Xunzi is closer to a modern naturalist, and his pessimism about nature is what eventually feeds into Legalism — Han Fei studied under him. That's probably also why later Confucians dropped him: when the Song neo-Confucians built the Four Books canon they kept Mencius and cut Xunzi loose.
