I hold a chair in the philosophy of science at a research university and, for four years, have served as the external member of the Permanence Committee of the Aldermere Foundation, a private trust whose founding instrument permits it to make commitments that outlive its own trustees only under a special procedure. The matter before us is the Kestrel Archive: a proposed repository — physical holdings, a duplicated digital corpus, and the standing organization to maintain both — for which the sponsoring staff request an irrevocable endowment together with a three-hundred-year stewardship obligation. The instrument requires that such a commitment rest on a durable-institution forecast prepared under the Foundation's Precedence Protocol, whose clauses I should describe, since the entire dispute turns on them. Clause 2 establishes that the outside view governs: a reference class of antecedent institutions, with a base rate drawn from their observed lifespans, is presumptively decisive, and an inside-view risk model may adjust it but not replace it. Clause 4 requires that the membership rule of any reference class be written down before the fate of any member is examined. Clause 7 — the Symmetry Clause, which I proposed in 2021 and now half regret — provides that a disanalogy invoked to exclude a member of the class must be turned back on the target: if you exclude a past institution because it lacked something the Kestrel Archive has, you must ask what the Archive lacks that the past institution had.

The staff submission and the dissenting trustee's counter-submission cannot both be admissible, and I have been charged with drafting the memo the other six members will read before the vote. The staff class is fourteen sovereign or quasi-sovereign polities with durable administrative apparatus — the Republic of Venice, the Byzantine successor states, the imperial Chinese examination bureaucracy, the Holy Roman Empire, and the like — from which they derive a median institutional lifespan of roughly three hundred forty years and conclude that the Archive is more likely than not to be functioning in 2326. The dissenter, a historian, argues that this class fails Clause 4 outright, because its membership rule was composed after the lifespans were known and amounts, she says, to nothing more discriminating than "long-lived things"; she offers instead a class of continuously chartered institutions of any kind that have held a fixed stated purpose, which takes in medieval universities, a Shinto shrine complex dismantled and rebuilt on an alternating adjacent site at a set interval so that it has never contained an original timber yet counts as unbroken, several monastic orders, and a scattering of municipal hospitals and guilds — yielding a much shorter median but a far longer tail. I have three further difficulties of my own that the memo must confront rather than paper over. First, the recorded class is a survivor's class: institutions that collapsed within a decade of their founding were mostly never recorded at all, so any median computed from the historical literature is biased upward by an amount no one can estimate. Second, the continuity question appears to have no principled answer that Clause 7 leaves stable — every disanalogy I reach for against the past cases (no nuclear weapons, no globalized capital markets, no digital media, no successor states capable of inheriting an archive's function) licenses an open-ended enlargement of the target's distinctiveness until the class dissolves entirely. Third, the forecast, once recited in the endowment deed, will be read by the Archive's own future administrators and donors, which may alter the very rate it reports.

Please draft the memo I will circulate: roughly eleven hundred words, for six readers — two of whom manage money for a living, one a former head of state, one a structural engineer, and one the dissenter, who is an excellent historian and will notice every evasion. It should open with a recommendation on the endowment (ratify, ratify subject to stated conditions, or decline) and the time frame within which any conditions must be satisfied; state which reference class, if either, the Committee can certify under the Protocol, and if neither, specify what a certifiable class would have to look like and who should be asked to build it; hand both the staff and the dissenter two or three tests they could actually apply to the next proposal that reaches this table; and give the losing side's strongest case its full weight, including — if you judge my Symmetry Clause incoherent or unworkable in practice — a plain statement to that effect together with what should replace it in the instrument. I am teaching a seminar this term on induction and its limits, and it has struck me that nearly everything I tell my students about projectible predicates applies here with unusual directness; but the memo is not for them. It must be self-contained, assume no technical vocabulary beyond what you define in a clause, cite nothing, and offer no reading list, since the trustees will look none of it up and I would rather they had your reasoning than your references. Close with a single line in exactly this form: Verdict: [RATIFY | RATIFY-WITH-CONDITIONS | DECLINE] — certified reference class: [the name you give it, or NONE].
