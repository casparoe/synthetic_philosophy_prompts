I sit on the five-person selection committee for the Whitcombe Fellowship in the History of Science and Technology, a two-year postdoctoral award our small foundation makes once every three years. The chair has asked each of us to circulate a short written opinion before Friday's meeting, and the report on the two finalists has fallen to me. I would like you to draft that document. The first candidate proposes a matched-case study of the English Midlands and the lower Yangzi delta around 1750, holding constant what she takes to be the relevant background conditions — real wages in grain terms, degree of market integration, household commercialization, state fiscal capacity — in order to establish why sustained mechanized industry appears in one and not the other; she treats the proximity of workable coal and the import of land-intensive goods from colonial plantations as the discriminating factors. The second candidate proposes an intellectual history of the question itself: how the old civilizational formulation of the divergence problem took the shape it did, what changed when the unit of comparison shifted from civilizations to regions, and what the standard answers have been made to do rhetorically in the scholarship since. Neither project is weak on the ordinary criteria — training, feasibility, archival access, writing sample — and the committee is split two to two with me as the swing vote. One of the historians is already on record saying that any objection to the first candidate's framing could be raised against every piece of comparative history ever written and is therefore uninformative; our program officer counters that her contrast is Europe-against-China in all but name, since her own causal story makes Britain's place in it a consequence of coal seams and shipping routes rather than anything anyone chose. For what it is worth, I come to this as a historian of scientific instruments rather than an economic historian, which is no doubt why I find the framing objection harder to wave away than my colleagues do, and why I have been asked to write this up rather than to vote first.

The complication I most need help with sits on page six of the first candidate's proposal. She reports a robustness exercise in which the same analysis is re-run against three different foils — the Dutch Republic, France, and the Yangzi delta — and finds that coal carries nearly all the discriminating weight when the foil is the delta, drops out entirely against the Netherlands, and gives way to fiscal capacity and coal-import logistics against France. She presents this as a virtue rather than a confession: her explanation is, in her own phrase, explicitly indexed to the contrast it answers, and she commits in advance to reporting which findings travel across foils and which do not. Our two historians disagree about whether this is the most impressive or the most alarming thing in the document. I suspect it is both, and I need the memo to say plainly whether a panel can act on that ambivalence or must come down one way.

Please write it as the thing I will actually circulate: roughly nine hundred words, addressed to the committee, with an unambiguous bottom line — fund the first candidate, fund the second, or fund neither and re-open the competition — together with instructions for what we should do if the vote ties on Friday. Give the reasons in a form both applicants could respond to if we send the panel's rationale to the unsuccessful one, and articulate criteria for contrastively framed proposals in general, so that our decision does not look like a preference for one field's prestige: at minimum, some account of when a question of the form "why X and not Y" is determinate enough to fund, and of whether an explanation that shifts with the choice of foil is defective, merely incomplete, or simply honest in a way its rivals are not. Treat the strongest consideration on the losing side at length rather than dispatching it — including, if you recommend the first candidate, the charge that the second project is meta-level, yields no findings about the divergence itself, and leans on the very comparative reasoning it exists to interrogate; and including, if you recommend the second, the trustees' standing instruction that the fellowship should support work a non-specialist would recognize as addressing a large question. Do not append a bibliography, reading list, or citations; we will not chase references, and the value of the memo is its reasoning. Nor should it turn into a seminar on the philosophy of explanation — invoke contrast spaces and the like only where they earn their place.
