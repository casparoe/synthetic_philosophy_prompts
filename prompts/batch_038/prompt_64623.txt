ok so context: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever, and in about three weeks I'm giving a 25 minute talk plus ~15 min of Q&A at a workshop put on by a public-integrity / anti-corruption group. The room is going to be mostly political scientists who study corruption and public procurement, plus a few people who've done the actual reform work — writing conflict-of-interest rules for agencies or for a multilateral lender. A couple of lawyers. Nobody in there codes for a living.

My talk argues that the conflict-of-interest framework is going to misfire badly when it gets imported into open-source infrastructure, and that most of what would get flagged as a COI in a public agency is load-bearing gift exchange in a maintainer community. Rough sketch of my argument, please attack it:

1. The COI machinery assumes you can name a principal whose interests the officeholder is supposed to serve, plus a defined office with a job description. In open source there's no principal and no office. A maintainer isn't an agent of their downstream users any more than the guy who shovels his neighbor's driveway is an agent of the neighborhood.
2. So the harm COI rules exist to prevent — a decision-maker steering public resources to himself under cover of a role — doesn't have a grip, and what the rules actually end up doing is reclassifying the ordinary funding of maintenance (sponsorships, paid support contracts, a vendor flying someone out to a maintainer summit) as suspicious.
3. My alternative: drop "conflict of interest" and replace it with something like stewardship-plus-disclosure. Concretely I want to pitch a public append-only ledger of transfers between vendors and maintainers of critical packages, where the norm is disclose-after-the-fact rather than recuse-beforehand, plus one hard rule: a maintainer who's recieved money from a vendor can't be the person who merges that vendor's changes to a security-relevant code path.

My gut says (3) is the strongest part and (1) is what gets torn apart, but I dont know.

What I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will raise — the ones a corruption scholar or a procurement lawyer would raise, not the ones I'd get from other engineers or from a HN thread — ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk. For each one: how they'd phrase it, in their vocabulary (I genuinely don't know the jargon, so use theirs and gloss it for me), the 90-second answer I should give, and a blunt note on where that answer is soft. Two extra constraints.
