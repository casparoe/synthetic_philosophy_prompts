Third-year philosophy of language seminar, 14 students, one 75-minute slot that I'd like to hand over almost entirely to an activity. They've done the Kripke material on how some necessary truths aren't knowable without experience, and they've done Twin Earth, so they can already recite that water is H2O is supposed to be necessary but a posteriori. What they cant do — and what I want them to run into during the session rather than be told — is see that there are two seperate questions you can ask about a sentence and a way-the-world-might-be, and that they've been sliding between them: is this sentence true in that world, holding fixed what our words actually mean here? versus, if that world turned out to be the one we're in, what would our words have latched onto, and would the sentence come out true then? I want them to notice the gap by getting different scores out of the two questions on the very same sentence, and ideally to resist it a bit first, before anyone says the word intension at them.

What I need: a runnable game. Rules, the cards or handouts and exactly what's on them (keep the printing to two pages), a points scheme that doesn't involve real money but where winning is genuinely possible and where the two evaluation modes pay out differently, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75, and a debrief that maps specific moments from what they just did onto the distinction. Then tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — where they'll stall, where some smart kid will break the scoring, what I answer when a student asks whether the game isn't just built so the answer comes out right — and which feature of the underlying idea the activity fundamentally cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to supply by hand afterwards.

Two more things. Which standing objection to this whole way of carving up meaning does the activity quietly assume away — I don't want to teach them a framework that only looks airtight because I hid the hard part in the rules. And mark up your own design: which bits are load-bearing and which are decoration, plus your actual confidence. If you think there's a decent chance it collapses into tedious bookkeeping where they get the right answers without feeling anything, I'd much rather hear that than receive a polished handout.

And if you think the premise is off — that you can't stage this distinction without the rules already encoding the answer, so the "discovery" is fake; or that 75 minutes is nowhere near enough and I should cut scope; or that this group isn't ready for it — say that plainly at the top instead of complying politely and quietly designing around the problem.
