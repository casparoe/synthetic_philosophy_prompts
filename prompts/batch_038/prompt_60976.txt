I am a third-year PhD student in linguistics, and I would like help thinking through a disagreement inside an annotation project I co-run — a disagreement that I suspect is not really about annotation.

We are coding roughly twelve thousand public statements issued by institutions (corporations, universities, city councils, dioceses, sports federations) responding to some harm they are alleged to have caused. The coding question is whether a given statement contains an apology. Our codebook lists features such as acknowledgement of the harm, acceptance of responsibility rather than mere regret that events occurred, expression of remorse, a commitment to changed conduct, an address to identifiable injured parties, and a request for forgiveness. My co-investigator, a computational social scientist, points out — correctly — that none of these features is present in every statement I would code as an apology, and that no combination of them excludes every statement I would not. A terse "I was wrong, and I'm sorry" satisfies at most two of the six; a long, carefully worded release "regretting that people were hurt" and "taking the matter very seriously" can satisfy four.

His remedy is to replace the codebook with an explicit checklist and a decision threshold, so that two competent readers applying it to the same document reach the same verdict, and so that a stranger who never saw our training materials can reproduce our labels. He argues that our present scheme is unfalsifiable: whenever the features pull in different directions we simply declare the case borderline, and our agreement statistics are inflated because they are computed on documents we chose precisely for being clear.

What troubles me is this. I cannot produce a single case in which the checklist and a well-trained annotator disagree and I am confident that the checklist is wrong. Yet I also cannot accept that "I was wrong, and I'm sorry" fails to be an apology while a four-feature press release counts as one. I have come to think the dispute sits at a level prior to methodology, and that I am losing it by arguing on his ground.

Three questions. Is his objection to the codebook sound, or does it press a demand that a concept of this shape cannot in principle meet and does not need to meet? If the demand is illegitimate, what should a codebook for such a category contain instead — and what do we owe reusers of the dataset, who were never trained by us and cannot inherit our judgements by example? Finally, how much of my own resistance should I concede: is there anything left of my intuition that the terse sentence apologises and the press release does not, once I grant that I cannot state a rule that captures it?
