I hold a view about ordinary politeness that I want stress-tested rather than affirmed.

The view: the small falsehoods of everyday civility — the flattering verdict on a mediocre meal, the softened refusal, the enthusiastic reply to an email one intends to ignore — are not deceptive in the morally relevant sense, because they occur under conventions that all competent participants know. Consider the two cultures usually held up as opposites. An employee in the Netherlands told without cushioning that their proposal will not work, and an employee in Japan told that the same proposal would be difficult, have on my account been communicated with to the same degree of accuracy; in each case the operative contract makes the speaker's actual content recoverable, and literal accuracy is simply not what is on offer. Candor and indirection are thus two implementations of the same good faith, and the difference between polite hedging and lying is not a difference in harm — the indirect refusal may sting more precisely because it is deniable — but a difference in entitlement: the hearer is licensed by shared convention to work out what was meant, and the speaker asserted nothing false.

What would count against this? I want the map of its exposure, not a defense of it.

Which cases, findings, or arguments should move me: situations in which the convention is not in fact shared, whether across cultures, generations, or hierarchies, or between incumbents and newcomers; situations in which the indirect formula demonstrably fails to transmit the intended content even to insiders who ought to have known better; and situations in which the appeal to a shared convention turns out to be nothing more than a description of the speaker's convenience.

How the view fares against the distinction between lying and misleading. If deliberately steering someone toward a false belief without asserting it is itself a way of deceiving, does my appeal to convention carry any moral weight, or does it merely push the problem up a level, to the question of whether the hearer was entitled to rely on the convention?

What the view predicts that its rivals do not — a deontological account on which the polite falsehood is a lie regardless of local norms, and a bluntly consequentialist account on which it is permissible whenever it works. Are there observable differences between the three, or is my position compatible with both of its rivals, which would itself be a defect?

Which of these objections a committed defender would concede as counting against them, and which they would dismiss as misreadings. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.

Finally, a candid verdict on whether the view risks anything at all. My worry is that shared norms are doing unearned work: almost any utterance can be redescribed as licensed by some convention the parties hold, which would leave the view unfalsifiable rather than merely well defended. Tell me whether that is a genuine problem for an account of this kind, or whether insulation of that sort is legitimate here.

For what it is worth, the consideration I would find hardest to resist is evidence that these conventions are asymmetrically distributed — reliably known by insiders and reliably missed by outsiders — since that would convert an appeal to shared understanding into an appeal to shared standing.

Lead with your bottom line: a single sentence stating how exposed you take the view to be. Then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of the question.
