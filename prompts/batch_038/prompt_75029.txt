I teach a seminar on contingency in history — the inevitabilists versus the people who think the past is a stack of near-misses — and every year some student wheels out *It's a Wonderful Life* as though Clarence's tour of Pottersville settles the debate. I've never had a crisp way to say whats actually wrong with that. Here's the distinction I've started using, which I'd like you to take seriously and push on rather than just accept: call an outcome *robust* with respect to some agent if, remove the agent, and some nearby substitute brings the outcome about anyway; call it *resilient* if it comes back in the same shape and on the same timetable. My suspicion is that the inevitabilists — Carr is the obvious case, waving off the might-have-beens of history as beside the historian's business, with Trevor-Roper answering that weighing alternatives is how you find out what the causes even were — only ever argue for robustness and then trade on resilience as if they'd earned it. And the film is interesting precisely because Bedford Falls looks like it has neither, but for a reason that isn't the one everyone reaches for: its not that George Bailey turns out to be a great man, it's that Potter is a monopolist with no competing substitute to slot into the vacancy, so the counterfactual is doing structural work while wearing the costume of a parable about individual worth.

What I want is the skeleton of that reading, not the essay. Exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no summing-up paragraph at the end:

1–3: the three scenes where robustness and resilience come apart most cleanly. The honeymoon bank run, Bailey Park, and the Pottersville tour are my candidates, but if you'd rather spend one of these on the bridge scene or on the fact that Potter is still standing in the final reel, make that case inside the item. Anchor each to what the distinction is doing there rather than narrating the scene back to me.

4: the strongest objection from someone who says Clarence's counterfactual was never an epistemic claim at all — it's a piece of moral suasion commissioned by an angelic bureaucracy to stop a suicide — and whether that rescues the film as counterfactual history or sinks it.

5: what the film gets at about inevitability that straight argument doesn't, and whether Gould's replay-the-tape idea helps here or just muddies a case where the agents have beliefs about their own replaceability, which Burgess Shale organisms don't.

Don't quote dialogue at me, I've seen it more times than I can count, paraphrase. And if you think my distinction is bad — that robustness and resilience collapse into one thing the moment you fix the granularity of outcomes — say so in item 1 instead of humoring me.
