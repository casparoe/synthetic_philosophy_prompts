I am at work on a novel whose central figure holds considerable authority over people who have, in practice, stopped deciding anything for themselves, and I have run into a difficulty in the drafts that I suspect is a conceptual problem rather than a craft problem. I want to know whether what I keep circling has been named.

In every early draft the man knows what he is doing. He is lucid about his own motives: he understands that he has lifted a burden from his charges for his need to be needed as much as for their relief, and he accepts the trade with open eyes. That version reads as a villain and the book collapses. The version that works is the one in which he believes his own mercy — in which he is not deceiving himself about any fact, since he knows precisely what he has arranged and would defend each arrangement on its merits, but is deceiving himself about why he arranged it. And the reason I think this is more than a character note is that the self-deception seems load-bearing rather than incidental. A man who knew would hesitate; hesitation shows; and the governed read visible strain in an authority as an opening. His efficacy depends on a smoothness that only unselfknowledge supplies, so the deception cannot be set down without the benefit failing.

I keep meeting the shape of it outside the book. A headmistress my mother was taught by, who settled every significant question in three hundred girls' lives and who, confronted at eighty, was not defiant but genuinely puzzled: she was certain she had been giving them something they could not have borne to hold. And a hospice physician I interviewed, who told a woman her husband was comfortable when he was not, and who years later heard from a colleague that he had done it for himself — a remark he received as slander rather than recognition, because it was true and could not be admitted.

So: is there an established concept for a benevolent deception of others whose necessary condition is the deceiver's own non-lucidity about his motives? I have found several near neighbours and none of them seems to fit. Ivan's inquisitor is the obvious comparison and differs in a single respect, though I think it is the one that matters: his lucidity is his tragedy, whereas in my case it is the absence of lucidity. There is the old idea of the edifying falsehood a ruler tells his city for its own good, but that is told knowingly, which is the opposite of my case. I half-remember an evolutionary argument to the effect that self-deception pays because a liar who believes himself leaks less — yet that concerns advantage over an adversary, not the preconditions of mercy. And there is the familiar politician who does a shameful thing for good reasons and remains guilty of it; he knows, and mine does not.

Tell me which of these comes nearest and what each fails to capture, and if none of them does the work, say so plainly and coin something with conditions rather than a definition by illustration: what must hold for the phenomenon to obtain, and what would reduce it to ordinary self-deception, or to paternalism wearing a nicer coat. I am particularly interested in four boundaries. Must the benefit be real, or does the configuration survive the discovery that the charges were in fact harmed? Does it survive partial awareness — a man who suspects and declines to look? Can an office, as distinct from a person, possess it, given that the institution outlives every incumbent's sincerity? And is self-deception about one's reasons marked off anywhere from self-deception about the facts, since my man's is entirely of the first kind? I am not after a reading list. I want the carving, and I want to know where its edges lie.
