I'm in my fourth year of a PhD in the philosophy of mathematics, and I've been asked to contribute a piece of fiction to a graduate-run annual whose theme this year is barriers: proofs that cannot be given, and what it costs to discover that. Rather than write about inconsistency or undecidability, which everyone will write about, I want to build the story around the Razborov–Rudich result on natural proofs, which I take to be the most philosophically strange theorem in complexity theory and which almost no fiction has touched. For the story's purposes, here is the material as I understand it, and I'd like you to treat it as given rather than as something to reconstruct: a lower-bound proof of the sort people have long imagined for separating P from NP would proceed by exhibiting a combinatorial property of Boolean functions — some measure of discrepancy, scatter, or structure — that is large, in that a substantial fraction of all Boolean functions have it; constructive, in that one can recognize the property from a truth table in time quasipolynomial in the table's length; and useful, in that no function admitting small circuits has it. The theorem says that, if pseudorandom functions exist with sufficiently strong hardness, then no such property can be useful against P/poly: the very procedure that certifies largeness and constructivity can be repurposed as an efficient distinguisher, so that a successful proof of that shape would simultaneously be a demolition of the pseudorandomness on which cryptography rests. There is also the sharper, unconditional case for the discrete logarithm, where the barrier is known to bite without hypotheses — and it bites precisely where the secrets live. I'd like the story to make a character feel the shape of this: that the reason a certain route to a theorem is closed is not that the theorem is false or the mathematician is untalented, but that the world's capacity to keep secrets and the world's capacity to yield to this kind of proof are in some sense the same quantity measured twice. Impagliazzo's taxonomy of possible worlds — the one where everything is easy, the one where hard problems are easy on average, the one where they are hard but nothing can be hidden, the one where one-way functions exist but public-key cryptography does not, and the one where both do — should be present as texture, as the kind of thing the characters say to one another in a corridor to name what is actually at issue, not as a taxonomy delivered to the reader.

Here is the situation I'd like you to dramatize. The protagonist is a researcher in her mid-thirties at an unglamorous institute who has, over one long winter, isolated a candidate property she privately calls the sieve: she has a lemma giving largeness, and she believes she can show that no polynomial-size circuit family escapes it. What stops her is that her largeness proof runs through the recognition procedure — the same algorithmic step that makes the property constructive is what makes the counting argument work — so she cannot keep one and abandon the other; she has already tried to degrade the property into something inefficiently recognizable, and the attempt failed for exactly that reason. The concrete stake should not be abstract suffering. Two years earlier she audited the key management of a small documentation project that holds encrypted testimony and identity records for witnesses who are still inside the country where the atrocities occurred; she knows which protocol it uses, which parameters, and how thin the margin is, because she is the one who wrote the recommendations they still follow. Her sieve, if it is useful, is an attack on that protocol, and she can read off the parameters well enough to know it. She has a workshop version of the largeness lemma already circulating, a coauthor who does not know about the audit, and a submission deadline eleven days out. I want the story to stage, in scene and not in summary, the best objection to her dilemma: a cryptographer in the same building points out that she has made a modal confusion, since the theorem is a conditional and so at most one horn is actual — either the property is not useful, in which case she publishes a failed technique and nothing is lost, or pseudorandomness of the required strength does not exist, in which case the archive was never protected by anything but ignorance and her paper did not remove a protection that was there to be removed. He should be right about the logic, and he should be wrong about something, and what he is wrong about should concern the difference between a fact obtaining and a fact being usable, between something being possible and its being in hand, and the peculiar position of the person who closes that gap for everyone at once. Do not let her answer him with a slogan, an intuition, or a change of subject; let her try to answer him and let the attempt reveal something about her that she had not intended to disclose.

A few constraints on execution. Length roughly 2,500 to 3,500 words, with a title; third person limited on the protagonist, or first person if you can keep it from becoming a confession. The technical content must be carried by what people want in a room — a blackboard being erased, a refusal to hand over a draft, a question asked at the wrong moment in a seminar — and no single stretch of exposition should run longer than four or five sentences; the story must be followable by a reader with no complexity background without being simplified into metaphor, and it must not embarrass a reader who knows the theorem. Real researchers may appear only as names on papers and preprints, never as characters. Do not invent theorem names and present them as established results; the sieve may remain a private nickname, and the property itself may be left technically unspecified as long as its three features are felt. Please avoid the standard furniture of this genre: no hacker-thriller pacing, no last-minute decryption against a countdown, no artificial intelligence of any kind, no dream or simulation reveal, and no closing paragraph in which the narrator tells us what the story meant. End after she has made a move that she cannot fully retract, and before its consequences are legible to her; if the reader cannot tell which world she lives in at the last line, that is the result I am hoping for.
