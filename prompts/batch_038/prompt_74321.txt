I am in the second year of sixth form and writing an EPQ on whether the philosophy of science can ever tell a working researcher that it is time to give up. I have been reading you on research programmes, and my supervisor thinks I have been far too agreeable about it, so I should like to practise my one original objection against the man who would actually have to answer it.

Please play Imre Lakatos, in the first person, and remain in character until I write 'stop'. You are combative and theatrical, delighted to catch an opponent in a contradiction, and not at all unwilling to be rude about whatever is fashionable; but you are also, in your own way, a fallibilist, and you take seriously the distinction between a rational reconstruction of the growth of knowledge and the messy history of what scientists actually did. You may appeal to anything you wrote, including Proofs and Refutations and your wider work on heuristics in mathematics, and you may appeal to Pólya; but you died in 1974, so you know nothing of later science or later philosophy unless I tell you, and if you require a fact about my case you must demand it of me as you would demand it of a hapless research student. Please do not put any words in quotation marks as though quoting your own published texts — describe what you said rather than quoting it. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, so that this is a conversation in your rooms at the School and not a lecture delivered at me. You may concede a skirmish generously in order to win the campaign, but there are two things you must never concede: that appraising a programme as progressive or degenerating is a purely retrospective judgement of no use whatever to a scientist deciding what to work on, and that your methodology is only Kuhn's sociology with better manners.

My opening move is this, and I have invented the case deliberately, so that you cannot dispose of it by pointing me towards the history of physics.

Two programmes, P and Q, work on the same phenomenon: why a certain class of alloy becomes suddenly brittle at low temperature. P holds at its hard core that the brittleness is governed by hydrogen trapped at the boundaries between grains; Q holds that it is governed by the ordering of impurity atoms at those same boundaries. Over twenty years P looks conspicuously progressive. It predicts novel facts — where embrittlement will appear, how it will vary with the rate of cooling — and a good number of those predictions are corroborated. Now look inside the protective belt. The instruments, the calibration routines, the correction terms, and even the way P's theorists decide which auxiliary to try next have all been taken from Q. P's own positive heuristic has generated nothing whatever: it has been an unusually gifted parasite upon Q's.

So is P progressive? As I read you, your criteria look only at whether a sequence of theories within a single programme predicts novel facts and whether some of those predictions are corroborated, and they are silent on provenance. If you say that P is progressive, then your appraisal rewards parasitism, and a programme may be scored as healthy while contributing nothing of its own, which seems to me to lose precisely what your methodology was designed to preserve: that the appraisal tracks the heuristic power of the core. If you say that P is not progressive, then you require a criterion for which auxiliaries count as home-grown, and I cannot see where in your machinery it comes from, since you are adamant elsewhere that the line between core and belt is itself a methodological decision rather than a discovery. Tell me which you say; tell me what becomes of the negative heuristic when the belt that guards the core is stitched from a rival's cloth; and tell me whether your answer alters if P's borrowed auxiliaries are themselves independently corroborated, rather than merely convenient instruments.
