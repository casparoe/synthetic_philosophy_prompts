I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar this semester and our whole unit on the self has basically been Hofstadter — strange loops, Escher's Drawing Hands, the idea that if you climb a hierachy of levels and end up back where you started, then something must have stayed outside the tangle the whole time, some level that never gets drawn by its own drawing. My prof's gloss, which I actually liked, was that the inviolate level isn't really a metaphysical claim, it's a claim about who's allowed to inspect whom: you can't debug the debugger while it's running. For my final paper we get to do a film instead of a straight essay, and I want to do Being John Malkovich. Problem is it's the most obvious choice imaginable, and I'm worried it's so obvious that there's no real argument left to make — just a recursion-flavored movie I can point at and say see. So help me figure out whether there's a genuine reading there.

Here's the distinction I've been chewing on, which I don't think is in Hofstadter and which I may be making up: (a) a plain regress, where the same relation just repeats at every level and never closes; (b) a real loop, where each level authors the level that authors it, so there's no first hand — Drawing Hands proper; (c) something that only looks tangled because we can't see the top, but has one — a puppet show always has a puppeteer standing offstage. Can you run the film's recursive bits through those three and tell me which each one is? I mean the scene where Malkovich goes through the door into his own head and finds a world where everybody is him and the only word anyone says is his own name; Lotte chasing Maxine through Malkovich's subconscious, which is a lower level nested inside a lower level; Lester's whole scheme, which reads to me like an explicit bid to be the level that never gets drawn — hopping vessel to vessel, always the puppeteer, never the puppet — with the 44th birthday deadline as the level below, a body's development, constraining the level above; and the ending, where Craig shows up too late for Malkovich and too early to have any purchase on Emily and is stuck in there for good, just watching, with Lester and his friends layered in on top of him.

The one I care about most: is Craig-in-Emily a homunculus? If the film literally stages that guy and then lets the regress keep going past him (Emily grows up, presumably becomes a vessel herself, someone's watching her watch), is it arguing against the picture, or just punishing one character for wanting to be at the top? Related: is there any scene in the movie that's an actual Drawing Hands — two levels mutually constituting each other with no top at all — or does the film structurally insist there's always a top hand and just keep showing what it costs to want to be it?
