Three years into a novel about a city that stops being a city. Drought, the grain barges thin out, the scribes' guild stops taking apprentices, and by the last third the palace is just a very large building with people cooking in it. My editor asked what the book is about and I said the word "collapse," and then sat there realizing I have no idea what that word is doing — whether it names an event, a judgement, or a mood I want the reader to have.

So: I want a decision tree I can run on my own manuscript.

Build it out of questions, not out of names. I've read the pop ones (Diamond, Tainter, the Easter Island argument) and I don't want a who's-who or a survey — I want the logical space, so the first thing you give me is the questions and the combinations they permit, and only then where the actual positions sit in it. Each branch node has to be something I could settle by looking at my draft, not by going and reading more: is the thing that's supposed to have died a set of structures (tax rolls, a script, the road upkeep), a population, a ruling group, or a way of being a person? Does the verdict flip depending on the timescale I pick — three generations, a century, one bad winter? Who is worse off afterwards, and does that have any bearing on whether we call it a collapse, or only on whether we call it a tragedy? Is "it just changed" ever a real answer, or a dodge dressed up as sophistication? Phrase every node so I could answer it without already knowing which leaf it drops me onto.

Six to eight nodes, and whatever leaves they generate — eight to twelve, say. Each leaf gets a name if one exists and "unnamed" if nobody actually holds it, plus one line on what the position costs you: the thing it has to deny or leave out to stay coherent.

Then the two bits I care most about. Flag the pairs of leaves your tree can't really seperate, where the questions just don't have the grip to pull them apart. And tell me which combinations in the space are empty — and for each, whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive to anybody, or because nobody's simply got round to it. That last category is where my next book lives, so be honest rather than encouraging.

Calibration, so I can check the tree actually sorts my book and not just the literature: if the script dies and the tax rolls end but the people left are eating better than they did under the palace, I still want to call something a collapse. If every institution survives intact but the population halves, I don't. If the language outlives the state and gets used to write down the new rulers' laws, that's a change of management, not an ending. And if the last speaker dies in a village where everything material is still standing, I think that's the worst case on my list, worse than the palace burning.

Hard limit: 800 words for the whole reply. Tree first, then the unseparable pairs and the empty cells, then two sentences on where my draft lands given the four calibration calls above. No preamble and nothing after.
