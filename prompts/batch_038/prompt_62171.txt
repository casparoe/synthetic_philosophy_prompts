I have been pressure-testing tranquilism — the view that what matters, finally, is only the absence of craving and the absence of suffering — against concrete cases, and my verdicts refuse to line up. In each of the following I have tried to hold the quantities of suffering and of unsatisfied craving roughly constant and vary only where the calm comes from.

1. A drug removes a drinker's craving for alcohol. He is calm, his life improves. Good, straightforwardly — and the calm is itself a good thing, not merely a privation.
2. The same drug at the same dose removes a parent's aching longing for a grown child who has cut contact. The parent is calm and reports no distress. Bad: a real loss, not a relief.
3. A monk, through years of practice rather than chemistry, extinguishes his craving for praise. Good, and indeed admirable.
4. A pianist loses the use of one hand and in time simply stops wanting to play; she is entirely content. Bad, though noticeably less bad than case 2.
5. A man craves the esteem of a friend who in fact despises him and performs friendship convincingly. The craving is satisfied in the sense that matters to desire-satisfaction theories, and nothing is suffered. Not good at all.
6. A singer craves acclaim and receives a standing ovation. That moment is genuinely good — yet her life overall seems worse than the monk's in case 3, despite containing more pleasure and fewer frustrated desires.
7. A person with chronic pain reaches full equanimity, no longer resisting or resenting it. Better than pain-plus-resistance, but still worse than the very same equanimity housed in a painless body.

My own tentative principle is that a craving may be set aside without loss when its object is a state of the craver, and not when its object concerns another person. It handles 1 and 2, but case 3 refutes it, since praise is nothing if not a fact about other minds. The case I keep circling is one I have not listed: compare a person constitutionally incapable of caring whether anyone else suffers — calm, content, untroubled — with someone who once cared and has had that caring extinguished. The two end states look identical from the inside, and I think the first is the worse of them.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidates, that delivers all seven verdicts plus this eighth one, or comes close? I know the standard responses — the two arrows, katastematic pleasure, the wirehead objection — and I want principles that go past them rather than restate them.
