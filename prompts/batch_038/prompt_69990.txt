Been going down a rabbit hole on the September 11th Victim Compensation Fund and I want someone to argue with me about it, because I don't think its ever gotten discussed as what it actually was: probably the most explicit large-scale interpersonal utility comparision a government has ever run, in public, with named individuals and dollar amounts attached.

Facts I'm taking as given, so you don't have to hedge on them. Congress set it up within days of the attacks. Kenneth Feinberg was appointed special master by the attorney general and had unusually broad discretion to write the rules himself. Taking the money meant waiving the right to sue the airlines, and part of the point was to head off a litigation free-for-all that could have bankrupted the carriers. Awards were built off each victim's projected lifetime earnings — age, income, occupation, dependents — plus a flat, income-independent noneconomic component (same dollars for every victim, with an increment for a spouse and for each dependent), minus collateral sources like life insurance. Net effect: families of the highly paid finance people in the towers got awards in the millions, families of dishwashers and cleaners got orders of magnitude less. Feinberg then used his discretion to squeeze the distribution, working from a stated rule of thumb that roughly 85% of the money should not go to the top 15% of claimant families, under the principle of "narrow the gap" between the largest and smallest awards. About 97% of eligible families took the deal, roughly $7 billion went out, and it was the high earners who felt stiffed relative to what a jury might have given them.

Now run it in both directions.

Direction one — what the theory of interpersonal utility comparison says about this. Start from Robbins: comparisons across persons are value judgments dressed up as science, so welfare economics should confine itself to Pareto-type claims. Then the Harsanyi/Sen side: you cannot aggregate anything at all without comparability, and Harsanyi's aggregation theorem says a few innocuous-looking conditions plus a social preference ordering buys you utilitarianism. Then do the mapping honestly.

- Is the lost-earnings component an interpersonal comparison, or a different animal — a replacement/compensation principle that never claims to measure anybody's welfare? If it is a comparison, spell out exactly what it assumes about the marginal utility of income. My read is that it assumes a dollar of lost earnings means the same welfare loss to a managing director's family as to a line cook's, which is the opposite of what diminishing marginal utility would tell you, and yet the whole thing was sold in vaguely utilitarian language. Adjudicate that rather than just noting the tension.
- Is the flat noneconomic piece a refusal to compare (everybody's death counts the same, no ranking of lives) or a covert comparison (identical cardinal pain-and-suffering loss across people, which is a substantive claim, not a neutral placeholder)? I've seen both readings and I don't think they can both be right.

Direction two — what the fund tells us about the theory. Which was doing the work in the compression: diminishing marginal utility of income, in which case Feinberg was being more utilitarian-consistent than the statute's individualized-earnings language and the law was the anomaly; or something non-utilitarian like equality, social solidarity, or just the political unsustainability of publishing an official ranking of lives? Those come apart. Tell me which you think it is and what feature of the fund's actual design would let you distinguish them. Bonus if you can say whether the high earners' complaint has any standing inside a utilitarian analysis at all, or whether it's entirely a claim about the litigation counterfactual.

Then say where the framework goes quiet instead of forcing it to speak. Three spots I suspect: (a) the comparisons are between households, not persons, and dependency structure is baked into the award, so whose utility function is even being measured; (b) compensating a loss that has already happened is a different decision problem from splitting a fixed pot to maximize the total, so the standard aggregation arguments may not apply — and if they don't, what should govern; (c) the ~97% takeup means we never observed the counterfactual, and I can't tell whether that's evidence the formula was acceptable or evidence that exhausted people had been told suing would bankrupt the airlines.

Last thing, and push back if you think it's dumb. My current take is that the fund's legitimacy depended on everyone believing the awards were non-comparative — individualized hearings, each family treated as a unique case, Feinberg personally sitting through most of them, no public table ranking lives — while the arithmetic underneath was relentlessly comparative and tracked income. If that's right, the lesson isn't that interpersonal comparisons are impossible or that they're fine; it's that institutions survive them by keeping them offstage, and the VCF is just an unusually legible instance. Tell me whether that holds up or whether I'm pattern-matching.
