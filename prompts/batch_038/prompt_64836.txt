I am in the third year of a doctoral programme in the history of economic thought; my dissertation concerns how pre-modern writers defended the commensuration of qualitatively unlike goods, and I am currently working through the money-and-exchange discussion in Book V of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* (roughly Bekker 1133a–1133b). I have reached a point at which re-reading is no longer helping me, and I would value a careful, text-driven analysis rather than a summary of the standard lines of interpretation.

Below is the passage I am working on, in the English text I have been using (the Loeb/Rackham translation as hosted on the Perseus Digital Library). The bracketed numerals are that edition's section divisions of the chapter; I have removed its footnote markers.

[8] "Now proportionate requital is effected by diagonal conjunction. For example, let A be a builder, B a shoemaker, C a house, and D a shoe. It is required that the builder shall receive from the shoemaker a portion of the product of his labor, and give him a portion of the product of his own. Now if proportionate equality between the products be first established, and then reciprocation take place, the requirement indicated will have been achieved; but if this is not done, the bargain is not equal, and intercourse does not continue. For it may happen that the product of one of the parties is worth more than that of the other, and in that case therefore they have to be equalized."

[9] "This holds good with the other arts as well; for they would have passed out of existence if the active element did not produce, and did not receive the equivalent in quantity and quality of what the passive element receives. For an association for interchange of services is not formed between two physicians, but between a physician and a farmer, and generally between persons who are different, and who may be unequal, though in that case they have to be equalized."

[10] "Hence all commodities exchanged must be able to be compared in some way. It is to meet this requirement that men have introduced money; money constitutes in a manner a middle term, for it is a measure of all things, and so of their superior or inferior value, that is to say, how many shoes are equivalent to a house or to a given quantity of food. As therefore a builder is to a shoemaker, so must such and such a number of shoes be to a house, [or to a given quantity of food]; for without this reciprocal proportion, there can be no exchange and no association; and it cannot be secured unless the commodities in question be equal in a sense."

[11] "It is therefore necessary that all commodities shall be measured by some one standard, as was said before. And this standard is in reality demand, which is what holds everything together, since if men cease to have wants or if their wants alter, exchange will go on no longer, or will be on different lines. But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will."

[12] "There will therefore be reciprocal proportion when the products have been equated, so that as farmer is to shoemaker, so may the shoemaker's product be to the farmer's product. And when they exchange their products they must reduce them to the form of a proportion, otherwise one of the two extremes will have both the excesses; whereas when they have their own, they then are equal, and can form an association together, because equality in this sense can be established in their case (farmer A, food C, shoemaker B, shoemaker's product equalized D); whereas if it were impossible for reciprocal proportion to be effected in this way, there could be no association between them."

[13] "That it is demand which, by serving as a single standard, holds such an association together, is shown by the fact that, when there is no demand for mutual service on the part of both or at least of one of the parties, no exchange takes place between them [as when someone needs something that one has oneself, for instance the state offering a license to export corn in exchange for wine]. This inequality of demand has therefore to be equalized."

[14] "Now money serves us as a guarantee of exchange in the future: supposing we need nothing at the moment, it ensures that exchange shall be possible when a need arises, for it meets the requirement of something we can produce in payment so as to obtain the thing we need. Money, it is true, is liable to the same fluctuation of demand as other commodities, for its purchasing power varies at different times; but it tends to be comparatively constant. Hence the proper thing is for all commodities to have their prices fixed; this will ensure that exchange, and consequently association, shall always be possible. Money then serves as a measure which makes things commensurable and so reduces them to equality. If there were no exchange there would be no association, and there can be no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability. Though therefore it is impossible for things so different to become commensurable in the strict sense, our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes."

[15] "There must therefore be some one standard, and this accepted by agreement (which is why it is called nomisma, customary currency); for such a standard makes all things commensurable, since all things can be measured by money. Let A be a house, B ten minae and C a bedstead." The house is then put at five minae, i.e. half of B, and the bedstead at one tenth of B, "it is now clear how many bedsteads are equal to one house, namely five."

[16] "It is clear that before money existed this is how the rate of exchange was actually stated—five beds for a house—since there is no real difference between that and the price of five beds for a house."

For several of my questions the Greek matters, so I give the clauses I have been staring at (Perseus Greek text): at [12], εἰ δὲ μή, ἀμφοτέρας ἕξει τὰς ὑπεροχὰς τὸ ἕτερον ἄκρον, and the term-list γεωργὸς α, τροφὴ γ, σκυτοτόμος β, τὸ ἔργον αὐτοῦ τὸ ἰσασμένον δ; at [13], ὅτι δ᾽ ἡ χρεία συνέχει ὥσπερ ἕν τι ὄν; at [14], τῇ μὲν οὖν ἀληθείᾳ ἀδύνατον τὰ τοσοῦτον διαφέροντα σύμμετρα γενέσθαι, πρὸς δὲ τὴν χρείαν ἐνδέχεται ἱκανῶς; and at [15], ἓν δή τι δεῖ εἶναι, τοῦτο δ᾽ ἐξ ὑποθέσεως: διὸ νόμισμα καλεῖται.

My questions, in the order in which they block me:

1. **Argument structure.** Please lay out the sequence of claims in [8]–[16] as an argument, identifying premises, intermediate conclusions, and the final conclusion, and marking exactly where money enters and in what capacity. I count at least three distinct jobs assigned to it — a middle term that measures all things ([10]), a conventional representative of demand ([11]), and a surety for exchange that has not yet become necessary ([14]) — and I cannot tell whether Aristotle means these as three descriptions of one function or as three functions introduced ad hoc. If they are one function, what is it?

2. **The diagonal, and my reconstruction of it.** Here is a hypothesis I would like you to test and, if it fails, correct. I suspect the odd order of the term-list at [12] — γεωργὸς α, τροφὴ γ, σκυτοτόμος β, τὸ ἔργον αὐτοῦ τὸ ἰσασμένον δ, i.e. farmer A, food C, shoemaker B, his product equalized D — is not accidental. The proportion stated is A:B::C:D (producer to producer as product to product), and exchange is the joining of the diagonals, A with D and C with B: the farmer takes the shoes, the shoemaker takes the food. On this reading "diagonal conjunction" is a claim about which pairs actually swap, and the warning that "one of the two extremes will have both the excesses" describes what goes wrong if the pairing is done along the sides of the proportion instead. Three sub-questions: (a) is the pairing A–D, B–C the right one, and does it follow from the text or only from my arrangement of it; (b) what are the *two* excesses in ἀμφοτέρας ἕξει τὰς ὑπεροχὰς, given that ὑπεροχή is singular in form but the adjective is plural — two surpluses accruing to one party, or the two sides of a single imbalance; (c) what work does ἰσασμένον ("equalized") do when applied to D — equalized by money, by the parties' agreement, or by some prior measure of demand, and does the perfect passive participle imply that this equalization has already happened before the proportion is written down (as [8] and [12] both seem to say)?

3. **The disappearance of the persons.** The proportion at [8]–[12] has four terms, two of them persons; the example at [15] has three terms — house, ten minae, bedstead — and no persons at all, with money occupying the middle position. How are these meant to be the same proportion? The readings I can construct are: (i) [15] is the degenerate case of [12] in which the persons have dropped out because their products have already been priced, so B (ten minae) is a stand-in for the demand the persons embody; (ii) [15] is a continuous proportion of a different logical type, and Aristotle has quietly substituted it for the diagonal one without remarking the change; (iii) [15] is not a proportion about exchange at all but a demonstration that a single unit suffices to state a ratio, with [16] then assimilating barter to pricing. Please say which is most defensible, what in the text decides it, and whether the decision changes the answer to question 5.

4. **What chreia is.** Rackham renders the term uniformly as "demand," but it seems to me to be doing at least three distinguishable jobs in this passage: a psychological state of the parties (wanting, and ceasing to want); an objective functional relation between goods and the requirements of a household or polis that is not self-sufficient in isolation; and a normative bond, mutual service as the condition of association persisting at all. Does the argument need one of these specifically, or does it slide between them? I ask because the consequences diverge sharply: on the first reading Aristotle is close to making relative prices a function of subjective want, on the second a function of contribution to a self-sufficient whole, and on the third the passage is about reciprocal justice and not about value in any sense I would recognise. Relatedly, I do not think Rackham's "our demand furnishes a sufficiently accurate common measure for practical purposes" is the most natural construal of πρὸς δὲ τὴν χρείαν ἐνδέχεται ἱκανῶς, which reads to me more like "with respect to need, it is possible sufficiently" — commensurability is achievable *relative to* need rather than *because of* the accuracy of our measuring instrument. Is my construal tenable? And either way, does the concession at [14] contradict the chain "no exchange without equality, and no equality without commensurability"? I want to know whether Aristotle is admitting that real exchange is systematically unequal but socially sustainable, or only that our standards are approximations of a commensuration that exists.

5. **ἐξ ὑποθέσεως.** "There must therefore be some one standard, and this accepted by agreement" renders ἓν δή τι δεῖ εἶναι, τοῦτο δ᾽ ἐξ ὑποθέσεως. Rackham reads the phrase socially (the standard is fixed by agreement), which then motivates the etymology of nomisma from nomos. But ἐξ ὑποθέσεως can equally be methodological: the unit is *posited*, as a geometer posits a unit magnitude, and the etymology then attaches to its conventional rather than natural status. Does anything in the immediate context favour one construal, and does the choice matter? It matters to me because the methodological reading would make Aristotle's money a unit of account logically prior to its role as medium of exchange, and I want to know whether that is available to me or whether I am importing a modern distinction.

6. **A numerical worry about the persons' ratio.** Take the case in [10] and [15] together: a house worth five minae, a bedstead (or, substituting the shoes of [8]) a product worth one mina. If the proportion is A:B::C:D and A:B is a ratio *between the producers*, then it looks as though the terms of trade must track the producers' relative standing, so that a builder of twice the worth of a shoemaker would require twice as many shoes for the same house. That consequence strikes me as both textually suggested and substantively absurd, and I cannot tell how to block it. Does [9] — "persons who are different, and who may be unequal, though in that case they have to be equalized" — license reading A:B as a disparity that must be *neutralized* before the proportion is formed, rather than as a ratio preserved in the price? Please work the arithmetic through on both readings and tell me whether the text yields a determinate answer or leaves the question open.

7. **The bracketed clause.** The corn-export example at [13] is bracketed as doubtful in the Greek text I am using. What does the argument lose if it is excised, and does its bracketing affect how strongly [13] can be pressed as evidence for the psychological reading of chreia in question 4?
