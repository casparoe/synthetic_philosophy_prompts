Thirty-odd years I spent grading rainfall and temperature forecasts, mostly with the Brier score, so when I retired and wandered into philosophy and found people arguing that your degrees of belief ought to be coherent because incoherent ones are guaranteed to come out worse under a rule like that — it felt like coming home. But then I started messing about inventing scoring rules of my own and now I couldnt tell you which of my inventions would wreck the argument and which are harmless variations on a theme. All of them grade a single belief p in a claim that turns out either true or false, so w is 1 or 0:

A. plain negative squared error, the old workhorse.
B. the logarithmic one, where a confident wrong answer costs you everything — penalty runs off to infinity.
C. squared error, but with forgiveness: anything landing within 0.1 of the truth scores zero, no penalty at all, the way we always had a tolerance band in the lab.
D. squared error plus a bonus for decisiveness: add 0.3 times |2p − 1|, so a mushy 0.5 gets marked down whatever the weather does.
E. squared error, except the penalty is waived whenever the truth was something you couldnt possibly have had evidence about, since it seems to me unfair to grade a man on luck.
F. no summing at all across your beliefs — only your single worst one counts, and the goal is to make that least-good belief as good as you can.

Put them in tiers, from the ones you'd happily build the whole accuracy-first edifice on down to the ones you'd never touch, and give a sentence or two per rule on what exactly breaks and what survives. Then tell me which ones you suspect are me stumbling blind into a dispute people already argue about, and where my instincts from forecasting are leading me astray. Plain prose only, please — no headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, just write it out to me like an email.
