I co-host a grad-student-run podcast for our philosophy department, and next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a thoroughgoing Schopenhauerian account of musical content — roughly, that music isn't about the emotions at all but is a presentation of willing itself, of striving and slackening, tension and release, with no object it's about. She uses it against both camps in the current debate: she denies that hearing sadness in music is a matter of being made sad, and she denies the formalist line that music just carries expressive properties we recognize the way we recognize the sadness of a willow. What's left, she says, is that music gives you a non-conceptual acquaintance with the will. My co-host and I split the prep and I drew the metaphysics half, which is unfortunate, because I've read the Schopenhauer passages on music twice and I still can't tell which bits she gets to keep and which bits she has to throw away.

Draft eight to ten interview questions, and for each one give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. She's extremely good at converting a metaphysical objection into a remark about what listening feels like, so I'd like the questions ordered so that retreat gets progressively harder to pull off, with the last one asking what would actually count against the view — some musical fact, some listener's report, some experiment. Please make sure at least one question lands on each of these: the hierarchy of voices (deep bass as the lowest grades of nature, the middle harmonic voices as plants and animals, melody as the will's highest objectification, human striving moving freely) — is that load-bearing for her or just period decoration, and if she drops it, what survives of the claim that music copies the will rather than the Ideas? The individuation problem: the will is supposed to sit outside space, time, and the principle of individuation, so why isn't every piece of music a copy of the same thing, and what accounts for the difference between a Mahler slow movement and an hour of sustained drone — doesn't she need back the very representational distinctions she rejected? The tension with the pessimism: aesthetic contemplation is meant to quiet the will, but music is the will's own motion, so why does listening feel like release rather than like being wound up, and which half does she give up? Vocal and program music — song, cantata, opera, Berlioz telling you in print what the piece is about. And what separates her volitional content from the views she's attacking, since its not obvious she's said anything more than "emotion" with the word "striving" swapped in.

Nothing generic — I don't want a single question about what drew her to Schopenhauer or how the music material fits the system as a whole. Keep the whole thing under 900 words: questions and follow-ups only, at most two sentences of setup per question, and no paragraphs defending why you chose each one. Also don't put words in her mouth beyond the view I sketched; I'd rather have questions that would work on any competent defender of it.
