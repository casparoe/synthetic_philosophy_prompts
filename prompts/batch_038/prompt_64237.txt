I write novels for a living, and I have come to distrust how confidently I judge my characters' relations to work and to rest. Here are the verdicts I keep arriving at; I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

The man who works eighty hours a week at a trade he despises in order to pay for his mother's care: admirable, without qualification.

The woman who works the same hours to pay for her children's private schooling, the local schools being perfectly adequate: not admirable — I find I want to write her as mistaken about her own life.

The clerk in Tokyo who collapses at his desk and dies, and whose firm issues a statement praising his devotion: the firm is culpable and the man pitiable rather than admirable, though I cannot write him as simply a victim, since in some sense he chose it.

The surgeon who works himself to death across the course of an epidemic: here I want the reader to admire the excess itself. This is the pair I cannot reconcile with the one above; the hours and the outcome are close to identical.

The woman of independent means who devotes her life to scholarship she never publishes: enviable, and not wasteful.

The salaried employee who has automated his post down to four honest hours a week, says nothing, and continues to draw his full pay: a cheat, though he delivers everything the job exists to deliver. The freelancer who bills by the result and works the same four hours is, in the same draft, a hero.

My own tentative principle is that work is good when it is necessary and leisure good when it has been earned; I can see at once that the unpublished scholar breaks it and the automated clerk does not. Give me a principle, or a small set of rival candidates, precise enough to be tested: state which clause is doing the work in each case, say plainly which of my verdicts the principle cannot keep, and tell me what it implies for two cases I have not yet written — the small-town businessman whose sense of a calling will not let him retire at seventy, and the civil servant who works his thirty-five hours and insists, as a matter of principle, that no one may reach him after six in the evening. If the principle must choose between treating work as an instrument for good ends and treating it as an activity with standards of its own, I want to know which way it goes, and what that will cost me in the drafts.
