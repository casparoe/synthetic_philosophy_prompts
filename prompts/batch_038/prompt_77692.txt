I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student, and this semester I'm in a seminar on the epistemology of automated systems while also holding the graduate-student seat on my institute's Model Deployment Review Panel. The seat is nominally about broader impacts, but in practice the other members mostly use me to ask whether a lab's safety argument actually holds together, and this month's agenda has a proposal I'd like help thinking through before I write anything. A lab here has been running a pilot of the iterated amplification-and-distillation scheme we covered in week four: a large model is paired with human reviewers who decompose a target job into subtasks small enough that a person can check each one, and the outputs of that amplified pipeline are then used to train a much smaller model intended for actual deployment. The deployment in question is a triage assistant for our research computing service, which reads internal requests and patches and drafts a prioritized response. What makes the proposal unusual is its second half: the team wants approval not just to deploy the distilled model, but to have it serve as one of the reviewers inside the amplification step for the *next* iteration, on the grounds that otherwise the whole program is bottlenecked by human attention, which does not scale.

Their pilot report is candid about two results, and the panel has split over what they mean. First, at the level of individual subtasks the distilled model reproduced the amplified system's judgments about 97% of the time on held-out data, but when the subtask outputs were recombined into complete triage reviews, agreement with the teacher fell to 81%, and the disagreements cluster in cases where the amplified system had to link signals spread across several subtasks. Second, where the teacher had hedged — flagging a concern as speculative, or noting that it needed more context — the student tends to reproduce the conclusion without the hedging, so its reviews read as confident even in exactly the places where the teacher was not. The team treats the second finding as a formatting bug and plans to train an explicit uncertainty signal to match the teacher's hedges; they treat the first as a competence gap rather than a safety gap, since the failure mode is under-flagging rather than overreaching, and they note that human auditors sample 3% of the student's output. My co-panelist, a statistician, keeps saying the 81% figure is meaningless until someone reports the base rate of requests where cross-subtask linkage actually matters, which the team has not done; the lab's lead says any further delay means the alignment problem gets solved by an oversight budget that cannot possibly scale, which is a risk in its own right.

Please draft the memo I'll circulate to the panel ahead of the meeting, roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, written to be acted on rather than to survey the literature. It needs a clear bottom line — approve, reject, or approve subject to conditions — and if it recommends conditions, those conditions should be operational: what evidence has to be produced, by whom, and in what time frame, given that the panel meets again in six weeks and the team wants to begin collecting next-iteration data before the end of term. I'd like it to engage the strongest version of the team's case, including the scaling argument and their competence-versus-safety distinction, and to say honestly whether that argument changes the verdict or merely constrains it. It should also give the panel something reusable — a short set of criteria we could apply at the next iteration rather than a one-off ruling, since this proposal will come back to us. Finally, and this matters to me personally as someone who is still learning this material: please separate what is settled among people who work on these systems from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested position as though it were consensus. Where your recommendation depends on a claim that reasonable experts disagree about — for instance, whether alignment survives being decomposed and reassembled, or what distillation transfers besides surface behavior — say so explicitly, and tell me what would change the recommendation if that claim turned out to be false.
