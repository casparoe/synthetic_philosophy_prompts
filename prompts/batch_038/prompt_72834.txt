I'm giving a 25-minute talk at a workshop on philosophy of AI and cognitive science next month and honestly I'm dreading the Q&A more than the talk itself. My thesis: the inversion of reasoning everyone credits to Darwin and then to Turing is actually two separate inversions that have gotten welded together, and the welding is what makes the whole debate feel stuck.

One is an inversion in the order of explanation — order and design get explained by processes that contain none of either, so comprehension drops out of the explanation of how the competence arose. The other is an inversion in the order of attribution — a system gets counted as intelligent on the strength of what it does out in the open, with no condition at all on how it got to be able to do it. My claim is that only the second one is doing any work in the current arguments about large language models. So when people say these systems have competence without comprehension, they aren't reporting a discovery about machines, they're restating an attribution rule they themselves adopted and then acting surprised by it.

Sketch of the arguement so you have something to target: (i) attribution-by-performance is a policy about what counts as evidence, not a metaphysical thesis; (ii) once you adopt it, claims about whether a system comprehends come out either trivially true or empty, and the same goes for denials; (iii) so the only live question is which explanatory inversion, if any, the models actually instantiate, and that's a question about the causal structure that produced the competence; (iv) therefore both the it's-just-stochastic-parroting objection and the it-passed-the-test-deal-with-it reply are non-responses that trade on the ambiguity between (i) and (iii).

The room: about 40 people, mostly empirical ML researchers who think understanding is a word that never got cashed out and who are allergic to anything that smells like vitalism; two developmental psychologists who hold that cognition has to be embodied and grounded to count as cognition; one person doing formal work on metrics for machine understanding; and a historian of philosophy who knows the Darwin/Turing lineage much better than I do and will police my attributions. Nobody there is a Lewis-style metaphysician, so please don't hand me incredulous-stare objections or anything about possible worlds — I won't get those.
