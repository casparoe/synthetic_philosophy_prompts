I'm trying to get clear on the whole "is the world getting less violent?" argument and I keep having the feeling that the two sides aren't even answering the same question, but I can't pin down where they diverge. Can you walk me through the conceptual geography of it?

Start here: I think there are at least three different quantities people slide back and forth between without noticing. (i) the chance that some randomly picked person suffers violence in a given year, (ii) the total amount of violence happening in the world, and (iii) how that violence is distributed across people and groups. Explain how these can come apart, and build a small concrete scenario to do it with — something like a country where the absolute number of homicides stays flat for forty years while the population doubles and the killings get concentrated into a shrinking subgroup. What happened to "violence" in that country on each of the three readings, and is there a fact about which reading the ordinary question is asking?

Then I want the measurement layer explained properly, not just gestured at. Recorded violence can go up while actual violence goes down, because reporting and record-keeping improve and because the boundaries of the category expand over time (things that used to be considered ordinary discipline or a private family matter get reclassified as violence). For war, the numbers depend enormously on method — counted bodies, household surveys, excess mortality — and on whether you count deaths indirectly caused by a war (disease, famine, displacement, collapsed health systems) as war deaths at all. And per-capita rates have their own problem: the denominator is a population whose age structure has shifted hugely, so a rate can move for demographic reasons that have nothing to do with anyone's behavior.

Then the question I actually care about: is there a fact of the matter? I can see three ways the debate might fail to have a determinate answer and I want you to explain each one and then tell me which really apply. (a) It's a merely verbal dispute that dissolves once you fix a definition. (b) Fixing the definition still doesn't settle it, because the harms aren't commensurable — to say whether things got better you need weights, some exchange rate between one murder and N assaults or M years of living under threat, and maybe there's no principled way to get those weights. (I'm thinking of how epidemiology just constructs disability weights for burden-of-disease estimates, and of the index-number headaches in measuring inflation, where the weighting scheme itself changes the answer.) (c) "Violence" isn't a natural kind at all — its boundaries track our normative attitudes, which is why the argument over whether structural or economic harm counts feels unresolvable rather than empirical. Different equally reasonable extensions of the term give different trends, and none of them is the answer.

Which of those three do you think is going on, and is it the same one at every layer? Also: which sub-questions in this whole mess do have determinate answers, and which one is the one people are actually fighting about when they think they're fighting about a trend in the world? I want the reasoning, not just the verdict.

If you think one of my three framings above is muddled or collapses into another, say so and fix it rather than working with it.

No citations or reading list, please — this is just for my own understanding.
