I co-teach a 90-minute research-methods session within an evidence-based-practice module for 24 second-year nursing students, seated at six tables of four. These students are fluent at describing effect sizes and remarkably casual about moving from what the audit shows to what the ward should do. I want an in-class game that makes them feel the is-ought gap from the inside, and feel the specific pull of the naturalistic fallacy, with no lecture beforehand and with no use of the terms "is-ought," "guillotine," "naturalistic fallacy," or "open question" until the debrief.

The skeleton I have in mind: each table gets a hand of purely descriptive cards drawn from one recurring clinical case (night-shift staffing ratios, an infection-rate audit, a family's stated preferences, a medication-error report) together with a very small number of "bridge" cards that are frankly normative, and a target recommendation they must justify. Every step of their chain must be licensed by a card in hand, and an auditor rotating from another table must be able to put a finger on the exact card that supplies the normativity at each step.

Two things I cannot figure out how to build in. First, a deliberate subset of trap cards phrased in thick or quasi-clinical evaluative vocabulary — "deteriorating," "non-compliant," "within a healthy range," "the normal course," "an appropriate level of care" — that read as descriptive and are worth substantially more if a team slips one through unchallenged than they cost if an auditor catches it. I want the payoff structure to make smuggling profitable but risky, so the students feel the temptation rather than being warned against it. Second, a round in which each table proposes a wholly descriptive definition of a normative term they have already been using ("harm," "benefit," "recovery"), and opponents score by producing a live open question against that definition — with some mechanism for penalizing the cheap logical escapes, both the added-disjunct trick and the inference from a role-occupying fact ("she is the charge nurse, therefore she ought to do what a charge nurse does"), which otherwise lets a smart table win the round on a technicality.

Please supply: enough of the card lists to run it as written, a scoring scheme requiring no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, the facilitator's moves and minimal scripted remarks at each transition, and a debrief that maps identifiable moments in play onto the gap, onto the definitional error, and onto the distinction between a premise that is merely missing and one that cannot be discharged at all. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with a group this size — in particular whether the thick-concept round degenerates into an argument about vocabulary rather than inference — and be frank about which feature of the philosophical problem a card game cannot reproduce, so that I know what I have to supply myself.
