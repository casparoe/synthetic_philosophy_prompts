So I'm in a 20th-century ethics seminar and we've spent the last few weeks on Williams — Jim and the Indians, negative responsibility, the one-thought-too-many bit about the man who can save his wife or a stranger. My study partner and I tried to turn the integrity objection into a general argument and it worked way too well on paper. She's telling the prof it's a reductio of impartial morality as such and I got volunteered to play skeptic. Heres the chain:

(1) A moral theory is action-guiding for an agent only if the agent can deliberate with it without giving up being the person who deliberates — that is, without having to hold their own deepest projects as things whose weight gets settled from the impartial point of view.
(2) For any impartial theory T, there are possible situations in which T requires the agent to sacrifice or betray a ground project for reasons that have nothing to do with that project (Jim facing the wall; the husband deciding whether to swim to his wife or the stranger).
(3) In those situations, deliberating with T means taking up the impartial standpoint towards one's own commitment.
(4) But a commitment you hold only on condition that it survives an impartial test is not the commitment you had — conditional holding of that kind is already a withdrawal from it, not a way of having it more carefully.
(5) So in those situations, deliberating with T leaves the agent without the very project the deliberation was supposed to be about.
(6) A theory whose application in a situation destroys the standpoint from which it was to be applied cannot have been guiding anyone in that situation.
(7) So no impartial moral theory is action-guiding in any situation of type (2).
(8) And since (2) holds of every impartial theory — including the ones that give integrity a lot of weight, because they still make that weight depend on getting the impartial balance right — no impartial moral theory is action-guiding at all.

Where does it break? Each line looks fine to me in isolation, and yet (8) cant be right, because if it were it would knock out Williams's own view too: even something built around practical necessity sometimes tells you your project has to give way, and I don't think he'd accept being told he has no theory whatsoever. I'd most like to keep (2) since it's just the cases, and honestly I'd like to keep (1) too, because that's the interesting idea — though I realize that's exactly what someone who wants the argument to fail would say, so I don't trust my own instinct here. Tell me which step to reject, explain why it looked so plausible to us (we both thought (4) was just a restatement of what integrity means), and then price the alternatives: what does a consequentialist give up by rejecting (1) versus (4) versus (6), and does the objection survive as a narrower point about Jim even if the general version collapses?
