So I've been going down a rabbit hole that started, embarrassingly, from an argument in a discord about whether NFTs "solve" art forgery, and I ended up reading Nelson Goodman's Languages of Art, which is way more formal and dry than I expected but I think has the actual conceptual machinery I'm looking for. Here's my confusion. Goodman splits the arts into autographic ones, where the original/fake distinction is meaningful because even a perfect copy is suspect, and allographic ones, where the work just is the class of things that comply with some score or character, so a copy can't be a forgery any more than a second printing of a novel is a fake novel. Separately there's this singular vs multiple thing — one unique object versus many instances — and I keep conflating the two distinctions and then catching myself, because an etching is obviously multiple but still autographic, since what makes an impression genuine is that it came off the artist's own plate rather than that it matches some spec. I think the load-bearing idea is that an art is allographic only if there's a real notation in play, and a notation needs characters that are disjoint and finitely articulated — like, no mark can comply with two different characters, and for any two marks there's a determinate fact about whether they comply with the same one. My question is: can you explain, at the level of what the notation is actually doing in the theory, why Goodman thinks you need that extra formal condition instead of just counting instances? I want the gut-level version of why "there are lots of them" isn't enough to make forgery impossible, and what work the disjointness/articulateness requirements are doing. Please don't give me a book report or a citation list, I have the book, I just can't get the intuition.

The reason I care is that I want to push it on a case that I don't think Goodman ever considered and that my discord friends and I can't resolve. Suppose a conceptual artist releases a deterministic little program plus a specific seed, and declares that the artwork is whatever image file a compliant run produces — she doesn't make or keep a canvas at all. Now three things happen. One: a very skilled painter studies the output and hand-paints a canvas that matches it pixel for pixel, without ever running the code, signs it, and sells it as an instance of the work. Two: someone runs the exact published code but swaps in a different seed, producing a different image, and sells that as an instance too. Three: someone rewrites the whole algorithm from scratch in another language so that it emits a byte-identical file, and starts minting instances from their version.

Which of those three is a forgery under Goodman's framework, and more importantly, what does the answer reveal about what the notation condition is tracking? My instinct is that case one is a fake and case three is not, but that instinct seems to depend on the causal history of the object rather than on compliance with a spec, which is exactly the thing the notation was supposed to make irrelevant. Is there a principled way to say that running-the-code is part of compliance, or does the whole autographic/allograpic line just dissolve once the "score" is executable? And a bonus sub-question I can't stop thinking about: if the painter in case one is genuinely more impressive than the artist, does the standard line about value inhering in the achievement rather than in the object give a different verdict than the notation line does, and are those two arguments actually independent of each other or is one smuggling in the other? I'd rather you engage with the case as stated and tell me where my reasoning goes wrong than hedge into "it depends how you define art."
