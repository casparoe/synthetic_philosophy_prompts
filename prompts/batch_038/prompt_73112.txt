I run market structure and product governance at a derivatives exchange: contract specification, reference-data and benchmark administration, position limits, clearing and default arrangements, and the abuse cases that come with them — corners, squeezes, and trades designed to move a fixing rather than to express a view. Twenty years of it, and essentially no training in political philosophy. I have been asked to comment on a proposal called futarchy, and I want to understand it properly before I do. My plan is to translate it into the only vocabulary in which I think clearly, and then to see what falls out of the translation.

So that you know which version I mean, here is the mechanism as I have had it described to me. Elected representatives settle, by ordinary democratic means, on a measure of national wellbeing to be maximised over some horizon. Candidate policies are then valued in prediction markets: for each policy there is a conditional contract that pays out on the wellbeing measure on the assumption that the policy is adopted. The policy whose conditional price is higher is the policy that gets adopted. The associated slogan is that the public votes on values but bets on beliefs — the choice of ends stays political, the estimation of means is handed to prices. If the version you know differs from this in some detail that matters for the translation, flag the difference and deal with both.

Please write from first principles. I do not want a reading list, a bibliography, or named defenders and critics, and I would rather you reasoned about the mechanism than recalled the literature around it. Use British English throughout, and do not explain my own terms back to me.

I would like the answer in five parts.

**1. A term sheet.** Draft the contract specification for one futarchic conditional policy contract in the form I would put to a product committee: underlying and its reference-data source, notional, payoff formula, observation window, resolution date, resolution agent and the governance framework it would have to operate under, issuer and who stands behind the obligation, margin and default handling, eligible participants, position limits, and the manipulation controls. Wherever the proposal does not supply a value for a field, leave the field blank and say so explicitly. Do not fill gaps with standard derivatives practice and then present the result as what the proposal says.

**2. Clause-by-clause translation.** Take these four claims and restate each in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: (a) that prediction markets aggregate dispersed information about the consequences of policy better than deliberation or expert committees do; (b) that the conditional price is an estimate of the expected value of the metric under that policy; (c) that values and beliefs can be separated in the way the slogan suggests, with the former voted on and the latter bet on; (d) that adopting whichever policy carries the higher conditional price is a legitimate way of reaching a collective decision.

**3. The residue report.** Present this as a table with the following columns: the claim as the proposal states it; the nearest counterpart in my vocabulary; a verdict of clean, distorted, or no counterpart; and a sentence or two on what the distortion or the gap consists in. Below the table, take each item marked distorted or no counterpart and give your view on which of two things it is: a limitation of my vocabulary, which has no room for something the proposal genuinely needs to say; or a sign that the proposal's claim was less determinate than it sounded. I am especially interested in what happens to a price when you ask whether it is an expectation at all rather than an equilibrium outcome carrying premia of various kinds, and in what disciplines the price of a conditional contract on a policy that is never adopted and therefore never resolves — but treat these as questions to work through, not as conclusions I am handing you.

**4. The reverse residue.** List what my vocabulary enables me to say about the proposal that the proposal's own vocabulary appears unable to say — about who trades and why, about the difference between expressing a view and moving a price when the price is itself the decision rule, about the governance of the metric that the contracts are written on, and about the difference between those who hold wealth and those who are affected. For each, say whether it is a genuine objection or one a proponent could plausibly absorb, and what the absorption would cost them.

**5. Verdict.** Finally, tell me whether the disagreement between this proposal and ordinary democratic decision-making survives translation as a substantive disagreement about institutions, or whether much of it relocates — into the term sheet, into the choice of metric, into the resolution agent — in a way that leaves the price signal doing rather less work than its advocates claim.

Throughout, be blunt, and be honest about the direction of travel. The moment you find yourself supplying a premise the proposal does not have — a well-specified underlying, a solvent issuer, a market deep enough to bear the risk — stop and mark it as an addition rather than passing it off as translation. Roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words.
