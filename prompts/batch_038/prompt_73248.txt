OK, background: I'm a third-year PhD student and my advisor talked me into giving a 25-minute talk this fall at a small workshop on the mathematics of bounded agents — she said it would be good for me and I agreed before I thought about who would be in the room. It's maybe twenty people. Five or six are theorists who have actually worked through the construction in the logical induction paper: they know why the day-by-day market works, what the trader-exploitation guarantee does and doesn't say, and roughly where the computational cost of the construction goes crazy. Three or four are ML people who read the abstract and are really there for the other talks. Two are philosophers — one formal epistemologist who thinks in terms of Dutch books, scoring rules and accuracy measures, and one who is much more interested in what it even means to call a belief state rational. The rest are grad students, some of whom will ask the most annoying question of the day. I've been told twice now that my talks come off as confident to the point of overclaiming, so I want to walk in knowing exactly which parts of my own argument can't take weight.

My thesis is that logical induction settles a coherence problem but not the problem people actually wanted settled, which is what to think about next. The sketch I'll present: (1) the central guarantee is asymptotic — each sentence's price converges, but the theorem hands you no usable rate and nothing you can compute in advance about how trustworthy today's prices are; (2) a bounded agent deciding whether to keep thinking for another hour needs beliefs about how much better its beliefs will be after that hour, i.e. something like a second-order price on its own future pricing function, and nothing in the framework supplies that; (3) the usual comeback — just treat the day-n prices as your degrees of belief, since the market is approximately consistent each day — doesn't bridge the gap, because approximate consistency bounds the total incoherence smeared across the whole market rather than the accuracy of the single sentence I care about, and "no efficient trader can make unbounded money" is a claim about a market run for unboundedly many days while a decision is a claim about one day and one sentence. So my slogan is that the framework tells you what a limit-perfect reasoner about logic looks like, and goes silent precisely where bounded reasoning gets hard. I know that's overconfident-sounding, which is exactly why I need this.

What I want from you is a prep sheet for the question period, not another defense of the thesis — give me something like eight objections, ordered by how much damage I'd take if I fumbled them, each phrased the way that particular slice of the room would phrase it. The theorist who read the proof does not talk like the formal epistemologist, and I want to be able to tell from the first sentence which of my slides to go back to. For each objection: a reply I can deliver standing up in under two minutes, a note on where that reply is softest, and a call on whether I should concede it outright, concede half of it, or hold the line. Mark separately the one or two where the honest move is "yes, that's an open problem, and here's why it's interesting" instead of trying to defend anything. Also include the strawman trap — the objection where agreeing with the questioner would cost me the whole talk even though the question sounds reasonable. And then tell me the question I'm praying nobody asks, the one that would expose the gap between what I claim and what I can actually back up, plus what the least-bad answer to it looks like.
