I teach a required second-year module in a school of journalism and media studies on the history of media criticism. Next week's session is seventy-five minutes on Kierkegaard's Two Ages — specifically the long closing section that circulates separately as The Present Age — for twenty-eight students with no philosophy beyond a first-year ethics course, several of whom already work on a student paper, and none of whom will have read anything but the four pages I have excerpted for them. I have a whiteboard and no slides. What I want from you is not an exposition of Kierkegaard but a plan for how I should expound him, framed around a difficulty you should take seriously: this group is committed, more or less explicitly, to the proposition that more voices is better, and reads any attack on collective opinion as an attack on democracy.

Please structure the plan in five labelled parts and keep the whole thing under 850 words.

First, at the top, one sentence stating the single thing I want them able to say on the way out. It must not be a slogan about social media.

Second, the dependency order: the components in the sequence they must be introduced, with a one-line justification each for why that item cannot come later. I want your judgement in particular on where the vocabulary has to be settled. The people, the crowd, the masses and the public are four different things in this text, and the abstraction that is the public is doing work none of the others can do; tell me which of them must be fixed first, and what breaks in the argument if I let the students slide between them.

Third, the carrying example. It must come from the students' own professional world rather than from philosophy, and it must meet two constraints: no crowd ever physically assembles in it, and no participant in it is individually identifiable, so that the case cannot be redescribed as a story about a mob. It must also not be an outrage, a scandal, a trending topic or a comment-section pile-on, because those are precisely what they will reach for and reaching for them is the error I am trying to prevent. Say what your example must have and what it must lack.

Fourth, the misconception map. I expect at least these: that the public simply means the audience or the readership; that Kierkegaard's remedy is regulatory, so that he turns out to be a conservative who wants the press constrained; that reflection is merely an excess of thinking over acting; that leveling is a complaint about material equality; and that the single individual he arrives at is a purely private inwardness with no bearing on public life. Some will also have picked up, elsewhere, the later and blunter claim that the crowd is untruth, and will assume it is the same doctrine as the 1846 analysis. And at least two will observe that Kierkegaard had just been savaged by a satirical paper and ask whether the whole thing is sour grapes. For each item, tell me at which point in the sequence I head it off and what I say there. The sour-grapes objection I do not want deflected; I want it used.

Fifth, the omission list: what I should refuse to explain at this level, and announce that I am refusing — pseudonymity and indirect communication, the stages, the plot of the novel under review, the Hegelian point about the principle of contradiction, the later attack on the established church. Tell me which of these can be dropped silently and which will do damage if dropped without a word.

Close with one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes whose answers would separate a student who has grasped that the public is an abstraction from one who thinks it means a great many people online.
