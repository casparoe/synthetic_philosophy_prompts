my cousin records a politics podcast and he's got this line he keeps reaching for: Arrow's impossibility theorem is the Gödel incompleteness theorem of voting. His pitch is that a formal system can't prove all its truths but nobody declares mathematics broken, and likewise a voting rule can't deliver all the fair-seeming outcomes we want but nobody should declare democracy broken. Then he slides from that to: so the fight over first-past-the-post vs ranked choice vs approval is basically sound and fury, the choice between rules is arbitrary at the level that matters, pick one and get on with governing.

Before he records, I want to know how much weight that analogy can actually bear. Can you lay out the correspondence table — what plays the formal system, the axiom set, the Gödel sentence, the diagonal construction, consistency, and the option of moving to a stronger system — and for each entry say whether it's a genuine match or just decorative vocabulary?

Then I want the disanalogies sorted into the ones that matter for his move and the ones that are noise. Things I'm already suspicious of: is there any self-reference in the standard proofs of Arrow at all, or is that just the vibe the word "incompleteness" gives you? Are Arrow's conditions (unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship) as non-negotiable as "contains enough arithmetic" is for Gödel, or is the interesting question precisely whether IIA is the right requirement? Does either theorem tell you what you get back by weakening a hypothesis, and if so, does that cut for or against my cousin's shrug? And in math, has "some truths aren't provable here" ever licensed "it's all arbitrary"?

Suppose that's right. Does a shared formal skeleton make the analogy load-bearing for his practical conclusion, or is that a different question entirely?
