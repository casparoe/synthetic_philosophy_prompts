I'm a third-year in political science and I TA a junior seminar on propaganda and public language that a visiting lecturer — I'll call her Halloran — built around Orwell's Politics and the English Language (the 1946 essay, the one where political writing is mostly "the defence of the indefensible") plus a pile of more recent work on euphemism and official speech. She has this marking habit thats driving me crazy. She reads the first draft of every student's op-ed and puts a single × in the margin next to certain sentences. No comments, no rubric, no explanation, ever. We asked her once and she just said the test is the same one in the essay and we'd recognize it once we'd run it enough times. She was out sick last week, I got handed the whole pile of second drafts, and apparently I'm expected to reproduce her ×'s so the class stays calibrated.

I went back through three semesters of drafts she already marked and pulled out every sentence I could find with a × next to it, plus the sentences sitting immediately around them that she let go. Here's the whole record:

×'d:
- "The ordinance resulted in the removal of roughly forty families from the riverbank."
- "There is a broad consensus that the initiative has delivered meaningful outcomes for key stakeholders."
- "The situation was resolved without further incident." (this is in a piece about a police shooting, and it's the only sentence she ×'d in that draft)
- "The unit pacified the village over the course of three days."
- "The protesters' thuggish conduct tells you what this movement is really about."

let go:
- "The city bulldozed about forty homes on the riverbank in April."
- "Every council member voted for the levy, including the two who campaigned against it in the spring."
- "The county's lead-abatement program cut children's blood-lead levels by 40 percent in two years."
- "I sat through three hearings and not one person from the district spoke in favor."

That third one is the case I can't fit. The essay's rules include never using a long word where a short one will do and never using a jargon or scientific term when an everyday equivalent exists, and "abatement" looks like exactly that — but she waved it through, while she ×'d a sentence about protesters that's got no Latinate padding at all. So whatever she's running isn't a diction rule, or it isn't only one.

The three sentences I actually have to rule on Thursday:

(a) "Six hundred gallons of raw sewage went into the creek sometime overnight."
(b) "The rioters looted the pharmacy on Delancey Street."
(c) "The judge came down hard on the utility, and the fines will pay for the new filtration plant."

The candidate rules I've been kicking around: mark it whenever the sentence hides who did what to whom; mark it whenever a word smuggles in a verdict the writer hasn't argued for; mark it whenever the reader can't form a mental picture of any actual event; mark it whenever a phrase arrives pre-assembled, i.e. ready-made strips of words; mark it only when harm is described in sanitized official vocabulary. Some of these look like they explain the whole record, some only part of it, and I think at least two of them disagree about (a) through (c).

So: which of those rules are actually consistent with all nine precedents, and are there better ones I haven't thought of? Then predict her mark on (a), (b), and (c) — for each one give me your best guess, the single precedent that's the closest analog, and what the surviving alternative rules would say instead. If the record genuinely underdetermines one of them, say so plainly and tell me what principle I'd be committing her to with each choice, because I'd rather know I'm making it up than pretend the precedents settled it. And finally, propose one further sentence I could slip into next week's batch that would separate the two rules you think are most likely — something where they have to come apart.

One more thing: I'd like you to be explicit about which parts of this are settled ground and which are live disputes among people who work on Orwell or on political language more generally. Like, is there any real consensus on whether his rules are about concealment versus about style, or on whether agentless constructions actually change what readers believe? Don't hand me a contested reading dressed up as the standard view — flag it as a reading I'd be taking on.
