Spent about four hours last night going around in circles with a friend of mine (she's in development economics, I'm doing a philosophy MA) about whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes, and she finally wrote the following out on the back of a menu to show that the standard way of indicting Bengal 1943 convicts literally every state on earth, and so convicts nobody:

(1) A famine is a political crime rather than a natural disaster just in case the deaths come from how food gets allocated instead of from there not being food to allocate.
(2) Sen's entitlement analysis is supposed to show that Bengal was exactly that case — no collapse in total availability, just rural wage laborers, boatmen and service households losing their ability to command rice through wartime inflation, the denial policies that stripped the coastal districts of boats and rice stocks, hoarding and speculation once price controls were abandoned, and an administration that never declared a famine or organized relief.
(3) So Bengal's deaths came from allocation, and by (1) that was a political crime: the administration killed those people rather than failing to save them.
(4) But apply (1) honestly. Every state allocates food, medicine and shelter through rules it maintains and could rewrite — what's taxed, what's subsidized, who's eligible for what, what happens to people who fall out of the labor market — and people die under those rules for want of things the rules make unavailable to them.
(5) So by (1) and (4), every such death in a functioning state is a political crime as well, and that state too is killing rather than letting die.
(6) If every state kills, then "kills" isn't picking anything out, and if the Bengal verdict rests on a distinction that also convicts Sweden, the verdict isn't doing the work we wanted from it.
(7) Therefore either Bengal wasn't a political crime, or it was one for reasons that have nothing to do with allocation versus availability — in which case the entitlement framework isn't a moral theory and shouldn't be used as one.

I want to keep (3) more than anything else on that list and I'd sooner give up (1), but I cant tell whether that's an argument or just squeamishness. Two things I couldn't answer her on. First, she says (2) is empirically shaky — that later work finds a real shortfall in Bengal once you count the loss of Burmese rice imports plus a cyclone, tidal flooding and crop disease the year before — and I have no way of assessing that, so tell me whether the argument's fate turns on (2) being right rather than telling me who said what. Second, she insists Ireland and the Great Leap Forward come out identically under (1) and I insisted they don't: Ireland had grain and livestock still leaving the ports while people starved, relief made conditional on surrendering your holding, and a Treasury that sincerely believed interference was the one thing you must not do; the Leap Forward had procurement quotas set from inflated yield reports, private plots and rural markets abolished, communal mess halls, officials who questioned the numbers getting purged, and grain still going out of the country. Same shape or not, and does it matter for locating the weak step?
