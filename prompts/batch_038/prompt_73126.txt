So I've talked myself into guest-running a fifty-minute section of our intro philosophy of mind course next week — the regular TA is at a conference — and the assigned slot is the fight over whether there's any such thing as original, underived aboutness, i.e. Searle's insistence that some states are intrinsically intentional versus Dennett's two-bitser move (the little AND/OR box in one of his essays) that collapses the distinction. I don't want you to explain the debate to me, I know it fine. What I want is a plan for how to teach it to *this* room in fifty minutes, because the audience is the problem: it's a cognitive science required course, so I'll have about twenty students who are mostly CS and neuroscience majors, comfortable with Shannon information, bits, state spaces, and code, and almost none of them have had any philosophy past whatever they picked up in a gen-ed ethics class. That means the standard way this gets taught — Chinese Room first, then the accusation of deriving intentionality from nothing — is going to land badly here. They'll hear "syntax isn't semantics" as a claim about computation and they'll think they already have the answer, and half of them will be annoyed on Searle's behalf in a way that's actually a *philosophically interesting* annoyance rather than confusion, so I want it treated as a resource, not something to swat down.

Concretely, here's what I need from you. First, the dependency structure: what has to be in place before what, with a sentence on why each step can't be moved earlier — I have a hunch the thing these students can be made to swallow early is that a physical state's carrying information about another state is a real, respectable, non-mysterious relation that doesn't need a mind anywhere near it, and that if I don't get that nailed down first the rest of the hour is mush. Second, the carrying example. Tell me what it should be and, just as important, what it must *not* have — the two-bitser itself might be the wrong choice precisely because it's a gadget about gadgets and these kids will start debugging the circuit diagram instead of looking at the interpretation. I'm also tempted by something wetter, a retinal ganglion cell or a frog's bug-detector, but I worry that smuggles in the assumption that the biology is doing semantic work, which is the very question. Make the case for one. Third, the misconception map, with the exact point in the sequence where you head each off, not at the end when it's too late. The ones I'm braced for: derived means second-rate or fake, so the dispute looks merely verbal; everything is intentional if you just take the stance, so there's no disagreement left to have; Dennett is a behaviorist or an eliminativist who thinks beliefs aren't real; and the one I dread most, which is that a CS major will say within five minutes that Shannon information is about correlation and nothing to do with aboutness at all, and honestly they have a point — I want a planned response that concedes something real rather than a deflection.

Also tell me what to leave out and have me say so out loud — I'm assuming the homunculus stuff, multiple realizability, and the whole consciousness-and-qualia question all get cut, but tell me where you'd draw the line and what one sentence to say when somebody asks why. And they will ask about large language models, guaranteed, probably in the first ten minutes; give me a parking move that doesn't feel like a dodge, plus a two-sentence version of what the two sides would actually have to say about it if we came back to it at the end. Finally, one closing question — not a comprehension check on the labels, nobody reciting original versus derived — but a small case they haven't seen, where I can tell from how they classify it and what they say about it which side they've ended up on and whether Searle's dividing line still looks like a line to them.
