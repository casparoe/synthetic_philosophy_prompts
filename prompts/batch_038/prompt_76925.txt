Last night I lost the better part of three hours to an argument with a friend — an engineer, and a careful one — and I have come away unsure what the analogy he kept reaching for was actually doing. I would like it audited properly rather than talked about.

The context is the usual one. I said that the continuing absence of any confirmed technosignature, after decades of looking, is itself evidence: it should push us towards the view that we are alone, or at least towards the view that the Galaxy contains no civilisation that has expanded or engineered at scale. He replied that this is like drawing a thimbleful of water from the ocean, finding no fish in it, and announcing that the sea is empty — the searches so far have inspected something like a thousand-odd stars out of a few hundred billion, across a narrow slice of the spectrum, over a handful of decades, so a null result is worth very nearly nothing. Pressed, he offered a second formulation: the Galaxy is a haystack, we have turned over one corner of it, and nobody would infer from that corner that there is no needle. I countered with an image of my own — that a civilisation at the relevant energy scale is more like a fire in a building with working alarms, where you do not need to inspect every room to know whether it is burning — and he said that simply assumes the fire is loud, which is precisely the point in dispute. That last exchange is where the argument stalled.

What I want is an audit of both of his analogies and of mine. Not a survey of the field, not a history of the debate, and nothing that depends on my chasing down sources.

1. For each analogy, set out the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the haystack, the needle, the sample taken, the sampling method, the size of the space, the detector and its threshold, the searcher and their effort budget. Then state in one sentence the conclusion each analogy is being asked to carry.

2. List the disanalogies, and separate the ones that matter for the intended inference from the ones that are merely cosmetic. My suspicion is that the load-bearing ones concern whether the target is assumed to exist in the first place, whether detection is all-or-nothing or a matter of power and threshold, whether the sampling is random or shaped by instrument sensitivity, and how the number of stars you can constrain scales with the transmitter power you are prepared to look for. Say whether that is right, and correct me where it is not.

3. Name what each analogy quietly imports from its source that the target lacks. His seems to import the existence of the needle, which is the thing at issue; mine seems to import the premise that a technological civilisation must be conspicuous across galactic distances. Are those the right charges, or is the smuggling happening somewhere else?

4. A large part of our stalemate, I now think, is that "the search" is not one kind of thing, and coverage is not one number. Please distinguish the classes of observation where the haystack objection genuinely bites — narrow-band surveys of individual stars, where the fraction of parameter space inspected really is vanishingly small, and where beam geometry, duty cycle and the assumption of a particular bandwidth mean the coverage fraction is not even well defined until you fix the hypothesis being tested — from the classes where coverage is close to complete and a null result therefore does real work: all-sky infrared surveys for waste heat above a given luminosity, imaging of the Solar System, the geological and ice-core record, and the sheer age of the Galaxy relative to any plausible expansion timescale. For each class, say which analogy is apt, and whether making the distinction dissolves the argument or only relocates it.

5. Tell me where his conclusion survives the disanalogies, where it does not, and how much of the quarrel between us was never about the analogy at all but about a quantitative premise — how loud the hypothesis says a civilisation must be. If that is where the weight sits, say what would settle it and what could not be settled even in principle.

6. If none of the three images is adequate, propose a better one: something that captures the real structure of the inference, in which a single null result constrains a family of hypotheses indexed by power, beam, and duty cycle rather than one hypothesis. Then state what your replacement analogy can support and what it cannot.

Please write the whole answer in American English. I would like the reasoning in prose and tables, with no reading list or bibliography attached, and I would rather you mark the steps where you are exercising judgement than present them as settled matters.
