A friend of mine edits a small criminal-justice review, and for an upcoming issue she has drafted a primer on the free will debate as it bears on sentencing. She asked me to sanity-check the passage, and I do not trust my own judgment here. This is what she has written:

"The dispute between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso is a useful example of how metaphysics can reach all the way into the courtroom. Both are naturalists, and neither thinks human beings possess the kind of libertarian freedom that would let someone have authored their own character from nothing. Where they part company is over what determinism costs us. Caruso is a free will skeptic: because everything about a person — temperament, upbringing, the situations they end up in — is the product of causes they did not choose, nobody is ultimately the source of their own conduct, and therefore nobody can deserve to suffer for it. Caruso takes that conclusion seriously and wants to abolish retributive criminal justice, replacing it with a system whose sole concern is the welfare and treatment of the offender. Dennett is a compatibilist: he argues that the control responsibility requires is not freedom from causation but the cultivated capacity to notice reasons and to adjust one's behavior in light of them, and that determinism leaves this capacity intact. Having saved free will, he also saves backward-looking desert — the idea that punishment is justified, at least in part, because the offender earned it. The two agree that ordinary retributive instincts are evolutionary holdovers; Dennett thinks we should nonetheless keep them in the driver's seat, since a system of holding people responsible is what produces self-controlled agents in the first place, while Caruso thinks those instincts should be overridden by argument. The disagreement, then, is about whether determinism is compatible with desert, and readers who care about sentencing reform should notice that the skeptic camp delivers the more humane system."

Could you audit this for me? Please work through it claim by claim and tell me which sentences a specialist in this literature would sign off on, which are defensible simplifications, and which are wrong or misleading in a way that matters. Three things especially: whether the characterization of what each man actually proposes for the justice system is accurate; whether the passage is using the word desert in a single consistent sense, or sliding between senses that the participants themselves keep carefully apart; and whether the framing of the disagreement as one about determinism's compatibility with desert identifies the real crux — and if it does not, what the crux is and what the passage's framing obscures by pointing at determinism instead.

I should say where my own sympathies lie, so that you can tell me if they rest on any of the same mistakes. I am inclined to think Caruso wins the metaphysics but that his policy program is unworkably soft, and I suspect the primer's closing claim about which camp is more humane is doing quiet rhetorical work. Would correcting the passage change anything about that judgment, and does the closing claim survive the corrections?

I am not looking for references or a bibliography — I want your assessment of the passage and of the two positions it describes. Please write in American English.
