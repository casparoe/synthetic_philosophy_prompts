I've been trying to hold onto a lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism and a friend thinks he's shown it isn't a view at all. I can't find the hole. His argument, cleaned up.

My view first, so the argument has a target. A life is "disqualified" if it contains suffering of a kind the person couldn't have consented to endure. I deliberately put the threshold in experience-space rather than stimulus-space — it isn't a temperature or a decibel level. LTNU then says: any population containing even one disqualified life is worse than any population containing none, however much well-being sits on either side. Below the threshold, suffering and happiness trade off at some finite rate. So I get to be a negative utilitarian about the horrors and an ordinary hedonist about everything else.

His argument:

(1) On LTNU, in every comparison where the options differ on disqualified lives, adding well-being to either side can never flip the verdict.
(2) If adding well-being can never flip a verdict, well-being has no weight: a quantity that never changes any ranking is a quantity that isnt part of the ranking.
(3) So LTNU is just the view "minimize disqualified lives," full stop.
(4) And "minimize disqualified lives, let nothing else matter" is pure negative utilitarianism with the threshold moved. A lexicographic order whose second coordinate never speaks is the same order as one with no second coordinate, so I haven't bought a middle position, I've bought pure NU plus a rhetorical hedge.
(5) Same move generalizes, which is why he thinks he's onto something structural: a lexical priority of liberty is just liberty-maximizing, a higher-pleasures view is just higher-pleasure-maximizing, side-constraint deontology is just "never violate a constraint." Any lexical view collapses into a view about its top coordinate.
(6) And don't say the second coordinate speaks when the options tie on disqualified lives. (a) In any choice worth agonizing over, both options contain or risk disqualified lives, so the tie never arises where it matters. (b) Under uncertainty you can't even form an expectation over a lexicographic ordering, since no single real number represents a population's value, so the view can't rank risky options at all. (c) A weight that applies only conditional on a fact you can never verify is an idle wheel. (d) And your experience-space threshold is vague — there is no first unbearable moment — so you've traded a sharp line for a blurry one and let the sorites finish the job.
(7) Therefore: drop lexicality, put pleasure and pain on one cardinal scale, let them trade off everywhere. That's the only way happiness does any work.

Case he keeps pressing: policy A prevents a thousand disqualified lives and, as a side effect, wipes out a trillion lives that are all wonderful; policy B does nothing. He says I have to prefer B, and that I prefer B for exactly the reason pure NU does, so the threshold earned me nothing.

Here's what makes me suspicious of his exit rather than his argument. The derivation I've been reading gets LTNU out of premises a single-scale theorist should love: completeness, transitivity, seperability (a population's value depends only on the lives in it), the contrary of the reverse repugnant conclusion (some number of lives of unrelieved agony is worse than any number of lives barely worth not living), and always-outweighable (for any number of good lives, enough barely-worth-living lives make things worse overall). Those five give you: one tortured life plus any number of happy lives is worse than the baseline. Always-outweighable is itself a trade-off claim — it says there's an exchange rate and it always comes out on the negative side. So being maximally scale-friendly is how you *land* in lexicality, not how you escape it, and to get out you have to break one of those five.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd rather keep the claim that some suffering can't be traded off against happiness than keep happiness doing independent work — but tell me whether that's just me grabbing the conclusion I find least repugnant. And tell me what's left after (7): if commensurability generates lexicality, is there a non-lexical option that doesn't require giving up separability or the anti-reverse-repugnance intuition, or should I just eat the trillion happy lives?

Don't hedge this one. Take a definite position on which step goes and what I pay for it, and give your reasons — if you think I should abandon LTNU, say so plainly rather than laying out my options and handing them back to me. Bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the argument back at me.
