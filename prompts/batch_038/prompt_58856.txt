I am convening a two-day interdisciplinary workshop — roughly equal numbers of philosophers of mind, computational neuroscientists, and people working on AI safety — and I have drawn the unenviable job of writing the framing document that opens the first session. The advertised question is whether the brain is literally a computer, but I have come to think that question cannot be addressed, let alone settled, until we fix what would make an answer to it substantive rather than a manner of speaking. The room is divided in a predictable way: the neuroscientists use the word computation as an unexamined synonym for whatever a circuit is doing; one colleague considers the matter closed because we can now run detailed simulations of cortical tissue; and two of the philosophers think the entire enterprise rests on a slide between a formal description we impose on a system and a causal process happening inside a skull. I do not want to referee that dispute in advance. I want to hand the participants a specification they can argue against, and I want to see for myself which parts of it are actually load-bearing.

So: write the desiderata — what an account of what it is for a physical system to compute must deliver before the claim that brains compute can carry any weight at all. State each requirement in a sentence or two, attach the case or consideration that motivates it (give me the case, not a slogan), and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really the standing commitments of a particular camp wearing the clothes of neutrality. For each, also say whether it constrains the notion of computation itself or only what that notion has to do for us. Here are candidates I have been turning over; tell me which are genuine, which are camp-prejudice, and which turn out to be empty: (i) some systems must fail to compute some things, since otherwise the thesis is true of a brick and says nothing; (ii) whether a system computes must not depend on anyone's choosing to interpret it that way, because the workshop wants a claim about brains and not about our descriptions of them; (iii) an implementation must be counterfactually robust — the right outputs must follow from the right inputs under intervention, not merely coincide with them across the system's actual history; (iv) the account must be gradable, since the duplication and partial-upload cases scheduled for the second session need implements-it-to-a-degree rather than a yes-or-no; (v) it must leave the thesis empirically assessable, in the sense that some conceivable experiment could bear on whether a given system runs a given computation; (vi) it must respect the difference between computing and merely being conveniently described as computing; (vii) it must settle where the computing system begins and ends, since a neuron, a skull, a brain together with its recording rig, and an animal together with its environment are all candidates; and (viii) it must not render the computational description explanatorily idle. I want to know which single requirement does the most work in knocking out otherwise attractive accounts, and — this is the part I most want — whether any subset of the eight is jointly unsatisfiable. My own suspicion is that (ii) and (iii) pull against one another, because the conditions that stop a rock from implementing everything look like conditions about how the system would behave under interventions we could only specify from outside it, and that (vii) may quietly do the same. If you agree, say precisely what has to give and what the resulting account loses.

After the list, and briefly, score the main contenders against it: accounts that take computation to be the rule-governed manipulation of internal states by a mechanism, accounts that identify it with a suitable mapping between physical and formal states under counterfactual constraints, accounts that tie it to semantic or intentional content in some way, and the deflationary view that everything computes everything so the question is badly posed. No citations, no bibliography, no reading list — I want your reasoning about the criteria, not references, and the weight should stay on the criteria rather than on the scorecard. Then close with an honest assessment of where the list itself is most likely to be wrong: which requirement you would sacrifice if you had to trade one away to keep the rest consistent, what argument or consideration would convince you that the joint-unsatisfiability claim fails, and whether any of the eight I supplied is doing no work whatsoever and should be struck.
