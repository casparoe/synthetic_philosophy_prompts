our new DEI lead has a go-to analogy she leans on constantly in these trainings, and I want to know how much weight it can actually carry before I sign off on the program she's pitching to the exec team.

The analogy: credibility is a currency. Some groups — women, junior staff, the one or two people of color in an otherwise white room — are being charged a bad exchange rate. Same number of words, fewer units of belief per word. So when a junior engineer reports a defect and gets a shrug, and a senior guy says the identical thing the next day and gets a task force, that isn't a personality thing, its a price: her speech is being converted at a discount.

The part I actually care about is what she uses it to argue. Because she frames it as a *rate* problem, the fix is a rate fix: run a calibration half-day, then start publishing the numbers — who gets interrupted, whose incident reports get acted on, whose name ends up on the memo — and her claim is that once the mispricing is visible, people will arbitrage it away. Managers have a standing incentive not to leave accurate information on the table, so the discount should drift back toward fair value once it's measured and legible. The budget ask is basically the dashboard plus the workshop.

So: audit it. Give me the correspondence table — what's the currency, who issues it, what counts as a transaction, is credibility a stock or a flow, what plays the role of the bank, who is the arbitrageur here, and what would "inflation" even mean. Then the disanalogies, and please separate the cosmetic ones from the ones that actually undercut the proposal, because I don't want to win on trivia. I'm already suspicious of three things: that a currency implies a conserved quantity somebody could hold more of, so that fixing the rate means someone else loses; that "exchange rate" implies a neutral market both parties are already inside, when I think in some of these cases the real phenomenon is that the person isn't in the market at all; and that arbitrage assumes the discount is a mistake about a fact — the hearer has misestimated how reliable this speaker is — whereas the identity-prejudice version of the deficit doesn't look much like a botched estimate, and might not even be a belief the hearer would endorse if you asked him directly.

And then the harder question: does the currency picture get any grip on hermeneutical injustice, the other half of Fricker's distinction? When the problem isn't that your testimony is discounted but that there's no shared word for what happened to you — no name for the pattern, so you can't even report it cleanly — what part of the analogy is supposed to be malfunctioning? The mint, the exchange, the fact that the ledger has no column for it? If the honest answer is "nothing, that's a different kind of problem and the metaphor is silent there," say so plainly, because then the dashboard fix is aimed at half the phenomenon and she should know which half.

Finally: does the arbitrage conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? Point out specifically which premise of her argument only looks plausible because of the currency framing. And if the shape is wrong, what's a better one — reputation network, clogged filter, dialect, something you'd propose — and what does *that* analogy license instead? I'd much rather hear that the right shape points at a different and more annoying intervention than get a compliment on our framing.
