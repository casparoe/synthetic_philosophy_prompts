I'm a staff engineer on a team that runs an LLM-as-judge pipeline: a scoring model rates candidate system outputs on a 0–10 scale, and several of our ranking and fine-tuning decisions optimize against that score as a proxy for "users are actually better served." I've half-read the Manheim and Garrabrant paper that splits Goodhart's law into regressional, extremal, causal, and adversarial variants, and I find myself reaching for those four labels for every divergence we see, which I suspect is a mistake, since plenty of divergences have nothing to do with Goodhart at all. I have no formal philosophy training, so rather than a lecture I'd like you to build me a diagnostic decision tree.

Requirements:

- Six to eight branch nodes, each phrased as a question my teammates could answer from logs, an A/B result, or a plot of proxy against target over time. No node may be answerable only by someone who already knows which label it leads to. Where a node needs data we don't currently collect, say so and name the cheapest measurement that would settle it.
- Ten to twelve leaves. All four of the Manheim–Garrabrant variants must appear, and specification gaming must be its own leaf, explicitly separated from the variant it's most often confused with. Beyond those, the leaves I actually need are the lookalikes: cases where proxy and target come apart for non-Goodhartian reasons — the target was never precise enough for there to be a fact about divergence; the target genuinely changed while the proxy stayed put; the population being measured changed; aggregation across slices is hiding what's happening; the judge is noisy but unbiased.
- Each leaf carries its name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it), the signature it leaves in the data, whether the remedy is better measurement or wanting something different, and one awkward consequence — what accepting that diagnosis commits us to.
- List the combinations your node structure makes logically available but that end up with no leaf, and for each say whether it's unoccupied because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
- Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate, and say what evidence would separate them.

Then run our real case through it. The judge's mean score climbed for about two months while a human-satisfaction survey climbed alongside it; the survey then flattened and began to fall while the judge score kept climbing. Two complicating facts: the judge score now also feeds back into the data used to train the systems being judged, and we changed our user mix partway through the window. If the tree genuinely cannot select a single leaf, say so instead of forcing a choice.

Finally, state how confident you are in the tree overall and in the classification of our case, and single out the one step — node or leaf — you are least sure of, with the reason.
