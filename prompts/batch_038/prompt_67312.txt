I sit on my high school's Student Handbook Review Board, a five-student panel that hears appeals from disciplinary citations. Our faculty adviser insists that we interpret the handbook the way a court interprets a statute, and he keeps asking whether we are textualists or purposivists without ever letting us settle it. Our record so far: (1) a student cited under "Cell phones must remain out of sight from first bell to last bell" whose phone vibrated audibly inside a zipped backpack during an exam; we dismissed, because the phone was not visible. (2) a student cited under "No food or drink in the computer lab" for carrying a sealed, unopened water bottle in his backpack; the handbook states that the rule exists to protect the machines, and we upheld the citation anyway. (3) a student cited under "Students may not use another person's login credentials" who logged into a classroom laptop with his teacher's account at the teacher's explicit direction in order to start a film for the class; we dismissed, with two members dissenting on the ground that the words admit no exception. (4) a student cited under "No recording devices anywhere on campus," a provision broadened last year from "No recording devices in locker rooms and restrooms"; we upheld, citing the amendment.

The case now before us: a student cited under "No food or drink in the auditorium during performances" for carrying a sealed water bottle into a recital. She never opened it, carried it out full, and an usher had already inspected her bag at the door. Which candidate principles of interpretation are consistent with all four precedents, and which are ruled out by them? Which single precedent is the closest analog, and what should we decide in order to remain consistent with ourselves? If our record genuinely underdetermines this case, what principle would each option commit us to in writing, and what further hypothetical case would finally force the competing readings apart? Please answer in American English.
