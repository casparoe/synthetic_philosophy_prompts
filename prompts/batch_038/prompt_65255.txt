So I'm auditing a phil of mind seminar this term and my roommate is a condensed matter PhD student, and we ended up in a 2am argument about whether panpsychism commits you to caring about things like superconducting magnets, and I'd rather see the argument as a story than as premises and objections. Could you write me one, maybe 1,500 words? Here's the setup: a national lab is being decommissioned for budget reasons, and part of the shutdown involves a persistent-current loop that's been superconducting continuously since 1979 — nobody has ever let it go normal, it's been sitting in its dewar through three rounds of funding cuts. One character is the engineer who has to write the quench procedure, and who has come around to thinking (not from any woo, just from having read the combination-problem stuff and from the fact that the loop's state has been macroscopically coherent for nearly fifty years) that there may be no principled way to rule out that there's something it's like to be that loop.

The other character is a philosopher the lab brings in as a consultant, and I want her to be right about something real rather than to be the buzzkill. Her line: if experience is fundamental then the micro-experiences aren't owned by anybody — indistinguishable particles can't be individuals, so there's no "someone" at the bottom of the pile to be harmed — and the only interesting question is whether integration actually makes a subject or just coordinates a heap of unowned valence. Give her a genuinely good version of that argument and give the engineer a real reply, not a "well, you can't prove it either" shrug. Also work in the fact that the current in that loop has no measurable decay and would outlast the building, the lease, and probably the university.

The bit I care most about: I want the ending to turn on actual physics rather than on someone changing their mind. My understanding is that the state of a closed superconducting ring is pinned by an integer — the fluxoid number — and that integer can't drift down smoothly, it has to slip, all at once; whereas you could also just warm the thing slowly through its transition and let it fade. Use that fork. The question the story should land on is whether it matters morally that an ending happens in one indivisible step instead of gradually, and the characters should not be permitted to say the words "panpsychism," "consciousness," or "sentience" anywhere in the piece — the reader has to work out what they're actually fighting about from what they do with the equipment. No lecturing: if a paragraph could be pasted straight into a journal article, cut it. And can you make it a little funny in places? There's a dedication plaque from 1979 on the cryostat and somebody's left a coffee mug on top of it, and my roommate would absolutely kill for that detail.
