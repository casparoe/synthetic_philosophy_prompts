I am not an academic and I have no training in philosophy; I worked for thirty years as a quantity surveyor. For the past five years I have been writing a play, and it has produced a problem I cannot think my way out of. I would rather have a rigorous answer that tells me I am wrong than a sympathetic one that leaves me where I started. Please write your reply in British English.

The play is about a woman who ran a small voluntary children's home in a Lancashire mill town from 1928 until 1951. She died in 1963, unmarried, with no children. Her diaries and about two hundred letters are in the county record office, and I have worked from them for four years. She has no descendants; the closest living relative I have found is the grandson of a niece, who has been kind and interested.

I have been operating under a rule of my own devising, which I have come to distrust. The rule is that I may invent what she did, but not who she was. I may place her in situations that never occurred, provided she behaves as her recorded dispositions would have made her behave; I may not give her a motive or a failing that the papers do not support. Three inventions are troubling me, and they do not feel alike.

First: a scene, late in the first act, in which she turns a boy away from the door on a cold night because she has no bed for him. The diaries never describe this. They do record years of overcrowding, and one entry in which she notes, without comment, that she has had to refuse admissions. So the scene is inferable, but the event did not happen. This is the one that keeps me awake, because I now think it is what audiences will remember and repeat, and the local history society has already written to ask whether it is in the diaries.

Second: the official who obstructs her throughout the play is a composite of three real men, and in the third act he accepts a payment. None of the three is recorded as doing anything of the kind. One of them is the great-grandfather of a serving councillor who has given me access to his family's papers.

Third: her companion of thirty years, who lived with her and co-signed everything. The letters are deeply affectionate and there are long unexplained gaps in them, but nothing is ever stated. In the play I make the relationship physical, once, offstage, in a scene neither of them discusses afterwards.

My rule permits the first invention, forbids the second, and is simply silent on the third. That ordering seems to me exactly backwards: the invented night at the door attaches to a real woman, while the invented bribe attaches to a man who is not really the man I based it on.

There is also a device in the play I should mention. It opens and closes with me on stage, reading aloud from the diaries, and I say plainly which scenes are made up. People have told me this settles everything. I suspect it settles nothing: the audience still leaves holding the invented night, and the candour is about me rather than about her.

Underneath all of this is the question I cannot answer. My brother, who teaches, says a dead woman cannot be embarrassed, cannot be hurt, and cannot find out, that nothing about her life is changed by what I put on a stage seventy years later, and that the whole matter is therefore really about the living — the councillor's family, the town, the audience. I accept the premises and cannot accept the conclusion, and I think my difficulty has to do with her obscurity. If I were writing about a famous person, my play would be one distortion among a thousand records, and anyone could check me against them. For her, my play may become the record. It may be the only version of her that survives at all. That seems to make my position worse rather than better, and I cannot say why.

So, concretely:

1. Is my rule the right rule? If it is not, what should replace it — something specific enough that I could apply it at the desk while drafting, rather than a general sentiment about artistic licence.
2. What should I actually do about each of the three inventions? Please treat them separately, since I am confident they do not share an answer.
3. Does admitting the fabrication from the stage discharge the obligation, and if not, what would come closer?
4. Is my brother right that nothing is owed to her? If something is owed, I want to understand what sort of thing it is: what is being damaged, who it is that is damaged, and whether the obligation survives her having no descendants to object and no reputation left to protect.
5. Does obscurity change the analysis — does having less of a record to distort mean I owe more, or less?

Please engage with these particulars rather than with the general ethics of dramatizing real lives, and if you think the way I have framed the whole problem is itself mistaken, tell me so and show me the better framing.
