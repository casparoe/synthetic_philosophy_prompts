Novelist here, fifth book out in the spring, and for the last couple of years I've been reading around in classical Indian epistemology at night — mostly the Nyāya people and their lists of pramāṇas. What hooked me is that they were willing to have four or five or six separate channels of knowing and then argue for centuries about which ones collapse into which. That's much finer-grained than anything I got in a survey course. I'm not a scholar and my Sanskrit is nonexistent, so please don't hand me a reading list or citations — I cant chase them and I don't want them. What I want is your judgement as the expert in that literature: does the thing I'm about to describe already have a name in it?

Here's the thing. I leave scenes out on purpose, and readers reliably supply them. Two from the current manuscript. (a) A woman's brother says, in dialogue, that their mother spent every day of that August in the house, too ill to leave her bed; eleven pages later the narrator notes that the mother's shoes are by the door with river mud caked into the welts. Nothing else is said about it. Every one of my early readers came away knowing that the mother had gone down to the river, would argue for it, and got irritated with me when I pointed out that the book never says so. (b) A chapter ends with a man putting a letter inside his coat; the next chapter he's on a train, coat empty, no letter. Readers will tell you flatly that he posted it at the station. Now, I don't want the name for "readers fill in gaps" — I have that phrase, it's useless to me. What seems distinctive, and maybe worth its own slot, is three things. First, no rule is doing the work. In the mud case the reader isn't applying any general connection — river mud doesn't always mean a secret trip, sick mothers don't always lie — they posit one particular unreported event purely because otherwise two things the book has handed them can't both stand, and that event is the cheapest way to make them stand together. Second, nobody is telling them anything. The author isn't asserting it, and the reader knows perfectly well the author isn't asserting it, so whatever warrant this has, it doesn't look like the warrant of taking a trustworthy person's word — the trustworthy person is pointedly not speaking. Third, and this is the part I have no handle on: it goes into memory as if it had been presented. Six months on, a reader will describe the mother's walk to the river in detail, with feeling, and be genuinely startled to be shown the page where it isn't there. It isn't stored as a guess they made; it's stored as something they were told, or something they saw.

So — think this through rather than pattern-matching to the nearest buzzword, and start by brainstorming candidates before you commit to one. Which items in that framework come closest? Is the "resolve an incompatibility by positing what was never observed" move the same thing one school wanted as its own channel and another tried to fold into inference, and if so what exactly breaks when you transplant it from the world into an artifact where a person planted the incompatibility for effect? Does my second point kill the testimony option outright, or is there some version of trustworthy-speech that covers a speaker who means you to supply what they witheld? And the memory business — confident recollection of a scene that was never given — is that a recognised failure anywhere in those debates, or would it have had no slot at all and belongs to a different discipline entirely? For each candidate you weigh, I want what it does and doesn't cover here and where the edges sit, not just a label dropped on the whole bundle. If nothing fits the bundle as a whole, coin me a term, define it so I can separate it from ordinary gap-filling on one side and from plain inference on the other, and tell me which of my two examples is the hard boundary case — does the posted letter belong with the river mud, or is it a different animal because there was never any contradiction to resolve?
