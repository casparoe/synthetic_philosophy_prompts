I am in the second year of a doctoral program that sits awkwardly between economic history and the philosophy of biology, and the chapter I am drafting keeps slipping into two incompatible vocabularies for what is supposed to be one question: whether history is locked in, or whether the same outcomes would have arrived anyway. On one side is the literature on technological and institutional path dependence, where the paradigm case is the persistence of the QWERTY keyboard layout and the theoretical machinery is increasing returns, network benefits, technical interrelatedness, irreversible investment, and coordination costs, together with the characterization of a path-dependent process as a non-ergodic one whose limiting outcome is shaped by temporally remote chance events rather than converging on a single distribution. On the other side is the debate in evolutionary biology about contingency and repeatability, framed by Gould's thought experiment about replaying the tape of life from the Burgess Shale fauna and by Conway Morris's reply that rampant convergence implies a strongly constrained, and therefore predictable, set of outcomes. Both literatures purport to tell me when an endpoint was inevitable and when it was an accident, and I suspect they are not using the same notion of inevitability.

Please compare the two frameworks carefully and structurally, rather than listing similarities and differences at random. At minimum I want the comparison to address the following. First, the object of the claim: in the economic case the interesting property seems to attach to the process, namely that small early events shift which of several accessible equilibria is eventually occupied, whereas in the biological case the claim usually attaches to the outcome, namely whether a particular form or lineage would arise again. Does increasing returns, which reliably produces some dominant standard without determining which one, count as path dependence or path independence once we distinguish token-level from type-level outcomes, and does convergence in biology answer the same question that Gould was asking? Second, the mechanism of stability: economic lock-in appears to leave alternatives reachable in principle but ruinously expensive to reach, because the barrier is made of switching costs, complementary assets, and failures of coordination, whereas developmental and architectural constraints are often described as making certain forms ungeneratable at all, so that no amount of selection pressure could produce them. Which of these two is actually doing the work in each side's flagship examples, and what would it look like empirically to distinguish a barrier of cost from a barrier of reachability? Third, the relationship between persistence and merit: the QWERTY case is contested precisely because its defenders argue that continued use reflects continuing advantage rather than frozen arbitrariness, and I take it that the near-optimality arguments about the standard genetic code play a structurally analogous role, since a code that is unusually robust to mutation and translational error would be expected to persist whether or not its origin was accidental. Does that parallel genuinely transfer, or does it break down because the relevant alternatives in the two cases differ in kind?

To give the comparison something to bite on, please work through one case that seems to belong to both literatures at once: the near-universality of the genetic code. It looks arbitrary in its assignment, it is shared across essentially all organisms, and any change to it would propagate through the whole genome at once, which makes it the biological analogue of a standard whose installed base cannot be migrated. I would like you to say whether its stability is better explained on the economic model, as a coordination equilibrium sustained by catastrophic switching costs, or on the developmental model, as a constraint that no selectable sequence of intermediates could have escaped, and what evidence would favor one over the other. Finally, a colleague has proposed a deflationary reconciliation that I cannot decide whether to accept: she says the two literatures are not rivals because they answer different questions, since economists ask which of the reachable equilibria obtains and biologists ask whether the equilibrium was reachable at all, so that talk of history being locked in is simply ambiguous and each camp is right in its own sense. Tell me whether that reconciliation is correct or whether it conceals a substantive disagreement, and describe what a case would have to look like for the two explanations to yield conflicting predictions about it. I should add that the chapter is due to my committee in five weeks and my co-author, who is an economist, keeps insisting that contingency talk in biology is untestable hand-waving, so I would value a clear judgment about which of the two frameworks offers the better tools for counterfactual reasoning and why. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; argue from the concepts themselves, and where I have summarized someone's position above, take my summary as the target you are reasoning about.
