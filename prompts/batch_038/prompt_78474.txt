I have read rationalist blogs and forums for the better part of two decades, which leaves one with a standing tic: a suspicion of counterfactuals, a habit of asking anyone who asserts what would have happened where on earth they got that from, and a presumption that counterfactuals are features of a model rather than of the territory. Watching Capra's *It's a Wonderful Life* again over Christmas, I found the tic applying itself to a film I had never thought of as philosophical, and it has not let go. I should like a piece of criticism — not a plot summary, not a warm thematic appreciation — that reads the film against the philosophy of conditionals in earnest: the indicative/counterfactual contrast drawn with the Oswald pair ("If Oswald didn't kill Kennedy, someone else did" set against "If Oswald hadn't killed Kennedy, someone else would have"), Lewis's similarity semantics and his ordering of what makes one world closer than another, and the suppositional and plan-laden accounts of indicatives offered by Edgington and Gibbard.

The film is, after all, one long piece of counterfactual reasoning. A man wishes he had never been born; an angel supplies a world in which the wish is granted; and from being shown that world the man concludes that his actual life has been worth something. I want that inference put under pressure. Specifically:

1. Is Pottersville the closest world in which George Bailey never existed, on Lewis's own criteria? Please resist the cheap objection that the film "changes too much": Lewis's weighting puts the avoidance of widespread law-violation and the match of particular fact over a wide region ahead of the match of small local details, so the ramifying differences — his brother's grave, his uncle in an institution, Mary frightened of a stranger in a library, Potter's town of bars and pawnshops, the derelict Granville house — may be precisely what the semantics predicts. If so, say so plainly. Then tell me where the film does get closeness wrong, if it does, and whether it is trading on a backtracking reading rather than the standard non-backtracking one.

2. The epistemology is where my suspicion bites. On Lewis's account a counterfactual is made true by similarity facts, and similarity facts are not the sort of thing anyone can be shown. What, then, is Clarence doing? Is the film committed to a robust realism on which counterfactuals are inspectable parts of the world and angels simply enjoy a better vantage point; is Clarence testifying, with the film quietly treating testimony about counterfactuals as legitimate; or is nothing being established at all, so that George's conversion is not an update of credence but a change in what he cares about? Take a position and argue for it. And deal with the awkwardness that Edgington's own examples treat counterfactuals as confirmable by ordinary empirical evidence — the man kept out of a building that then collapses is told that if he had gone in he would have been hurt, and the actual facts seem to bear it out. If actual-world evidence can support a counterfactual there, why can a displayed world not support one here?

3. What work is the subjunctive doing that an indicative could not? George's speech to the depositors during the run on the building and loan is a string of indicatives about what will happen to the town if Potter takes it over, and those are Adams-friendly: assertable in proportion to conditional credence. Note the uncomfortable fact that in the actual world Potter has just pocketed eight thousand dollars and is never caught, so the film's moral accounting is settled by a display about a world that does not obtain, while the world that does obtain contains an unrepaired theft by the same man. Is the counterfactual load-bearing, or is it stage machinery for a conclusion the indicatives would have delivered anyway?

4. Clarence earns his wings only if George does not jump, which the film establishes in its opening scene. The counterfactual is therefore presented to George by an agent with a payoff in his accepting it. Does that alter the evidential situation, and does George's joyful acceptance at the end count as rational persuasion or as capitulation to a well-designed demonstration?

5. The bell that rings at the end, and the film's business about each such ringing marking an angel getting his wings, is a conditional of exactly the ambiguous kind the literature frets over — generic, material, or a claim about other worlds — and the film appears to verify it on the spot. Is the film in on the joke? And what is the status of Clarence's inscription in the flyleaf of *Tom Sawyer*, "no man is a failure who has friends": a generalisation meant to license counterfactuals, or something that is not in the business of being true at all?

Finally, be honest about where the lens breaks. My own hunch is that the closing ten minutes contain no inference whatsoever: George runs through the streets greeting actual people, money arrives in boxes and jars, his brother raises a toast, and none of it has the form of a premise. If you think the conditional apparatus is simply the wrong instrument for the ending, say so and say what the right instrument is.

Constraints, please. No synopsis — I know the film. No bibliography, reading list, or suggestions for further reading, and no quotation from the film or the philosophers, since I shall not be checking you and would rather you paraphrase accurately than quote plausibly. Name and unpack just enough technical apparatus for a reader who is fluent in Bayesian talk and decision theory but has never met a possible-worlds semantics; do not pad it. Aim for something between 1,400 and 1,800 words, and close with a short paragraph saying what the film contributes to the debate over conditionals that argument on its own could not — or, if your conclusion is that it contributes nothing, say that instead and say what it is good for all the same.
