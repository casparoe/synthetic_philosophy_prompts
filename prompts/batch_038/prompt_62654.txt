Here's the question:

"Explain the paradox of deontology. Why does it arise for deontological theories but not for consequentialist ones? Then assess one proposed response to it. (600 words max.)"

And here's the answer:

The paradox of deontology is the tension between two things a deontologist wants to say. First, that certain acts — killing an innocent person, breaking a promise, torturing someone — are prohibited even when performing them would prevent more acts of the very same kind. Second, that they're prohibited because those acts are bad, and bad in a way that gives us reason to want there to be fewer of them. Put the two together and you get a problem. If killings are bad, then a world with one killing in it is better than a world with five, and the deontologist agrees with that. But then it looks irrational for her to refuse to bring about the better world when she can, especially since the cost of refusing is just more of the thing she says is bad. So deontology ends up telling us to minimize violations by never minimizing violations, which is incoherent.

Consequentialists don't have this problem. Their theory already says to maximize the good, so in the one-versus-five case they kill the one and they're consistent about it. The paradox only bites a theory that ranks outcomes but then refuses to let the ranking drive action directly.

The standard escape is the agent-relative / agent-neutral distinction, and I think it basically works. The consequentialist's reason has the form "there be fewer killings," which is a reason for anyone to act on. The deontologist's reason has the form "that I not kill," which is indexed to me. So there isn't one single quantity that the two of us are pushing in opposite directions, and the charge of incoherence loses its grip.

Sen's goal-rights system is the best worked-out version of this escape, because it builds the agent-relative restrictions directly into the ranking of outcomes. The theory can still rank states of affairs while honoring the restriction, so the deontologist is no longer committed to bringing about a worse overall outcome, and the paradox dissolves. Kagan's Limits of Morality then gives the most careful defense of this moderate, threshold-based deontology: his conclusion is that a threshold is what ordinary morality needs in order to avoid the paradox, with the constraint holding below it and consequences taking over above it.

But this only pushes the problem back a step. If the deontologist agrees that fewer killings is better, then she has agreed that violations are the kind of thing to be reduced, and she is committed to permitting the reduction. The agent-relative move doesn't change that, so the paradox survives it and threshold deontology is the only option left standing.
