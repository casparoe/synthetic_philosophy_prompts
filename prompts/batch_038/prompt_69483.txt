I'm in a reading group on anthropic reasoning, and the member who leads it defends the doomsday argument in its urn form: picture an urn holding one ticket for every human being who will ever live, numbered in order of birth; you are handed one ticket without looking, and it turns out to bear a number on the order of a hundred billion; had the urn held a trillion or a quadrillion tickets, the chance of your drawing one so early would have been minuscule, so you should conclude the urn is probably not much larger than roughly twice what your ticket indicates. He then uses the picture to support two further claims. First, that all the ordinary evidence about historical population growth, carrying capacity, and the feasibility of space colonization is simply outmatched by the ticket number, which ought to dominate the inference. Second — the part I find most interesting — that the familiar objection that there is no actual sampling process here, no lottery, no mechanism that selected me from among all humans, is beside the point, because the urn was never meant to describe a process. On his reading it is only a device for stating that, before I learned my birth rank, I had no more reason to expect to be any one of the humans who will ever live than any other; strip away the machinery and what remains is a principle of indifference over birth ranks, which is all the argument ever needed.

I would like a careful audit of the analogy rather than another summary of the argument. Please set out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — matching the urn, the tickets, the numbering, the draw, the drawer's ignorance, and the size of the urn to whatever plays each of those roles in the doomsday case, and flagging any element of the analogy that has no honest counterpart at all. Then work through the disanalogies and separate the ones that are merely metaphysical from the ones that bear on the inference, since I expect some of my suspicions are red herrings. The list I have: the tickets all exist before the draw, whereas most of the humans in question do not yet exist; the drawer stands outside the urn and is not one of the tickets, whereas I am one of the members of the reference class; the number on the ticket is a property the urn has independently of who reaches in, whereas my birth rank looks more like a fact about me than a datum handed to me; the urn's contents are unambiguous, whereas the boundaries of the reference class — which ancestors and descendants count, whether modified or non-biological successors count, whether we count births or observer-moments — can be shifted to move the conclusion by orders of magnitude; and unlike the urn, the total number of future people is partly a function of what people like me decide to do, so the credence the argument recommends bears on the very quantity it is a credence about.

Finally, tell me whether the deflationary reading rescues the analogy or whether the inference depends on features that reading throws away. If all that remains is a principle of indifference over birth ranks, is there still anything doing the work the draw does in the urn case — something that licenses treating my learning my own rank as evidence about the total — or has the analogy been supplying that license unnoticed the whole time? I would also like to know whether his first claim can be carried by the analogy at all, given that the urn scenario contains no independent evidence about the number of tickets to compete with the draw, while the doomsday scenario is saturated with such evidence. And if the urn is the wrong picture, say what should replace it: some self-locating setup that represents indexical belief without pretending to a sampling process, or nothing at all, in which case I want the reason why this argument cannot be honestly presented by analogy.
