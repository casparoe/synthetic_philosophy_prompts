A friend and I argued until two in the morning this week about Halden, the maximum-security prison in Norway — no watchtowers or razor wire, cells with private bathrooms and unbarred windows, unarmed officers who work and eat alongside the men and women held there, and recidivism figures that get quoted at every American who calls the place a hotel. My friend's summary of the difference was this: Norway has stopped punishing people and started treating them. On his account a prison is a hospital — the offender is a patient, the crime a symptom of a damaged self, the sentence a course of treatment, the officer a clinician, release a discharge. And once you accept that framing, he said, the objection that Halden is soft on crime simply evaporates, since nobody thinks a hospital is being soft on its patients.

I have two doubts and I cannot tell which is doing more work.

The first is about the analogy itself: I suspect it is carrying the entire conclusion, and that what my friend admires about Halden he would still admire under a description that involved no medicine at all. The second is that I do not think the analogy describes the case offered in its support. Norwegian penal doctrine holds that the deprivation of liberty is itself the punishment and that conditions inside should resemble life outside so far as security allows; the term is fixed by a court in proportion to the offence rather than by anybody's prognosis; and as late as 1968, when Norwegian prisoner-reform advocates organized, the system still relied heavily on a treatment model in which the length of confinement tracked perceived therapeutic progress. If I have this right, Halden may be among the least medical of prisons: a bounded sentence served in normalized conditions, with treatment offered rather than imposed.

So please audit the analogy for me. Give me the correspondence table — patient, disease, diagnosis, treatment, clinician, consent, discharge, criterion of success — saying what actually plays each role in a prison and where the pairing is strained even before it is load-bearing. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use my friend put it to from the ones that are merely local colour. I expect at least these to be in play: treatment answers to a condition and need presuppose no wrongdoing, while punishment answers to a past act and presupposes an agent; treatment is bounded by prognosis and punishment by proportionality; treatment is for the patient's own good, while punishment is at least partly owed to victims and to the polity that names the act a wrong; a physician has a client and a warden does not. And if release follows cure, tell me what follows for the incurable.

Then say what the analogy quietly imports that my friend would not concede if asked directly — something about offending as symptom rather than choice, about the state acting for the offender's good rather than in the name of those he wronged, about who decides when the person goes home and to whom that person answers. Judge whether his conclusion survives once those are on the table, and whether either retreat I could see coming does better: prison as a school, or prison as quarantine — noting that quarantine is itself a public-health notion, so tell me whether that abandons the medical frame or completes it. Finally, if there is a less distorting comparison for what Halden actually does, give it to me, and say what it licenses and what it forbids.
