I sit on the governance board of a land trust — fourteen of us, perpetual endowment, conservation mission written into the charter in 1998. We've been stuck for two years on whether to entrench that mission so future boards can't repurpose the land, and I've started to think the fight isn't actually about the clause.

What's on the table: a mission lock, repealable only by four-fifths of the board plus a court finding that the original purpose has become impossible. The counter-proposal: a fifteen-year sunset with reauthorization by simple majority.

Camp A says lock it. Without entrenchment you don't have a trust, you have a slush fund with nice manners; the entire point of donors giving land in perpetuity is that later people can't change their minds. Camp B says every entrenchment is just a fancy voting threshold, and that boards under pressure don't repeal, they reinterpret — "impossible" gets read as "expensive" within a decade — so the lock produces hypocrisy instead of constraint, and is worse than a sunset because a sunset at least forces the question out into the open.

The frustrating part is that we reach for the same evidence and read it in opposite directions. Someone brings up a sister trust that amended in 2011. A says the amendment proves the clause worked: used once, for exactly the emergency it named. B says the amendment was drafted the year before that emergency and the emergency was manufactured to justifiy it. Nobody moves.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides aren't disagreeing about how much to bind. They have different pictures of what binding even is across a change in the values of the people being bound. A thinks the clause works on the future board's option set. B thinks it can only work on their beliefs about what they're allowed to want, and beliefs get revised around the clause rather than by it. And there's a circularity neither side ever names: the sunset's reauthorization vote will be taken by people whose preferences were partly formed by the rule they're reauthorizing, so B's "let the future decide" isn't a neutral alternative, it's the same mechanism with extra steps.

Is that right? Tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, a fight over what counts as a case of a rule genuinely binding, bedrock intuitions about whether a body can stay the same agent across a change in what it values, or some hidden empirical question about how boards really behave that we're gesturing at badly. And tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a kind of evidence, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected. I don't want a survey of precommitment theory and I don't want you to pick a side — I want to know whether this is resolvable in principle or whether we should just call the vote and stop pretending we're arging about the same thing.
