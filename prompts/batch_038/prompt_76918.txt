**Draft: "The Ledger"**

Start with the embarrassment. You decide to raise your hand and your hand goes up. Meanwhile a physiologist will tell you the rising was fully accounted for by a chain of neural events, each link having a physical cause. Jaegwon Kim spent a couple of decades pressing the consequences: if the physical world is causally closed, so that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, and if effects aren't systematically overdetermined by two independent sufficient causes, then the mental either has to be identical to the physical or it has to be pushed out of the causal story altogether. The nonreductive physicalist — someone who wants mental properties to be genuinely distinct from neural ones but not spooky — is, on Kim's telling, trying to have it both ways. That's the gist of his attack on the very idea of nonreductive physicalism back in the late eighties, and the argument has been the center of gravity in the debate ever since.

My claim is that the reason the argument feels so hard to resist is not metaphysical. It's bookkeeping. When you hear "the neurons already did it," you're hearing something structurally identical to "that invoice has already been paid." Causal work is being treated as a fixed quantity that gets allocated among candidate causes, governed by a no-double-entry rule: one effect, one debit, and any second claimant is a fraud or a redundancy. That rule is a norm about how to keep accounts, and it has been smuggled into the argument as a claim about the furniture of the world. You can hear the smuggling in the vocabulary of the debate itself, which is full of talk about work being "left over," about the mental having "nothing to do," about exclusion, surpluses and remainders. Nobody talks that way about ordinary causation, where we happily cite the ball's being a baseball and its having a certain mass and momentum in the same breath about the same broken window.

Notice that the standard replies accept the ledger and argue about the entries. The identity theorist says the mental and physical entries are the same entry — a consolidation of the books, not a rejection of double-entry. The proportionality reply is subtler: think of Yablo's pigeon, trained to peck at anything red and then presented with a scarlet triangle; the pecking is better accounted for by the redness than by the scarlet-ness, because the redness is what would have held across the relevant alternatives. That's a real insight about grain, but it's still an argument about which line item to record, not about whether there's a single fixed sum. And the interventionist replies — the ones that say a mental property causes something just in case an intervention on it changes the outcome — accept that only one variable gets the credit and then fight over which one, while relying on interventions that hold the physical fixed and wiggle the mental, which is a thing nobody can actually do to anybody.

So here's what I want to propose instead. Restrict the counterfactuals to what I'll call achievable interventions: interventions on a system that come with a physical route by which some real physical system could undergo them. You can't hold neurons fixed and vary a belief by magic, but you can vary a belief the way beliefs get varied — by testimony, by perception, by argument, by a drug — and each of those is a physically available path. Now ask the invariance question only across that restricted family. My claim is that the mental variable survives and the neural variable doesn't, for a mundane reason: the paths by which beliefs actually get changed land on very different neural configurations in different people and in the same person at different times, which is just the multiple realizability point worn as a method rather than as a metaphysics. Across achievable interventions, the belief is the stable predictor of the behavior; the neural realizer is not. Mental causation, on this picture, isn't leftover work the neurons couldn't use. It's difference-making along the routes the world actually offers.

The worry I can't shake: haven't I just built the answer into the restriction? To vary the mental you must vary the physical, so a Kim loyalist will say the physical is doing the work and the mental is riding along, and that my "achievable" filter is doing nothing but hiding that fact. Worse, if my criterion is invariance across a chosen family of paths, I've replaced a question about the world with a modeling decision — which is exactly the sin I accused the ledger of committing. Maybe the honest ending is not that the exclusion argument fails, but that it dissolves: it turns out to be a dispute about how to keep the books, and once you see that, the question "is the mind really a cause?" loses the determinate answer both sides assumed it had. A deflation, not a refutation.
