I am a junior at a public high school, and our philosophy club is putting on an evening of scripted debates in which pairs of students read arguments aloud to an audience of classmates, parents, and teachers. I have taken on writing one of the scripts, and I would like it to be the most substantive item on the program rather than an exchange of slogans. Please write the script for me, in American English throughout.

My topic is the objection sometimes called cluelessness: the claim that consequentialism cannot guide action because the long-run consequences of ordinary acts run far beyond our capacity to predict, so we are never in a position to say whether an act made the world better or worse. I want the script to be a dialogue between two characters who are both intelligent and both honest — one who thinks this objection is fatal, one who thinks it can be answered — arguing about a particular case rather than about definitions. Neither should be set up to lose.

Here is the situation I have in mind. The two characters are volunteering on a Saturday morning at a school event where students pack and ship insecticide-treated bed nets to a region where malaria is a leading cause of child death. One of them is satisfied that the work is obviously worth doing: by the organizers' estimate, the shipment will keep roughly forty children in a single district alive over the next few years who would otherwise have died. The other raises the worry that has been eating at them: those forty children will grow up, marry, and have children; a century and a half from now, the people living in that region — and, through migration and further ramification, arguably the people living anywhere — will not be the same people who would otherwise have existed. If the effects run that deep, the character asks, what reason do we have to think packing nets was better than going home?

I want the defender of consequentialism to answer by making their case precise rather than by waving at uncertainty. Have them introduce a deliberately simple model: treat each downstream consequence as a coin flip whose payoffs are symmetric, so that for every chain of good effects there is a roughly equally likely chain of bad ones, and the far future therefore cancels out in expectation. Let them coin a name for this right there in the conversation — something like the Washout Principle — and treat it as a proposal being invented on the spot, not as a doctrine anyone has heard of. Then let the skeptic attack it at the joint: the coin-flip picture assumes the branches are comparable and evenly weighted, but consequences that change who exists do not generate offsetting branches, they generate futures that may not be rankable against each other at all, and there is no obvious reason to suppose perturbations of that kind are symmetric when small changes compound and the direction of the compounding is exactly what we cannot see.

Please build in the following moves, in whatever order serves the argument best:

- A clean separation of two complaints that the skeptic initially runs together: that we do not know the probabilities of far-future outcomes, and that even if we did, the outcomes might not be comparable. The defender should force the choice, and the skeptic should say which of the two they actually need.
- The defender's point that cluelessness cannot counsel inaction, because staying home also has unforeseeable downstream effects. The skeptic must answer this rather than slide past it.
- A genuine exchange over whether the objection is specific to consequentialism or sinks anything that purports to tell you what to do, including whatever asymmetry each side can find in that comparison.
- The distinction between consequentialism as a test of whether an act was right and consequentialism as a method for deciding what to do. The defender will likely reach for it; I want the skeptic to press hard on whether it rescues anything that mattered to the defenders in the first place, because half my audience will think that escape is too easy.
- One real concession from each side, and a closing two or three exchanges in which they state what the disagreement has narrowed down to, without either one winning outright.

On craft: aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words; two named speakers; stage directions only where they change the sense of a line. No philosopher names, no book titles, no references to the literature — the arguments should stand entirely on their own reasoning. Every speech must engage the speech immediately before it; I do not want alternating monologues that happen to share a topic. The skeptic should not be a fatalist who has given up on doing good, and the defender should not be a booster; both want the nets shipped, and they disagree about whether that fact proves anything. Keep the diction plain enough to be followed aloud but not watered down, and when either character uses a technical term, have the other gloss it in the next line.
