For the past two years I have co-convened a working group — half of us from a staff college ethics centre, half from a university philosophy department — charged with producing a teaching module for mid-career officers on a single question: whether the symmetrical permissions of the law of armed conflict track the underlying moral permissions to kill in war. The module has never got past the third session, because the group reproduces the same impasse every time. One camp holds that symmetry is not merely a legal convenience but reflects something true about the moral position of combatants as such: that a soldier is not the author of his state's cause, that his standing is closer to a duellist's or to that of a coerced participant than to that of an aggressor, and that jus ad bellum and jus in bello must be kept separate if the latter is to do any work at all. The other camp holds that permissions to kill must derive from the moral status of the threat one faces and one's own responsibility for it, so that combatants fighting for an unjust cause are, in the typical case, not permitted to kill those defending against them, and that the symmetry of the law is a regrettable institutional compromise justified by epistemic limits, reciprocity and abuse risk rather than by deep morality. What makes the stalemate peculiar is that the second camp has largely conceded the practical point — several of its most committed members now argue the law should stay symmetrical anyway — and has been joined by intermediate positions I find hard to place: the suggestion that communal authorization can transfer the reasons of a political community to those who fight on its behalf, generating permissions neither side would otherwise have; the claim that unjust combatants can acquire duties to bear defensive harm even where liability does not apply; and the attempt to close the law/morality gap altogether by treating the law's evidence-relative standards as themselves morally load-bearing rather than as pragmatic second-best. So the parties increasingly agree on what the law should say, increasingly agree on the empirical facts about abuse and compliance, and still have not moved an inch on the theoretical question.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept, is that this is not a disagreement about moral facts but about the deliverable: each side is answering a question addressed to a different recipient, and neither has said which. One is asking what an individual fighter is permitted to do in the moment; another is asking what rules an institution should publicly codify; another is asking what a prosecutor could justifiably charge; another is asking what makes a political community's use of force legitimate in the eyes of those subject to it. If that is right, the impasse is a symptom of an unstated parameter rather than of intractable moral disagreement. But I can name at least four rival diagnoses and cannot decide between them: that the dispute is verbal, turning on whether 'justified' absorbs or excludes excusing conditions; that the sides differ about the evidential standard at which permissions are assessed, and would converge if they fixed it; that they disagree about which cases count as data at all — constructed vignettes about conscripts versus the record of actual wars — so they are not arguing from a shared evidence base; or that a hidden empirical question about the compliance and abuse consequences of asymmetric teaching is doing all the real work and nobody has admitted it. One thing I can offer is a crude bit of our own data. We ran the same vignette, with manipulations, past three audiences. Among serving officers, permissibility judgments tracked whether the fighter had himself been attacked first and whether he was a volunteer or a conscript, and were almost wholly insensitive to our manipulation of the justice of his side's cause. Among civilian philosophers, judgments tracked the justice of the cause and were insensitive to volunteer status. Military lawyers split on a third axis entirely, answering in terms of what could be proved afterwards. Both first two audiences were equally sensitive to whether the fighter's conduct was discriminate and proportionate. When shown the other group's pattern, each side said the other's inputs were simply not moral inputs.

Please take the role of a philosopher of war ethics who is fluent both in the analytic debate and in how the law of armed conflict is actually taught, briefed and applied, and think carefully rather than reaching for the standard summary. Begin with a short piece of open brainstorming in which you lay out the space of candidate diagnoses of this disagreement — including any I have not listed — before committing to one. Then give me the diagnosis you find most defensible, argued for rather than asserted, and grounded in what each side actually says and what it is trying to produce: show me the specific moves in the symmetry camp's reasoning and in the responsibility-based camp's reasoning that your diagnosis explains, and the moves it cannot explain. Say explicitly whether you take the disagreement to be substantive, verbal, a difference in standards of evidence or in what counts as a case, two questions sharing one label, or a sign that the question is ill-posed; and address my hypothesis directly, including whether the audience pattern I described discriminates between the rival diagnoses or is equally consistent with all of them. Finally, tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a reframing of the deliverable, a formal result, an empirical test, a change of question — and, if your honest view is that no progress should be expected, argue for that and say what the working group should do with its remaining year instead. Identify, if you can, the single case on which the two camps issue verdicts that no reframing could reconcile, since that is what I want the module built around. Do not give me a survey of the literature, a reading list, or any citations; attribute claims to positions and arguments, not to publications.
