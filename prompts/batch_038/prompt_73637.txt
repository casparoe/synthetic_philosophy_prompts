Ok so — I'm about 80% through a novel and there's a chapter I've been dodging for months, and honestly I think you might be better at it than me.

The book is a comic novel with a completely straight face. Chapter 31 contains nothing but a document: a report, reproduced in full, from a commission set up after the peace conference to work out, in percentages, who was liable for the war that started in August 1914. The commission is invented — please don't use the real one and don't put any real people in it, no historians, no ministers, no signatories anyone could look up. The register I want is an accident investigation: aviation safety board, or an inquiry into a factory fatality, but 1921 and in the idiom of a minute book. Deadpan throughout. Nothing winks.

What I need, roughly 900 to 1100 words, laid out in numbered sections with a couple of footnotes and one small table:

- terms of reference, where the commission explains that it is making a finding about loss and damage and not a finding about guilt, and then spends the rest of the report failing to hold that line
- a timeline of the event in which every individual entry is defensible and the sequence adds up to nothing anybody meant
- immediate cause, contributing factors, root cause analysis — the last one being a chain of whys that keeps getting longer and never terminates, and where the commission eventually has to concede that a railway timetable has ended up in the same category as a decision
- findings on apportionment: a table of percentage liability by state that does not sum to 100, with a footnote explaining the excess
- a dissenting annex from one member, in a noticeably different register
- recommendations to prevent recurrence, which should be the bleakest thing in the piece without ever sounding bleak

The jokes have to come out of the actual problem rather than from bureaucracy-is-funny. So the comedy should be generated by: whether a state is even the kind of thing that can intend anything (the commission keeps hunting for a party to charge and every candidate turns out to be a committee, a general staff, or a monarch who was on a yacht); the gap between causing something and being responsible for it; but-for causation going haywire in a case where everything was overdetermined and everyone had a mobilisation plan they insist they only used defensively; the fact that the treaty article everyone fought about for a century was drafted to lay a legal basis for a bill and was then read as a moral verdict — it doesn't use the word guilt, it talks about loss and damage arising from "the aggression of Germany and her allies," and that slippage from an accounting category into a verdict is the engine I want the whole piece to run on; and the two rival ways of telling the story, the one where a government already had expansionist war aims worked out and used the crisis as the occasion, and the one where everybody was frightened, in a hurry, and half asleep. Those two should sit in the report as majority versus dissent and map onto the real decades-long argument without ever naming it or anyone in it.

No verbatim quotes from treaties, telegrams or memoirs — paraphrase everything in the commission's legalese. And it has to work in two directions at once: a reader who knows the historiography should think I did my homework and laugh at the seperate registers of majority and dissent, while a reader who's never heard of the war guilt clause just sees a bunch of men in a room trying to divide a catastrophe into shares and quietly failing at it.
