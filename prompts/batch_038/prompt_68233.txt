I spend my working life on replicated state machines: Raft and Paxos in production, enough of the Byzantine fault-tolerance literature to follow the impossibility results, and a strong feel for what a protocol can and cannot guarantee about the value its nodes converge on. My philosophy amounts to one undergraduate course taken some years ago. Over the past year I have been reading, slowly and without a teacher, around the disagreement debate — conciliationism and steadfastness, the equal-weight view, the total-evidence response, the uniqueness thesis, the charge that conciliationism leaves one spineless — and I have found that nothing stays in my head until I have recast it in the vocabulary I actually think in: nodes, messages, failure models, quorums, safety and liveness, weighting by reliability, independent versus correlated failures, what is known locally and what has to be learned from inputs. So I should like you to perform that recasting for me, and then, more importantly, to tell me where it breaks down.

Take the components of the debate in turn and give me the closest counterpart each one has in my vocabulary, together with an honest verdict on how close that counterpart really is: what it is for two agents to be peers, the recommendation that one discount one's own belief on learning of the disagreement, the objection that doing so on every occasion makes one incapable of holding anything against contrary opinion, and the rejoinder that the relevant evidence includes not merely the fact of disagreement but whatever supported one's original view in the first place. I want you to be vigilant about smuggling. My framework carries quiet assumptions — that there is a determinate correct value the nodes are tracking, that a node's own state is a legitimate input rather than the very thing under dispute, that failures are independent enough for agreement to carry evidential weight, that a node has some purchase on its own reliability. Where the philosophers deny or complicate one of these, flag it explicitly rather than folding it into the translation and letting it look like a result.

Then the residue report, which is the part I am really after. Which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion I should be told about, and which have no expression at all in a vocabulary of nodes and messages — and for each of the last two categories, whether the deficiency is mine, in that my framework is missing a concept it ought to possess, or theirs, in that the philosophical claim turns out to be less determinate than its confident presentation suggested. I should like a view on which side the largest single piece of residue belongs to. Two particular things I want addressed. First, I suspect the translation is not neutral: casting disagreement as message-passing seems to make steadfastness look like a node that ignores its inputs, which would prejudge the argument in conciliationism's favour, and I want to know whether that is an artefact of my framing or a genuine argument I have stumbled into. Second, my own hunch is that "peer" is ambiguous between sharing reliability parameters and sharing an input log, and that the two come apart precisely where the debate becomes interesting; tell me whether that distinction can be drawn in my vocabulary at all, and what it explains if it can.

No references and no reading list, please: I am not in a position to chase them and I am not asking you to invent them. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and no side-by-side concordance of terms. British English throughout.
