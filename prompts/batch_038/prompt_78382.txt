I've got an oral coming up where I have to state my own interpretive position on the French Revolution on the spot and then defend it, and before I write it down I want to know whether it actually hangs together. I assembled it out of things that persuaded me at different times, and I'm worried it's a pile of parts rather than a position.

Here it is.

(a) The best explanation of the Revolution is ideological, or political-cultural. What made 1789 thinkable was that politics got taken over by abstraction: the men of letters who'd never held office ended up supplying the vocabulary in which the entire country argued about itself, and that vocabulary was rationalist, universalist, and had no feel for inherited institutions. Tocqueville's point, roughly. Ideas did the work; the fiscal crisis only opened the door.

(b) But the Revolution wasn't a real break. The centralized state was already there — the intendants, the king's council, the administrative tutelage over parishes — and what 1789-99 mostly did was strip off the decorative survivals and hand the machine to new operators. Napoleon finished a building the monarchy had already roofed. So "rupture" is the wrong frame for what the Revolution actually achieved.

(c) I dont buy the class-struggle story, at any rate not the bourgeoisie-against-aristocracy version. The two elites were fusing, the categories don't line up with who ended up on which side, and I think the whole structuralist apparatus is a way of avoiding the fact that nobody in 1789 knew what they were doing.

(d) That said, I think the Terror really was driven from below by social pressure — the sans-culottes, the Maximum, the anger about hoarding and the price of bread, the sections pushing the Convention further than it wanted to go. 1793-94 makes no sense as pure ideology or as pure elite maneuvering.

(e) I hate "revolutions devour their children" as an explanation. It's fatalist mush. The Terror was contingent: wartime emergency, the defection of the generals, the federalist revolts, sheer panic. It could have gone otherwise, and there is no thermidor built into the nature of things.

(f) And yet I do think there's a generalizable lesson and I want to be allowed to say it out loud: when you try to rebuild a society from scratch on an abstract egalitarian blueprint, you end up building a centralized, coercive state, and the purer the blueprint the worse the coercion. I take that to be a lesson about politics and not only about France, and I use it when I think about other revolutionary projects.

(g) Methodologically I'm suspicious of counterfactuals. Historians should explain what happened, and I don't think "it could have gone otherwise" is a real historical claim that anyone can support.

What I want: tell me which of these actually pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they conflict until you take them apart. Rank the real ones by severity rather than dumping them on me as seven equally bad worries, and for each give me the cheapest revision plus what that revision costs — I don't want a fix that quietly changes the subject so the tension disappears by redefinition. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most damage at once.

I genuinely don't know where the weak joint is, and the order isn't signalling anything, so don't be polite about it. Also don't hand me a survey of what the different schools say and then ask which I find more compelling — take a position. Say which of these you think is actually false rather than merely awkward to hold alongside the others, and say why. If you think I've got some of the history wrong as opposed to inconsistent, call that out separately, and be explicit about every place where your verdict leans on a contested reading of the events instead of on my own commitments.
