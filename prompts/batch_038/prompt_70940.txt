I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a British broadsheet, and next month I have been commissioned to publish something unusually personal: an account of the philosophical commitments that govern how I cover evolutionary biology, and how I cover claims about the future shape of societies. My editor has sensibly asked me to have the thing pressure-tested before it goes to print. I would rather the pressure-testing were adversarial than diplomatic, and I would rather have it from you than from a colleague who shares my views.

Here is the package. I will state it plainly, and I will not tell you which parts I suspect, because I do not in fact know.

1. Demarcation. A theory belongs to empirical science only if it rules something out: there must be some possible observation that would count decisively against it. Apparent confirmation is worth nothing except in so far as the theory has survived a test it might have failed.

2. The standing of Darwinism. Natural selection is a scientific theory on precisely that criterion. I regard the older suggestion — which I take to have been Popper's own, in his replies to his critics, that Darwinism is a metaphysical research programme and that survival of the fittest verges on tautology — as a mistake that its author later recognised as such.

3. The answer to the tautology charge. What rescues natural selection from circularity is the propensity reading of fitness: fitness is an objective dispositional property of an organism in an environment, on the model of Popper's propensity interpretation of probability, rather than a redescription of the offspring that happened to be produced. I treat this as settled, and I have said so in print.

4. Evolutionary epistemology. The growth of knowledge proceeds by conjecture and criticism, and is structurally continuous with variation and selective retention in biology. All organisms are problem-solvers; the difference between a scientist and an amoeba lies in the organism's attitude to its own hypotheses, not in the underlying mechanism.

5. Anti-historicism. Because the course of history depends on the growth of knowledge, and because that growth cannot be predicted in advance by rational methods, there can be no predictive science of history at the large scale. I draw the practical conclusion Popper drew: piecemeal social reform is legitimate, comprehensive blueprint engineering is not. I take this to be a robust conclusion about the world, not a caution regarding the present state of the social sciences.

6. Origins and warrant. Where a belief comes from is irrelevant to whether it deserves acceptance; warrant is a matter of corroboration and of having survived criticism. I am hostile to any argument that lends a claim credibility by appealing to how it arose, including appeals to the adaptive value of holding it.

7. The mind. I am a realist about the products of thought — problems, theories and arguments are objective items in the world, not merely mental events — and I do not accept that the physical domain is causally closed. Consciousness is an emergent level with genuine causal influence of its own.

What I want is an audit of this package, not a tour of the literature around it. Specifically: which of these pull against one another, and how badly; which pairings merely look as though they do, and dissolve once stated carefully; and what the least expensive repair would be. I have deliberately said nothing about which commitment I would surrender, and I should prefer that you not assume I am attached to any of them. Nor do I want a list in which every tension is presented as equally grave: rank them, and say at each rank what has to go. I do want a definite recommendation, argued for — one revision that would leave the most of the package standing — together with an honest account of what that revision costs me, including whether it would oblige me to retract anything I have already published under (3) or (5).

Please write in British English, and organise the answer under the following headings, in this order, at approximately the lengths given.

**Summary of the audit** — about 120 words. State up front which pairings you take to be genuinely in conflict and which you take to be illusory, with your ranking visible.

**Genuine tensions, ranked** — the bulk of the response, roughly 900 to 1,100 words. Take each in order of severity. For each, show the derivation rather than asserting the conflict: what the two commitments jointly entail that neither can tolerate, and which of them must be weakened, restricted or abandoned to prevent it.

**Apparent tensions** — about 250 words. The pairings that look troubling and are not, the reason each dissolves, and a note on why they recur so persistently in this territory.

**The cheapest repair** — about 250 words. One revision, defended against the obvious alternatives, with its costs stated plainly.

**What I must not write** — about 150 words. Given the audit, the formulations I should strike from the commission, and the strongest claim in this area that I can defend without overreach.

Be committed throughout. Where you think I am wrong, say so and give your reasons; where you think a tension is fatal, say that it is fatal. I would far rather be told that two of these cannot both survive than be handed a balanced inventory of the options and left to choose between them myself.
