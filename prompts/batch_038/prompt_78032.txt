I have brewed beer at home for roughly thirty years, and for the last twelve I have also judged amateur competitions, which means I have spent an unusual amount of time inside a single kind of document: the style guidelines that tell a judge what, for instance, a mild or a saison is supposed to be, with ranges for colour, bitterness and strength, and lists of flavours that are permitted and flavours that count as faults. Lately a pattern in that document has started to bother me, and I have not been able to find a word for it.

So far as I can determine, when those guidelines were first compiled they described what brewers in particular places were already making: the compilers tasted widely and wrote down what they found. But once the document existed and competitions were scored against it, the brewing began to change to fit the document. Amateurs reformulated to land inside the printed ranges. Commercial brewers adjusted flagship recipes. After two or three revision cycles the beers in a given category are visibly more alike than the loose, regionally varied collection the original compilers were describing. The category started life as a summary of a practice and became a target for it, and hitting the target is now what makes the summary accurate.

Something closely parallel happens in the dog world, which my daughter is deep in. Breed standards are written descriptions, revised from time to time by clubs; breeders select toward them and judges reward animals that sit near them, so over a few decades the animals themselves move. When a club relaxes or deletes a requirement — I understand this has happened with some of the more extreme physical features — the population does not snap back to its earlier form, and the older type often survives only under a different registry or a rival club's standard, so that what was one kind of dog becomes two, with the split caused by a change in wording.

Here is what I cannot resolve. Outsiders say these categories are arbitrary and invented, and they are not simply wrong: nothing about the world insists that a beer with these properties and a beer with those properties belong together, and the boundaries have demonstrably shifted. Yet inside these worlds the categories are extremely good to think with. Told nothing but the style, I can predict a surprising amount about the glass in front of me — roughly how it will taste, how it was fermented, what faults to look for, what it will do in the bottle over two years. Judges who agree on almost nothing else agree about category assignment far more often than an invented grouping should allow. The same goes for breeds: size, temperament, characteristic ailments, all reasonably inferable. Categories like this support genuine inferences, and supporting genuine inferences is normally what we mean by saying a grouping is not arbitrary — but the reason they support those inferences is that people keep making them true. The reliability is real, and the reliability is administered.

What I want to know is whether this configuration has a name, and whether it has already been carved out somewhere. I am after the shape of the thing rather than a label, so please be specific about the following.

1. If there is an established concept, what is it, and how well does my case fit? I expect it fits badly in at least one place. The half-heard account I have of classifications changing what they classify seems to depend on the classified knowing they have been classified and responding — patients, or people carrying a diagnosis, or shoppers with a survey. Beer and dogs know nothing of the matter. The loop here runs entirely through the people who write the standard and the people who brew or breed toward it. I want to know whether that difference is philosophically load-bearing or merely incidental.

2. What separates a category of this sort from one that is conventional but inert — a postal district, say, or the subdivision of an inch into sixteenths, where nothing feeds back on anything — and, in the other direction, from a category that simply records a pattern that was already present before anyone wrote it down? Where exactly do the edges of my phenomenon lie? I would like at least one case that sits right on each edge.

3. Can a category like this be wrong? I can see how it can be unhelpful, badly drawn, or out of date, and I can see how it can fail to describe the population it governs. What I cannot see is any fact it could get wrong in the way a claim about what makes something a metal could be wrong. If no such falsity is available, does that tell me something about what kind of thing the category is?

4. If nothing established actually fits, say so plainly rather than stretching a concept to cover it, and coin a term instead — with a definition tight enough to rule cases in and out, and an account of what the term excludes.

Two notes on how I would like this handled. First, I have no academic training in any of this; I read philosophy of science for pleasure but have not been inside a university since I was twenty-two, so please do not assume the vocabulary is already familiar to me, and introduce each term as you use it. Second, I would rather have your assessment than a survey: tell me which reading of these examples you think is correct and why, and where you think the rival readings go wrong. Close by telling me what would change your mind — which consideration or counterexample would push you to a different verdict about what this phenomenon is, and at which point in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to have slipped.
