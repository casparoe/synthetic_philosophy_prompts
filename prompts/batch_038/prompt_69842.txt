The final paper for my graduate seminar in formal epistemology consists of five questions of 25 marks each; candidates answer two in three hours, and a full answer runs to roughly 900–1,100 words. The cohort is largely first- and second-year PhD students who have had a year of Bayesian machinery and can be assumed to handle conditional-probability notation without gloss. I would like you to write one of the five questions, on van Fraassen's Reflection principle, together with everything I need in order to mark it.

The question must not be answerable from the standard summary. Restating the principle, listing the familiar cases in which an agent expects her future opinion to be manufactured rather than informed — sedation, hypnotic suggestion, propaganda, an experiment one knows in advance will mislead — and declaring Reflection false is the response I expect from half the cohort, and it should earn no more than a bare pass. Nor should candidates collect marks for observing that Reflection is a species of deference to a better-informed self and stopping there; that observation is where the work ought to begin.

What I want the question to turn on is a variant I have been using in class, for which I coined the name Retroflection: the mirror-image principle on which later credences defer to earlier ones, so that the credence you will hold in H at a later time, conditional on your having held credence q in H at an earlier time, is itself q. On the paper this must be flagged as my stipulation and attributed to nobody. The question should present one compact case in which an agent knows at an earlier time exactly what her later credence in some hypothesis will be, and knows that the change will not have been produced by anything she now counts as evidence: she will be thirty-six hours into an on-call shift, exhausted, and newly susceptible to a colleague she presently regards as a smooth talker. Bets on the hypothesis should be offered to her at both times, at odds matching her respective credences, so that a sure loss is visible to her in advance; and a pre-commitment device should be available to her at the earlier time that would prevent her later self from accepting the second bet.

Candidates should then be asked to do three things. First, derive Reflection from the assumption that the agent expects her later credences to be obtained by conditionalizing on evidence she now regards as veridical, and identify the premise that carries the weight. Second, determine whether the diachronic Dutch book argument generalizes to Retroflection, and, if it does, say what that shows about whether Reflection is a requirement of coherence or a substantive claim about the agent's own reliability. Third, explain what the availability of the pre-commitment device does to the argument, given that the sure loss can be avoided by binding the later self instead of by revising the earlier credence. They should close by saying whether the use van Fraassen makes of Reflection in defence of Lewis's Principal Principle is affected by the verdicts they have reached.

Please supply: the question exactly as it will appear on the paper, notation included, and self-contained enough that no reading is required in order to attempt it; a model answer of about 900 words; a marking scheme of six must-hit points whose marks total 25, each accompanied by a single line naming the characteristic way candidates lose marks on it; and a closing paragraph stating precisely which move separates a first-class answer from a competent one. Include no bibliography and no suggested reading, and do not attribute any principle, case, or turn of phrase to a named author other than in the two instances permitted above.
