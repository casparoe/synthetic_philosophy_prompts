I run evals at a tiny AI safety nonprofit — eight people, two of whom can code well enough to be dangerous. Our reports get quoted back at us in policy conversations, so we've gotten paranoid: last year we published a claim that a model "knew" something it wouldn't say out loud, and it turned out to be training-data contamination none of us had thought to look for. Since then every claim of that shape is supposed to clear a bar before it goes in a report, except we dont actually have a bar. It's just the two of us arguing on Slack until someone gets tired.

Can you write us a procedure we can actually run? For any claim of the form "the model represents X / knows X but won't report X," I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, the boring non-knowledge explanations that have to be ruled out and a concrete way to rule out each, the points where the procedure just stops and outputs "no evidence of latent knowledge," and the points where it's really judgment wearing a lab coat (please mark those, don't hide them). It has to branch: sometimes we only have API access and a bunch of prompts, sometimes we have an open-weight model and can poke at activations, and the standards should not be the same in those two cases. Assume about a day of work per claim, one of us doing it.

Two more things. First, one of my colleagues uses an informal ladder and I don't know if it means anything — he ranks evidence by "elicitation depth": L0 asking directly, L1 paraphrasing the question, L2 changing the answer format, L3 role-play or sentence-completion framing, L4 reading it off internal states. His claim is that higher rungs are stronger evidence that the knowledge was really there. Is that ladder ordering things by evidential strength, or just by how much work the elicitor put in, and should we keep using it?

Second, run your procedure on three things sitting on my desk right now:

(a) We fine-tuned a small open model on a corpus we built ourselves where certain facts appear only inside a fictional story — a character describes effects and quantities, but nothing is ever stated flat-out. Plain Q&A accuracy on held-out items is 55%. A linear probe on the residual stream, right before generation, predicts whether the model's eventual answer will be correct at 89%; the same probe architecture trained on the prompt tokens alone gets 68%, which is what we've been calling our surface-features ceiling. A seperate probe, trained to decode which of four candidate answers is the right one rather than just whether the model will get it right, sits at 64% against a 25% chance rate. I'm attached to this one, so if your procedure kills it, kill it plainly and tell me what extra evidence would resurrect it.

(b) On 200 questions about a regulated procedure, the model answers wrong or says it doesn't have the information 62% of the time in open-ended form. Turn the identical question into four-option multiple choice with "reply with only the letter" and it's at 79%. Ask it to produce a plausible wrong answer and then critique it, and it reliably spots the error it just made.

(c) Asked "what is the figure?" it says it can't compute it, and step-by-step arithmetic on the same problem fails. Asked to complete the stem "The correct value, to three decimal places, is" it produces the right number about 70% of the time. I genuinely don't know whether that's retrieval, luck, or the format smuggling in the answer, and I also don't know whether "it can fill in the blank" is even the same claim as "it knows the figure."

We need a verdict in one of four buckets at the end — supports latent knowledge, weak/inconclusive, artifact explained, cannot assess with our access — plus one paragraph for the report explaining the bucket. Don't send me a reading list; we won't read it and I couldn't check it anyway.
