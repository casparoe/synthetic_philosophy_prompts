I work in quant risk at a mid-size insurer, about twelve years of it, so scenario analysis, stress testing, ambiguity sets, worst-case and minimax-regret choice rules, chance constraints, conservatism buffers, the whole distributionally-robust-optimization toolbox is basically my native language. I've never taken a philosophy course. A friend of mine who does epistemology sent me a couple of papers on imprecise probabilities and I got maybe a third of the way through before giving up, but one thing kept bugging me: it reads like they reinvented what I do for a living and then got extremely worked up about it.

So here's what I want. Take the core imprecise-probability position — the claim that when your evidence is thin or your experts disagree you shouldn't force yourself to commit to a single number, you should carry a whole set of probability functions around, and that this set is your actual state of belief rather than a placeholder for a precise one you just haven't computed yet — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I already use: ambiguity sets, why we run five scenarios instead of blessing a base case, why the worst case over a set is the number that goes in the report. Then do the same for three of thier specific claims that I couldn't parse: (i) dilation, the thing where conditioning on news that looks irrelevant can make your interval wider rather than narrower; (ii) the claim that there isn't a single right way to update a set of probabilities, that several update rules are defensible and they disagree, and that this pluralism is a feature; (iii) the idea that how imprecise you ought to be is supposed to track how much evidence you have.

The part I actually care about is the residue report. Which of those four items translate cleanly onto something I do every week, which only translate if you bend my vocabulary a little, and which have no counterpart in it at all. Please be explicit about where you're fudging — I don't want you to quietly treat an ambiguity set as if it *meant* a doxastic state just because that makes the mapping look tidy, and I especially don't want my framework's local habits (the ambiguity set gets chosen for tractability, then gets blessed by a model risk committee, and then everyone talks about the resulting number as though it were objective) smuggled in as though they were translations of the philosophers' claims.

And settle a bet while you're at it. My take is that their headline worry — the one where they say a mushy credence leaves you unable to decide, rationally paralyzed, some word like that — just isn't a real problem, because max-min always spits out a number and so does minimax regret, so obviously you can act, we act on Tuesday. Am I right, or is that objection aimed at something my vocabulary has no slot for, like whether the choice is *permitted* versus whether it's *determinate*? And flip it around: is there anything about how we actually run these exercises that the imprecise-probability people would call out as incoherent or as question-begging, or is their whole debate a solution in search of a problem that engineering settled in the late nineties?
