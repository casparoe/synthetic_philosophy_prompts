For eleven years I've run a small reading group in my living room — eight of us, all of whom came up on the rationalist blogs and forums a decade and a half ago and have since drifted into ordinary tech jobs. We read slowly and argue constantly. Nobody is a professional philosopher, though three of us have read more analytic ethics than is strictly healthy for our day jobs. In two weeks it's my turn to lead, ninety minutes, whiteboard only, and the topic I've picked is what the literature calls the paradox of deontology: the thought that agent-centered restrictions look self-defeating, because if the point of the prohibition is that people not be tortured, then a world with less torture in it is better by the prohibition's own lights, and yet the deontologist forbids me from bringing that world about by torturing one.

I don't want you to explain this to me. I want a plan for how I should explain it to them.

The specific difficulty is that my audience is wrong in a particular way before I open my mouth. They are consequentialists by temperament for whom expected-value arithmetic is a reflex, they sorted themselves on trolley cases years ago, and they treat agent-relative reasons as a confusion awaiting dissolution. Their default move will be that the deontologist is secretly one of three things: a threshold consequentialist who hasn't admitted the threshold, a rule-consequentialist about dispositions and character, or someone protecting his own clean hands. If the session runs through the standard setup, it will be absorbed as a new label for an error they believe they already diagnosed.

Here is my own gloss, which I want you to build the plan around — and to correct bluntly if you think it's lopsided or wrong. The paradox only gets its grip from the assumption that the reason behind a restriction must be statable from nowhere, as a ranking of whole states of the world. On that assumption the deontologist is committed to wanting constraint-violations minimized globally and therefore to minimizing them himself, and his only escape is to deny the assumption: to hold that some reasons are indexed to the agent's own act in a way that does not generalize into a reason to make everyone else's acts come out that way too. Call it the two-ledgers picture. What the deontologist wants for the world and what he may do himself are kept in separate books, and the paradox is the charge that the second book has no legitimate entries. If that gloss is right, build the arc toward it. If it isn't, say so plainly, tell me what to use instead, and rebuild the arc around your substitute.

Then the plan itself:

The order. What must be in place before what, and why the dependencies run that way — in particular, which pieces of machinery (a constraint that isn't a function of outcomes; agent-centered restrictions as distinct from agent-centered prerogatives) have to be nailed down before the paradox is even statable, and which of those pieces my group will try to skip.

One carrying example, and only one. The trolley problem and the transplant surgeon are both disqualified: the first is over-familiar and pre-sorted, the second makes the numbers do the work instead of the restriction. Tell me what your example must have and what it must lack, and where in the sequence I introduce it and how much I let it grow.

The misconception map. Take the three dismissals above, add whatever else you predict from this kind of room, and for each tell me the point in the sequence where I head it off and roughly what I say at that point. I want the defuses ordered, not listed.

The autonomous-systems wrinkle. Three of the eight design policies that get applied at scale, so when I say an agent must not kill one to prevent five, they hear that a policy must not, and they think the paradox evaporates because a policy fixed in advance and applied everywhere looks more like legislation than like an act. Tell me whether to lean on that or to shut it down inside the first ten minutes, commit one way, and give me the single sentence I'd use.

One question for the last five minutes that distinguishes the idea having landed from the room merely being agreeable — something whose answer differs depending on whether they can state the deontologist's reason for refraining without it collapsing into either a claim about outcomes or a claim about rules.

What to leave out at this level, and what I say out loud about leaving it out. I currently intend to cut double effect, thresholds, the Kantian grounding, and the debate about numbers; tell me if any of those is load-bearing for the sequence and therefore cannot be cut.

Two ground rules. No readings, no bibliography, no citations — nobody in that room will look anything up and I'm not handing out a list. And I would rather have a committed plan than a menu: pick the example, pick the order, take a side on my gloss and on the policy wrinkle, and give your reasons. Where a step depends on a contested reading of the deontologist's own commitments, flag it in a clause instead of hedging the whole plan.
