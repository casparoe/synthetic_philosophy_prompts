I teach a fourteen-student upper-level seminar, cross-listed between philosophy and history, on necessity and contingency in historical explanation. Last year I set an essay question asking whether a major historical transition was inevitable, and nine of the fourteen scripts were functionally identical: two pages rehearsing determinism against free will, a ceremonial gesture at the great-man theory, and a closing paragraph concluding that history is too complex for either extreme. It was unmarkable in the sense that mattered — the scripts told me nothing about which students understood the material and which had absorbed a genre. This year I want a single take-home essay question that cannot be answered that way. The students have done six weeks on the possible-worlds semantics for counterfactuals: they have seen the small-miracle account of what makes an antecedent true, the distinction between backtracking and non-backtracking readings, and the trouble that preemption and overdetermination cause for counterfactual analyses of causation. They have also done four weeks on historiographical method, including how historians defend claims about what would otherwise have happened. The question may assume all of that freely and should not spend marks on restating it.

Here is the material I want the question built around, and I'd like you to draft it as the passage that appears on the exam paper. A historian — invented, and I'll say so on the paper — argues that British industrialization was already inevitable by 1750, and offers three counterfactual arguments that he presents as mutually independent. First: had the atmospheric engine never been improved into an efficient rotative one, the progressive exhaustion of shallow coal seams would still have made mechanical drainage and haulage economically unavoidable, so steam would have arrived on a slightly different schedule. Second: had parliamentary enclosure stalled, rising grain prices would still have made capital-intensive farming the profitable option for any rational landholder. Third: had the Atlantic trade been suppressed a generation earlier, the merchant capital already formed would have found domestic outlets, because textile demand was rising regardless. He concludes that because three separate roads, each with a different starting point removed, all arrive at the same destination, the destination was structurally fixed rather than contingent on any particular person, firm, or accident. The trap I want the question to set is that this is both a genuine modal error and, in a weaker form, a genuine insight, and the good script must do both halves. It must say what the convergence argument actually establishes — robustness of an outcome under a hand-picked finite set of local perturbations, every one of which holds fixed a single common background of coal geology, demographic pressure, and property institutions, and every one of which is a case of redundant causation in which the backup cause is asserted to do the work rather than shown to — and it must say what would be required to get from that to inevitability, namely some measure over the relevant space of antecedent worlds plus a non-question-begging account of which background features are held fixed and why those. I also want the question to force students onto the problem of constructing the antecedent at all: whether the historian is entitled to the counterfactuals he is using, given that making one true while holding the rest of the structural background intact requires either a local miracle whose further consequences he has no grip on or a backtracking reading on which the background differs too and the argument loses its force. Credit heavily the student who notices that the three counterfactuals are not independent in the sense the argument needs — that they are variations inside one scenario rather than samples drawn from a space — and credit the student who observes that if every cause of an outcome is genuinely redundant, "inevitable" may be a defensible way of describing a high density of sufficient conditions, provided the measure problem has been answered. A script that merely says counterfactuals about history are untestable and therefore the argument fails should receive a low mark, and the marking scheme should say so explicitly rather than leaving it to my discretion.

The deliverable is four things. The exam question itself, with the passage embedded, a stated word limit of 1,500 and a mark total of 25. A model answer of roughly 800 words. A marking scheme organised around six must-hit points, with the marks each carries and, for each, one line naming the specific way candidates typically fail it — for instance, reaching for determinism where what is at issue is a claim about the distribution of sufficient causes. And then a short section separating what distinguishes a first-class script from a competent B, plus three or four red-flag sentences that reliably indicate a student has substituted a label for an argument. Two house rules. Neither the question nor the model answer may cite, name-drop, or allude bibliographically to any real work; students should be told on the paper that they may refer to ideas descriptively rather than by author, because several of them will be writing from memory under time pressure. And please do not let any part of the assessment turn on a fact about the actual economic history of eighteenth-century Britain — the setting is deliberate dressing, the students should be told that it is, and every mark should be winnable or losable on modal and methodological grounds alone.
