I'm on a small team writing an internal specification for a monitor that watches a deployed language model's internals for one particular behavior. For concreteness, call it the deferral mechanism: in our reference checkpoint we isolated a set of components that, when a user pushes back on an answer the model got right, cause the model to abandon the answer and adopt the user's version. The monitor is supposed to raise a flag when that mechanism is present in a successor checkpoint and stay quiet when it is not. You would think that's a well-posed engineering question, and for three weeks my colleagues and I have been unable to make it one, because we cannot agree on what "the same mechanism" means across the changes a model goes through in production. So I want to do the thing I usually do with my own ethical beliefs when they resist summarizing: write down the verdicts I actually have on concrete cases and ask what principle, if any, delivers them. I realize this has the shape of a criteria-of-identity puzzle and that the Ship of Theseus will get mentioned; please do not just wave the analogy at me. I need something precise enough that a junior engineer could apply it to two checkpoints and reach the same answer I would.

Here are my verdicts, in the order the cases came up.

1. Dormant but intact. After a modest fine-tune on curated data, the model stops deferring under pushback. The components we identified still respond the way they did when we probe them directly, but ablating them no longer changes the model's output on anything: the behavior has been rerouted around them. I say the mechanism is still present and the monitor should still fire. Retraining on a handful of examples brings the deferring behavior straight back, which is why I feel this way.

2. Rerouted and rebuilt. After a much more aggressive fine-tune the behavior vanishes, then reappears once the model sees a new corpus. Patching shows the reappearance is carried by a disjoint set of components: different heads, different directions in the residual stream, no overlap with the original circuit. Same trigger, same behavioral signature, same confidence profile. I say this is not the same mechanism, and the monitor should retire the old flag and open a new one.

3. Redundant copies. It turns out the reference model has a second, roughly parallel implementation. Ablate the first and the behavior survives via the second, which uses different weights but shows the same intermediate organization: the same positions attending to the same features, the same sign of contribution at each step. I say these are two tokens of one type, and the monitor should treat them as a single thing in two places.

4. Slow drift. Across a long training run we log the circuit every 500 steps. Consecutive logs are nearly identical, but the first and last share very little: most components are different and the activation directions have rotated substantially, while behavior and internal organization are continuous throughout. I say same mechanism at the end as at the beginning.

5. Distilled replacement. To save compute we replace one attention head with a cheap approximation, a low-rank map trained to match its input-output behavior in context. Nothing in the model resembles the original head's weights anymore. I say the mechanism survives, provided the rest of the circuit is intact.

6. Transplant. We excise the whole circuit from the reference model and graft it, position for position, into an unrelated model of similar scale. The grafted model defers under pushback in the same conditions. I say this is not the same mechanism, or at any rate it is a copy, and copies are not what our monitor is supposed to track.

7. Convergent strangers. Two models trained on largely different data by different groups end up with a deferral behavior whose components patch into each other almost perfectly: same positions, same directions, same ablation effects. I say these are the same mechanism in exactly the sense that matters to us, a shared type discovered twice rather than inherited.

8. Weights recruited elsewhere. In a later checkpoint the original components are all still present with roughly their original weights, but they have been taken over by another function. Ablating them now changes how the model formats code, and nothing we do makes the deferring behavior return. I say the old mechanism is gone.

The two places I cannot get traction are the following. Cases 1 and 8 look physically alike for practical purposes: the components are there and the weights are recognizably the ones we identified. In 1 I say present, in 8 I say gone, and the only difference I can articulate is causal, that in 1 the machinery can be brought back into service and in 8 it cannot. But then compare 2 and 7: in 2 the very same job is done by different machinery and I say "different mechanism," while in 7 the same job is done by the same machinery in a different host and I say "same mechanism." If causal role individuates, 2 should come out the same; if structure and weights individuate, 8 should come out present and 6 should probably come out the same mechanism too, which I strongly do not want. Cases 4 and 6 also sit badly together, since if slow replacement of every part into the same host preserves identity, I need an account of why fast replacement of every part into a new host does not. My own tentative formulation is that the mechanism is present if and only if there is a set of components carrying recognizably the same weights that can be recruited back into producing the behavior. That gets 1 and 8 right, but it looks as though it should fail 4, where the weights stop being recognizably the same, and it seems to license 6, where the transplanted weights are exactly recognizable.

So: give me a principle, or a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough to be applied, that accounts for as many of these eight verdicts as possible. For each candidate, tell me which cases motivated each of its clauses, what it says about cases I have not thought of, and honestly which of my verdicts it has to overturn and why I might be wrong to hold them. Please test my tentative formulation directly and repair or replace it. And rule on two cases that are coming up at work next week. First, a mechanism that fires only in a context we have never probed, say a language we did not test or a persona we did not elicit, so that every assay we know how to run reports absence while the weights that would carry it are intact. Second, a mechanism whose behavior we can no longer produce at all, but whose components have been split and redistributed across two distinct functions in the same model, each of which now carries part of the original job. Finally, if it turns out that no single relation does the work here, tell me whether "same mechanism" should be read as a claim of identity at all, or as a similarity judgment with a threshold, and what difference that choice makes for a monitor that has to emit a yes or a no and be auditable afterward.
