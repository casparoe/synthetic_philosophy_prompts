I co-host a small phil-of-mind podcast — 45-minute episodes, audience is mostly grad students and curious non-specialists, so we can be technical but not jargon-dense. Next week's guest is a philosopher whose position I want to stress-test rather than just advertise. She's a qualia skeptic of a particular sort: she grants the whole classic negative case (nothing can be intrinsic, private, ineffable, and directly apprehended all at once, so the posit collapses) but she refuses to land on deflationism or on "just be a functionalist." Her positive claim is that in the familiar coffee-taster sort of case — someone who loved a certain coffee for years and now finds it undrinkable, or a wine drinker whose tastes migrate — it's a mistake to ask whether the feel changed or whether the judgment about the feel changed, because there's a third thing that also changes and its the one doing the real work: the standard the person applies when they classify their own present state against their memory of past ones. She calls this calibration drift, and she says it explains why the realist and the eliminativist are each half right. The subject is correct that something changed and correct that they can't pin down what, but wrong to model the "what" as a private intrinsic property sitting behind the reports.

Write me eight to ten interview questions, ordered so the episode builds. No restatement questions ("so, tell us about calibration drift") and nothing about her intellectual biography — she gets plenty of that everywhere else. What I do want covered:

- one that presses on whether calibration drift is anything more than the "the judgment changed" option wearing a new label, and what content is actually added by talking about a standard rather than a disposition to judge;
- one on spectrum inversion and inverted-earth style cases: does her view make them coherent or incoherent, and what does she think turns on the answer;
- one on the cases where calibration plausibly stays anchored while experience shifts fast and hard — anesthesia, a migraine aura, a drug that changes color saturation — since those look like the cleanest place to seperate her story from a pure judgment story;
- one on the introspection side: if all there is is calibration plus report, why does it seem to the subject that there's something it's like at all, and does she want to call that seeming an illusion, a bad description of something real, or neither;
- and a closing question asking her to name an observation or experiment that would come out differently under her view than under plain representationalism — and, if she can't name one, what work the view is doing.

For each, give me a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, not to a strawman. She's extremely good at dissolving pressure by saying the disagreement is merely verbal, so build at least two of the follow-ups so that reaching for that line costs her something concrete. And if you think one of my bullets is just a bad question — too easy, or one she's answered a hundred times — say why and swap in a better one in its place.
