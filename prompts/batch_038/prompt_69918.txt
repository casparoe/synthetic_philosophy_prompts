I'm helping to scope a chapter for an edited volume on the ethical upshot of panpsychism, and before anyone argues for a position I want the specification rather than the arguments: what would a theory of the moral significance of micro-experience have to deliver to count as adequate? Give me the requirements list — the questions it must answer, the cases it must handle, the distinctions it may not collapse, and the constraints from the metaphysics it cannot contradict — with the consideration behind each, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps (constitutive panpsychism versus cosmopsychism, for instance, or Russellian monism versus a more robust phenomenal realism). Please keep the cases concrete rather than schematic: whether a kilogram of ordinary rock, containing on the order of 10^25 atoms, thereby carries more moral weight than a brain with its tens of billions of neurons; whether boiling a pot of water is a greater harm than killing a mouse; whether a micro-phenomenal property can be valenced at all, given that our experience seems to draw on a far richer palette than anything a single electron could instantiate; and, given that the fundamental entities of field theory are not persistent, individually trackable little subjects, what the bearer of a micro-experience even is and whether it can be harmed. Close with two things: which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — my own suspicion is that requiring moral weight wherever there is experience, requiring that weight to be graded by how much experience there is, and requiring the theory to change what we ought to do cannot all be met at once, and if that is right the list is itself the result. Skip the survey of who has proposed what; I want the criteria.
