I report on computing and public policy for a general-interest magazine, and I am drafting a piece about pretrial risk instruments. The empirical backdrop will be familiar to you: a newsroom analysis of scores assigned to people arrested in one Florida county found that Black defendants were wrongly flagged as likely to reoffend at roughly twice the rate of white defendants, while white defendants were more often wrongly cleared as low risk; the vendor answered that its instrument was well calibrated, in that defendants of different races who received the same score went on to be re-arrested at similar rates. Both statements turned out to be true of the same data, and that is where the controversy has been stuck ever since.

A civil-rights attorney I have corresponded with for years has sent me the following chain, which he says disposes of the matter entirely. He is a careful man and I do not think he is being sloppy, but I cannot accept where it ends and I cannot locate the point at which it stops following. He gave it to me in this form.

(1) What makes a risk instrument unfair, as distinct from merely inaccurate, is that it distributes its errors unevenly between groups: if members of one group are likelier than members of another to be wrongly scored as high risk, or likelier to be wrongly scored as low risk, the instrument is unfair to them.

(2) Fairness therefore requires that false positive rates and false negative rates be equal across the groups being scored.

(3) Fairness also requires calibration: the same score must correspond to the same observed event rate for everyone who receives it, since a defendant told that her score means a sixty percent chance of re-arrest is entitled to that figure being a sixty percent chance whoever she is.

(4) The impossibility results for risk scores show that when two groups have different base rates for the outcome, no score can be calibrated and equalize both error rates at once; the only escapes are exact equality of base rates and a perfectly accurate score.

(5) Base rates are never equal in any domain worth regulating, because a variable that predicted nothing would never have been adopted in the first place.

(6) So no risk instrument actually in use meets the requirements in (1) through (3), and each of them is unfair to someone.

(7) Which of the three conditions a given deployment satisfies is not settled by the mathematics but chosen by whoever deploys the score; in choosing it, that party chooses which group absorbs the excess errors, and making that choice without the consent of the groups concerned is precisely what makes it unfair.

(8) Hence the only way to avoid the unfairness is to stop scoring: prohibit statistical risk instruments and decide each case on its particulars.

(9) But if (1) states what unfairness is, individualized judgment falls under it too, since any decision procedure however informal assigns error rates in practice, and there is no reason to expect an unaided judge to equalize them across groups when base rates differ.

(10) It follows that no procedure for allocating a consequential burden — sentencing, clinical triage, credit, admissions, benefit eligibility — can be fair wherever the groups affected differ in base rates.

(11) A standard that nothing can meet is not a standard.

(12) Therefore (1) is not a requirement of justice, and allegations of algorithmic unfairness, whatever else they express, are not tracking anything real.

He presents (12) as a reductio of the fairness literature rather than of anything he himself holds, and he is quite pleased with (8), which he still wants.

I have a deadline and no time to chase the literature, so I want the reasoning rather than a reading list. Specifically:

Which step do you reject? I do not know whether this is a chain with a concealed equivocation or a sound chain containing one premise that is merely expensive to keep, and I would rather be told plainly than reassured.

For each of (2), (3), (5), (7), (11) and (1) itself: what does giving it up cost, and what has to be put in its place if the rest of the argument is to be salvaged?

Does anything change if the quantity being predicted is re-arrest rather than reoffending — that is, if the label the score is calibrated against is itself downstream of enforcement decisions?

If the chain fails somewhere between (6) and (12), is there a shorter argument in the neighborhood that does go through, and what is the strongest claim of that kind I could responsibly print?

Two constraints on your answer. I would rather keep (4): I have no intention of running a paragraph implying that the mathematics has been misdescribed, and I do not think it has been. And I remain attached to something like the thought behind (11), in a weakened form, because a piece that ends by telling readers there is nothing to complain about is not a piece I want to have written. Tell me what each of those attachments costs me, and whether they can both be afforded at once.
