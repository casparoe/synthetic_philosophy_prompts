I convene a fourth-year seminar on teleological explanation in the life sciences: ninety minutes, sixteen students, roughly half reading philosophy and half reading evolutionary biology. All of them have been assigned Gould and Lewontin's 1979 paper on the spandrels of San Marco and the adaptationist programme. When I have taught this session before, I have made the mistake of walking through the paper in its own order, and the result is that the students leave able to recite the word "spandrel" as a term of dismissal while being unable to construct or to evaluate a single spandrel argument. I intend to restructure the session entirely, and I want a plan for the explanation rather than the explanation itself.

The composition of the group is the difficulty. The biologists arrive convinced that they absorbed all of this in their second-year lectures and that the controversy was settled long ago in their discipline's favour; the philosophers arrive with no developmental biology at all but with a well-drilled instinct to treat every empirical dispute as a merely verbal one. Please address the following.

1. Specify the conceptual prerequisites that must be in place before the paper's argument is intelligible at all, in the order they must be introduced, and say what each one depends on. I have in mind at least the separation of a trait's current utility from the question of its origination, and the distinction between a null hypothesis and a shifting of the burden of proof. For each prerequisite, indicate which of the two halves of the room will find it patronising and how to present it so that it does not feel that way to them.

2. Decide for me whether the session should open with the architectural analogy or with a biological trait, and argue the choice. I want the reasoning in both directions: what is lost by opening with the vault (the risk that the metaphor arrives as ornament and is never cashed out), and what is lost by opening with the organism (the risk that the students fixate on the empirical verdict about that particular structure and never see the logic).

3. Nominate one case to carry the whole session, and state what that case must have and what it must lack: which features make the by-product reading genuinely available to a student, which features stop the case from degenerating into either "we have simply not yet found the function" or "this is transparently an adaptation", and whether the case can also be pushed through the subsequent move in which a by-product acquires a function of its own. If the honest answer is that no single case can do all three jobs, say so and tell me how to divide the labour.

4. Advise on the four argumentative styles the paper identifies for breaking the chain of adaptationist reasoning: whether to hand them to the students in advance as a taxonomy or to let them fall out of the case, and which of the four is the weakest and should be flagged as such at the point of introduction. The students will otherwise take all four to be equally probative, and one of them in particular invites the inference from absence of evidence to evidence of absence.

5. Give me the misconceptions, separately for the two halves of the room, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than answered afterwards. I expect at least: that the critique is anti-adaptationism rather than a critique of a style of argument; that a spandrel cannot be useful, or cannot be elaborated by later selection; that the paper licenses the inference from "no proposed function" to "not an adaptation"; and the biologists' conviction that the whole debate was a philosophical fuss about nothing. For each, say what it costs me to defuse it where you propose.

6. Tell me what I should decline to cover at this level, and how to announce the refusal rather than simply omitting it. Candidates include the comparative-method machinery for detecting phylogenetic inertia, the architectural niceties about which structures in the basilica are properly called pendentives rather than spandrels, and the fact that the empirical verdicts in the literature remain contested and that critics have charged the authors with underestimating how pervasive adaptation is.

7. End with one question I can put to the room in the final ten minutes that would show whether they have grasped the logical structure rather than the slogan — and tell me what a bad but fluent answer to it would sound like.

Two further requirements. First, the plan must work for students who have read nothing beyond the assigned paper; do not supply bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not build any segment around a work they will not have encountered. Second, write the whole response in British English.
