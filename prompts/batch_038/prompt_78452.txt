I am marking a second-year metaphysics problem set and would like an independent assessment of one answer before I settle a mark of my own. The question was: "Explain how Black's two-spheres case is supposed to refute the Identity of Indiscernibles, and assess the two most promising replies available to a defender of the principle. (600 words.)" The mark scheme allocates 40 per cent to accurate exposition of the case and the principle, and 60 per cent to the assessment of the replies.

The student wrote:

> Black asks us to imagine a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres, of the same diameter, colour and temperature, standing a fixed distance from one another in an otherwise empty space. Everything true of the one is true of the other. The Identity of Indiscernibles (PII) says that if two things share all their properties they are identical; its converse, the indiscernibility of identicals, is uncontroversial. Black's universe is therefore meant to be a case of two numerically distinct things that are perfectly indiscernible, and hence a counterexample to PII.
>
> The first reply is that the spheres are not in fact indiscernible, since each bears relations the other does not: one is to the left of the other, and each occupies a different point of space. Black's answer is that in a perfectly symmetrical universe there is no left and no right, so no such description picks out one sphere rather than the other; and the Leibnizian will add that absolute space is precisely what PII is supposed to rule out. That seems right: were points of space themselves individuals, PII would come out true only because space had done the individuating.
>
> A more recent reply appeals to weak discernibility. The spheres stand to one another in an irreflexive and symmetric relation, being a fixed distance from, which neither bears to itself, and that is enough to ground their numerical diversity in something other than itself. But it does more than that. If the spheres are discernible in this weak sense, then they are discernible, and the defender of PII can say the principle is preserved after all: the two spheres have turned out to differ in a property, namely standing in that relation.
>
> The second reply is Adams's. Adams suggests we stipulate that one sphere is Al and the other Bob, and holds that each has a primitive thisness, the property of being identical with that very thing, which the other lacks. This saves PII, but only by admitting non-qualitative properties, which arguably abandons the motivation for PII in the first place. Hacking objects differently, that Black has not genuinely described a possible universe at all.
>
> My conclusion is that every attempt to discern the spheres fails, and so PII is false in a universe like Black's. This has a further consequence worth stating. If a substance is nothing over and above the bundle of properties it instantiates, then two exactly similar spheres would be one and the same bundle and there could not be two of them; since there can be two of them, bundle theories of substance are refuted along with PII.

Please give the answer a mark out of 100 together with the specific comments that justify it. Reply with exactly five numbered items, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first should state the mark and a single overall judgement, and the remaining four should each take up one particular claim in the answer and say what is sound about it or exactly where and why it goes wrong. Write the comments so that they could be returned to the student unaltered.
