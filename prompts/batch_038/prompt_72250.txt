I am drafting a paper on higher-order evidence, and I have reached the point where a balanced survey of the two camps is useless to me: I need to commit to a number and see its weak points marked. The proposition I want your credence on is this.

(P) It is possible — for a finite agent of roughly our kind, not for an idealized reasoner — to satisfy the best available account of epistemic rationality, all things considered rather than merely by one local standard, while believing p and simultaneously believing that her total evidence does not support p.

Let me be clear about what (P) is not. It is not the empirical claim that people ever feel self-undermined, or assert what they do not believe, or reason in ways that look akratic from the outside; obviously they do. (P) is the normative claim that no true theory of epistemic rationality rules out the combination as such — that there is no constraint requiring an agent's first-order beliefs to line up with her own considered view of what her evidence supports.

Give me your credence in (P) on a 0–1 scale, and then give me the derivation. At a minimum the decomposition should cover: (a) whether a true theory of epistemic rationality contains such a constraint at all, and if so whether it is a requirement whose violation makes the agent irrational or merely an ideal whose failure leaves her rational but imperfect; (b) whether higher-order evidence can be systematically misleading — whether one's evidence about one's evidence can diverge from the facts about one's evidence — since if it cannot, the constraint costs nothing and looks cheap to accept; (c) whether "rational" denotes a single all-things-considered status or a family of local statuses that can come apart, and hence whether (P) is trivially true on one reading and trivially false on another; and (d) whether the standard cases marshalled for (P) — impairment by a substance whose effects one knows about, peer disagreement, testimony that undermines its own uptake — describe genuinely possible agents, or are artifacts of our describing the agent from an external vantage point she does not occupy. Assign each a rough probability, say in a line or two what puts it there, and show explicitly how they combine into your number, with the arithmetic rather than a bare figure.

Then the case I want tested against that decomposition. Dana is an intelligence analyst. Six hours ago she took, knowingly and voluntarily, a compound whose effects she has been reliably briefed on: for its duration it biases her in a specific, known direction, toward over-weighting apparent patterns and coincidences in the material in front of her. It leaves her arithmetic, her memory of the briefing, and her general reasoning intact, and she knows exactly what is happening to her. She is now assessing intercepts bearing on a hypothesis H; she finds that they support H, and she has applied the correction the bias seems to call for. The twist I want you to work with rather than argue against is this: Dana also believes, on the strength of the same reliable briefing plus reflection she takes to be sound, that the operation of applying the correction is itself performed by the biased faculty, that any correction she computes is therefore liable to be over- or under-applied in the direction of the bias, and that she has no bias-free procedure available with which to audit the audit. She holds all of this clearly and at once, and she ends up believing H while believing that her evidence, correctly assessed, does not support H.

So: does this case raise or lower your credence in (P), and which term does it move? Does the closed loop — the absence of any unbiased vantage point from which to check the check — do genuine normative work, or does it merely describe a bounded agent for whom "fully rational, all things considered" was never a well-defined target? And is Dana's state better described as akratic belief, or as a first-order belief plus a second-order judgment that is itself the thing most in need of revision?

Three short items to close. Which single sub-question is your number most sensitive to; what argument or discovery would move it furthest; and which term are you least confident in. For context, my credence in (P) is about 0.7 and my adviser's about 0.3, and after arguing it twice we still cannot locate the disagreement — so tell me which term or terms most plausibly account for the gap, and whether that gap looks substantive or like a dispute over what "rational, all things considered" is tracking.

End with your final credence, to two decimal places, on its own last line in the form \answer{0.xx}.
