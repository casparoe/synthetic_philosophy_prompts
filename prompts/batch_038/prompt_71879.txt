Half the room treated the Project Prakash result — congenitally blind kids whose cataracts were removed, then tested on matching an object they'd just felt to one they were now looking at, near-chance at first but picking up the mapping startlingly fast over the next few days — as finally answering a three-century-old question with actual data. The other half said that experiment isn't about Molyneux's question at all, that it's obviously testing something else, and then couldn't agree among themselves about what the real question is or what would settle it. My own sense is that the two halves arent arguing about the same thing and that "did the newly sighted person see the cube?" is doing double or triple duty, but I can't get the ambiguity into focus.

My working hypotheses, none of which I'm confident about: (a) it's a fight about concept individuation — whether the visual representation has to be the very same concept as the tactual one for a yes answer to count, and whether a matching task already presupposes the cross-modal identity it was supposed to be testing; (b) the empirically-minded people are asking about the architecture of shape representation (is there one amodal shape code, or two modality-specific ones plus learned links?) while the armchair people are asking whether a concept of cubicity could be acquired by touch alone and then applied to sight, and neither side notices the switch; (c) there's a hidden dispute about what counts as data, since Molyneux stipulated an adult with a lifetime of tactual knowledge and asked about the moment "before he touched them," whereas the real subjects are children with years of visual deprivation, poor acuity, and total freedom to look around and move; (d) the verb is slipery — discriminating two objects by sight isn't the same as correctly saying which one is the cube, and one might succeed where the other fails.
