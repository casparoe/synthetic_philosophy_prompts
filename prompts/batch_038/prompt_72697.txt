**The step-out test: does My Lai settle Browning vs. Goldhagen?**

Everyone narrates the Browning/Goldhagen argument as situation versus culture. I think that misdescribes what actually divides them, and the reason is that both sides accept the same load-bearing fact about Jozefow.

Here's the fact. In July 1968 Major Trapp, commanding Reserve Police Battalion 101, told his men what the job was — round up the town's Jews, shoot the ones who couldn't march — and offered anyone who felt unable to do it the chance to step out and take other duties instead. Fewer than a dozen of roughly five hundred men took him up on it. Browning explains that with conformity, peer pressure, the shame of leaving your mates the dirty work, deference to authority. Goldhagen's response, roughly: if exit was cheap and almost nobody exited, external pressure can't be the mechanism — the mechanism has to be belief, specifically the eliminationist antisemitism he argues was a widespread German cultural-cognitive model. Browning also records one man who demanded release, got it, and was promoted afterwards, which cuts the same way.

So the premise both sides share is that refusal was possible at low cost. That's what makes Jozefow the pivotal case rather than one case among many, and once you see it the disagreement shrinks: both accounts are really about internalized pressure, and they differ mainly over whether that pressure is generic (any cohesive group produces it) or content-specific (you need a particular model of the victim for it to bite). Browning's mechanism should work in any battalion. Goldhagen's needs a specific cultural input.

That gives you a test. Find an episode with the same structure — cheap exit, near-total compliance — but a different cultural input, and see whose account the outcome favors.

My Lai, 16 March 1968, is the obvious candidate. Charlie Company, 1st Battalion, 20th Infantry, had been in country three months without ever making contact with the enemy and had taken 28 casualties to mines and booby traps — exactly the frustration-and-brutalization ingredient Browning lists. The hamlet was believed to hold the VC 48th Local Force Battalion, which wasn't there; the area was treated as free-fire and the operation ran on a body-count metric. Between 347 and 504 unarmed civilians were killed. Calley did a lot of the shooting himself and had to push men to fire; several declined and, so far as I can tell, nothing was done to them for declining. Hugh Thompson landed his helicopter between soldiers and a group of villagers, threatened to fire on Americans, lifted people out, and reported it up the chain. Ron Ridenhour, hearing about it from men in the brigade, wrote to the White House and Congress a year later. Twenty-six soldiers were charged, one was convicted, and he served three and a half years under house arrest.

Now run Goldhagen's inference on that. Exit was cheap, most didn't exit, therefore belief did the work — and the belief is available: the slur, the dehumanization, the body count. My Lai looks like a replication of Jozefow with the cultural variable swapped, and the inference survives the swap. Which is bad news for Browning.

Except it isn't, and here's the twist I want to build the piece around. My Lai was an outlier that got prosecuted and covered up; the killing in the East was policy that ran for years. So the same inference that "explains" My Lai cannot also explain the difference in scale and duration — and that difference is precisely what a theory of perpetrator behavior is supposed to explain. What varied, I want to argue, is neither culture nor situation but routinization: whether the dehumanization is embedded in standing practice so that killing requires no fresh decision, or improvised locally so that it does. Herbert Kelman's authorization / routinization / dehumanization triad gets you to that place, but it gets you there situationally, which is why I worry my position collapses back into Browning.

Tentative thesis: Browning and Goldhagen may both be right about Battalion 101 and both wrong about what generalizes, because the case they fought over was selected for having cheap exit — and cheap-exit cases are exactly the ones where the situation/culture distinction loses traction.
