I am preparing a chapter on territorial rights for a graduate seminar, and a colleague's argument has backed me into a corner I cannot talk my way out of. Since the real cases carry too much baggage for a clean test, he built the argument around a hypothetical state; I will give you the facts he stipulates, because several of them matter to the reasoning.

**The case.** The Republic of Valtre contains Estmark, a coastal region whose residents speak Estish, a language used by 62 percent of them and by 3 percent of Valtre's population as a whole. Estmark has had its own parliament since 1979, with competence over education, health, and policing. Its borders were fixed in 1848 by Valtre's interior ministry for cadastral convenience; those borders include the Karn Valley, whose 40,000 residents speak a third language and who elect their own valley assembly with statutory power to call local referendums, and they exclude two Estish-speaking mill towns that lie just across the river in a neighboring province. Estmark produces 21 percent of Valtre's GDP and contributes more to the central treasury than it draws back; 41 percent of its residents were born elsewhere in Valtre or have a parent who was. Last year its parliament put a single-sentence question to the electorate — should Estmark become an independent state — and 54 percent voted yes on 78 percent turnout. Its capital, Marren, which holds 38 percent of Estmark's population and 44 percent of its tax base, voted 67 percent no. The Karn Valley assembly then resolved that it would seek to remain in Valtre, adding that its own assembly satisfies every institutional criterion Estmark has invoked for itself.

**The argument.** (1) If a territorially concentrated population, acting through its own elected assembly, votes by a clear majority on a clearly worded question to become an independent state, that population acquires a right to secede. (2) The right is primary: it does not depend on the parent state's having done any injustice, since conditioning self-determination on injustice would withhold it from precisely those groups that are best treated. (3) The demos entitled to vote is the population of the territory whose assembly conducted the vote; no other way of identifying it avoids begging the question. (4) Estmark satisfies (1) through (3). (5) So Estmark has a right to secede from Valtre. (6) By (3), the criterion likewise identifies a demos wherever an elected assembly exists, so it identifies one for the Karn Valley and one for Marren. (7) By (1) and (2), nothing further bars them: neither must show injustice, and neither needs Valtre's or Estmark's consent. (8) So on (1) through (3) the Karn Valley may leave an independent Estmark, Marren may leave either, any district of Marren may leave Marren, and any affluent district of any city in Valtre may leave Valtre, since (1) sets no floor on size and no bar on nesting. (9) A principle that dissolves every state into its neighborhoods, and that lets the wealthiest district of a country exit its obligations to the rest of it, cannot be a principle of self-determination. (10) Therefore (1) is false: a plebiscitary majority does not by itself create a right to secede.

My colleague says (10) follows and that I should accept it. I think something has gone wrong, but I genuinely cannot tell whether one of the steps fails or whether all of them hold and I simply have to live with (10). Please diagnose it: name the step you reject, explain what made it look acceptable when you first read it, and price the repair. In particular I want to know whether any criterion for identifying the demos can avoid being a redescription of the borders Valtre's interior ministry happened to draw in 1848, and whether such a criterion can explain what distinguishes the Karn Valley from Estmark other than that it is smaller. Tell me also what the theorist who makes secession conditional on injustice pays, given the charge in (2), and what is paid by the procedural view — that a clear vote obliges the other parties to negotiate rather than authorizing departure — in the case where negotiations offer Estmark devolution while Marren and the Karn Valley refuse to go. Finally, say whether (9) states one objection or two, and whether the complaint about fragmentation survives if the complaint about wealthy exit does not.

I would rather preserve the thought in (4), that 54 percent of a people answering a clear question is not morally inert, than accept (10). Tell me what that costs me. Please write your answer in American English.
