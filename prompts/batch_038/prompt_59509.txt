I'm a second-year PhD student TA-ing an upper-level philosophy of mind course; our unit on minds and machines closes next week and the instructor has asked me to draft one practice essay question for the final. Please take on the role of a philosopher of mind who also has real command of mathematical logic, and who knows both Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach and the Lucas–Penrose debate closely. Think carefully before drafting: open with a short brainstorming passage in which you list three or four candidate cruxes and reject the ones a well-prepared student could get past by recitation alone.

Then produce the following.

1. One essay question, answerable in 45 minutes and worth 25 marks, built around the MU puzzle and Hofstadter's idea of jumping out of the system. It must not be answerable by reproducing the invariant argument (that the number of I's in a string derived from MI is never a multiple of three) or by restating the Gödel-sentence argument against mechanism. The pressure point I want is this: in the MIU case the meta-level insight is itself fully mechanizable, since derivability from MI is decidable, so the puzzle appears to undercut the very move it is standardly used to illustrate. The question should force candidates to say what jumping out of the system would have to amount to in order to support a conclusion like Lucas's or Penrose's; whether the gap between MIU and arithmetic is a difference in kind (soundness assumptions, the fact that consistency is not recursively detectable) or only in degree; and how to answer Hofstadter's own point that a machine can be built to jump out of a system too, since that is simply a richer system at a higher level.

2. A model answer of roughly 700 words that commits to a definite position and argues for it, rather than surveying the terrain.

3. A marking scheme out of 25: five must-hit points with marks allocated to each, a one-line note on the most common way candidates botch each point, and an explicit statement of what separates a first-class answer from a competent but unremarkable one.
