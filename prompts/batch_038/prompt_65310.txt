I teach a first-year graduate seminar in decision theory, cross-listed with the philosophy of economics program, and I am writing the long question for our three-hour final. The last three weeks have been on Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory and the arguments for and against it, and I am unhappy with the questions I have used in the past, because a student who has read the encyclopedia entry can answer them without ever touching a number. I want a question in which the arithmetic is the philosophy: the students should have to compute, and the computations should force the conceptual points out of them rather than the other way around. Please write the question as it will appear on the paper, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

Here is the case I have built, which I would like you to use. Feel free to polish the prose, but keep the probabilities, payoffs, utility assumption, and risk function exactly as given. A coastal transmission authority must choose between a standard reinforcement program and an experimental modular barrier program before a forecast storm season. The state space is partitioned into three events by how the storm track develops: E1, probability 0.89, in which the system passes north of the estuary; E2, probability 0.10, a moderate direct hit; E3, probability 0.01, an extreme direct hit. Benefits are measured in millions of dollars of avoided damage and utility is linear in dollars, so that the three possible utility levels are 0, 10, and 50, with 0 the worst outcome in the problem. In Problem 1, the standard program yields 10 on every event (it includes a permanent capacity upgrade that pays off whatever the storm does), while the experimental program yields 10 on E1, 50 on E2 (it also shields the adjacent industrial park), and 0 on E3, because in the extreme tail its retrofit anchors compromise the existing seawall. Problem 2 concerns a second, smaller facility, a pumping station on high ground that is threatened only by the direct-hit tracks and whose payoffs are stipulated as follows: the standard program yields 10 on E2 and E3 and 0 on E1; the experimental program yields 50 on E2 and 0 on E1 and E3. The authority's risk function is r(p) = p^3. The question should restate the risk-weighted functional for the students, with outcomes ordered worst to best as x1 < x2 < ... < xn, in the form REU = u(x1) + the sum over i from 2 to n of r(P(X ≥ xi)) times [u(xi) − u(x(i−1))], because I want the convention about cumulative rather than outcome-wise probabilities fixed on the page instead of left to memory.

The question should have four parts, worth 30 marks in total, to be answered in roughly 90 minutes with a calculator and no notes. Part (a): compute expected utility and risk-weighted expected utility for all four acts and state the resulting preference pattern. Part (b): that pattern cannot be represented by any expected utility maximizer, however concave her utility function; students must show why, and must then explain where the risk aversion lives in a rank-dependent model as opposed to a curvature-of-utility model. This is the crux of the question, because half my class still believes a sufficiently concave utility function could do the same work. Part (c): the milder weighting function r(p) = p^2 does not generate the authority's pattern, and neither does a sufficiently steep one such as r(p) = p^20; students should explain both failures, noting that they are failures of different halves of the pattern for different reasons, and determine the full range of exponents k for which r(p) = p^k reproduces it. Part (d): suppose the authority faces the Problem 1 choice in each of two independent years, and must either commit now to a two-year program or decide afresh each year. Students should derive the distribution of the two-year totals for the experimental program, compute the risk-weighted value of the two-year package, compare it with twice the value of the one-year act, and then say what happens as the number of years grows, and whether the resulting long-run objection is fatal to the theory or only to a particular way of using it. The question should close by asking which of Savage's axioms the authority's pattern violates and what the theory manages to keep.

For deliverables: the printed question with instructions and mark allocations; a model answer of about 900 to 1,100 words showing the computations in full; and a marking scheme stating, part by part, which specific moves earn marks and which errors cost them. Be explicit about what separates an A from a B — for instance, whether the student weights each outcome by its own probability instead of by the cumulative probability of doing at least as well, whether the baseline term is handled correctly, whether the violated axiom is named and the retained structure characterized rather than merely gestured at, whether the student can explain the non-additivity of the risk-weighted functional across independent gambles rather than just remarking on it, and whether the long-run discussion distinguishes evaluating the aggregate package from evaluating a sequence of separate choices made with information in hand. List the recurring mistakes you expect, each with the marks it should cost. Please include no citations, bibliography, or reading list in either the question or the model answer, and do not allocate marks for attributing views to particular authors: the numbers and the reasoning are what I am grading.
