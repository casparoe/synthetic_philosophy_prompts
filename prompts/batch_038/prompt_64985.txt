I sit on the ethics committee of a movement-disorders clinic, and this term we have been asked to rewrite the informed-consent packet for subthalamic deep brain stimulation. The request was triggered by a specific case: a patient of ours, roughly two years post-implantation, was charged with a substantial fraud offence. His family has written to the committee arguing that the person who committed it was not the person they knew, and that the change was produced by the therapy rather than by him. Two facts complicate things. The system we implant allows the patient to adjust stimulation amplitude within a range his neurologist programmed, and he did adjust it upward on his own initiative, months before the offences began, after a nurse had mentioned in passing that some patients report changes in impulse control. And a second patient in our programme has an almost identical stimulation history — same target, same upward adjustments within the same range, same warning — but differs only in that he had, before implantation, explicitly said he wanted the therapy even if it changed his appetites, and had written that down.

The committee has split. One camp says both patients remain fully responsible for what they do under stimulation, and that the packet should say so plainly, because nothing about the causal history of a disposition removes it from the space of reasons for which a person acts. The other camp says the packet would be actively misleading if it implied this, because in both patients the springs of action were installed by a process the patients did not author, and the earlier decision to turn the amplitude up cannot launder that.

Before I have to draft language, I want to work through the dispute properly, and I have been rehearsing it as an argument between two people. Please write that argument as a dialogue.

Specifications:

- The two speakers are the members of our committee who have been co-drafting the packet: a psychiatrist with substantial training in moral philosophy, who holds the first view, and a philosopher of action, who holds the second. Both are articulate, well-read, and arguing in good faith the evening before the committee votes. Give them names and keep them in the room; the clinical setting may be used but should not become a medical drama.
- The incompatibilist should be a sourcehood theorist, not someone resting the case on the absence of alternative possibilities, and the compatibilist should be a reasons-responsiveness or quality-of-will theorist, not someone who simply asserts that determinism is irrelevant. Both should be able to state the other's position, at some point, in wording the other explicitly accepts.
- They must actually engage the stipulated facts rather than retreat to generic manipulation scenarios. In particular they should fight over three things: whether the patient's own upward adjustment of the amplitude, made after a warning, restores or fails to restore responsibility, and what the incompatibilist has to say about the case in which the tracing reply looks strongest; whether the prior written endorsement in the matched patient makes any difference at all, and if it does not, what that shows about which features of a history are doing the work; and the family's actual claim, which is not that the patient lacked control but that the agent who acted was not the same person they knew — each speaker should say whether that framing helps their side or embarrasses it.
- Neither should convert, and neither should be allowed to win by caricature. Have them end by jointly identifying what the disagreement really turns on, and by each naming something that would count against their own view, and then have each say, in one or two sentences, what they think the consent packet ought to tell a prospective patient about later conduct under stimulation.
- Argue about the patient, not about the literature: no name-dropping, and no reliance on the reader's recall of famous cases. Roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words.
