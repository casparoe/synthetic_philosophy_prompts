I serve on a hospital's technology-assessment committee, and I need to answer an argument that ended a discussion last week before it had properly begun. The committee has fifteen members: two of us work with the device in question daily, a third has read the trial data closely, and the remaining twelve have no contact with the evidence beyond what they hear in the room, where they reliably fall in behind whoever speaks first. The question before us was whether to recommend the device for a narrow indication; there were three candidate recommendations on the table rather than two, and alongside them a separate proposal to pool each member's numerical estimate of a complication rate and act on the pooled figure. When I suggested we might do better simply to defer to the members who know the literature, the chair waved the objection away with the old proverb 'two heads are better than one' and added that this was, after all, what Condorcet had proved.

I want to come back at the next meeting with something better than a hunch that he is misusing a theorem. So please lay out the distinct precise claims that proverb could be standing in for, running from the ones that are true but empty, through the ones that are substantive and defensible, to the ones that are simply false. For each, state what it requires: how many alternatives the choice may have, how competent the individual members must be and on what question, what independence amounts to and how much of it is needed, whether the claim concerns a large group in the limit or a group of a specific finite size like ours, and whether the claim is about a majority verdict, about a pooled numerical estimate, or about a choice between two individual deciders. My suspicion is that a single sentence is being made to cover all three of those at once, and that the differences are exactly what matters here.

Then take a position rather than leaving me the menu. Tell me which reading, if any, the chair was entitled to given the panel as I have described it; whether Condorcet's result reaches a vote among three options; whether it reaches the pooling of our complication-rate estimates at all, or whether that is a different mechanism with different conditions; and where the proverb is doing rhetorical work that no theorem can underwrite. I would rather have your definite judgment with reasons behind it than a balanced survey. Where the answer genuinely turns on an empirical fact about committees that cannot be settled from the armchair, say so and name the fact it turns on — but do not retreat into the complexity of the issue where the issue is not actually complex.
