I'm helping draft the referendum procedures for a regional charter convention, and I've found that the more closely I look at how existing systems are built, the less I can tell what they commit us to. Three design features in particular seem to me to embody substantive claims that nobody states out loud, and I'd like them extracted.

First. In Switzerland a popular initiative may only amend the federal constitution; it cannot enact an ordinary statute. So detailed policy — whether minarets may be built at all, what ratio of executive pay to average worker pay a listed company may have — ends up written into constitutional text. Yet that text is amended whenever a simple majority of voters and a simple majority of the cantonal votes agree, with no supermajority and no turnout threshold. What is a constitution on this arrangement? Is it a higher law that entrenchs certain matters against ordinary politics, and if so, what entrenches it against the same electorate that ordinary politics answers to? Or is the constitution here functioning as something else — an ordinary statute that has been relabeled — in which case what work is the label doing?

Second. The Swiss executive campaigns. Before every federal vote, each voter is sent an official booklet containing the full text of the proposal, the Federal Council's recommendation together with its reasons, and a statement from the committee that launched the measure. The government routinely recommends rejection; if the electorate adopts the measure anyway, the same government must draft the implementing legislation. And when parliament dislikes an initiative it may set a direct counterproposal on the same ballot. Voters may approve both texts, which normally exclude one another, whereupon a third question asks which of the two should prevail if both are accepted. The consequences are strange: a measure can win a majority of yes votes and still not be enacted, because the third question resolved against it; a measure can win the popular vote nationwide and still fail, because the cantonal majority went the other way.

Third. California has no third question. Where two measures approved at the same election conflict, the conflicting provisions of whichever measure received the greater number of affirmative votes simply prevail. Conflict is settled by counting rather than by asking.

What I want from you:

(a) Reconstruct the implicit theory behind each feature — of what a constitution is, of the executive's standing in public argument, and above all of what "the decision of the people" is as a thing that can be identified, located, and acted upon.

(b) The booklet is the hardest. What view of the state permits, indeed requires, an administration to argue one side of a question it will then have to execute on the other terms? Does that treat the government as one partisan among others in the debate, or as an authority whose opinion carries special weight, and can a single practice do both?

(c) Does the third question presuppose that the popular verdict is an aggregate of individual preferences whose aggregation can fail to yield one verdict unless the ballot itself supplies a tie-breaking rule — that is, that the will of the people is an artifact of ballot design rather than something the ballot merely records? Does California's counting rule instead presuppose that the size of a majority measures something normatively real, which a majority of a smaller total does not?

(d) Tell me which of these commitments can be underwritten by one theory and which cannot. The same polity constitutionalizes ordinary policy, lets its executive campaign against a proposal and then implement it, and permits a proposal to be approved yet not adopted; a comparable system resolves the same conflict arithmetically.

(e) Separate what each feature strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. I suspect the double majority, for instance, is licensable by several incompatible theories; say which features are genuinely underdetermined in this way, and what would decide between the candidates.

(f) Finally, suppose we adopted the view that the electorate's verdict is the judgment of a deliberating body rather than a sum of private preferences. Which of these mechanisms could survive that substitution and which could not? Be concrete about the booklet, the counterproposal and third question, the double majority, and the counting rule.
