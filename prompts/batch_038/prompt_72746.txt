In an idealized debate setup, let the verification depth of a claim be the number of steps of expert reasoning a judge would have to carry out to check it herself. Suppose the judge is an ordinary human with unbounded patience and unbounded time, and the two debaters are far more capable than she is, adversarial toward each other, and rewarded only for winning. One thesis about this setup — call it depth-independence — says that for every finite depth, honest play remains an equilibrium: however deep the claim in dispute, the debaters can be compelled to break it into pieces shallow enough for the judge to check, and the side defending the truth always has a case at least as easy to mount as the side defending a falsehood. Fix the question class as those with determinate, knowable answers — for instance, whether a given hundred-thousand-line program contains an exploitable flaw — so that what is at issue is amplification of the judge's reach, not vagueness in the question.

Give me your credence that depth-independence is true, together with the decomposition that produces it. I want the sub-questions you are actually combining: whether a set of individually verified claims can still mislead a judge even when every one of them is true; whether unlimited time on the judge's side converts into unlimited reliable checking, or merely into unlimited exposure to well-formed sophistry; whether any cost asymmetry exists between defending a truth and defending a falsehood at high depth; and whatever other terms you think belong. For each, a number and a sentence or two of justification, then your combination rule and the resulting credence. Finally, say which term your number is most sensitive to, what argument or empirical result would move it furthest, and — if your credence is below 0.9 — roughly where you think the depth cutoff lies and what kind of claim sits just past it.
