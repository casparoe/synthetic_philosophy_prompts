So I help run a tiny philosophy podcast out of my department — two of us, we record in a seminar room with a mic that is absolutly terrible, and we mostly invite people whose work we're already reading for seminars anyway. Next month's guest is a metaphysician working on laws of nature. She's a Humean, roughly in the Lewis best-system tradition, but her version is idiosyncratic enough that I can't just crib the standard set of objections off the internet, and my cohost's plan is to ask "but isn't that kind of deflationary?" and then nod for twenty minutes. I'd like to do better.

Her view, as far as I can tell from the manuscript she sent over: instead of trading off simplicity against strength over systems that axiomatize the mosaic of local matters of fact, she ranks candidate systems by how much of the mosaic a fixed idealized reasoner can reconstruct from the axioms within a bounded number of inference steps. She calls the optimal trade-off curve the "reconstruction frontier," and the systems sitting on it are the ones that state the laws. Two commitments I especially want to press: (a) she thinks this makes lawhood relative to a reasoner's capacities without making it mind-dependent in any objectionable sense, because the frontier allegedly converges across all bounded reasoners above some competence threshold; and (b) she claims her version is immune to the undermining worry — the old complaint that Humean laws can assign high probability to histories whose own best system says something different — on the grounds that undermining worlds simply aren't reconstructible from the axioms in the relevant way.

Write me eight to ten interview questions, each with a one-or-two-line follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. Things I want covered, roughly one question apiece: the convergence claim and what would count as its failing (what if a reasoner with much bigger working memory gets a visibly different frontier — does she bite the bullet that the laws differ, or posit a canonical reasoner, and if so on what non-arbitrary grounds?); whether specifying an "idealized reasoner with bounded inference steps" smuggles in nomic or lawlike notions, so that she's answered the circularity complaint against the best-system account only to reintroduce it one level up; what her view says about lawhood in a universe with no cognizers in it, including the first few hundred million years of ours; and a question that lets her take on the governing/explanation objection in her own terms and then presses whether "unification for a bounded reasoner" is a claim about the world or a claim about us. I'd also like one on whether the frontier can be flat enough that lawhood comes out vague, and whether she welcomes that or thinks it's a cost; one on whether her account says anything different from vanilla Lewisian best-system about actual cases — ideally forcing her to name one candidate law that one account admits and the other doesn't, including ceteris paribus laws in the special sciences; and a closing question she genuinely cannot answer by restating the view. For the follow-ups, tell me what reply you're anticipating so I can see why the follow-up bites. Keep each question to something she could actually answer in two or three minutes of speech rather than a five-part essay prompt, don't strawman her — I want questions she'd agree are fair — and skip the generic warmup stuff; if you give me an opener make it substantive but low-stakes, something that gets her stating the frontier idea without a lecture. Also nothing that depends on her having read some particular paper, since I'll be asking these on wether or not I've got the citations right.
