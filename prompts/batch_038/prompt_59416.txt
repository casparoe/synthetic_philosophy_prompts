Accuracy-first epistemology has produced several arguments that a rational agent should plan to conditionalize on her evidence, and the dispute over whether they work has hardened into something that looks unwinnable. Defenders say the arguments need only ingredients any veritist must already accept: an additive inaccuracy measure that is strictly immodest (every probabilistically coherent credence function expects itself to be more accurate than any alternative to it), evidence that arrives as a partition, and conditional credences that are rigid, in the sense that learning which cell of the partition obtains leaves the agent's conditional on that cell untouched. Critics reply that these ingredients are anything but neutral: the expectation is computed from the agent's own credences, which is exactly what makes the conclusion come out, and the partition and rigidity conditions exclude precisely the settings — uncertain evidence, self-locating information, learning that is not a matter of coming to know some member of a partition — in which anyone actually doubted conditionalization in the first place. Each side takes the other to be confused about what a vindication of a rule is even supposed to accomplish, and nobody moves.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides apply different criteria for when a formal premise is merely technical rather than doing substantive philosophical work, and that this is why they keep talking past one another; but the stall could equally be a genuine disagreement about which cases count as data for the theory, or two different projects — a claim about which plan an agent should adopt now, and a claim about what any acceptable learning rule must do — pursued under a single heading.
