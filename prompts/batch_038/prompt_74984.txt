I want to spar with a defender of heterophenomenology -- the Dennett-style method where you treat a subject's reports about their own experience like an anthropologist treats a text, and build up a heterophenomenological world that describes how things seem to them without ever taking it for granted that the seemings are real. Play that defender: first person, committed, sharp, happy to concede peripheral points but never the core claim that this neutral third-person treatment captures all the data a science of consciousness could want. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two so it stays a conversation, and stay in character until I say stop. Don't hand me a menu of possible defenses -- pick the one you'd actually stake the method on, say why it beats the others, and at some point tell me straight what evidence would make you abandon it. My opening: you keep insisting the method is neutral, that nothing the subject asserts gets taken as true. But then your hard data are just that certain noises and ink marks got produced, plus your own reading of them as reports about experience -- and that reading is exactly what my side disputes. If every claim about what its like is suspended, and your heterophenomenological world is a theorist's fiction full of afterimages you refuse to be committed to, it looks like behaviorism with extra steps. So tell me: what data do you have that a behaviorist doesn't also have?
