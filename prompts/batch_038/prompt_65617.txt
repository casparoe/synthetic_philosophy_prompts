I'm writing the "how we represent uncertainty" section of a proposal for a decision-support tool used by a county public-health board. The situation is this: the evidence is thin and conflicting (two small studies pointing different ways, an expert panel that splits down the middle, historical data that's arguably not transferable), the board has to act anyway, and they've told us bluntly that they hate being handed a single number that pretends to more precision than anybody has. My instinct is to go with imprecise probabilities — credal sets, or lower and upper previsions in Walley's sense — but my committee wants the framework choice defended against the alternatives, and I'd rather do that honestly than cheerlead for my own view.

So: write me the desiderata. What does a framework for representing evidentially underdetermined uncertainty have to deliver to count as adequate *for this job*? Give me the phenomena it must capture, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the results it has to stay consistent with — with the motivating case or consideration behind each item, and a mark on each saying whether it's a genuine requirement or a prejudice of one camp. The ones I especially want pinned down:

- whether there are inputs where a precise number would be a lie, and what exactly that claim amounts to — I want a worked case, not a restatement of the intuition
- what the framework owes you on updating, including whether the set is allowed to get *wider* on new evidence, and whether an agent can know in advance that she's about to dilate (reflection-principle territory)
- whether it has to produce a determinate recommendation, and if not, what it owes the board instead when they ask what to actually do on Tuesday
- what decision rule it's commited to (maximin, E-admissibility, gamma-maximin, something else) and which classic constraints on decision rules — sure-thing, no dynamic money pump, no getting trapped by a sequence of individually reasonable bets — that rule buys and which it sells
- whether an accuracy-dominance style argument, run over sets of credence functions rather than single ones, cuts for or against wide sets
- the reduction worry: what stops a precise second-order probability distribution over candidate chances from doing all the same work with less machinery? If the honest answer is that nothing does, say so and say what's left over

Then flag any subset of these that you think can't be jointly satisfied — I suspect there's one, and I'd much rather my committee hear it from me than discover it in review. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most contenders, possibly including mine. Two-sentence verdicts at the end on precise Bayesianism, credal sets, and second-order probabilities, no more than that. No citations or reading list — I've got the lit, I just need the spec.
