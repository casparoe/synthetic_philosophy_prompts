Their standard is the net impression a reasonable reader is left with, not whether the sentence is false, and noone on my team has any way to argue about a case except vibes. Can you write a decision procedure a copywriter with no philosophy background could run in 15 minutes on a draft line: the checks in order, what counts as passing each one, and then the fix (reword, add a qualifier, or kill the claim)?

Ground it in whatever the Gricean machinery actually gives you — the maxims, said vs. implicated, scalar implicature, generalized vs. particularized, cancellability — but be straight with me about which of those distinctions survive contact with a deadline and which you're quietly dropping. I especially want the tension handled honestly: the textbook fix is to cancel the implicature with an explicit qualifier, and that's exactly what makes the copy stop selling, so the procedure needs to rank fixes by how much of the unwanted implicature actually survives each one.

Run it on three examples, one of which should come out cleared even though I'd want it flagged, and mark every place where the procedure stops and hands the question to a human being. Then tell me where it's most likely to misfire, and what would change your mind about the whole framing — e.g. if a relevance-theory account or just leaning on the legal net-impression standard directly would do better here than Grice.
