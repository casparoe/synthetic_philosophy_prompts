Last night a friend and I spent four hours on a single question — whether the Enlightenment is better understood as the wellspring of modern emancipation or as one of the wellspring's of modern domination — and we got nowhere, chiefly because I now suspect we were arguing about two different books. He had read a work of criticism whose argument he claims to have reproduced faithfully; I had read no such work. Before we resume, I would like to know whether his reproduction holds up.

Here is his synopsis, in his words and in his order:

"Their argument is that Enlightenment reason was never neutral. The same thinkers who were writing about universal rights and cosmopolitan hospitality were writing treatises sorting human beings into ranked varieties — Kant is their favorite example — so universalism was from the outset a way of announcing that we count and you do not. Progress and civilization were not descriptions but instruments: they supplied the vocabulary in which conquest could be represented as a favor conferred. And the deeper point is that you cannot peel the emancipatory content away from the oppressive content, because the oppression is not an accident that befell a pure idea; it is generated by the idea's own form, since positing one universal human nature is precisely what licenses defining some people as nearer to animals. Hence their conclusion: appeals to universal reason ought to be given up, because every such appeal reinstates the hierarchy it claims to transcend."

He is well read and he is not inventing anything, which is why I want a careful audit rather than a rebuttal. My own move in the argument was to say that the whole thing collapses on contact: if these critics want to condemn enslavement, classification, and empire as wrong at all, they need exactly the universal norms they are supposedly abandoning, so the position is either self-refuting or secretly universalist. He replied that I had flattened a dialectical argument into a slogan. I do not know which of us is right, and I would rather know that than win.

Please audit the synopsis rather than adjudicate our quarrel. Go through it claim by claim and tell me which parts a serious critic of the Enlightenment would recognize as their own, which parts are defensible popular simplifications that a specialist would rephrase, and which parts are misstatements of what such critics actually hold. Two transitions interest me most: whether the slide from "Enlightenment authors also produced racial classification" to "the form of universalism itself generates exclusion" is a step these critics characteristically take or one imported into their argument, and whether abandoning universal norms is a conclusion they reach or a conclusion laid on them by their opponents. Then tell me what survives of my objection once the synopsis is corrected: what would have to be true for a self-refutation charge to bite, what an immanent critique is and how it differs from a critique launched from outside the target's own standards, and whether a critic arguing immanently is vulnerable to that charge at all. If the objection has real force against some versions of this critique and none against others, say which versions and what turns on the difference. Finally, tell me what I should concede to my friend if his charge about flattening is fair.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — a verdict on the synopsis as a whole — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and please do not restate my question back to me.
