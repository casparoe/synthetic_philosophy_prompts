I'm teaching a unit on the self and I've paired Vonnegut's Slaughterhouse-Five with Dennett's center-of-narrative-gravity picture — the self isn't a thing in the skull, it's an abstractum, the way a center of gravity is: you posit it because it earns its keep in prediction. I want a reading of Billy Pilgrim against that idea, and two specific pressure points.

One: Billy doesn't spin his own story, he imports it. He gets it from Kilgore Trout's pulp novels and then from the Tralfamadorians, whose whole cosmology looks like a Trout plot with better production values. Call that borrowed gravity — a self whose narrative is authored somewhere else — and tell me whether Dennett's account has any resources for distinguishing borrowed from homegrown, or whether for him it makes no difference and that's a feature not a bug.

Two: the Tralfamadorian view is anti-narrative in content. Nothing flows, nobody really dies, so it goes. That reads less like an instance of narrativity than a rejection of it, which seems to put Billy on the wrong side of the theory. So is the novel handing Dennett a limit case or an actual counterexample? Be straight with me about where the book fights the reading — I don't want Billy flattened into an illustration. Don't quote the novel, we'll have the text in the room.
