I am a retired physical chemist; I spent some forty years working on reaction kinetics and have come to philosophy late and without formal training. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind who works on the major theories of consciousness and is willing to be patient with an outsider. Think carefully before answering, and do not simply tell me the passage is wrong in general terms.

In a widely sold popular book on consciousness that I picked up, the author disposes of the "higher-order" family of theories in a single paragraph. It reads:

"Higher-order theories hold that a state of mind is conscious just when the creature has a second mental state aimed at it — an inner sense, in effect an inward eye, that registers the first. Because that registering state is itself a mental state, and on this account a mental state is conscious only when something registers it, the theory needs a third state to make the second conscious, and a fourth to make the third, and so on without end. Its defenders have never found a way to stop the slide, which is why the family is now mostly of historical interest. What the view really amounts to is identifying consciousness with introspection: to be in pain consciously is to notice that you are in pain. That is also why it cannot accommodate the consciousness of dogs, cats, and human infants, who plainly lack the concepts required to think about their own thinking. The one genuine advance it made was to insist that a mental state can exist unconsciously, a point that earlier theories had refused."

Begin by setting out, in five or six lines, the distinctions a specialist would want in hand before evaluating that paragraph. Then go through the passage sentence by sentence and tell me which claims are accurate, which are fair simplifications, which are subtle misstatements, and which a proponent of the view would flatly deny. Please be especially concrete about the infinite-regress charge: as a scientist I am inclined to think that a regress is fatal and that the author is simply right, so tell me whether that instinct survives contact with the way the theory is actually formulated, and what the standard reply is. Note also whether the passage is describing one view or quietly running several distinct versions together.

Finally, assess the two conclusions the author draws — that the family is largely obsolete, and that it cannot allow consciousness to non-human animals and pre-linguistic infants. Do these follow from anything true in the passage, or only from the misstatements? And state what a corrected version of the view would actually commit one to on those two points.
