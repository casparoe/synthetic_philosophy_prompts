I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy working on formal epistemology, and next week I am presenting a section of my dissertation chapter at our department's work-in-progress group. The section concerns the scope of the Dutch book argument for probabilism, and I have hit a wall. My flatmate bets on horses and, over the weekend, made an offhand remark that has stuck with me: as he understands it, at a traditional bookmaker a punter can only ever buy — she can back a runner, but nobody will purchase that position back off her, and unless she goes to an exchange she has no way of laying anything. Meanwhile my advisor's standing line in seminar is that incoherence just *is* vulnerability to a sure loss, full stop. I suspect those two claims cannot both be true in the unqualified form he gives them, and I would like a carefully engineered case that drives the wedge, rather than an essay about the general dialectic.

So: construct a thought experiment to the following specifications. I want a single realistic scenario — a paragraph, no science fiction, ordinary sums of money — in which an agent's credences flagrantly violate the probability axioms over an exhaustive, pairwise exclusive set of three possibilities, summing to something like 0.80 with nothing assigned to any residual (there is no residual; the three possibilities exhaust the space), and in which nonetheless no Dutch book can be made against her. The immunity must come from one structural feature of the betting situation and nothing else: the only wagers she can ever be offered are ones she *buys*, at prices the counterparty sets at or just below her own valuations, and no one in this market will ever take the other side of a position she holds. Her incoherence should arise from a broadly sensible habit — a deliberate policy of never committing more than a fixed proportion of her confidence to any shortlist of outcomes, a policy formed after some past humiliation and here misapplied to a set that is in fact exhaustive — and she must state her numbers openly rather than conceal them. Constraints on the case: she is otherwise a perfectly ordinary expected-value maximizer with linear utility in money over stakes that are trivial relative to her wealth; no imprecise, set-valued, or non-additive credences; no non-classical logic, no non-measurable propositions, no infinities, no appeals to bounded computational resources; and the counterparty knows her credences exactly and may offer any finite package of bets at any positive prices she would accept. Let me say what the case is *for*, since that fixes what counts as cheating: I intend to argue that the theorem's conclusion is conditional on assumptions about market structure, so a scenario that secures immunity merely by making the bookie unable to approach her, by making her beliefs unknowable, or by stipulating that she refuses certain wagers for reasons outside her credences, is not a success — the immunity has to be generated by the acceptance rule together with the one-sided menu.

Please write the whole answer in British English. Alongside the scenario itself, I need the following, in this order. First, state her credence function and the class of bets available to her with enough precision that the rest can be checked. Second, prove the immunity in general — for an arbitrary finite package, arbitrary stakes, and arbitrary payoff vectors that a sell-only counterparty could offer — rather than verifying it on the obvious three-bet example; I want an argument I can put on a slide. Third, identify exactly which assumption of the standard theorem is doing the work and formulate it as an explicit condition (something in the neighbourhood of the two-sidedness of the bet quotient), so that I can see what my advisor's slogan is silently relying on. Fourth, restore that assumption — suppose a counterparty will buy bets *from* her at her own valuations — and derive the largest sure loss extractable as a function of her total deficiency from unity, showing that the two-sided book reinstates vulnerability in precisely that amount. Fifth, tell me whether the immunity survives the diachronic setting: if the counterparty can offer her bets both before and after she receives further information, does a Dutch strategy get a purchase on her, and if so, what additional market power is doing the work? Sixth, run the converse direction under the same restriction: can you build an agent whose credences are perfectly probabilistic and who is nevertheless guaranteed to lose money; if you cannot, say why not, and name the extra ingredient such a case would require. Finally, give me an honest closing note — which feature of your construction is genuinely load-bearing, what happens to the result if I tweak it (allow one single lay transaction, make her reserve of withheld confidence vary with the stakes, let the exhaustive set be merely nearly exhaustive), and the strongest reply available to a defender of the Dutch book argument. On that last point I particularly want to know whether the argument was ever about actual exploitability or only about dispositions to accept wagers, and whether that reading rescues the norm of probabilism or simply concedes the point I am trying to make.
