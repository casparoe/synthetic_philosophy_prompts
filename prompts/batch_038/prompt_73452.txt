Write me a glossy private-school prospectus — the kind of brochure a prep academy mails to prospective parents — for a school whose entire pupil body is made up of Turing's child-machines. Around 600 words, with the usual brochure furniture: Our Ethos, Curriculum, Pastoral Care, Assessment, Admissions Targets, and a short FAQ for Parents. Keep the register deadpan and unbroken: every difficulty gets reframed as a feature, nobody winks at the reader.

The jokes have to come from getting Turing's learning-machine proposals right, not from generic robot gags. So work in, as school policy: that the pupils arrive as child-programs to be educated rather than engineered, discipline run on rewards and punishments, the deliberate sprinkling of randomness through the timetable so no pupil settles into a repetitive loop, the boast that a teacher's judgment gets you there much faster than the slow grind of random mutation, the sense-organs provision in admissions, and the leaver's promise that an average interrogator won't do better than 70 per cent at spotting them after five minutes of questioning. Someone who's read the learning-machine section of Computing Machinery and Intelligence should tick these off one by one; someone who hasn't should still enjoy it as a parody of school marketing.

What you're satirising is the brochure voice and the habit of dressing up a training loop as an education — don't strawman Turing. And let one of his actual unresolved problems surface near the end still wearing the marketing smile: either the complaint that a discipline system which can only say good or bad teaches far too slowly, so teachers have to be let at the pupils properly, or the awkwardness of a school that claims to know its pupils' minds while conceding it can't see inside them. Don't resolve it. No citations and don't quote Turing — paraphrase everything.
