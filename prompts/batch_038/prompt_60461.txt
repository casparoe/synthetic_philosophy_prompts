I am shortlisted for an eleven-month postdoctoral fellowship at a centre for social data science, and the application asks for a one-page project statement to be read by a committee split roughly evenly between philosophers of social science and quantitative sociologists. Please write the statement. I will supply the references myself afterwards, so argue with positions and arguments rather than name-dropping, and do not invent citations, titles, dates, or empirical findings.

The half-formed idea I want turned into a project concerns a question that everyone in the online communities I have read for twenty years believes they already have an answer to: whether Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z are real kinds or a habit of journalism. The two standard answers — the realist who says a birth cohort shares a stable cluster of dispositions produced by a common historical upbringing, and the deflationist who says a birth-year band is an arbitrary slice through continuous demographic variation — both assume there is a determinate empirical fact about cohort effects that better data or better metaphysics could settle. My suspicion is that the assumption is false, and that this is precisely what has made the argument feel interminable.

Demographers have long recognised that age, period, and cohort are exactly collinear, since birth cohort is period minus age. Any observed temporal trend can accordingly be decomposed into indefinitely many combinations of a life-cycle effect, a historical-period effect, and a cohort effect, and the trend data alone never select among them; every published decomposition therefore carries an identifying assumption that did the real work. If that is right, the dispute about whether generations are real is not only a dispute about whether a clustering exists in the population. It is a dispute about which auxiliary assumption is licensed, and by what — which is a question the usual realist-versus-eliminativist framing cannot even pose.

The twist I find most interesting, and which I would like the statement to treat as a genuine possibility rather than a flourish: if a label comes to be applied to people by institutions and media, and people come to see themselves under it, the label may make its own identifying assumption true by changing the behaviour it classifies. A looping effect could then produce a real cohort effect that trend data would still be structurally unable to certify — which would be a strange place for a naturalist about social kinds to end up, and worth spelling out.

What I need from the statement:

- The question stated so that it has a determinate answer, and so that a sceptic can see in advance what would count against it.
- Why the existing framings leave it open, including the natural-kinds toolkit the philosophers on the committee will know well — homeostatic property clusters, interactive kinds, looping effects — without summarising that material back at them.
- The method, staged across the eleven months, saying what I would actually be doing: reconstructing the identification argument in a form a philosopher can argue with; articulating candidate sources of warrant for an identifying assumption (a specified mechanism, a dated historical shock, the self-application of the label) and the conditions under which each licenses a cohort claim; then testing those conditions against three or four familiar public claims of the usual sort — that political attitudes crystallise in early adulthood, that a cohort's baseline anxiety differs from its predecessors at the same age — using arguments rather than new statistics.
- What a successful result looks like separately to each half of the committee, and what would count as failure. I would count it as failure if the conditions end up so permissive that any birth-year band qualifies, or so strict that they collapse back into the deflationist's conclusion.
- An honest assessment of scope: what could make eleven months unrealistic, and what I would cut first.

Write it as a plan for work not yet done, not as a defence of a verdict I have already reached. Ideally a quantitative sociologist who considers this a settled methodological matter and a philosopher who considers it a settled metaphysical matter should each find something in it to object to.
