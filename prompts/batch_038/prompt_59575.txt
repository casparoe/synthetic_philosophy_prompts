I TA'd a poli-sci sequence on collective action last year and I cobbled this argument together for the final discussion section, mostly to annoy a guy in the seminar who kept saying the free rider problem is basically solved and nobody should lose sleep over it anymore. It was supposed to be a reductio of the whole framework. Reading it back over the break I think it proves way to much, and I can't tell whether the problem is the argument or my head.

Here's the thing, numbered so you can tell me exactly where it goes wrong. (1) In a public-goods situation, an agent who is rational and motivated only by their own payoff won't contribute, because their contributing doesn't change whether the good gets provided, while it does cost them something. (2) The standard fix is to monitor and sanction free riders, so that contributing becomes the payoff-maximizing move. (3) But monitoring and sanctioning are themselves costly, and the benefits of a well-run enforcement scheme — deterrence, a sustained supply of the good — fall on everyone in the group whether or not they paid to monitor. (4) So enforcement is itself a public good, and by (1) no one will supply it either. (5) The same reasoning runs one level up, to monitoring the monitors, and again above that: at every order the rational self-interested agent free rides, and there's no finite level where the chain stops. (6) Hence in any group of purely rational, purely self-interested agents, no cooperative scheme can get off the ground — not by direct contribution, and not by enforcement at any level. (7) And yet real communities do get these schemes going and hold them up for generations: irrigation associations dividing water in rotation, inshore fisheries, alpine grazing commons, village forest management, where the people involved are clearly not fools, saints, or confused about what serves them. (8) Therefore (1) is false: self-interest plus rationality is perfectly compatible with contributing, and the free-rider problem isn't a constraint on behavior at all, just an artifact of an unrealistic model.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost you? I'd rather keep (1) than (7), honestly, since giving up (1) looks like giving up the point of the whole framework, but I can't tell whether the rot is in the regress or in the last step. Two things I specifically want pressed on. First, does (6) actually follow from (1) through (5), or am I quietly re-describing "public good" as I climb the orders — I have a vague sense that higher-order enforcement isn't the same kind of good as the first-order one, but "the costs shrink as you go up" sounds like hand-waving when I say it out loud. Second, take the usual escape hatches one at a time — Olson-style selective incentives, repeated interaction and the threat of future punishment, internalized norms and guilt, and the small-group, face-to-face, graduated-sanction setups people point to in the empirical cases — and for each one tell me whether it actually contradicts (1) or whether it just moves us into a setting where (1)'s antecedent never got triggered, which would leave the framework intact and my (8) dead. And if (8) is too strong, what's the strongest conclusion this argument does license? No reading list, please — I'm not looking for references, just the diagnosis and the price of each repair.
