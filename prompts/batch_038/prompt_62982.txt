I'm giving the after-dinner talk at our department's annual combinatorics retreat, and the organizers have asked every speaker to open with a short joke before getting to the serious content. My own area is positional games, so the room will be full of people who have taught the strategy-stealing argument at least once in their lives: they know that in a symmetric game where an extra piece of your own colour can only ever help you, the second player cannot have a winning strategy, because the first player may open with an arbitrary move and then adopt whatever that winning strategy was supposed to be, playing a further arbitrary move on the rare occasions the strategy calls for a square he already occupies. What they also know, and what almost nobody outside the room does, is that the entire argument rests on that "can only help" clause — which is why it settles Hex and Chomp and tells us essentially nothing about chess, where a player can be compelled to move and moving is precisely the worst thing available to him. The office-mate I workshopped this with kept producing mathematicians-walk-into-a-bar material, which I consider grounds for ending a friendship.

Write me a joke whose humour comes from that hinge specifically, rather than from the technical vocabulary: the comedy should live in the gap between a non-constructive guarantee that an extra move cannot hurt and a situation in which being obliged to move is exactly what loses the game. It has to work for someone who has only ever met the argument in its Hex formulation, while carrying a second layer for anyone who also knows the name of the chess phenomenon — though you need not use the name, and you must on no account stop to define it. Please avoid puns on the names of mathematicians, bar-and-priest framings, generic placeholder characters, and anything that depends on knowing the solution to some particular game or on a fact a listener could not get from the joke itself. If you want a container, a wager against an opponent who gets to design the game and then graciously concedes the first move is a natural one, since it lets the second player pick the rules under which stealing goes wrong — but find a better vessel if there is one.

Deliver it as exactly five numbered lines of one or two sentences each, and those five lines must constitute the entire joke: a beat establishing the bargain, a beat in which the theorist deploys the argument with total confidence, a beat in which the position quietly turns, and the punchline inside the final line or two. No introduction, no title, no gloss afterwards explaining why it is funny, and no alternate versions or notes on the bits you had to cut — if the joke requires a footnote, it is not yet the joke.
