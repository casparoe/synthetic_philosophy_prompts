I'm assembling the closing week of an advanced undergraduate seminar on the demandingness objection to consequentialism, and I want to end it by screening roughly the last twenty minutes of Schindler's List: the sequence after the German surrender, when the factory falls silent and Schindler — having already spent his entire fortune — breaks down and tallies his car and his party pin as further lives he might have bought, followed by the color epilogue in which the real survivors walk to his grave in Jerusalem beside the actors who played them. What I don't want is the easy reading, in which the film merely dramatizes Singer's marginal-sacrifice principle and the students leave assuming the objection has been settled by tears rather than argument. Write me a serious critical reading of these sequences against the debate, treating the film with as much respect as the philosophy.

Three things I'd like the reading to work through. First, test a distinction I'm still trying to pin down for myself: between what a principle costs an agent at a given moment and what it leaves standing once the agent has paid — call it the unexhausted remainder, the way a demand expands to absorb whatever resources are brought to it, so that no sacrifice ever presents itself as enough. Is Schindler's collapse evidence that demandingness is a structural feature of beneficence rather than a threshold question about how much is too much? Second, set that against Liam Murphy's compliance condition and collective principle of beneficence, which deny that someone who complies owes compensation for everyone else's failure to comply. Is Schindler grieving precisely the surplus Murphy says he doesn't owe, and does the film vindicate Murphy's diagnosis of the intuition or make Murphy look complacent? Third, where do Susan Wolf's case against moral saints and Williams on integrity and agent-regret bite: is what Schindler feels a moral emotion about outcomes at all, or regret about the profiteer he used to be — and does the film's celebration of a man left penniless by his own rescue scheme contradict Wolf, or illustrate her?

Then tell me where the reading gives out. The extremity of the circumstances may disqualify the film as evidence about ordinary beneficence; the epilogue shifts the register from narrative to testimony and the object of attention from agent to beneficiaries, which may quietly change who is being praised and on what grounds. Don't reduce the film to an illustration and don't bend the arguments to fit it — there's no need to quote the dialogue, just describe the scenes — and close by saying what these sequences contribute to the demandingness debate that a sequence of arguments cannot.
