ok so I have to send in a one-pager stating my position on well-being before a workshop next month and I want to know whether the thing actually holds together before I commit it to writing. Here's what I'd say:

1. Welfare hedonism. The only thing good or bad for a person in itself is how their experience goes — pleasure good, pain bad — and everything else (achievement, friendship, knowledge, being loved) is at best instrumentally valuable or valuable in some other way. I don't think I owe much here against desire theories, because I think desire satisfaction only matters to you when you actually feel it.

2. I accept the heterogeneity objection in full. There's no single introspectible quality running through all the pleasures: the warm bath, the click when a proof finally goes through, laughing at a genuinely bad pun, sex, a song you've heard a thousand times, the relief of sitting down after standing all day. I can't find anything phenomenally common to these. So I've dropped the picture of pleasure as one felt ingredient that gets added on top of experiences.

3. Instead — I've been calling it role hedonism — pleasure is whatever occupies the enjoyment role, and the enjoyment role gets specified causally: to be enjoying something is to want the experience to keep going, to keep doing it, to be averse to its stopping. The unifier is the functional profile, not a feel.

4. But I still want to say that what makes pleasure good is that it feels good — not that it carries some special property, just that the goodness is in the experiencing, from the inside. If some state had the whole enjoyment-role profile and yet felt like nothing to the subject, it wouldnt be good for them. This is also my answer to the charge that role hedonism is just desire theory in a trenchcoat: the wants in (3) have to be wants aimed at experienced character.

5. Case verdicts I'm attached to. The masochist who wants the pain to continue isn't, just in virtue of wanting it, taking pleasure in it. And the calm, mildly contented person reading on a Sunday afternoon, who'd happily put the book down the second anything better came along, IS taking pleasure, even though they have essentially no disposition to sustain the state.

6. I also think the affective neuroscience is broadly on my side: there does look to be one system doing the work across all these heterogeneous cases, and I'd want to say thats what (3) is groping toward when it talks about a role.

7. On the machine case I bite the bullet. The person plugged in is doing well in welfare terms; they're missing out on stuff that matters for other reasons — contact with reality, agency, whatever it is — but welfare isn't the whole of value and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

And separately: is (4) buying me anything that (3) doesn't already give me, or is it just (3) said twice with more confidence?
