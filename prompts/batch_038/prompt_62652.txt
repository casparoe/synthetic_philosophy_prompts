I'm helping run a workshop where two people who've been at each other for years about the end of the western Roman empire finally have to co-author something. One is a discontinuity/collapse person: the fifth and sixth centuries in the west involved real, measurable loss — of state capacity, market integration, material living standards, security — and soft-pedalling that as "transformation" is motivated reasoning. The other is a transformation/accommodation person: the political break was much smaller than the rhetoric implies, continuity of population, landholding, bishops, law and Latin was the norm rather than the exception, and "collapse" is a loaded word doing normative work the evidence doesn't license.

Write the joint statement they could both actually sign. Not a debate transcript, not a verdict on who's right — a document with both names on it.

I want:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. Be demanding here: not "both sides agree the western empire fragmented politically," but the level of specificity where signing costs something. What do the archaeological and textual record license and not license about regional variation, about who the post-Roman rulers were and what they claimed to be, about which proxy measures are trustworthy and which are contaminated by recovery and visibility bias? If a claim both would sign turns out to be one neither would find interesting, that's a sign you're too high up the ladder.

2. A disagreements table, columns: the dispute; how the discontinuity side states it in wording it accepts; how the transformation side states it in wording it accepts; whether the residual gap is substantive or verbal; what would settle it. For the verbal column, do the work rather than labelling it — say precisely which part of "did Rome fall" is a fight over whether the term picks out system-level disintegration or person-level badness, and which part is a straight empirical disagreement that could in principle go one way or the other.

3. Cruxes with pre-registered predictions, and this is the part I care about most, so it needs a concrete case rather than hand-waving. Both parties commit to predictions about a site before seeing the rest of the sequence. Use something like this: a lowland British villa site in continuous occupation from roughly 350 to 600, where the excavated portion shows tile-roofed stone ranges replaced by timber halls inside the same maintained enclosure, fine wheel-thrown ware giving way to hand-made local fabrics, cattle metacarpals shrinking by maybe eight percent — but the enclosure ditch keeps getting recut, the adjacent cemetery stays in use, and there's a single imported continental vessel in a sixth-century layer. Have each side write down in advance what they predict for the unexcavated 600–750 continuation of that same site: settlement scale, degree of craft specialization, coin and sherd densities, diet isotopes, the language and ritual furniture of the burials. Then say which of those predictions genuinely discriminate between the two views, and which ones both sides would shrug at either answer to. A prediction neither camp risks anything on shouldn't be listed as a crux.

4. A short section of joint dismissals — things both would agree arent carrying the explanatory weight they're usually given. I suspect a single decisive battle, and probably one or two of the fashionable monocausal candidates as well. State it carefully enough that neither has to pretend the factor is irrelevant, only that it isn't load-bearing here.

5. Somewhere in the statement, have them propose the operational index of material complexity both would agree to use when arguing about whether a given change counts as decline — and state what the index must exclude so neither side smuggles its priors in. Per capita or aggregate? Only things that leave durable traces, or also things we know existed but vanish from the record? Weighted by what the inhabitants themselves would have valued, or by some external standard of living? I think a lot of the fight is sitting in this unwritten specification and nobody ever pins it down.

6. A closing paragraph signed by both, honestly assessing whether either position has been qualified to the point where no conceivable evidence would move it — specifically, whether "transformation" can absorb any amount of material loss and still be true, and whether "collapse" can absorb any amount of institutional and demographic continuity and still be true. If one of those is the worse sin, say which and why.

No reading lists or citations, I don't need them. Keep it in the voice of a document meant to be published over two signatures who don't fully trust each other.
