You're an expert in democratic theory and election administration — social choice, the philosophy of voting, the actual practice of drafting ballot language, all of it. Think carefully before you write, and don't jump straight to the deliverable.

Here's my situation. I work in a small state office that certifies ballot language for statewide initiative measures. Two lawyers and me, none of us with any social-choice training. For every measure that clears the signature count we currently do exactly one thing: check that the official title and summary are accurate and non-argumentative, sign off, done. Budget is about an hour per measure, less in a crunch year when forty are queued up between September and the filing deadline. What we certify is what voters actually read on the ballot, so when it goes wrong it goes wrong publicly and it's my name on it.

New wrinkle from our director: we now run a second screen before certifying. We have to say whether the question is *answerable* — meaning, is there a determinate thing that happens on each of the two answers, and could a voter reading our summary tell which of those two things they'd be choosing. If the answer is no, note that we have no power to kill a measure and aren't supposed to want one. Our whole action space is: certify as drafted, certify with a rewritten explanatory statement that puts the ambiguity on the page where voters can see it, or flag a legal question up to the AG. That's it.

Write me the procedure we'd run. Specifically:

- the questions to work through, in order, with what counts as a *sufficient* answer at each step and what a failure looks like on paper (not in theory — what would I be seeing in a filing)
- where the procedure terminates in "certify," where it forces a rewritten explanatory statement, and where it hands the thing to a human because the question underneath is about values and not about facts, and which human
- every step where the honest description is "this is a judgment call we've dressed up as a check." Label those explicitly. I would rather have them marked than buried, because I'll be defending the signoff in a public meeting someday.

On grounding: base it on what social choice and the philosophy of democracy actually establish, but be a good citizen about it. My colleagues wave famous impossibility results at me constantly and I want that to stop — so tell me which of the big results do **not** bite on a single yes/no question and why not, and for the ones that do bite (collapsing a three-way option set into one binary, whatever else you think is real here), say precisely what they license us to conclude and don't oversell it. A procedure that rests on a result that doesn't apply is worse than no procedure.

Two hard cases it has to absorb without special pleading:
(a) a measure drafted as a bare instruction to the legislature to go enact something, rather than as self-executing text. The Swiss treat that as a distinct category of popular initiative with its own validity test, and I think that's instructive even though we don't have their machinery — what does answerability even mean when the thing being voted on is a direction to a future body?
(b) a purely advisory question where nothing legally follows from either answer. Do we certify it, and on what theory?

Then run the procedure on three filings. Show your work at each step, don't just give me the verdict.

1. A one-sentence measure withdrawing the state from a multi-state compact, with no transition provisions. The yes campaign is already running ads saying a no vote means the legislature should go renegotiate better terms. The no campaign is saying a no vote means we stay exactly as we are. Both statements are in circulation now and our summary is silent on it. (The 2016 UK referendum is the obvious cautionary case — a two-option choice with no exit terms attached and a statute that didn't itself do anything — but don't build the procedure around it, we don't have that political setting.)

2. One sales-tax increase funding three named projects: a riverfront park, a municipal pool, and a transit line. Single subject is claimed on the basis that all three serve recreation and mobility. Our AG will tell us the single-subject rule is satisfied, and she's right — the courts here read it so loosely that basically nothing has ever failed it. I want to know whether the answerability screen catches something the single-subject screen structurally can't, and if it does, what the procedure tells me to do given that I can't strike anything.

3. The one I don't want. A measure striking one category out of the state civil rights statute. One sentence, one subject, self-executing, no transition gap, and the consequences of yes and of no are each perfectly clear and describable — our summary could be written in nine lines with nothing contestable in them. I expect the procedure to certify this and I want it to say so plainly. If your procedure blocks it instead, I want you to explain exactly which step is doing the blocking and why that's a substantive judgment we have no mandate, no competence, and no democratic standing to make.

Finish with a short section on what this procedure cannot do that we should stop pretending it can — including, I suspect, anything about whether the outcome will be good, and anything about how much a 51% win licenses the winning side to claim afterwards.
