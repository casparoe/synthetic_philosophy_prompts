I teach an upper-level undergraduate course in the philosophy of mathematics. The students arrive with one term of first-order logic — they know what a formal theory, a derivation, and a model are, and they can state the difference between consistency and soundness — but they have not seen the incompleteness proofs, and the course does no formal proof theory. The final assessment includes a take-home essay marked out of 20, with these bands: 17–20, the claim in the question is assessed rather than expounded, the hypotheses of the theorems are stated correctly, and the popular overreaches are identified and explained; 13–16, substantially accurate exposition with some confusion about what the theorems do and do not establish; 9–12, broadly recognisable account containing at least one significant conceptual error; below 9, the theorems themselves are misstated.

This year's question was:

"Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that mathematical truth outstrips what any formal system can prove." Assess this claim.

Below is one submitted answer, complete and unedited.

---

The claim is broadly right, though it is usually stated in a way that runs several distinct things together.

Take a formal theory T whose axioms can be listed by an algorithm, which is consistent, and which contains enough arithmetic to talk about numbers and their properties; Peano arithmetic is the standard example. Because proofs in T can be coded as numbers, T can itself express the claim that a given sentence has a proof in T. Gödel used this to construct, by a diagonal argument, a sentence G in the language of T which says of itself that it has no proof in T. If T proved G, there would be a number coding that proof, and so what G says would be false; but T can verify the concrete numerical facts involved, and so T would be inconsistent. Hence a consistent T does not prove G. Gödel needed the further hypothesis of ω-consistency to show that T does not prove the negation of G either; Rosser later showed that plain consistency suffices. So G is undecidable in T.

Two points about the scope of the result are worth stressing. First, nothing in the argument turns on how much arithmetic T contains, provided only that T is consistent and its axioms are effectively given, so the theorem applies to every consistent theory of the natural numbers, however weak. Second, since the argument works for any such T, there will always be arithmetical sentences that no formal method whatsoever can settle. That is the sense in which mathematical truth outruns proof: G is true — it does correctly report that it has no proof in T — but T cannot prove it. Nor is the result a mere trick of self-reference. G is a version of the liar sentence, and its status shows that arithmetical truth is a notion that cannot be collapsed into provability within a system.

The second incompleteness theorem sharpens the point. If T is consistent, then T cannot prove the sentence Con(T) formalising the claim that T is consistent, provided T's proof predicate satisfies certain internal conditions. It follows that the consistency of arithmetic can never be established: no proof of Con(PA) is available to us at all. This is why Hilbert's programme is generally regarded as refuted, and why we cannot in the end be certain that arithmetic is free of contradiction.

One might hope to escape by strengthening the theory, but this does not help. Adding Con(T) to the axioms of T leaves G undecidable, since the enlarged theory is itself subject to the theorem and has a Gödel sentence of its own. Incompleteness is therefore a permanent feature of the formal approach rather than a defect of one particular system.

The theorem is also commonly said to show that arithmetic has non-standard models, that is, models of T containing numbers that cannot be reached from zero in finitely many steps. G is false in these models, which is why it cannot be proved.

Finally, the theorem is held to matter beyond mathematics. In the 1931 paper Gödel himself pointed out that a machine, being a formal system, would have undecidable sentences of its own, whereas a mathematician can see that those sentences are true; it follows that the human mind is not a formal system. This is the strongest version of the claim in the question: not merely that no single system captures arithmetic, but that arithmetical truth is not capturable by any mechanical procedure at all.

On balance, then, the claim is correct, provided it is read as a claim about formal systems in general rather than about any particular one.
