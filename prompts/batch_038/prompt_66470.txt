I TA a second-year philosophy of language course and every year the exact same thing happens: the students get the definition of implicature, memorize it, and then use it as a dumping ground for anything non-literal. On the last problem set I had people call the extra content carried by "but," the background assumption triggered by "regret," and a garden-variety some-but-not-all inference all "conversational implicatures," and reading the textbook definitions back at them didn't move anyone. So for the discussion-section handout I want to build something that sorts them by what they'd actually say about concrete examples, not by which label they can recite.

What I'm after is a decision tree (or a self-scoring quiz that's really the same tree in disguise) that starts a student at "this utterance seems to convey something beyond its literal, truth-conditional content" and walks them down to a name for that something. Every branch node has to be an operation the student can perform on the sentence with a pencil — can the speaker tack on a clause denying the extra content without contradicting themselves or sounding bizarre? does the content survive when you negate the sentence, or bury it under "if," or under "does Mary know that"? does it vanish if you restate the same literal content with totally different words? — and you should never name the diagnostic in the node, because if it says "is it cancellable?" they'll just look up cancellable and pattern-match instead of testing. Then give me the leaves: literal/at-issue content, generalized conversational implicature, particularized conversational implicature, conventional implicature, presupposition, and one escape hatch leaf for "the tests came back inconsistent, this doesn't sit inside the Gricean machinery at all." Build it out of the logical space of test-results first — what happens to a bit of content that's non-cancellable but detachable, or cancellable and projecting, and so on — and only then drop the categories into the cells. Tell me which combinations no category actually occupies and diagnose each one: incoherent, or unstable, or just somewhere nobody's bothered looking. And flag any pair of leaves your tree honestly can't separate; I'm fairly sure presupposition and conventional implicature are going to be brutal to tell apart with tests a second-year can run, and I'd rather the handout say so than pretend.

For the items, I need ten-ish, each a short invented utterance with just enough surrounding context to make it felicitous, and all of it mundane — office fridges, a landlord, a gym buddy, a group chat about whose turn it is to drive. Please do not use any of the famous examples or anything recognizably close to them; if a sentence could plausibly have appeared in a textbook before 2000 I don't want it. Two or three of the ten should be traps where the obvious first guess is wrong, and at least one should genuinely split the tests so the student gets dumped into the escape-hatch leaf. Include at minimum: one built around "even" or "still," one scalar case, one that only works because of a specific detail of the context, one presupposition trigger sitting under negation, and one that looks like an implicature but is arguably just literal content. End with a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the leaves rather than onto the labels. One last thing — if you can find a natural place for the wrinkle that manner-of-saying implicatures behave differently from quantity implicatures w.r.t. whether paraphrase kills them, put it in, but don't force it; I'd rather have a clean seperate-able tree than a clever one that confuses people.
