Last spring, largely to settle an argument with my brother, I sent a saliva sample to one of the direct-to-consumer genetic testing companies. Alongside the ancestry breakdown, the report included a set of trait pages, and the one for type 2 diabetes offered me a number that was supposed to be about *me* — my genetic risk, expressed relative to other customers. I am a biostatistician by training, so I know how such a figure is constructed, and I also know the orthodoxy I repeat to my students: that heritability is a ratio of variances estimated in a particular population over a particular range of environments, and that transferring it to an individual is a category mistake. The stock illustration is phenylketonuria — a trait can be all but entirely heritable and yet wholly preventable by altering the diet, so heritability, the line goes, says nothing about individuals and nothing about what can be changed.

That orthodoxy now strikes me as too quick, and I want a rigorous argument against it rather than a careful restatement of it.

Please argue for this thesis: the individual-level question is not meaningless, it is merely ambiguous, and once it is disambiguated at least one of its readings has a determinate answer that narrow-sense heritability licenses.

Begin by separating at least three readings of "how much of my diabetes risk comes from my genes rather than my environment":

1. a share-of-production reading, on which genes and environment are supposed to supply fractions of the trait itself, the way flour and sugar contribute to a cake;
2. a contrastive reading, on which what is asked is how much of the difference between my phenotype and that of a comparable person, or of the population average, is attributable to the genetic differences between us;
3. an interventionist reading, on which what is asked is how my phenotype would have differed under some specified alternative environment.

Grant, for the sake of the argument, that reading (1) is ill-posed and that the cake analogy has done real damage. Then argue that reading (2) is well-posed and determinate, and that narrow-sense heritability is exactly the quantity that governs it: explain why the additive-genetic-variance ratio is not merely a summary of a population's variance but the coefficient of determination of the best linear prediction of phenotype from genotype, and hence a claim about individuals' expected deviations, and be explicit about what an individual's breeding value is a claim *about*. Then say how much of reading (3) survives, and what must be supplied — a reference distribution of environments, presumably — before it too becomes determinate.

The argument must confront rather than footnote the three objections I expect to be strongest:

(a) The expectation objection. A breeding value averages over the environments a population occupies, and no actual person inhabits the average environment. So the individual-level answer appears to describe a hypothetical counterpart of me rather than me.

(b) The reference-class objection. The answer to reading (2) shifts with the choice of reference population and of environmental range, so there is arguably no fact about me, only a fact about me relative to a selection I made. Say whether that relativity is a genuine defect or benign indexicality, and press the comparison with ordinary comparative predicates: is the genetic share of an individual's deviation any worse off epistemically than the claim that I am tall?

(c) The non-additivity objection. The decomposition is a linear approximation. Dominance, epistasis, and gene–environment interaction are one thing; gene–environment correlation is worse, since the additive component then absorbs environmental effects that travel with genotype — parents supply both alleles and households — and assortative mating and population structure inflate the estimate further. Say what is left of the thesis once these are taken seriously, and whether what is left degrades into something trivially true.

I want an actual argument, with premises and inferential steps visible, not a balanced survey and not a hedge distributed evenly across the options. If you think part of the thesis is false, say so and defend the part you can: I would rather have a narrower conclusion honestly reached than the whole thesis supported loosely. Please do not include citations or a reading list — I will supply references myself — and argue with positions and reasoning rather than with names, dates, and titles. Roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words.

At the end of the argument, state how confident you are in the central claim you have defended, and single out the one step you are least sure of, saying what would settle it. Then close with a final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <one sentence stating the individual-level claim, if any, that you take narrow-sense heritability to license>
