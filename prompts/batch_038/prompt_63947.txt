**Question 3 (12 marks).**
(a) Set out the spectrum argument against the transitivity of all-things-considered betterness as carefully as you can. State each premise you rely on, and illustrate the argument with a concrete series. [4]
(b) Explain why the conclusion is thought to be unacceptable, and identify what a theorist who accepts it must give up. [3]
(c) Assess at least one of the following responses: the vagueness response; the money-pump objection to intransitivity; the response that the members of the series are not comparable. Say whether the response you discuss succeeds. [5]

**Student script.**
(a) Run the argument on experiences. Let A₁ be a year of intense torture. Let A₂ be a somewhat longer stretch of torture that is very slightly less intense, A₃ a longer stretch again of pain slightly milder still, and so on down the series until Aₙ, a long life containing nothing worse than occasional aches and hangnails. Two comparative claims do the work. First, at every adjacent pair: Aᵢ₊₁ is worse than Aᵢ, since the small reduction in intensity is more than offset by the increase in duration, and this is meant to hold however finely we slice the series. Second, at the ends: Aₙ is better than A₁, since anyone would prefer a long life of trivial discomfort to a year of torture. If worseness is transitive, the first claim entails that Aₙ is worse than A₁, which contradicts the second. The spectrum argument is usually credited to Parfit, and Temkin has argued at length that we should accept the conclusion rather than abandon either comparative judgement: all-things-considered betterness is intransitive.

(b) The conclusion is unwelcome because betterness is supposed to supply a ranking. Under an intransitive relation a set of outcomes can have no member that is better than all the others, even where every pair of members is comparable, so an agent instructed to bring about the best outcome receives no determinate instruction; and ordinary deliberation assumes that if A is better than B and B better than C, then choosing C over A is a mistake. Temkin's way of softening the blow is to point to the plurality of the grounds of comparison: intensity, duration, number and the character of the lives compared bear on the verdict in different ways, and what makes A better than B near one end of the series need not be what makes B better than C near the other. On that view, giving up transitivity costs us only the assumption that value is a single scale, and we had independent reason to doubt that anyway.

(c) I will take the vagueness response. Its claim is that the descriptive and comparative predicates governing the series are vague, and that there is no sharp boundary anywhere in it at which the pairwise verdict flips. Transitivity is nevertheless preserved: because there is a cutoff we cannot identify, the chain of pairwise judgements must fail somewhere, so the argument is blocked without our having to locate where. The response is attractive in leaving the logic of betterness untouched, but I think it incomplete — it tells us why we cannot know which premise fails, yet if there is genuinely no fact about where the series turns, the argument lacks determinate premises capable of failing.

A third response denies that the members of the series are comparable at all, a denial sometimes tested by the small-improvement argument: if A and B cannot be ranked, and a slightly improved A still cannot be ranked against B, they were incomparable rather than equally good. On this response the spectrum fails for the same reason it fails if we simply accept intransitivity: in both cases not all of the comparative judgements driving the argument come out true, and in both cases what goes wrong is a failure of ranking rather than a failure of the logic of betterness.

Finally, whatever we say about the spectrum, intransitivity sits badly with choice. An agent whose preferences cycle can be money-pumped: offered a sequence of small paid swaps, each of which she accepts because it improves things by her lights, she ends up holding the option she started with, minus her payments. It is true that this objection assumes myopic choice, step by step; a sophisticated agent who plans ahead will decline the sequence, and it is widely granted that such an agent is not exploitable. Even so, the availability of a pump shows that an agent with intransitive preferences is rationally defective, and a relation which would leave rational agents systematically worse off cannot be the relation that settles what is really better. So the spectrum argument cannot after all be right about betterness itself.
