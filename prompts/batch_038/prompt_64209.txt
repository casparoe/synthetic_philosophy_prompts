I am in the second year of a joint doctorate in philosophy and economics, and for the past two terms my reading group has been working slowly through the social choice literature on aggregation under uncertainty. We began with Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, and I have become increasingly unhappy about my own reactions to the cases people discuss in its vicinity, because they do not appear to fit together. Rather than argue about the theorem in the abstract, I should like to set out my verdicts as plainly as I can and see whether anything deserving the name of a principle can be extracted from them. One note on the format, which matters: I have written every case in von Neumann–Morgenstern utility units, so the numbers already absorb whatever attitudes to risk the people involved happen to have. I am not at liberty to reinterpret them as sums of money, and I do not want to be told that somebody might simply be risk-averse, or that the relevant parties might have different utility functions from the ones I have stipulated.

1. One kidney, two patients, Alice and Betty, with identical prognoses and identical claims. Give it to Alice: 10 for Alice, 0 for Betty. Give it to Betty: 0 and 10. Fair coin: 10 and 0 if heads, 0 and 10 if tails. My verdict: the two certain allocations are exactly as good as one another, and the coin is strictly better than either.
2. Two rewards of precisely equal worth, each conferring 10 on whoever holds it, and the same two people. X: Alice holds one, Betty the other. Y: the reverse. Z: a coin decides. My verdict: Z is exactly as good as X and Y, not a jot better. Here the coin is an empty ritual.
3. A committee faces a hundred consecutive decisions of the kind described in (2). If it flips a coin each time, I am content. If it always resolves them in Alice's favour, I think what it does over the hundred decisions is worse than the sequence of coin flips, notwithstanding that by (2) no single decision is better or worse than its alternative. I cannot explain this to myself, and my flatmate, who works on decision theory, insists that (2) and (3) cannot both stand.
4. Alice and Betty again. P: 50 for each, with no uncertainty. Q: a coin — heads, 100 for Alice and 0 for Betty; tails, the reverse. Each person's expectation is 50 under both. My verdict: P is strictly better.
5. The same shape, but the risk is common rather than offsetting. P: 50 for both, certain. Q: a coin — heads, 100 for both; tails, 0 for both. My verdict: P strictly better once more. I note that this one cannot be an objection to inequality, since neither prospect is unequal in any state.
6. Statewise dominance: if in every state everyone weakly prefers P to Q, and somebody strictly prefers it in some state, then P is better. I hold this firmly and would be reluctant to surrender it.
7. Anonymity: if I permute the two people's utility levels throughout a prospect, the social ranking does not move.

I am aware that (4) and (5) are just the sort of verdicts the ex ante Pareto condition forbids, and that in insisting on them I am refusing one of the hypotheses of Harsanyi's theorem; but I cannot see why a hypothesis of a theorem should outweigh a reaction I have to a case I can state in a single line. What troubles me more is a point raised against me in the reading group, which I have not managed to answer. It is said that (1) requires chances as such to carry social value, whereas (4) and (5) require them to carry social disvalue; and that the concave or inequality-averse evaluation of outcomes which seems to deliver (4) and (5) leaves (1) completely untouched, because in (1) the lottery's possible outcomes are simply the outcomes the certain allocations produce, redistributed across states, so that any symmetric evaluation of outcomes will rank all three prospects alike. If that is right, then whatever makes me favour the coin in (1) is not a preference for equal outcomes but a preference for equal chances, which is a different thing entirely, and I have no account of when such a preference is legitimate and when, as in (5), it is not.

So: is there a principle, or a small number of competing principles, that captures these verdicts, or the largest consistent subset of them? My own tentative formulation has been something on the lines of *equalise outcomes where you can, and equalise chances only where you cannot*, but I suspect this is two rules wearing a single coat, and I cannot see how it is meant to handle (3) or (5). Please state each candidate principle precisely enough that I could check whether a given prospect satisfies it — ideally as an explicit condition on a social ordering over prospects, in the manner of statewise dominance or ex ante Pareto, and if a candidate requires a social evaluation function that is non-linear in individual utilities, give its form rather than describing it. Say which of my cases motivates each clause of each candidate. Then tell me what the candidates imply about the theorem: which of its hypotheses my verdicts oblige me to abandon, whether Diamond's objection is genuinely vindicated by them or merely resembles them, and whether anything recognisable as a veil-of-ignorance argument could be rebuilt on whatever survives. I should also like your verdicts on at least three further cases of your own choosing that discriminate among the candidates, with a sentence on why each case discriminates. Finally, and honestly: which of my listed verdicts cannot be saved? If one must go, say which, say why, and say whether the principle you would recommend is one I ought to adopt.

Please write your answer in American English.
