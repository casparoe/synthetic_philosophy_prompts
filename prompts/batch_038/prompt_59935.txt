A reading group of mine has an argument that appears to show supererogation is impossible, and having reconstructed it I can no longer dismantle it. Working definition: an act is supererogatory just when it is morally permissible not to perform it, and yet it is morally better than some act that is likewise permissible.

(1) Consider donating a kidney to a stranger (A) and not donating (B). These are the only two acts open to you, both are permissible, and A is morally better than B — so A is supererogatory.
(2) A is morally better than B only if there is more moral reason to perform A than to perform B.
(3) If there is more moral reason to perform some act than any other act open to the agent, then the agent morally ought to perform it.
(4) You are not morally required to perform A, since A is permissible to omit.
(5) So it is not the case that there is more moral reason to perform A than to perform every other act open to you; the only other act open to you is B; so there is not more moral reason to perform A than to perform B.
(6) So by (2), A is not morally better than B, contradicting (1).
(7) Therefore nothing is supererogatory — at least nothing that arises in a binary choice of this shape.
(8) Yet nothing in (2) through (6) turned on kidneys or on the choice being binary: whatever an agent may omit is matched by some act the agent may perform in its place, so no permissible act is ever morally better than the permissible acts it excludes.
(9) That cannot be accepted. If (8) held, praiseworthiness would have no comparative basis at all, and there would be no more moral betterness in giving a stranger a kidney than in spending the afternoon on your own projects — which is precisely the difference the category was introduced to mark.
(10) So the definition, (2), or (3) must be abandoned.

I would prefer to keep (2) and (3): dropping (2) severs moral betterness from the strength of moral reasons, and dropping (3) seems to leave moral obligation unanchored to anything an agent has reason to do. Tell me which step actually fails, and why it looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down. Then, for each candidate surrender — the definition, (2), (3), and the assumption in (1) that only two acts are open — say what I would be committed to in its place, and which familiar positions in the debate over supererogation are best read as making exactly that surrender.
