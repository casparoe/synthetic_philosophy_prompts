The room is mostly working adults, plus a couple of cross-registering philosophy undergraduates and one physics major. The final was a take-home essay. **QUESTION** (1,000 words; 100 points). Max Black's 1952 dialogue in *Mind* invites us to consider a universe containing nothing but two iron spheres, exactly alike in diameter, color, temperature, chemical composition, and every other intrinsic respect, and held a fixed distance apart. (a) Identify the Leibnizian principle this scenario is meant to threaten, and explain why the scenario threatens it. (b) Explain the reply that appeals to weak discernibility, and say whether that reply rescues the principle under attack or only a weaker relative of it. (c) Say whether the reply generalizes to identical elementary particles, and justify your answer.

**STUDENT ANSWER** (approximately 850 words).

¶1. The scenario is aimed at the principle of sufficient reason, otherwise known in this connection as the identity of indiscernibles, which says that nothing in the world exactly resembles anything else, so that any two things can be told apart by some property. Leibniz supported it by reasoning from God's perfection: a perfect being would have no reason to create two things alike in every respect rather than one of them, and so would not do it.

¶2. Black's claim is that we can form a coherent conception of such a universe, and that conceivability of this kind is good evidence of metaphysical possibility. If the universe is possible, then it contains two things that differ only numerically, and the principle fails at least as a necessary truth holding in every possible world. Note that nothing here contradicts Leibniz's Law: if the spheres are two, they are not identical, and Leibniz's Law says only that identicals are indiscernible. What the example attacks is the converse of that principle.

¶3. The usual answer, which goes back to Quine, is that the spheres are weakly discernible. Each stands at a certain distance from the other, and standing at that distance from something is an irreflexive relation, since no sphere stands that distance from itself. Weak discernibility requires only that some irreflexive relation hold between the objects, not that we be able to name them or single them out individually, so the obvious objection — that describing the relation already presupposes that we can tell the spheres apart — never gets off the ground. It follows, I think, that the identity of indiscernibles survives in its ordinary monadic form, because the property of standing at that distance from another sphere belongs to each of Black's spheres and would not belong to a sphere that was alone in an otherwise empty universe. The reply therefore vindicates the principle Leibniz stated rather than substituting a weaker cousin for it.

¶4. If the reply fails, then the spheres differ only numerically, and haecceitism is true. There is an independent reason to think something like this is right about the actual world. Newton's analysis of rotation shows that acceleration produces effects — the profile of the water's surface in a spinning bucket — that no relation among the visible bodies can account for, so space itself must be a real thing with parts of its own. Once space is a substance, the two spheres occupy two different places in it, and they are discernible after all by their absolute positions. The identity of indiscernibles is then safe without any appeal to weak discernibility, and Black's example turns out to be a case in which the description of a world was simply left incomplete.

¶5. For part (c): the same reasoning carries over to physics, though not uniformly. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids two fermions in one system from sharing all their quantum numbers, so fermions — the electrons, protons, and neutrons that make up ordinary matter — are absolutely discernible, and the identity of indiscernibles holds for the particles out of which the world around us is built. Bosons are the real difficulty. Any number of them may occupy the same state, and a pair of bosons sharing one state is Black's universe in physical dress: there is no relation, weak or otherwise, that holds between them without also holding of each taken on its own, so they are not even weakly discernible. Quantum mechanics therefore splits the verdict: the principle holds where it matters and fails where it does not.

¶6. So Black's example does not refute the principle Leibniz held, but it does show that the principle must be stated with care about what counts as a property, and that the version worth defending is the one that permits relations. Physics, on balance, supports the principle for the matter we are made of.
