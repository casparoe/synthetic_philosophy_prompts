I am three years into a novel, and I have run up against a difficulty that may not be a difficulty about craft at all, though I cannot yet tell. It concerns a shape that keeps recurring in my scenes unbidden, and I would rather learn now whether the shape is a real one or a muddle I have been dressing in good sentences. Here is the principal instance. A woman discovers that her brother has committed a crime that ruined a stranger's family, and she can either go to the police or keep silent; her mother is dying and would not survive the disclosure, and her brother's own children would be destroyed by it. She keeps silent. Twenty years later she is still sending money, anonymously and at real cost to herself, to the family of the man her brother hurt. Nobody in the book supposes that she chose wrongly — her brother's case is presented as genuinely strong, her mother dies in peace, and the narrative never flinches from her decision. Yet when her daughter finds the receipts and the bank slips, the daughter does not say that her mother was generous, or that her mother must have felt terrible; she says that her mother owed them, and still does, and the reader is meant to accept the correction rather than discount it as a young woman's severity.

There are two smaller versions of the same move elsewhere in the draft, which is how I know it is a pattern and not a set piece. A man breaks a long-standing promise to his oldest friend in order to keep a promise to his wife, and in the chapters that follow he describes himself, in his own head, as still being on the hook to the friend; the friend's resentment is written as legitimate rather than as the sulk of someone who has failed to understand that the promise was voided by the competing claim. And a pianist gives up performing to care for her father, makes the right decision by every measure the book supplies, and then says, in old age, that she still owes something — not to a person, but to the music. I cannot tell whether that last sentence is doing honest work or whether it is sentimentality I have not earned. What I keep producing, in each case, is an obligation that was not discharged, whose non-discharge was not a wrong, and which nevertheless does not lapse: it goes on generating demands, it can be addressed to a determinate someone, other characters recognise it and describe it as owed rather than as admirable, and the agent's private feelings are the least load-bearing part of it — which is why I do not think I am simply writing about guilt, or about remorse, or about the wish to feel bad in a becoming way. I know there is a good deal of philosophy about whether conflicts of duty can be real at all, and I have come across the arguments about statesmen who must do appalling things for the sake of the public good, and about the distress of an agent who has caused harm without being at fault for causing it. None of those is my case. In my scenes nobody concludes that anything impermissible happened, and yet something is left over with the whole structure of a debt — a direction, a creditor, a sense that settling it later would be the payment of what was due rather than a gift.

So I want to know whether this has a name. What I am after is the carving, not the label: the nearest established candidates, what each of them requires that my scenes fail to supply, which of them comes closest, and, if none does, a coinage with a definition sharp enough that I could work against it. In particular I need the edges marked. Does the residue survive forgiveness by the person it runs to? Does it presuppose that the agent is blameless for having got into the conflict, or does it also arise when she manoeuvred herself into it? Does it extend to a betrayed vocation or value, where there is no creditor, or is my pianist talking nonsense? Is the fact that third parties acknowledge it part of the phenomenon or an ornament of my own invention? And how is it to be kept distinct from ordinary compensation, from supererogation, and from remorse, without simply dissolving into one of the three? A prior question sits underneath all of this, and it may be the one I actually need answered. If an obligation of this kind persists after it has become impossible to fulfil it, and if it persists as a demand rather than as a memory, does that commit me to holding that both obligations remained in force all the way through the choice — which would make my woman's predicament a genuine conflict rather than a case in which one duty simply outranked another and ceased to exist? I would like to know whether writing these scenes well binds me to a position on that question, and if it does, which position it binds me to, and whether there is a way of writing them that stays neutral. Please no reading lists and no citations; I have neither the training nor the time to follow them up, and a list of titles would tell me nothing about whether the shape I keep drawing is a shape that exists.
