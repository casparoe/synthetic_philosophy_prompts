I would like you to take on the role of an expert in contemporary egalitarian political philosophy who also has substantial experience teaching that material to audiences with no academic background, and I would like you to think carefully before answering, because the difficulty in my situation is not the theory but the sequencing. I volunteer as coordinator for a small hardship fund attached to a community centre: we receive requests for emergency grants — rent arrears, a boiler that has died in January, a car repair without which someone loses their shift work, a dental bill — and a committee of about twelve volunteers meets monthly to decide them. None of us has studied philosophy. Several of the older members hold fairly stern views about self-reliance, and a couple of the younger ones hold the opposite view, that any question about a person's own choices is intrusive and unkind. I am running a ninety-minute training evening for the committee in a back room with a whiteboard, a flip chart, and tea, and I want to teach them one distinction that I became convinced, after reading around the subject on my own, is exactly the thing they are already groping for without having a name for it: the difference between misfortune that simply befalls a person and misfortune that is how a deliberate, knowingly taken risk turned out — sometimes called brute luck versus option luck. I do not remember where I first read it and I am not asking you to reconstruct the source, so please do not supply or guess at references, and please do not put anything in quotation marks as if quoting a text.

What I need from you is the plan for that evening, not the explanation itself. Please begin by sketching, in a few lines of brainstorming, the dependency structure of the material: which component has to be in place before which, and what would collapse if the order were reversed — for instance, I suspect the committee cannot yet distinguish "this was their choice" from "this is their fault," or "they are responsible for this outcome" from "they deserve to bear it," and I suspect the distinction between the two kinds of luck is unstable until those confusions are cleared. Then give me the running order with rough timings; the single case that should carry the whole session, drawn from the sort of file we actually see, together with what that case must contain and what it must conspicuously lack (it must not be a case in which the applicant is obviously sympathetic or obviously unsympathetic, and it must not turn on sums of money, or we will spend the evening arguing about sums); the misconceptions I should expect, in the order they will surface, and the precise moment in the sequence at which each should be headed off rather than corrected afterwards — my own list includes "brute luck means nobody is to blame," "option luck means they had it coming," "this is about what sort of person you are rather than about the history of one particular outcome," "we should ask what they would have decided if they had known better," and the worry that any of this lets us read minds about how informed a person's risk really was; a closing question or short exercise that would show me the distinction has actually landed rather than merely been agreed with politely; and an explicit list of what I should leave out at this level, with the reason, said out loud to the group rather than quietly omitted.

Two further things. First, address the misuse problem directly: I am fairly confident that the instant this distinction is available, a faction of the committee will treat it as a licence to refuse grants, and I want your judgement on how to teach the distinction without handing them the weapon — including whether the technical labels should be introduced at all or whether I am better off keeping the whole thing in plain language for ninety minutes and naming it only at the end, with your reasoning either way. Second, decide what to do about the strongest objection I have come across, which is that once you ask what a person could realistically have chosen given how they were raised and what they could afford, the line between the two kinds of luck stops looking clean; and that perhaps the point of a fund like ours is not to compensate people for bad luck at all, but to refuse to let some members of a community be pushed into a lesser standing than others. Tell me whether that belongs in the session, at which point, in how many sentences, or whether it should be deferred and announced as deferred — and please resist the temptation to give me a reading list, since the whole exercise has to work with what I can say out loud in a back room.
