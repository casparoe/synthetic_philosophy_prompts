For my Victorian philosophy paper I'm stuck on Sidgwick's psychical research side. Everyone in my seminar treats it as an embarassing hobby, but he was the SPR's first president and I think it was doing real work for him: he couldn't get prudence and morality to line up without something like a morally ordered cosmos, and evidence for survival was meant to be the empirical fallback.

So suppose Sidgwick somehow got handed the last forty years of psi research plus the methods fights around it: the ganzfeld experiments and the meta-analysis war over them, where one camp reports a solid effect and a later reanalysis of a bigger set finds nothing; Bem's 2011 precognition paper in a top journal and the replications that flopped; the argument that standard significance testing can never really accumulate evidence *for* the null, so that whole controversy was underpowered at the level of logic rather than data; preregistration and the decline effect; triple-blinded mediumship protocols; cardiac arrest studies with hidden targets only visible from the ceiling; the Princeton random-number-generator work and its global-scale spinoff; and the SPR's own 1894 census of hallucinations (17,000 people, 1,684 reporting an apparition) redone today as a big-data survey.

Which of those would he count as actual progress toward the evidence he wanted, and which as just better hygiene that leaves the question exactly where it was? Where has the field gone backwards from 1900? And the bit I care about most: would a Sidgwick reading all this be more or less hopeful about the dualism of the practical reason than the one who died in 1900?

Give me the bottom line in two sentences first, then a table with these columns: development | verdict (evidence gain / methods gain only / no change / setback) | which of his commitments it bears on | reasoning, 2-3 sentences | how much this rests on things he actually said vs. extrapolation (high/medium/low).
