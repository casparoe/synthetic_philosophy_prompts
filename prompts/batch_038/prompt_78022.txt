so for my graphic design elective I'm hand-drawing my own little typeface on graph paper, all 26 lowercase, and somewhere around the letter a I fell down a rabbit hole I can't get out of. I ended up reading about that Letter Spirit thing, the project where people tried to get a program to draw the whole lowercase alphabet in one invented but internally consistent style on a tiny grid, and it kept talking about the "spirit" of a letter like that was a normal thing to say, and now I can't stop asking: what actually makes every a an a? My serif one, the double-story printed one, the single-story curly one you write by hand in italics, the one on a stop sign that's basically a triangle with a bar, the one a kid in kindergarten makes that looks like a squashed o. They don't seem to share any shape at all. Before I go read a pile of typography theory I want the spec first: write out what any account of letter identity has to be able to do to count as adequate.

by that I mean the full list — the cases it has to handle without cheating, the distinctions it's not allowed to collapse, and the findings about how people actually read that it can't contradict — with the case or the reason behind each one. here's some raw material, feel free to add more, I'm sure I'm missing stuff: (1) a and A are the same letter even though they share like zero strokes; (2) the single-story handwritten a and the double-story printed a are the same letter and also share nothing; (3) my morph sequence — I drew 100 tiles where the a slides by tiny steps into an o, and there's obviously a point where it stops being an a, but what fixes that point?; (4) an illegible-but-intended a, like some logo mark where the designer swears it's an a and nobody else sees it; (5) a mirror-reversed a, same letter or different?; (6) æ — one letter, two letters, does it depend on which language's alphabet you're counting; (7) a stroke patient's shaky a that everyone still reads instantly; (8) the fact that people spot letters faster inside real words than on their own, which seems like bad news for any story where a letter is identified before context; (9) greek alpha and latin a; (10) the difference between something being an a and being a good, canonical, textbook a, which I think a lot of answers just blur together. also tell me what the account is FOR, because I think adequacy depends on that — mine needs to be good enough to settle my morph sequence and to tell me whether my own drawings are legit, not good enough to program a font editor.
