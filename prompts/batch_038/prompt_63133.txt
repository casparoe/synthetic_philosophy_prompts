I am in the final weeks of a graduate seminar on social ontology, and my term paper is going to be about why the literature on joint action has not moved in forty years, so I would like your help getting clear on the structure of the disagreement rather than on any particular verdict.

The two camps are familiar to the point of exhaustion. On one side are accounts that locate shared intention entirely in the attitudes of the individual participants: either a primitive collective mode of intending that lives in each participant's head and is not further analysable, or a structure of ordinary individual planning attitudes whose subplans mesh, whose holders are mutually responsive to one another's intentions and to the activity itself, and which are accompanied by whatever degree of mutual belief the account deems necessary. On the other side are accounts on which the plural subject cannot be reconstructed from that structure at all, because what does the real work is a joint commitment: the commitment is what generates the obligation to play one's part and the corresponding standing to rebuke a participant who bails, and neither meshing plans nor mutual responsiveness can produce anything of that normative kind. Each side recycles the same complaints. The individualists say the plural subject is either a mysterious group mind or an idle posit that does no causal-explanatory work, and that a primitive we-intention explains nothing. The collectivists say the individualist conditions are jointly insufficient, that they permit shared intention in cases where nobody owes anybody anything, and that a primitive we-mode attitude smuggles in the collectivity it was meant to explicate. Five weeks of seminar, and nobody budged.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than endorse, is that the two sides are not answering the same question: one is asking what has to be true of these individuals, as planning and intending agents, for their behaviour to constitute a single course of action, and the other is asking what makes it the case that these participants owe one another the performance and are entitled to resent its absence. On this reading the arguments cross over rather than collide, and the apparent metaphysical dispute about group minds is an artefact of two projects sharing one heading. I can see at least three rivals: that there is a genuine substantive disagreement about whether irreducibly plural attitudes exist, buried under the difference in emphasis; that the dispute is verbal, turning on what 'shared' and 'we' are permitted to mean; or that it is a disagreement about explanatory standards, the individualist treating individual-level causal explanation as a constraint on social ontology and the collectivist denying that this is a constraint at all.

Three things, please:

First, the diagnosis, argued from what each side actually says about cases and about the other's conditions. Do not survey the positions; assume I have read them, and tell me instead which of the readings above best explains the impasse, or propose a better one.

Second, a test of the diagnosis. If the camps really are answering different questions, their criteria should never conflict, since no set of facts could force them apart. So construct a case in which the individualist conditions and the joint-commitment conditions come apart, or argue that no such case can be constructed. Candidates might include a flash mob whose members execute separate instructions and never meet; a queue that holds perfectly on a wet Tuesday morning though no one would think of rebuking anyone who stepped out of it; two people who have co-edited the same document for years without ever exchanging a word about the arrangement, each tracking the other's contributions; or an individual who plans around a navigation system's directives with the same meshing and responsiveness she would show a human partner. Tell me which of these, if any, actually discriminates between the accounts, and what that implies for the diagnosis.

Third, what would count as progress. Is there a distinction, a piece of argument, or a formal result about whether the various sets of conditions are interderivable that could move the debate? Would an empirical finding do it, for instance from developmental or comparative work showing the normative residue appearing without the planning structure, or the planning structure without it? Or is the honest conclusion that the impasse is stable, and if so, is that because the question is ill-posed or merely because it is hard?
