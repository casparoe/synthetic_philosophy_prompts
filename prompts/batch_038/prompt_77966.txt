Next month I am giving a paper at a one-day workshop on time and structure in social explanation, hosted by the economic history group of a history department. I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate, and the assigned commentator is a macroeconomist who works on persistence. The room will be about twenty-five people: five or six cliometricians who spend their lives in panel regressions and identification strategies; two or three historians who came up in the Annales tradition and are quietly hostile to anyone formalizing Braudel, one of whom works on sixteenth-century price conjunctures; a paleoclimatologist who does proxy reconstruction and has said publicly that she is tired of historians using her field as a deus ex machina; a global historian who studies colonial extractive institutions and treats any talk of deep structural constraint as one short step from an argument that some regions were destined to fall behind; and a philosopher of social science interested in levels of explanation and causal exclusion.

My thesis is that Braudel's three time scales are usually misread as a ranking of subject matter — geography over conjuncture over events, the slow over the fast, with event-level history dismissed as foam on the surface — when they are better read as a claim about the time constants of social systems, and that on this reading the longue durée becomes a testable methodological thesis rather than a matter of taste.

Here is the argument I want the objections aimed at:

1. For any candidate explanatory factor X, define its restoration horizon τ(X) as the time a system perturbed in X takes to return to its prior trajectory — or, where it never returns, the time the deviation takes to be absorbed into a new one. τ is a property of a system and a factor jointly, not a property of a topic.

2. Braudel's ordering is then the empirical claim that, for the early modern Mediterranean, the largest τ belong to geographical and material factors, conjunctures are medium-τ, and events are small-τ noise around a slower drift. His disparagement of event-level history is not a claim about what matters to human beings but a claim about τ.

3. τ is estimable — half-lives from persistence coefficients, impulse-response decay, decomposition into low-frequency components — so the ordering is falsifiable and may come out differently in different systems.

4. My empirical chapter ranks three shocks on a panel of roughly 1,200 European towns, 1300–1800: a mortality shock, a legal-charter shock, and a change in transport cost. The charter shock has the longest measured half-life. On my account this makes a political factor structurally deeper than a geographical one in that system, and I present it as a success for the framework rather than a refutation of Braudel: it confirms the time-constant reading and overturns only the topic ranking, which that reading denies he was committed to.

Please prepare a Q&A prep sheet — not a defense of Braudel, and not a survey of the historiography. I want:

- The seven or eight objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, each attributed to the part of the audience it comes from and phrased the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine;
- For each, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes, plus a candid note on where that reply is soft;
- A flag on which objections are attacks on my formalization and which are attacks on Braudel or on structural explanation in general, since I can afford to lose the second kind and not the first;
- The two concessions I should volunteer in the opening five minutes so they cannot be extorted later;
- The single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the workshop;
- The objection I am hoping nobody asks — the one that goes after a load-bearing assumption rather than a detail — together with your judgment of whether it is merely damaging or genuinely fatal.

Be direct about which of the four moves above is weakest. If you think move 2 misreads Braudel, deliver that verdict the way the Annales historians in the room would deliver it, rather than reassuring me.
