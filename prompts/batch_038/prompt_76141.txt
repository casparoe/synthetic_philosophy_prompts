I am a PhD student in the philosophy of social science, and last month our methods reading group devoted a session to explanation by appeal to culture, using the Protestant ethic thesis as the test case. Two of the participants — call them Ines, an economic historian, and Tomás, who works on interpretive methodology — argued for most of the hour, and I was taking notes. When I circulated my summary, three people said it was even-handed to the point of uselessness, and I still cannot tell who had the better of it. Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

(1) Ines opened by insisting that the thesis is a causal claim — a body of religious ideas brought about a distinctive form of economic conduct — and that it has been empirically refuted. Acquisitiveness is older than the Reformation, and commercial enterprise flourished in Catholic Italy and the southern Low Countries long before Calvin. More damning, she said, is the quantitative work: an econometric study of Prussian counties found that predominantly Protestant areas had both higher incomes and higher literacy, and that the income differential largely disappears once schooling is controlled for. So the operative mechanism was education, not an ethic. She added that it is to the thesis's credit that it makes a claim capable of failing, and that it failed.

(2) Tomás replied that this treats Weber as offering a variance decomposition, which he never did. The explanandum is not capitalism but a historically specific spirit: gain pursued methodically, as a duty and an end in itself, rather than as a means to consumption. The relation he posits between that spirit and ascetic Protestantism is one of elective affinity, and he explicitly refused to substitute a one-sided spiritualist account for a one-sided materialist one. As for the Prussian result, he said it is not a rival mechanism but a link inside Weber's own chain: the confessions demanded scripture-reading, which is why Protestant areas schooled more. Identifying a mediator is not identifying an alternative cause.

(3) Ines said that this is precisely what makes the thesis untestable, and that an interpretation which no observation could defeat is not an explanation. If the Prussian data are absorbed as a mediator, the Italian data are deflected by redescribing the explanandum, and both the materialist and the spiritualist one-sidedness are disclaimed, nothing is left that could come out false. She raised a second problem: the spirit is characterized by the same maxims about time and money that the conduct it is meant to explain exhibits, and an attitude defined by its outcome cannot explain that outcome.

(4) Tomás said the circularity charge is anticipated in the text: the spirit is characterized only provisionally at the outset and defined properly at the end of the analysis, precisely so that the concept is not read off from the explanandum. And he denied the falsifiability point, arguing that causal imputation in Weber is counterfactual all the way through — the question is how the development would have run had the religiously conditioned ethic been absent. What Weber denies is only that such a counterfactual about a unique configuration can be licensed by a general law. Demanding falsifiability of a singular causal claim, he said, is applying a standard from the wrong discipline.

(5) Ines answered that a counterfactual about a unique configuration is exactly what needs a standard rather than escaping one, and she put a challenge: state the thesis in a form that could come out false. Her own diagnosis was that history supports at most a selection claim, not a motivational one. Ascetic Protestantism taught nobody to do anything new; it sorted who entered trade — dissenters shut out of universities, offices, and guilds pushed talent toward commerce and manufacture, while their congregations supplied credit networks and trustworthy counterparties. A sorting mechanism produces the same correlation a motivational one would. On that reading the spirit causes nothing: it causes who acts, not what is done, and all the moral psychology about calling and inner-worldly asceticism drops out of the explanation.

(6) Tomás conceded that sorting is a different causal target — the distribution of a type of agent rather than a token act — but denied that it exhausts the phenomenon, since sorting explains why those people were in the trade and not why the trade was run methodically and its surplus reinvested rather than consumed. He then offered a weaker formulation he thought survives: the ethic's contribution was permissive. Traditional Christian teaching treated acquisitiveness and usury as sin, which acted as a normative and psychological brake; the Reformation lifted the prohibition on gain pursued as a vocation. Removing a brake is a cause of the motion that follows, so the thesis can be restated as the claim that the ethic made the conduct permissible and therefore sustainable across generations — weaker than motivation, but not empty.

(7) Ines said this restatement is a licence, because it turns the thesis into its own converse. If the operative causal fact is the lifting of a prohibition, the explanatory work is being done by the Church's constraint rather than by Protestantism's content, and the same logic makes Catholic moral theology the real independent variable with the Reformation as a mere release. She also objected that permissive causation is compatible with anything: on that standard the Reformation caused capitalism in the way that opening a window causes a room to cool, while everything substantive — who was in the room, and how warm it already was — is left to the sorting story and the schools.

(8) Tomás closed by saying that cause was never the only thing at issue. Weber's method requires that an interpretation be adequate on the level of meaning as well as causally adequate: the believer's own reasons must make the action intelligible as the action it is, and that is a claim about content rather than about a mechanism that pushes. Ines's demand that the ethic be one factor among factors misconstrues meaningful action, since the belief is not a mental event alongside the conduct but the description under which the conduct is what it is. On this view the Prussian regressions cannot even be addressed to the thesis, because they are written in the wrong vocabulary.

They asked me to referee and I would rather not guess. Please adjudicate this move by move. For each of the eight moves, tell me whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with a short justification; where a move mixes a sound point with a bad one, separate them. Then answer four specific questions: does a schooling mechanism refute the thesis or fit inside it, and does that depend on how the explanandum is specified? Is the circularity objection defused by the reply that the spirit is defined only provisionally at the start? Is the selection/motivation distinction a real difference in causal structure here, or a distinction without a difference, given that both would predict the same county-level correlation? And is the permissive or brake-removal formulation a genuine rescue of the thesis or a surrender that relocates the cause in Catholic moral theology? Finally, say which of the two is more right overall, and identify what both of them are missing — I suspect each is trading on a different notion of what culture is supposed to be doing, but I cannot pin down whether their disagreement is substantive or terminological, and I don't know whether move (8) is a real insight or an equivocation that quietly abandons move (4).

I am not looking for references, a reading list, or anything you would need to look up; I want your judgment about the arguments as actually made.
