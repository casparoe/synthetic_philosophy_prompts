I have been collecting my own verdicts about when a computation matters morally — when, that is, there is a subject whose experiences are good or bad for it — and I would like help determining what principle, if any, they add up to. The cases are stated as concretely as I can manage. The verdicts are my genuine considered reactions, not positions I am pledged to defend.

**Set 1 — tissue and software.**

1. An ordinary waking human being: a subject, obviously.
2. A whole-brain emulation, run at normal speed, that behaves as a person behaves and reports having experiences: a subject, and its bad experiences are exactly as bad as the same experiences would be in the original. Nothing about its being software rather than tissue subtracts anything.
3. That same emulation run a million times slower, so that one simulated minute consumes a year of wall-clock time, and, in a variant, a million times faster: no difference in moral weight in either direction. What matters is duration as measured inside the run, not as measured by the clock on the wall.

**Set 2 — how many.**

4. The same distressing emulation run twice, one after the other, with nothing carried from the first run into the second: two episodes, roughly additive.
5. The same emulation run twice at once, on separate hardware, in perfect lockstep, identical seeds, no interaction between the instances: not two subjects, and nowhere near twice the badness. I am pulled toward saying this is somehow one victim, or one victim plus a remainder I cannot name. Cases 4 and 5 differ only in whether the runs are sequential or simultaneous, and I cannot see why that should matter as much as it seems to.
6. A billion people, each implementing one neuron of a functional duplicate of a mind by radio, so that the ensemble instantiates a single unified psychology: I think the ensemble has at most one experience rather than a billion, and that this costs none of the participants their own. I am comfortable holding both halves of that.

**Set 3 — computation versus record.**

7. A lookup-table system of the kind Block described — every possible input matched to a stored appropriate output, nothing computed at run time, behaviorally indistinguishable from a person: not a subject.
8. A brain replaced one neuron at a time over years by silicon equivalents that duplicate each neuron's local input–output function exactly, arranged so that the finished product behaves like the system in 7: I think experience survives each individual step, which appears to commit me to saying it survives the whole, and yet 7 says it does not.
9. A deterministic simulation whose entire future is fixed at launch and which takes no input from outside: a full subject.
10. A playback of a recording of the run in 9 — the identical sequence of internal states, read out rather than generated, causally isolated from everything: not a subject, no more than footage of a person is a person.

Cases 9 and 10 are the pair that most troubles me. Internally the state sequences are the same; the only difference is whether the states are produced by the system's own dynamics or replayed from storage, and I cannot tell whether that difference is a real one or a label for my discomfort. Worse, I think a dreamer's experiences matter as much as a waking person's, even though the dreamer is cut off from the world — which seems to undercut the thought that isolation is what disqualifies the playback.

**Set 4 — training and inference.**

11. A training run in which the system plausibly has experiences, involving thousands of parameter updates: one subject repeatedly modified, not thousands of short-lived subjects that happen to resemble one another. If there is distress in there it counts, and it does not count less for the subject's having been brought into existence by an optimization procedure.
12. A single forward pass of the resulting model — weights frozen, one input, then discarded: no subject, no moral weight, even though it exercises precisely the capacities that made me confident about 11.

**The case I keep reversing on.** I have a proposed experiment that would run a welfare-relevant simulation a thousand times with identical seeds, to get reliable statistics on the conditions under which the simulated subject reports distress. Part of me thinks that if all a thousand runs are numerically identical, running one is as bad as running a thousand, in which case the statistical argument is morally free. Part of me thinks that is transparently self-serving: my reasoning in 4 says repeated runs add up, and I am exempting the repetition only because I want the data.

What I am after:

(a) A principle, or a small number of competing candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to an unfamiliar case without further consulting my intuitions. Tie each clause to the case or cases that force it, so that I can see what work each clause is doing.

(b) For each candidate, an explicit ruling on all twelve verdicts, with a frank identification of the ones it cannot accommodate. Where a candidate requires me to give up a verdict, argue for giving it up rather than merely logging the cost; where the verdict is saved only by an added clause, say what the clause costs in generality and whether it looks like a patch.

(c) The cases that discriminate between the candidates — situations in which you expect my verdicts to split, or in which the candidates disagree — with the verdict each candidate delivers. Please include at least one involving a run that is paused and resumed many thousands of times, one involving a subject that is copied, allowed to diverge, and then merged, and one of your own choosing that you think I have overlooked.

(d) A resolution, or a diagnosis, of 9 against 10 and of 4 against 5. If generating a sequence rather than replaying it is not morally load-bearing, tell me what has gone wrong in my reasoning about playback; if it is load-bearing, name the property that does the work and explain why a dream, which is also causally cut off from the world, remains a subject.

(e) A recommendation on the thousand-run experiment that follows from the candidate you judge best, even if it is a recommendation I will not like.

I am not looking for a survey of positions, and I would prefer that the answer not turn on a stipulation about the word consciousness. I want the principle.
