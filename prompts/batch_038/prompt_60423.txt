I lead policy work for an online community platform with roughly two million monthly users — a network of semi-autonomous subcommunities, each with its own moderator team. Our head of trust and safety has an argument he uses constantly, in meetings and in internal memos, and it has become the platform's default way of thinking about norm change. The argument runs as follows.

Community norms are conventions in David Lewis's sense. Like driving on the right-hand side of the road, or which side of a doorway people pass on, they are regularities that solve a recurring coordination problem: everyone would rather that everyone conformed to some single pattern than that people diverged, and which pattern it is matters far less than that it be the same for everyone. From this he draws three conclusions, all of which drive real decisions:

1. The content of a community norm is largely morally arbitrary; the interesting questions about it are equilibrium-selection questions rather than ethical ones. So the moderation team should not spend effort arguing about whether a norm is right, only about whether it can be made to stick.

2. Because norms are equilibria, they can be shifted by supplying a new focal point rather than by changing anyone's beliefs. A sufficiently salient, well-publicized, credible signal — his current proposal is to get the fifty most-followed users in each community to commit, simultaneously and publicly, to a new practice — will move the community to the new equilibrium at close to zero persuasion cost.

3. Resistance to a norm change is therefore coordination friction: switching costs, inertia, uncertainty about what others will do. It is not evidence about the merits of the proposed norm, and treating it as such, as several of our moderators insist on doing, is a category error.

He has recently applied the framework to two live cases, and this is where I became worried. In case A, we want to replace a norm of public call-outs — users naming and shaming rule-breakers in-thread, often to applause — with a norm of reporting to moderators and letting sanctions happen out of view. In case B, we want to introduce content warnings on posts describing self-harm, in communities where posting such material without warning is the settled norm and where a substantial minority of users have said explicitly that they regard un-warned disclosure as a matter of principle: that they should not have to manage other people's reactions, and that asking them to is itself the harm.

My own counter-analogy, which I have been deploying badly and without much confidence, is that a community's norms are more like its grammar than like its traffic rules — partly arbitrary, certainly, but load-bearing for what can be said and understood among these particular people, and therefore not switchable by announcement.

Please audit his analogy rather than mine, though I would like a verdict on mine at the end. Specifically:

- Set out the correspondence table. What in a platform community plays the role of the driver, the road, the alternative equilibria, the cost of unilateral deviation, and the mechanism by which expectations about others' behavior get transmitted? Where the analogy simply has no counterpart, say so rather than straining.

- Test the argument against Lewis's own account rather than against the popularized version of it. Which of the conditions he imposes on a regularity's being a convention fail for case A, fail for case B, or fail for platform norms generally — and which of them is his argument silently relying on? I am most suspicious of the requirement that the parties have roughly the same preferences over the possible combinations of actions, and of what follows if that requirement is not met; tell me if I am worrying about the wrong condition.

- Identify what the analogy smuggles in. Which features of the traffic-rule case — an enforcement authority with jurisdiction, a population of anonymous one-off interactants, a payoff structure in which nobody cares which side of the road anyone else uses so long as it is uniform, an absence of any view about what driving-on-the-right means — are absent from platform norms, or present in the opposite direction?

- Then say which of the three conclusions survive, in what weakened form, and for which of the two cases. I suspect that conclusion 2 survives for some platform norms and fails badly for others, that conclusion 3 is the one doing the most damage, because it licenses us to discount user testimony about a norm's merits as mere friction, and that conclusion 1 is the one I have been attacking most and least effectively. Argue this rather than assuming it, and tell me where I am wrong.

- Finally, if the Lewisian frame is the wrong one, what is the right one? Offer a better analogy for platform norms — drawn from the game-theoretic work on how norms emerge and stabilize after Lewis, or from anywhere else you find more illuminating — that preserves whatever explanatory work his analogy genuinely does and supplies what it misses. If you think a distinction in that later literature between mere conventions and norms held in place by something stronger earns its keep here, say so; if you think it is taxonomic bookkeeping with no consequences for what we should do, say that instead.

One constraint on the answer: it has to be usable by people who will not read Lewis and have no patience for metaethics. So the analysis should bottom out in concrete recommendations about what we should and should not attempt in cases A and B, and about which of his three claims we should stop repeating in meetings.
