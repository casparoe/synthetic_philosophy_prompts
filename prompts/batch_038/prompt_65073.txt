I direct the standards secretariat of a European payments-and-registry consortium. Our members — some forty of them, mostly mid-sized processors, a few national registries, a handful of large banks — are bound by two kinds of text. Requirements are audited: a member that fails one gets a finding, a remediation plan, and ultimately suspension. Conventions are published but not audited, on the working assumption that they will hold because everyone expects everyone else to follow them and no one gains by defecting alone. Roughly four times a month a working group asks us to classify a candidate rule one way or the other, and the classification is consequential in both directions. Publish as a convention something that will not hold on its own, and interoperability degrades silently: we learn about it from incident reports months later, by which time members have divergent implementations and migrating back is expensive. Impose audit on something that was already self-sustaining, and we burn scarce audit capacity, members resent it, and we teach them that conformity is something done to them rather than something they do.

I want the classification built on Lewis's account of convention rather than on intuition, because Lewis gives conditions a population either satisfies or does not, and I suspect most of our committee disputes are really disputes about which condition is failing. The difficulty is that my team has no training in game theory or philosophy; we are document reviewers with about ninety minutes per candidate and access only to the evidence a secretariat actually holds — implementation surveys, ticket logs, minutes, the fact that three members' parsers reject a given field. So the deliverable is a worksheet: an ordered sequence of questions, each with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer from that kind of evidence, and each ending in one of a small set of terminal outcomes — this will hold unaudited; this cannot hold unaudited; this cannot be classified on the evidence available, and here is the single observation that would settle it.

Please write that worksheet, and attend to the following.

- Tell me which of Lewis's conditions must survive translation into questions a non-specialist can answer from documents, and which can be dropped without changing the verdicts we reach. My guess is that common knowledge and the clause about a convention being an arbitrary solution with alternatives are the load-bearing ones; say so plainly if I am wrong, and say what the procedure loses if they are dropped.
- Give me diagnostic questions that separate a genuine convention from the four things our members constantly confuse with one: a regularity sustained by a shared tool or vendor default rather than by mutual expectations; a regularity sustained only by the audit itself; a regularity that merely coincides with everyone's independent interest, so that there is no alternative anyone would ever choose; and a regularity that everyone conforms to while everyone privately wishes it were different.
- Where the procedure must hand the decision to the working-group chair because the question is one of values rather than fact — who bears a migration cost, whether a minority's implementation may be overridden — mark that in the worksheet at the step where it arises, not in a general caveat at the end.
- Flag every step that is a judgment call wearing a procedural costume. I would much rather be told that "almost everyone" is being quietly operationalised as "all but two members" than discover it during an appeal.
- Tell me what the procedure cannot do. I have been given to understand that the analysis is static — it classifies a regularity as of now and says nothing about whether it will still hold in five years — and persistence is precisely what our classification turns on. Correct me if Lewis offers more on that than I have been led to believe, and either way say how much of the gap the worksheet can honestly close and where it merely papers over it.

Then add a short section on requests of the other kind. Perhaps half of what reaches us is not a request to classify an existing practice but a request to create one: a working group has noticed drift and wants us to publish a convention that will bootstrap itself into existence. Say what the worksheet has to say about that — what would have to be true, in Lewis's terms, for a published text to generate the mutual expectations that make a convention self-enforcing; what typically goes wrong when a body like ours tries it; and what the cheapest reliable substitute is when it does go wrong.

Finally, run the worksheet on three candidates, showing the answers step by step and the terminal outcome for each.

1. Every member encodes a settlement-timing field in local time with a UTC offset rather than in UTC. No one has ever been required to do this; it appears to have spread because the two dominant middleware vendors ship that as their default. Our incident log shows roughly one inter-member dispute a year traceable to it.
2. Members are required, and audited, to acknowledge an inbound message within a fixed window. The last three audits found no violations. A working group proposes reclassifying this as a convention on the grounds that it is now universally followed and the audit is therefore redundant.
3. Members address one another by a legacy institution-code scheme that everyone on the committee agrees is worse than the newer alternative we published four years ago. Adoption of the alternative has reached eleven members and has not moved in eighteen months.

At least one of these should come out against me. Candidate 2 in particular is one I have already argued for at committee, and I want the worksheet's verdict on it whether or not it embarrasses me; please do not soften a verdict to spare me, and if you think one of my three cases is badly constructed as a test of the procedure, say so rather than answering it anyway.

Do not send me a reading list and do not cite anything — the team will read neither, and the worksheet has to stand on its own. Close by telling me how confident you are in the procedure you have given me: confident enough that I should let a reviewer with no philosophy training run it unsupervised on a live candidate, or not, and what supervision you would want in the meantime. Then single out the one claim in your answer that you are least sure of, and say what evidence or argument would show it false.
