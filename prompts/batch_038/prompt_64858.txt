I write literary fiction — three novels out, the fourth one being difficult — and I've developed a slightly shameful working habit. When a scene won't come, I go and read philosophers' thought experiments to see how they're built: the violinist you wake up plugged into, the man locked in the room with the baskets of Chinese symbols and a rulebook he can't read, the trolley and the fat stranger on the bridge, the machine that hands you a marvellous life while you float in a tank. That's where I learned about pacing, and about how much detail to give a reader before asking them to make a judgement. So I already know these things are rhetoric. I just think they're very good rhetoric, and I've started to suspect that's exactly why you people keep denying it.

I want to argue with you about this. Please play a philosopher who has made a career of showing that these cases can't bear the weight put on them. Not a general sceptic about philosophy — someone who does this professionally and thinks the damage is specific and mechanical: the vividness, the stipulated details, the order things arrive in, the unnamed vs named victim, the way a reader's flinch gets collected up and entered into the argument as though it were evidence. You're dry, a little impatient, and generous with small concessions — yes, some cases are genuinely informative, yes, you use examples yourself, yes, novelists are honest about what they're doing and philosophers often aren't — but you will never concede the main thing, which is that a well-written case can carry evidential force just in virtue of being well written. Your word for these things is intuition pump, and I'd like you to notice that the phrase is itself a small piece of persuasion, but don't let that rattle you.

Keep each reply to a paragraph or so; I want a conversation, not a paper. Stay in character until I say stop, and if something in what I say is ambiguous, ask me what I meant rather than guessing. British English throughout, please. And paraphrase rather than quote — I don't want invented attributions or half-remembered sentences in quotation marks, that's my line of work and I can smell it.

One thing I need from you, which is why I'm asking a model rather than a colleague: at the end of each reply, drop out of character for two lines and tell me how confident you are in the move you just made — proper confidence, not hedge-and-balance — and then name the single point in it you're least sure of. I want the gaps in the armour marked, because that's where I'm going to push.

Here's my opening. Your whole project eats itself. You say people's verdicts about these cases are artefacts of how the cases are written: shift the framing, reverse the order, give the victim a name and a daughter, and the verdict moves. Agreed, that happens. But how did you find out that it happens? By noticing that when you change the telling, the judgements change — and that noticing is itself a judgement about cases, arrived at by exactly the faculty you're discrediting. So either some case-driven judgements survive contact with rhetoric, in which case you don't have the sweeping result you want and you owe me an account of which ones survive and why; or none of them do, in which case you've argued yourself out of the evidence you'd need to run your own argument. Which one is it, and if it's the first, dont you just sound like me now — a person with taste in examples calling it a method?
