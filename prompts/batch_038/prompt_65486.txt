I teach one eighty-minute seminar in a second-year undergraduate module on judgment and decision making, and my assigned slot is Herbert Simon's satisficing and bounded rationality. The cohort is about forty students from psychology, economics, and computer science; they have all had a first-year statistics course, one lecture on Bayesian decision theory the week before mine, and the week after mine is the heuristics-and-biases material. Several of the computer science students are simultaneously building small search or optimization projects for another module, which is why I care a great deal about the framing. What I want from you is a plan for how to teach the idea, not an exposition of it — I know what satisficing is, and a restatement of the view is the one thing that will waste my hour.

The three assimilations I expect them to arrive with, and want broken, are these: that satisficing is a bias, i.e. laziness or failure of nerve dressed up as a theory; that it is trivially equivalent to maximizing a utility function net of search costs, which is what the modelers in the room will insist; and that the subject is really about personality type, because some of them have picked up the popular maximizer-versus-satisficer framing that circulates from Barry Schwartz's work and will assume the question is which kind of person you should be. Please design the session's explanatory arc with those in view: the order in which the components must be introduced — aspiration level, the cost of continued search, the fact that the agent cannot represent the full option set in the first place, the adaptivity of aspiration levels in response to what the search turns up, procedural as against substantive rationality, and the point that the environment's structure is part of what makes a simple rule succeed — together with an explicit account of what depends on what and which reorderings would cost something. Where in that sequence does the regress worry belong: if the agent must decide how much search to undertake, that decision is itself a search problem with its own costs, which is precisely what blocks the "it's just optimization with a price on information" reduction?

I also want you to settle the carrying example and police it. I had been going to build the whole session around the haystack image — searching for a needle and stopping as soon as you find one sharp enough for the job at hand — but tell me whether that, or apartment hunting, or something drawn from the students' own optimization projects is the stronger carrier, and specify exactly what the example must exhibit and what it must conspicuously lack, since a badly chosen example will quietly reintroduce the conflation I am trying to prevent. Then map the misconceptions in the order the students will actually raise them, and for each say the point in the sequence where it must be headed off rather than answered at the end; I expect some version of "satisficing is just lazy maximizing," "you can always restate it as constrained optimization," "bounded rationality is what the heuristics people study," and "aspiration levels are arbitrary, so nothing is being explained." Tell me as well whether the ecological-rationality material — the claim that fast, frugal heuristics can outperform optimization in noisy environments — belongs in my session or would sabotage the following week, and if it belongs in, where. Finish with one question I can put to the room in the last five minutes whose pattern of answers would separate a student who understood satisficing as a claim about the structure of the choice problem from a student who merely learned a respectable word for settling, and with a short list of what I should openly refuse to cover at this level and announce as refused rather than silently omit. One last request: give me the two-minute bridge out of expected utility theory into the topic that does not begin by announcing that Simon rejected it, because if I open with that they will spend the rest of the hour waiting for a punchline that never arrives.
