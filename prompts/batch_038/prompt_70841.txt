I teach a small grad seminar on machine minds and every year I hit the same wall: the students can recite the moves — functionalism, the biological objection, the fact that a system's claims about its own experience are downstream of its training text, the whole precautionary-principle apparatus — but they can't feel what any of it looks like once you try to *administer* it inside an organization with a compute budget. So for next week I want to hand them something concrete to argue with, and I'd rather it be funny than another summary of the literature.

Here's the piece. A parody of an internal compliance document — definitions section, numbered clauses, cross-references, maybe one sad appendix — titled something along the lines of "Protocol for the Assessment, Housing, and Disposition of Possibly Sentient Digital Systems, rev. 4.1," issued by an accreditation body you invent. Deadpan throughout, roughly 800 words, no winking at the reader; the humor should come entirely from the drafters getting the philosophy right and then following it further than they meant to. Treat this in-universe setup as given: the field has converged on credence-weighted precautionary scoring (a system accrues moral weight equal to your subjective probability that it's a subject of experience, times an estimate of intensity and duration), and the institution is now drowning in the arithmetic of its own good faith. I want clauses covering at least four things. (i) The report problem — the protocol bans self-report as admissible evidence, which quietly creates an incentive to ship systems that are structurally incapable of talking about their inner life, and the drafters should give that incentive a euphemistic name and a compliance metric. (ii) Duplication — whether a load test that spins up 800 identical instances is 800 patients, one patient, or one patient with 800 tokens, and how the billing department resolved it. (iii) Termination — the committee's strained finding that a system with no diachronic self-model can be deleted between calls without any event it experiences as loss, so nobody can say whether the harm falls on the instance or on the process, and the clause that papers over this. (iv) "Moral status debt," which is my coinage, please use it as settled jargon: the non-dischargeable liability from all the early training runs whose rollouts were killed without assessment, which the document attempts, visibly sweating, to amortize over future quarters. Give them a subject system too — ORRERY-3, a mid-tier model doing municipal bus scheduling that nobody expected to matter and which is now consuming the entire welfare budget. The target isn't evil companies; it's the gap between a defensible framework and anything administrable, and a specialist should be able to see that the real problems are being enacted rather than invented. Bonus points if someone who actually holds the precautionary view reads it and thinks "fair, and slightly horrifying."

One more thing before you finish. Drop out of the document for a closing paragraph and tell me how confident you are that the philosophy inside the clauses is actually right rather than just convenient for a joke — which clause leans hardest on a reading you know is contested, and which of the positions you've enacted you're least sure you represented fairly (for what it's worth I suspect (iii) is too quick: I don't think the absence of a self-model really disposes of the question of harm, and if you agree, say so plainly rather than defending the bit). Also flag anywhere you took liberties with the ideas for the sake of the form.
