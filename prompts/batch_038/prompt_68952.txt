You are writing as a philosopher of biology who has worked carefully through the adaptationism debates that followed Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper. Please brainstorm two or three candidate principles before settling on one, and note which clause you would cut first if the account turns out to be too baroque.

I have read a great deal of this literature secondhand, mostly through blog arguments, and I would like to know what my own verdicts commit me to. Here they are, in the order they occurred to me.

1. The redness of my blood: not an adaptation. Heme chemistry did that; selection was after oxygen transport. But the pH-sensitivity of the very same molecule plainly was selected. So whatever principle I hold has to attach to properties of traits, not to traits.
2. Male nipples: not an adaptation, just a byproduct of a shared developmental template. I am confident here.
3. The female orgasm: the structurally identical argument seems available, yet I am far less confident, and I suspect my confidence is tracking how politically loaded the question has become rather than the evidence. Tell me whether that suspicion is itself a mistake.
4. The human chin: I keep wanting to call it an adaptation, because it is universal in my species, absent in apes, and I feel that somebody must have been better off for having it — while also conceding it might simply fall out of the relative growth rates of two jaw fields. Unresolved.
5. The appendix: I have read there is a proposal that it acts as a reservoir for gut flora, and I still refuse to call it an adaptation. Meanwhile goosebumps, which seem to do nothing useful now at all, I am happy to call an adaptation, merely a vestigial one. Note that I am letting present utility matter in one case and not the other.
6. Reading and arithmetic: not adaptations, though the visual and numerical machinery they recruit is. A co-opted use does not make the co-opted thing designed.
7. The recurrent laryngeal nerve's detour down the neck and back up: neither adaptation nor spandrel, but a record of phylogenetic history. My taxonomy evidently needs a third category.

Is there a single principle that delivers all seven? State it precisely, clause by clause, with the case motivating each clause; then say which of these verdicts a simpler principle would force me to abandon, and what your preferred principle predicts for cases I have not raised — the descended human larynx, laughter, music, the attractiveness of facial symmetry, menopause. Finally, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested; I assume that whether "adaptation" is fixed by origin or by current function is one of the live disputes, but correct me if I have that wrong, and do not dress a contested position up as the consensus.
