The cohort I am responsible for is forty-two students from roughly fifteen countries, about a third of whom have already worked in a ministry or an NGO, and in three weeks I deliver their ninety-minute opening session in a required public-policy master's programme: one whiteboard, no slides, no prior social-science methods. The session exists to install a single distinction they will need for the rest of the term — tight versus loose cultures, that is, how strongly a society's norms are held and how much deviance from them it tolerates — because in the implementation unit that follows they will be reading about why an identically worded regulation produces radically different compliance in different jurisdictions, and I want this to be the instrument they reach for. What I need from you is not an explanation of tightness and looseness but a plan for how I should explain it: the order the components must go in and what depends on what. My own instinct is to open with the fact that one and the same society is tight in some situations and loose in others — a funeral versus a nightclub, a courtroom versus a park — before anyone is permitted to say a country's name aloud, precisely so that the construct does not get filed under national character. Tell me whether that ordering is right, and what has to be in place before it can land: I suspect they need norm strength and severity of sanctioning separated as two distinct dimensions before variation-by-situation means anything to them, but I do not know how to make that separation vivid in under five minutes, nor whether it should be made abstractly or through the example itself.

Then tell me what carries the session. I want one example, or at most a matched pair of miniatures, that I can return to at every stage of the ninety minutes rather than a parade of illustrations. The constraints matter: it cannot be a country-versus-country comparison; it cannot concern a sexual, religious, or ethnic norm, because the room is too mixed and it would consume the session; it cannot be anything they will arrive with a rehearsed opinion about; and it should let me show both that tightness purchases something real and that it costs something real, so that the payoff is not a slogan. Say explicitly what your chosen case must have and what it must lack, and say what you would substitute if it fails in the room.

The misconception map is what I most need, and I want it sequenced rather than listed. Here are the ones I am bracing for; for each, tell me whether it is genuinely a misconception, partly correct, or in fact what I should want them to end up believing: that tightness is a description of an authoritarian government, so the concept is really about politics; that loose is simply the freer and therefore better end of a scale; that a culture is a property of a nation and hence of each of its members; that once one has been told tightness tracks population density, resource scarcity, natural disaster and disease history, the stricter societies are retroactively justified in their strictness; and that the whole construct is a stereotype with a number attached. Add the two or three I have not thought of, and for every one of them mark the exact point in the sequence at which it must be headed off rather than repaired afterwards — I have learned that announcing I will come back to something later guarantees I never do. Two further requests. Within the first quarter of an hour somebody will say that in their country people would never behave in some particular way; build the plan so that the remark becomes usable material instead of an incident I have to referee, and so that it does not trigger a round of competing national portraits. And at the end, give me one thing to set them — a question or a small judgment call answerable on paper in three minutes — that would show me they have acquired the distinction rather than merely agreed with me, and that would come out wrong if all they had learned was to say that it depends on the situation. Finally, tell me what I should deliberately omit at this level and announce as omitted: the measurement instruments, the merely correlational status of the threat findings, the relationship between tightness and individualism–collectivism, and above all the normative question of how much rule-following a society ought to demand of its members. That last one is what half the room will want to litigate in the first ten minutes, so tell me what to say that defers it honestly without making it look as though I am ducking it.
