You are, for the purposes of this request, a professional philosopher of epistemology who has also spent years designing judgment-and-decision-making exercises for classrooms — someone equally at home in the literature on overconfident forecasting and in the practical problem of getting twenty teenagers to do arithmetic on the floor of a noisy room. Please think carefully before answering, and please begin by brainstorming rather than committing: sketch three or four distinct activity skeletons that could serve my purpose, note in a sentence or two what each one would teach and what it structurally could not, and only then choose one and develop it in full.

I am seventeen and I run my school's decision-making society. We meet twice a week for forty-five minutes and typical attendance is nineteen students aged fifteen to seventeen. Most have had one term of elementary probability — they know what a mean and a standard deviation are — none have studied statistics formally, and none have encountered the psychological literature on forecasting. My faculty sponsor has given me both meetings of one week for this activity. Materials are limited to paper, pens, index cards, masking tape, a whiteboard, a phone timer, and one six-sided die per group. There is no budget and no real money: any payoff scheme must use a point currency I invent, redeemable for nothing more consequential than bragging rights and first choice of snacks.

What I want the students to feel — and to feel before anyone utters the phrases planning fallacy, inside view, outside view, or reference class forecasting — is the following. First, that a careful, detailed, sincerely held plan can make a forecast worse rather than better, and that the vividness of the plan is precisely what does the damage. Second, that being handed the base rate does not by itself break the spell: students who have just been shown the distribution of everyone else's results will still place themselves above it. Third, that choosing the comparison class is itself a judgment and not a lookup, and that there is a motivated move always available — narrow the class until your own case looks exceptional. I would like at least one group to make that move out loud and be required to defend it to the others. Fourth, that epistemic modesty is a practice with a price attached, not a mood. And critically, I do not want the takeaway to be that the outside view always wins: some groups should genuinely turn out to be exceptional, so that the lesson is calibrated adjustment rather than surrender to averages.

Constraints on the design. Participants must make real forecasts with real points at stake, and the scoring must be proper — I understand that a naive reward for the nearest guess does not punish overconfidence in the way I need — yet it must be computable by hand, in under two minutes, by a sixteen-year-old. Design the elicitation format (point estimate, probability across five buckets, both?) and show me the arithmetic explicitly. The reference-class data must exist and be real rather than invented in the room: my plan is to run the task once beforehand with a different class and hand the results over as a case file containing several candidate reference classes with different base rates — everyone who attempted it, only groups using the same materials, only groups with a practice round, only groups containing a member who had done something similar before. Develop that idea, or replace it with something better, but do not let the base rates be conjured on the spot. I would also like at least one role — a librarian or red-team position, say — whose holder is forbidden from hearing the group's plan and is responsible only for assembling the comparison cases, as an institutional analogue of separating forecasters from promoters. Decide whether you need one session or both, and give me a minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, the room setup, the exact wording of any instructions I should read aloud, and the point values.

Then the debrief: map particular moments of what happened in the room onto particular parts of the distinction; give me five or six questions with the answers I should be listening for; and tell me what to say when a student objects that the base rate simply does not apply to us. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — the group that games the scoring rule by spreading probability evenly across every bucket, the student who has genuinely done the task a hundred times at home, the public humiliation of being spectacularly wrong in front of one's friends, the group that finishes early and starts heckling — and how to handle each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the inside/outside view distinction this activity cannot reproduce, and which I must therefore supply in words.

Please do not send me a reading list or citations; I want the design itself, not the bibliography. Where my constraints genuinely conflict, say so, and tell me which one you chose to break and why.
