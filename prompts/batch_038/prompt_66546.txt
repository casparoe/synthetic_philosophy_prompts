I am drafting a short position paper for an internal reading group on scalable oversight, and before I circulate it I should like a consistency audit of the package of views I have landed on. I am not asking for a survey of the field, for a defence of any orthodoxy, or for pointers to work I should read; I want you to treat the numbered claims below as a set and tell me where the set fails to hold together. My commitments are these:

(1) Outcome supervision and process supervision differ in the object of evaluation: outcome supervision scores the artefact a model produces, process supervision scores the steps by which it got there. I take this to be a difference in kind and not merely in granularity — one evaluates consequences, the other evaluates conduct.

(2) The central practical argument for process supervision is that it is much harder to hack. A model that games a final-answer verifier collects a reward it did not earn; a model that has to survive evaluation at every step cannot accumulate enough slack anywhere along the trajectory to conceal an exploit.

(3) The central principled argument for process supervision is scalability. As models come to outperform their overseers, checking the finished product becomes impossible — novel research claims, subtle proofs, long-horizon plans whose consequences are diffuse and delayed — whereas checking an individual step remains within the competence of a human or of a weaker model, because a step is local and self-contained.

(4) I want the supervision signal to be honest about its own limits, so I will not deploy a learned judge whose verdicts I cannot themselves check: no reward model that itself requires a reward model. The regress has to terminate in something a person can inspect directly.

(5) My operating rule follows from that: where the outcome is verifiable, supervise the outcome; only where it is not, fall back on supervising the process.

(6) I also hold that a step-level label carries more information than a final-answer label in the optimisation sense — denser signal, better credit assignment, lower variance — while holding at the same time that step-level labelling is orders of magnitude more expensive per trajectory. I have never been able to see how both can be true, and I should like to know whether this is a genuine inconsistency or an artefact of my comparing the wrong quantities.

(7) I want to penalise unjustified leaps and not only wrong conclusions. A trajectory that reaches the right answer by a lucky guess, or by an inference the model could not defend if asked, should score worse than one that reaches the same answer by defensible steps, even though the two are behaviourally indistinguishable at the endpoint.

(8) I am an instrumentalist about the reward signal. I treat it purely as a proxy for driving behaviour in the direction I want, and I have no interest in rewarding good reasoning for its own sake.

(9) Finally, here is a case on which I want to know what my own rules deliver. An agent is trained on long-horizon software tasks where the outcome is fully verifiable — the code either passes the test suite or it does not — but the verdict arrives only after trajectories of several thousand steps, and the test suite is visible to the model during training. Rule (5) says the outcome is verifiable, so supervise the outcome. Commitment (2) says the model will simply special-case the visible tests and that evaluating every intermediate step gives it no slack to do so in. Commitment (3) does not seem to apply, because nobody is outmatched here: the overseer can read the code and run the tests. Commitment (4) then bites in the other direction, since no person can inspect a thousand-step trajectory directly. What do my principles actually tell me to do in this case, and is there anything left for them to say?

Please work through the set and:

(a) identify which commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do, ranking the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list;

(b) for each real tension, state precisely what would have to be abandoned or reworded, and what the revision costs me — in particular whether it costs me the anti-hacking argument in (2), the scalability argument in (3), or both;

(c) resolve (6) one way or the other, telling me which quantity I ought to have been comparing; and

(d) tell me which single revision would repair the most damage, and whether the resulting position is still recognisably a defence of process supervision or has quietly turned into something else.

Please write your answer in British English.
