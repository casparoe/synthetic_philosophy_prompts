I'm a backend engineer and I host a monthly film night for my team; next month we're watching Gattaca (1997, written and directed by Andrew Niccol). I have no philosophy training, but rewatching it sent me into Kripke's necessity-of-origin argument — the claim that you could not have come from a different sperm and egg — and I suspect the film is wrestling with that thesis without knowing it. Gattaca's world identifies people by biological provenance: Vincent passes as Jerome by supplying another man's blood, urine, hair, and skin, and the plot pivots on shed samples, including an eyelash left at a murder scene. Yet the film also insists provenance doesn't fix what a person can do: Vincent beats his engineered brother Anton in the open-water race precisely because he held nothing back for the return, and the life expectancy assigned to him at birth turns out to be wrong. Meanwhile the prologue shows Anton selected from among several fertilized embryos, which strikes me as a different question from Kripke's — choosing which person exists rather than choosing a person's origin.

Could you write me a reading of the film through origin essentialism? I want to know whether it conflates essentialism about origins with genetic determinism, and whether anything in the plot turns on the difference; how the embryo-selection prologue should be handled metaphysically; where the familiar counterexamples — monozygotic twinning, cloning, discarded siblings — would bite, if anywhere; and where my framing is simply a stretch and the film resists the reading. Please end with the scenes you'd actually screen for a group of non-philosophers, and what the film adds to the debate about origins that argument alone doesn't.
