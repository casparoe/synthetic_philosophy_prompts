So when a US agency runs a cost-benefit analysis of a regulation it has to discount: a statistical life saved or a ton of carbon avoided in 2125 gets entered at some small fraction of the same thing next year, and the rate is picked by looking at what private capital or government debt actually returns. Meanwhile the same government licenses a nuclear waste repository on the requirement that it isolate its contents for ten thousand years, as if harms that remote counted at full face value — and it spends plenty of effort protecting people from their own impatience, with automatic enrollment in retirement saving and caps on payday lending.

What does the discounting requirement actually presuppose about what we owe the future? Is it committed to pure time preference, later welfare mattering less just because its later, or can it run entirely on other premises — that future people will be richer so their marginal utility is lower, or that the rate merely tracks the opportunity cost of capital? Tell me which rival views the practice rules out and which it's only consistent with, since I suspect several very different theories license the same arithmetic. Then: can it live alongside the ten-thousand-year waste standard and the anti-impatience programs, or is one of those quietly smuggling in a philosophy the discounting denies? And if you concluded pure time preference was ethically indefensible — a weakness of imagination rather than a principle — what would have to change in how these analyses get built, besides the number?
