I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and I have gotten myself into difficulty on a book that is due in the spring. The working title is Finite Stakes, and its third section is a long two-hander set at an airport gate: Miriam, a retired actuary who spent thirty years pricing catastrophe bonds and who came to Pascal late in life through his correspondence with Fermat on the division of stakes, is accosted by a young man who claims to speak for a machine intelligence not yet built. He tells her that if she hands over the eleven hundred dollars in her envelope there is a chance — he will not say how small, but he volunteers that it might be one in a hundred trillion trillion — that his patron will bring about an astronomically large number of saved and flourishing lives, and that if she refuses, the same arithmetic runs in the other direction. She has to answer him in the voice of a woman who has actually read the Wager fragment rather than the greeting-card version of it, and my editor has warned me, fairly, that readers will check the mathematics. So I want help understanding the passage itself before I let her open her mouth. My French is rusty and this is my own rendering, made for the novel's notes from the stretch of the fragment in which Pascal imagines a coin being tossed and asks his interlocutor to call it: heads, God exists; tails, he does not. Weigh, he says, what you stand to gain against what you stand to lose: call heads and you gain everything if you win and lose nothing if you lose, so call it without flinching. The interlocutor objects that he may be putting too much on the table. Pascal replies that they should look into that: the coin being even, if what you could win were only two lives for the one you risk, you would still be right to play; if it were three lives, it would be senseless, since you cannot decline to play at all, not to hazard one life to take three where the odds of losing and winning are the same. But what is on offer here is an infinity of infinitely happy lives, with one chance of winning and a finite number of chances of losing, and what you put up is finite. Wherever the infinite comes in, he says, the partition is destroyed; there is no less disproportion between the infinite and the finite than between that one chance of winning and the chances massed against it; you hazard the finite against the infinite, and the hazard of gaining is no smaller than the hazard of losing. The fragment then turns, as I am sure you know, away from arithmetic altogether and toward the passions and a regimen of behaving as though one already believed. Since the rendering is mine, read through its clumsiness — but tell me if you think I have distorted something that matters.

Four things I need from you, and they are not the four things I would have asked a month ago. First: does the stipulation that the coin is even do any real work? Pascal seems to need it for the two-lives and three-lives cases, where a bad price can make a bet bad; but once the payoff is infinite, is the equality of the chances load-bearing at all, or is it scaffolding he could strike without touching the conclusion? Second: what is the partition line actually saying? I have a hunch that it is a claim about magnitudes rather than about odds — something continuous with his mathematical thinking about the infinite and the infinitesimal — and that the phrase about the disproportion between the infinite and the finite is doing more work than translators let on. Tell me whether that hunch can be made precise, and what would have to be true of it for the sentence to be more than rhetoric. Third, and this is the crux for my scene: does this fragment license my young man at the gate? He insists the stake is finite, that the losing chances are finite, and that the winning payoff is beyond any number I can write down — which is to say that he has reproduced Pascal's own three conditions and merely made the probability explicit and made it tiny. Miriam's instinct is that something in the fragment blocks him, but she cannot yet say what: whether it is the even coin, or the demand that the number of chances against be finite, or the fact that Pascal's wager is put to a person once, about the whole of a life, whereas my young man can come back tomorrow with a larger figure. Fourth: adjudicate a disagreement I cannot settle. An old friend of mine who spent his career in probability theory says the objection to the mugger is not probabilistic but about iterability — that Pascal's fragment has no defense against an unbounded series of demands, since each one individually satisfies the fragment's conditions. A former colleague of mine who read theology says the whole arithmetic is a shell, a trap set for a gambler's imagination, and that the argument is really the closing turn about the passions and the regimen of practice, so that reading it as a calculation is the original sin of its reception. Which of these two is closer to right, or are they both missing the same thing? If your answer implies a sentence Miriam could plausibly say aloud at the gate, give it to me at the end.

Two constraints on how you answer. Please write in American English, and please do not send me to the literature: I have no way of checking a bibliography, and a list of secondary sources would be worse than useless to me, since the scene has to stand on its own two feet. Reason from the passage as I have given it, and where you are relying on something about the original that my rendering may have flattened, say so explicitly rather than silently correcting me. I do want you to commit — tell me which of the two readings you think is right, and tell me about the even coin and the partition line in your own voice rather than hedging behind competing interpretations. But having committed, tell me what would change your mind: which argument, or which feature of the passage, would move you to the other side; where in your own reasoning you think the weakest link sits, given that you are working from a novelist's translation and not from the French; and, if my young man's case is genuinely blocked by the fragment, what the most serious reply on his behalf would be — the one I should put in Miriam's mouth so that she is defeating a real opponent and not a scarecrow.
