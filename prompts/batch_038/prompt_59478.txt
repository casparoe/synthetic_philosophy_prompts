I've been doing this thing for about eleven months now and I want to get it down as a story before I lose the feel of it, because I don't think I can explain it in prose without sounding like a productivity blog. Background: Franklin's moral perfection project from the autobiography — the thirteen virtues with their little precepts, a page per virtue, each page ruled in red into seven columns for the days of the week and crossed by thirteen lines, and you put a black spot on a line for every fault you catch yourself in that day, with one virtue "on duty" per week so you're only really attending to that one while the other twelve ride along on chance. He says somewhere in there that "habit took the advantage of inattention," which is the whole problem in six words. He also gave up, sort of: he tells a story about a man who buys an ax and wants the entire head ground as bright as the edge, gets the smith to agree, cranks the wheel himself until his arms are gone and the ax comes out blotchy, and decides he'll take it as is because a speckled ax suits him better anyway. And the detail I love — he wore his first paper book full of holes from scraping out old spots to make room for new ones, so he moved the whole grid onto the ivory leaves of a memorandum book where you could wipe the marks clean and start a fresh course.

Here's the thing I hit around week forty, and it's the part I want the story to be about. To mark a fault honestly you have to notice the fault, and noticing costs exactly the attention you were supposed to be spending on the virtue that's on duty that week. So the marking generates new faults on lines that had been clean — I snap at someone because I'm monitoring my own tone, I miss a deadline because I'm reconstructing my morning to figure out whether something counted as a breach of Sincerity. I fixed this the way you'd fix a bug in a spreadsheet: I added a fourteenth line, at the bottom, for faults committed in the course of recording. It stayed blank for nine weeks. Then I caught myself not looking at it, and realized the fourteenth line is blank for the same reason the first thirteen looked clean in week one — nobody's checking. There's no fifteenth line and there can't be, and the humility entry ("Imitate Jesus and Socrates") is the one that never gets a spot, which is either the good news or the bad news, I can't tell which.

So: write me a short story, somewhere around 1,600–2,400 words, that dramatizes this. Not an essay with a character bolted on, and please no scene where somebody articulates the theme out loud. Some specs, because they're the parts I keep getting wrong when I try it myself:

- Setting: the overnight shift at a mid-priced hotel off a state highway, 11 to 7 — a night auditor, so the job is literally balancing the day's numbers while the building sleeps. The chart lives folded inside the audit binder. Paper and pencil, obviously; no app, no spreadsheet, and the object has to show wear.
- Franklin doesn't appear and nobody summarizes the autobiography in dialogue. If the speckled ax shows up at all it comes out of a person's mouth as a joke or an insult, not as a lesson.
- The fourteenth line has to be load-bearing in the plot, not just mentioned in paragraph two. Something has to happen because of it, or in the space where checking it would have been.
- Include one other person — a coworker, a guest at 4 a.m., whoever — who sees the chart and misreads it in a specific and plausible way. Not "that's so toxic," something more concrete and wrong.
- End mid-shift, on a decision about a mark: make one, wipe one, or set the pencil down. Don't tell me what the character concludes the practice means, and don't resolve whether they got better or just got better at recording. I want that to still be genuinely undecidable when I finish.

Give it a title too. If you need a voice, something dry and physical is better than anything lyrical — the smell of the carpet cleaner, the hum of the ice machine, that kind of thing.
