Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of four hours about how much a citizen should be able to keep from the state, and whenever my argument seemed to land he retired to the same sentence: if you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear. What made it hard to answer was that he deployed it twice, pointing in opposite directions. He began with it as an objection to a data protection law of the German kind — a citizen with nothing discreditable on their record has no interest at stake in what the state collects, so consent requirements, purpose limitation and the rest are ceremony for the guilty. Later, when I said that information flows in more than one direction, he produced a counter-example: Norway and Finland publish individual income tax data, which I concede I do not object to, and he took my concession as proof that my real principle was nothing more than distrust of whichever government I happened to dislike. I still think both uses fail, but I could not locate the joint at which they come apart, and I suspect the slogan's rhetorical strength lies in how many different claims it can be made to carry.

So treat the sentence as a piece of language to be disambiguated rather than as a position to be refuted. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, ordered from the platitudinous to the radical, and for each say what it commits one to, whether it is true, and whether it would actually license either of my friend's two conclusions — the anti-data-protection one and the tax-publicity one, assessed separately. Please stay attentive to the parameters that seem to be doing silent work: whose "nothing to hide" is in question (mine, or the population's in aggregate); the direction of disclosure (citizen to state, state to citizen, citizen to citizen); what exactly is being hidden (facts about past conduct, or the authority to decide when and to whom one is disclosed, which is roughly how the German census ruling of 1983 framed the interest); the time index (nothing to hide under today's rules and technologies, versus nothing that will remain unobjectionable under future ones); and the function of the system in view — detection and punishment, or continuous modulation of conduct, as in social-credit scoring, where a spotless record is precisely no protection at all.

Then tell me which reading the people who repeat the slogan most likely mean, and where the slide between readings happens when it is used to end an argument. Most of all I want to know whether any single reading handles both of his cases without ad-hocery, so that I can say whether his charge of double standards is answerable or merely resistible. Finally, please mark how confident you are in each verdict you give, and single out the one point in the analysis you are least sure of.
