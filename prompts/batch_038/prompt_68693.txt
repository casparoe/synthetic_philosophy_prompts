me and a few mates do a monthly "big questions" night down the pub and next month I've got to lay out where I've landed on free will and blame. I never studied any of this, it's all podcasts and a couple of library books and too much thinking on the bus, so before I say it out loud to people I want the load-bearing bits checked. Here's my list, roughly:

1. Determinism isn't the interesting threat. Even if every event is fixed by the past plus the laws, I don't see how that's the thing that makes blame unfair. What matters for responsibility is whether the action came out of the person — their own reasons, their own character, their own choosing — not whether the universe had other options on the shelf.

2. The implant cases convince me. Guy's got a chip in his head, someone's monitoring it, and if it looks like he's about to choose anything other than doing the bad thing, the chip fires and makes him do it anyway. In the actual run of events it never fires, he does it himself for his own reasons. I think he's exactly as blameworthy as the identical guy with no chip.

3. But I also hold that blame is only fair if the person could of avoided doing the thing. No way out, no blame. That one feels non-negotiable to me.

4. Related: I don't think we have free will if by that you mean "could genuinely have done otherwise." I just don't think that's the sense responsibility needs.

5. I'm not moved by the "well there's always some little bit of freedom left over" replies — the hesitation, the moment of forming the intention, whatever. Those flickery leftovers don't seem like the kind of thing that could hold up the whole weight of being blameworthy. And anyway I reckon you could always redesign the chip so it catches those too, so there's no stable place for them to retreat to.

6. Thought experiments should be more or less buildable. I don't want a theory of blame resting on a device that couldn't exist. If the setup needs magic, it doesn't get to do any work.

7. Though I do find the trolley cases useful, and the one about plugging into a machine that gives you a perfect fake life, and I think those ship-of-Theseus style cases genuinely tell you something about who a person is. Those feel different to me from an impossible chip, but I can't say how.

8. I've also been telling people the implant is a distraction, and my preferred version has no implant at all. Just an infallible predictor: something that knows for certain what he'll do, and it can't be wrong, that's a law of nature. No intervention, nothing fires, he does it himself. I think that's the cleaner case and it should convince even the people who keep hunting round for leftover flickers.

9. Last one. When someone really had no way out — they were forced, or something was inside their head making them do it — I think the right response isn't blame at all, it's closer to grief or horror. Blame has to be aimed at what the person's will was like, and if the will wasn't in it there's nothing to aim at.

So: go through these and tell me which ones actually pull against each other and which only look like they do. I want them ranked by how bad they are, not just listed off as equally dodgy, and for each real conflict I want the cheapest fix plus what that fix costs me elsewhere on the list. Then at the end tell me the single revision that would clean up the most. If there's a name for a distinction I'm failing to make, give me the name but explain it plainly — I don't want a lit review or a reading list, I want the audit.
