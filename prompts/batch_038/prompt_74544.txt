Okay so I'm a second-year grad student and I've spent the last eight months coding famines for my supervisor's dataset — it's for an NGO that wants a defensible way of saying which famines were crimes and which were disasters, mostly so they can write advocacy reports without getting accused of partisanship. My supervisor Miriam coded the anchor cases herself, I've been coding the rest, and we compare notes every fortnight. She's currently offline in an archive for three weeks (terrible wifi, answers email about once a week if you're lucky), and on Friday I have to hand the funder a draft coding manual that actually states the rule we're applying. Problem: neither of us has ever written the rule down. We've just been agreeing on cases, and I've started to worry we've been agreeing for different reasons. It came to a head last week when I got into an argument in a pub with a friend who's Irish about whether the Famine was a crime, and I realised I literally could not say what my own position was, which is a bit of a problem for someone whose thesis chapter is about this.

Here's her anchor coding. The scale is three-way: D = natural disaster (weather or biology did it and the state did what could reasonably be expected), M = mixed/political failure (culpable and structural, but not something you'd put in a criminal frame), C = political crime (there are identifiable perpetrators and the starvation is properly described as something done to people). The notes in brackets are mine, from the coding meetings.

1. Ukraine 1932–33 → C. [procurement quotas raised after the harvest was known to have failed, seed grain taken, villages blacklisted and cut off from supplies, borders closed, no passports for peasants, grain still going out, officials on the ground writing to Moscow]
2. Bengal 1943 → C. [partial crop shortfall in one province, wartime inflation and speculation, the denial policies that stripped the coastal districts of boats and rice surpluses, shipping requests refused, wartime press censorship, the victims had no vote and no seat at any table]
3. China 1959–61 → C. [procurement on the basis of inflated yields, exports continuing through the worst year, the purge at Lushan after anyone who reported the truth, hukou keeping people from leaving the villages where they were starving, statistics falsified all the way up]
4. Ireland 1845–50 → M, which I did not expect. [genuine biological trigger that wiped out the only thing the landless class ate; grain and livestock kept leaving the country, so it isn't a disaster; but the state didn't take the food, the market did, and it did ship in maize and run public works and soup kitchens, throttled as they were by ideology about pauperisation and the quarter-acre clause; no plan to starve anyone that I can find in the record]
5. North Korea 1994–98 → M, and she flagged this as the one she's least happy with. [floods plus the loss of cheap Soviet fuel and fertiliser, the ration system just stopped working, the regime put the army first and was slow and obstructive about outside aid, zero press freedom or voice of any kind; but her note says something like "they didn't take the grain, they stopped handing it out — not the same act"]
6. Botswana, the drought years of the early-to-mid '80s → D. [serious rainfall failure and huge livestock losses, but an employment-based relief program that actually got cash and food to people, elections and a domestic press, no extraction, and no mass starvation]

Now the case I have to code by Friday. It's a synthetic one she wrote for the methods appendix — deliberately a composite, so the funder can't say we're taking sides on a live situation — and I have never been able to guess where she'd put it. Features as she wrote them: two failed rainy seasons in a peripheral semi-arid region; the regional harvest down by more than half, national production roughly at trend, the country a net food importer anyway. Competitive elections, an opposition that raised the crisis in parliament, a domestic press reporting the deaths from month five, the affected region holding about 6 percent of the seats. The state marketing board kept buying from the region at a fixed official price throughout and shipping the grain to the capital and to an export contract; treasury lawyers advised that unilateral suspension would trip a cross-default clause and close off the credit lines needed to buy imported food; on paper those board purchases were the region's main cash income. From month three the government had donor early-warning bulletins and its own district mortality returns; from month six it ran food-for-work reaching maybe a third of those assessed as needing help, with sites sited for administrative convenience rather than near anyone. There was a strategic grain reserve it chose not to draw down becuase the treasury thought that would spook urban prices. And in the internal minutes we're given, nobody proposes that these people starve — the drought is discussed as weather and the procurement as a contractual obligation.
