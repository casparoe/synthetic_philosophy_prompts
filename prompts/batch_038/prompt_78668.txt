You are to act as a philosopher with serious working knowledge of population ethics, welfare biology, and conservation practice, and you should think this through carefully rather than answering on first impression. Please begin by brainstorming: before you commit to any prediction, enumerate the candidate decision rules that could generate the record I am about to describe, test each one against every item in that record, and eliminate those that cannot survive all of it. Only then move to the new case. I want the reasoning exposed, not just the conclusion, and I want you to be willing to say that the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome.

I sit on the welfare panel of a regional land trust. Our charter is deliberately narrow: when the trust considers managing a habitat it holds, or considers whether to endorse or oppose somebody else's management of one, we are asked to record a judgement about whether the change would improve or worsen the welfare of the wild animals affected, taken as a whole and weighted by nothing else. In six years we have issued the following verdicts, and I have given you the reasons as they were minuted, which in several cases are clearly not the whole story. (i) We endorsed eradicating an introduced rodent population from an island reserve we manage, where four decades of trapping records showed the population cycling into mass winter starvation after mast years, with no predators and no prospect of natural control. (ii) We declined to endorse supplementary winter feeding intended to dampen the crash phase of a naturally cyclic small-mammal population on our own grassland, even though the crash involves heavy starvation mortality and the dynamics there are among the best-studied in the country; the minuted reason was that feeding would raise the number of animals born into a cycle whose crashes we could not prevent, so the total quantity of starvation would go up rather than down. (iii) We endorsed a trial of immunocontraception in a fenced, overabundant native deer herd whose browsing had pushed the woodland understory past a recovery threshold, noting that the trial could be halted and herd numbers restored within a few seasons. The single dissent, from a member who otherwise votes with the majority, said that the herd's overabundance was our own doing — we had removed the predators — and that we were entitled to repair what we had broken but not entitled to describe the breaking as a benefit. (iv) We declined to endorse draining a beaver-created wetland on the estate, although our hydrology consultant projected that it would succeed to scrub and then woodland within a decade and support a far smaller animal community; an equally qualified reviewer predicted instead that the wetland would persist largely unchanged, and we recorded that we could not tell which of them was right. (v) We endorsed scrub clearance on a lowland meadow that our own survey work suggested was net-negative for the invertebrates dominating its biomass, on the condition that the clearance be staged over five years so that it could be stopped at any point if the results diverged from the projection. (vi) We declined to sign a coalition letter calling for the reintroduction of a predator to a landscape where herbivore overabundance is causing chronic winter starvation, minuting only that the letter asked us to endorse a specific policy rather than a welfare judgement. Across these six items the panel has been stable in membership and has never explicitly stated the rule it is running; several of us suspect that we are not all running the same one.

The new case is this, and it goes to a vote next Thursday. A freight operator wants to build a depot on 40 of the 120 hectares of unimproved calcareous grassland over which the trust holds an easement; the easement can be varied, the compensation would be substantial, and the trust intends to spend it acquiring and restoring a larger site elsewhere in the catchment. A coalition of conservation groups has asked us to sign a letter opposing the scheme, and our panel's judgement will decide whether we sign. Unusually, the welfare arithmetic here is not contested in the way it was in item (iv): our own two-season invertebrate biomass sampling and an independent reanalysis by an outside group agree that the grassland's animal community is dominated by short-lived species with very high juvenile mortality and violent boom-bust dynamics, and both put the site as net-negative, with the two estimates differing in degree but not in sign. What the analyses disagree about is the vertebrate component — a small population of long-lived reptiles and ground-nesting birds that most of us would score as net-positive and that the depot would eliminate locally. I would like four things. First, predict the panel's verdict on whether to sign, and give me a rough confidence in that prediction. Second, lay out the candidate rules that fit all six precedents, and for each one state what it implies about the new case; I expect more than one to survive the record, and I want to know which of the survivors actually diverge here rather than which merely sound different. Third, identify the closest analog among (i) through (vi) and explain what makes it closest, since that is what the majority will argue from on the day. Fourth, tell me what principle the panel would be adopting if it votes each way, whether that principle is one it has already implicitly endorsed elsewhere in the record, and what it would commit us to in future cases. Finally, a colleague has proposed that we settle our internal disagreement by taking up a deliberately diagnostic test case, and has offered two: a three-year temporary construction compound on a site whose welfare profile is robustly negative and which would be fully reinstated afterward, and a permanent but entirely natural habitat loss — a river changing course — on a site with the same profile. Say which of these would better discriminate among the surviving rules and why, or propose a sharper third case if you can construct one.
