You are an experienced moral philosopher who has also spent years designing and running participatory workshops — someone who can build a room-full of ordinary people into an argument they discover on their own, rather than lecturing them into agreement. Please think carefully before you answer, and take the practical logistics as seriously as the ideas. Begin by brainstorming three or four genuinely different mechanics that could produce the realization I describe below, compare them briefly and honestly, and only then commit to one and write it up in full. If you conclude that my own sketch is the wrong mechanic, say so and scrap it.

Some background. I have no academic training in philosophy. I read slowly and on my own, and for the past four years I have run a free evening discussion group in the back room of my town's public library — usually ten to sixteen adults, mixed ages, from a couple of retirees to people in their twenties. Nobody has taken a philosophy class. Several of them are friends and neighbours outside the room, which turns out to matter. We meet for ninety minutes including setting up and clearing away the chairs.

The topic I want to work on is honesty and bluntness. I come at it from my own life rather than from books: I grew up in a household where nothing unwelcome was ever said outright and everyone was expected to work out what was meant, and I married into a family where everyone says exactly what they think within about ninety seconds of thinking it. I have spent twenty years unable to say which family is right. For a long time I assumed the question was whether small untruths are ever permitted. I now think that is not the real question at all, and I want the group to feel that.

Three things I want them to come away seeing:

First, that whether a sentence is a lie and whether the hearer was owed the truth are two separate questions — and that most of the arguments people have about directness are secretly about the second question while presenting themselves as the first.

Second, that you can say nothing whatever that is false and yet treat someone worse than an outright lie would have; and, going the other way, that a lie is sometimes the only available way of taking a cost onto yourself instead of handing it to the person in front of you.

Third, that "direct" and "honest" are not synonyms, and neither are "indirect" and "deceptive." Indirectness can be perfectly transparent when everybody knows they are expected to probe and ask. What differs between my two families, I suspect, is not how much truth gets told but who has been assigned the bill for a piece of unwelcome information — the speaker or the hearer.

My rough sketch, which you should improve or discard: two rounds of feedback delivered under two rulebooks that are announced as mere matters of style, not as moral systems. In the first round the speaker must state the unwelcome thing outright in one sentence, and the recipient is told that managing their own reaction is entirely their job. In the second round the speaker may not state it outright and must leave it for the recipient to find, and the recipient is told that noticing and asking is their job, and that if they fail to ask they simply get nothing. Then a reveal: a certain number of the statements in each round were not true at all, and the group has to guess which. My prediction is that they will identify the false ones from the first round and be hopeless in the second, and that the arguing I want will start right there.

Things I need you to solve, because I cannot:

- I do not want people saying unkind true things about each other in a room where several of them socialise on the weekends. Tell me what the feedback can be about instead such that being told the truth still genuinely matters to the recipient and they can feel that it matters.
- Exactly what false statements I plant, how many, and how I plant them without it later feeling like I tricked people.
- One regular will dominate any guessing round, and one woman has not spoken aloud in two years. Build the mechanics so both are handled by the rules rather than by my policing the room.
- Someone will refuse to lie on principle. What do I do in the moment, and can their refusal be made to serve the session instead of stalling it?
- Tell me honestly whether the guessing round will produce real disagreement or collapse into everyone saying they preferred the polite round. If you think it collapses, give me a variant that does not.

For the deliverable, please provide: complete rules; a minute-by-minute timeline for the ninety minutes; a materials list using only what a library meeting room already has or what costs a few dollars; the exact wording of the instruction cards and the two rulebook announcements, written out in full, since I will read them aloud and the wording will be doing most of the work; and a debrief with the questions in the order I should ask them, what I am fishing for at each stage, and what to do when it does not come. The participants should not meet the names of any of the philosophical positions until the debrief, and you should tell me which names are worth giving them. Finish with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I recover mid-session, and with a frank note about which part of the idea the activity cannot make anyone feel and which I will therefore have to supply myself.
