I work on observability tooling for a cloud platform — dashboards, alerting, the plumbing that tells an on-call engineer whether a service is genuinely healthy or merely reporting that it is. I have no formal philosophy training; what I know I've picked up over the last year, mostly in the evenings, after an argument broke out at work about whether the models we ship could have anything worth calling an inner life. Four of us have been meeting monthly since, and last week I brought in an argument against epiphenomenalism that I'd reconstructed myself from a popular book on consciousness. It was supposed to be a clean refutation of the view that experience is a causally inert by-product of the brain. As I wrote it out, though, it kept generalizing past its target, and by the end it seemed to be refuting something I refuse to give up. I'd like someone to tell me where it breaks, because I've gone through it several times and every step still looks correct to me in isolation.

Here is the argument as I stated it. Take an ordinary case: I have a migraine, I believe I'm in pain, and I say so out loud.

1. Physical causal closure holds: any physical event that has a cause has a sufficient physical cause.
2. My coming to hold the belief that I am in pain, and my saying that I am in pain, are physical events.
3. So there is a complete causal account of how I came to hold that belief which mentions only physical facts and never has to cite a phenomenal fact.
4. Now suppose epiphenomenalism is true: phenomenal facts are real, are distinct from every physical fact, and have no physical effects.
5. Then the fact that it hurts — the phenomenal fact that makes my belief true — figures in the production of that belief not at all.
6. A belief can count as justified, or as knowledge, only if its truth isn't a brute coincidence; something in the causal history that produced it has to be hooked up to what makes it true. In my own line of work the analogue is exact: if a health dashboard matched the true state of a service perfectly and we could find no code path anywhere linking the two, we would call the match luck, treat the dashboard as untrustworthy, and go looking for the leak.
7. Under epiphenomenalism there is no such hookup. The match between my pains and my pain-beliefs is an unexplained correlation, as surprising as a shadow that always happens to move in step with the traveller.
8. So under epiphenomenalism my beliefs about my own present experience are unjustified, and I cannot know that I'm in pain.
9. That consequence is intolerable, which is exactly why (8) is taken to refute epiphenomenalism.
10. But (3) did no special work for epiphenomenalism. It follows from causal closure plus the physicality of belief-formation, and both are accepted by most of the other camps in this debate — nonreductive physicalists, Russellian monists, panpsychists, property dualists who insist experience does push neurons around.
11. On each of those views, the complete causal story of my belief still cites only physical facts, and the phenomenal facts, to the extent that they aren't literally those physical facts, appear nowhere in it.
12. So by (6) through (8), my phenomenal beliefs are unjustified on each of those views as well.
13. The only escape looks like strict identity: if phenomenal facts just are certain physical facts, then what appears in the causal story is the very fact that makes my belief true.
14. But that escape is hollow. Causes do their causal work in virtue of some of their properties rather than others, and the state in question produces my belief in virtue of its neurophysiological properties, not in virtue of how it feels; its hurting isn't doing any work in the chain.
15. So on every view available, including the identity theorist's, nobody can know anything about their own experience.
16. Which is absurd. I am more confident that I currently have a headache than I am of (1), of (6), or of any other line on this list, and the argument itself was assembled by someone in the middle of having experiences.
17. Therefore at least one premise is false — but the argument gives me no way to find which, and each of them seemed obvious when I wrote it down.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Tell me which step you reject and what rejecting it costs, and tell me why that step looked so innocuous when I first wrote it — I have a vague suspicion that some term in the middle of the argument is doing two jobs at once, something in the neighborhood of cause or explanation or connection, but I can't isolate it, and I can't rule out that the suspicion is just me refusing to accept (15). If you're going to make me choose, I'd most like to keep (1), (6), and (16): causal closure, the principle that knowledge can't rest on brute coincidence, and the fact that I know about my own headache right now. Say which of those three has to go and what I lose with it. Then price the alternatives — identity theorists, Russellian monists, interactionist dualists, and eliminativists or illusionists about phenomenology — on what each camp has to pay to block the argument at the step you identify, and say which of those prices you'd actually be willing to pay. One last thing I'd like your view on: does it matter whether the correlation in (7) is between two things that are lawfully connected even if not causally connected, and would running the same reasoning on my belief that water is H2O, or on my belief that a colleague is in pain, help the argument or expose it?
