I am reporting a piece about the machinery through which climate science becomes something a large body of governments can be said to agree on, and I have gotten stuck on a procedural detail that I suspect carries more philosophical weight than anyone in the room would admit. Let me describe the arrangement as I understand it, so that your answer can rest on the description rather than on recall.

The Panel's working-group reports are written by a few hundred nominated scientists over several years and pass through repeated rounds of expert and government review. The chapters themselves are formally accepted by the scientists at a plenary session — a comparatively light act of endorsement, roughly that the chapter fairly represents the literature. A much shorter Summary for Policymakers, drafted by those same scientists, is then taken up line by line by the delegations of the roughly 195 member governments, over days of session, and must clear without any delegation sustaining an objection. Sentences are reworded, magnitudes are recalibrated, hedges are inserted, and lines that cannot be carried are bracketed or struck. What is then cited in the press, what the consensus claim rests on, and what feeds the UN negotiations is that approved Summary, not the chapters; the chapters are not reopened. The Panel holds that the approved Summary must remain faithful to the underlying report, and it describes its own work as relevant to policy but never prescriptive of it.

What I need for the piece is a reconstruction of what this arrangement takes for granted, not a verdict on the Panel's usefulness. So:

What theory of knowledge and of group agency does line-by-line approval presuppose? What does a delegation's failure to object to a sentence amount to — an assertion by the body, a belief, a commitment not to contest, a bargain? What has to be true about the relation between the attitudes of the parts and the attitudes of the whole for the Summary to be something anyone can be said to claim?

The unit at which agreement is recorded is the sentence, but the object that is cited is the document. Different coalitions can carry different lines, so there need not be any single delegation, or any single scientist, who endorses the conjunction; and the conjunction may sit awkwardly with what the authors concluded. Does the procedure assume that sentence-level assent aggregates into document-level assent, and if so, is that assumption ever licensed rather than merely hopeful?

Deletion is the fallback: a line that cannot be agreed disappears, while the finding survives in the accepted chapter. What does that imply about the epistemic status of the Summary — that it tracks what is true, or what is uncontested? Are those the same quantity under this design, and what happens to the authority of the document in the cases where they come apart?

Does the distinction between accepting the chapters and approving the Summary encode a real philosophical difference between an author's warrant and a party's assent? Is it coherent to require that a text approved by interested states be faithful to a text that no state approved?

And the tension I cannot resolve: how does a procedure in which governments negotiate the wording of factual sentences square with the claim to be neutral with respect to policy? Which of the two is the practice actually committed to, and what would the abandoned commitment look like if we built the institution around it instead — publishing the distribution of authors' confidence with no government approval at all, recording which delegations objected to which lines, or treating the Summary as a registry of non-objections rather than as a claim about the world?

Throughout, please separate what the procedure strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and mark the places where the design is underdetermined — where an epistemic story, a legitimacy story, and a coordination story would all license exactly the same rules.
