You're going to play the part of a philosopher of mind who has taught the causal exclusion debate to rooms that are half engineers and half philosophers, and who is a bit obsessive about stating the premises exactly rather than waving at them.

Situation: I'm running a 90-minute workshop with a mixed crowd — some people do mechanistic interpretability work on neural nets, some are philosophy of mind grad students, and a couple are neuroscientists who will bristle at anything that sounds like a word game. I want to open with a sorting exercise: a decision tree that walks each participant from a concrete two-level artifact to a named position in the exclusion debate. The whole point is that nobody should be able to answer it by pattern-matching to "mental causation," so every node has to be about the artifact and never about minds.

The artifact: pick one and stick with it throughout, so the cases build — a docking robot, an autopilot, or a trading bot, your call. We have a complete low-level account of it (voltages, weight updates, whatever) and a high-level one ("it wanted to reach the port, it believed the channel was to the left").

Before you write the tree, do the unglamorous work out loud. List the premises that actually generate the exclusion problem for the high level — closure or completeness of the low level, some exclusion principle, a ban on systematic overdetermination, the non-identity of high-level and low-level properties, and whatever else you think belongs on the list (say whether you'd add something like a causal inheritance principle, and whether exclusion is meant to be a priori or an empirical generalization). Then work out which subsets of those premises are jointly consistent and which aren't, and use that to pick a branching order that seperates the largest number of positions as early as possible. A paragraph or two of that reasoning, then the tree.

Tree rules:
- five to seven branch nodes, each phrased as a concrete scenario or a specific claim about the artifact that a participant can answer without knowing which way the branch points. No node may contain a term of art or ask anything like "do you accept causal closure."
- at least nine leaves. These have to appear somewhere: reduction/identity of the high-level property with the low-level one; a nonreductive view that keeps causation running through realization; a difference-making or intervention-flavored account; a proportionality or determinable-vs-determinate response; somebody who accepts systematic overdetermination cheerfully; somebody who makes causation level-relative or interest-relative; somebody who splits causing from explaining and gives the high level only the second; an eliminativist about the high level; an interactionist dualist analogue; and a "this question is malformed" deflationist. Add more if the space calls for them.
- include at least one node that splits the people who say "obviously both levels cause it" — that answer can mean denial of exclusion, or level-relative causation, or a causing/explaining split, and those are three different destinations.
- mark every combination of answers that looks unoccupied and diagnose it briefly: incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.
- flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot really distinguish instead of papering over it.

Then a key, visually separate: for each leaf, one or two sentences on what that answer commits you to about actual mental causation once you swap the robot for a person, including which premise of the argument you end up rejecting.

House rules: no bibliography, no citations, don't invent papers or quotes. Naming standard positions is fine; naming a philosopher is fine only if you're confident, otherwise describe the view rather than attribute it. Plain-text indented outline I can paste onto slides, not mermaid. Roughly 900 to 1300 words total. Think carefully, and don't let the premise analysis get compressed into the tree — the payoff I'm after is participants discovering which premise they've been quietly denying for years.
