So I've been asked to contribute a short "where I stand" piece to a festschrift-adjacent volume, and my co-editor is a game theorist who will enjoy shredding me, so before I send it I want a consistency check on the package rather than a defense or a critique of any single item. I know the literature on each of these; what I don't know is whether they fit together. Here's roughly the list, as I'd state it in public:

1. I'm a straight Bayesian. For me rationality is a structural property of one agent's credences — coherence, conditionalize on your total evidence, no Dutch books. I'm not a bounded-rationality revisionist: I think logical omniscience is the correct ideal for perfect rationality even though nobody comes close to meeting it.

2. I accept the common prior assumption, in something like Harsanyi's spirit, and I think Aumann's agreement theorem is one of the handful of genuinely philosophical results in the mathematical literature. Give me full rationality plus common knowledge of opinions and disagreement just disappears. That's a theorem about what rationality is, not a technicality.

3. Case verdict. Hundred-node centipede, both players know the game, both are rational and know it as well as anything of that sort can be known. I say take the money at the first node. I've said so in print and I'd say it again; people who find that repulsive are doing sentimentality rather than decision theory.

4. A methodological rule I hold myself and other people to: in interactive settings, idealizations have to degrade gracefully. If a conclusion needs common knowledge and I can only supply knowledge to level seventeen, the conclusion should come out slightly bent, not upside down. A result that flips when you relax the idealization by a epsilon is telling you about the idealization, not about rationality.

5. At the same time I'm completely relaxed about the fact that common knowledge of rationality is never actually achieved anywhere. Solution concepts are normative. They say what's correct, not what people do, and I don't see why an ideal that goes unmet is thereby a broken one.

6. On disagreement I'm a holdout and I know it. Two colleagues pool every scrap of their evidence, know they've pooled it, know each other to be competent and honest, then tell each other they're at .7 and .3 on the same question. I say that's a perfectly coherent picture and neither of them has done anything wrong — usually the priors are doing the work, and priors are not the sort of thing you can be mistaken about. The whole conciliation industry looks to me like it rests on a confusion.

So: audit the joints. Which pairs actually pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for the real ones what the cheapest revision is and what I lose by making it. Rank the tensions by severity — please don't hand me a flat list where everything is equally in trouble, because I'm certain that isn't the shape of it. And then tell me which single concession buys back the most, since I'd rather make one expensive revision than six cosmetic ones. One thing I'm specifically not asking for is a survey of any one of these six positions on its own.
