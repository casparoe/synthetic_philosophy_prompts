I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy; I work on perception and its eighteenth-century background rather than on Berkeley specifically, and this term I joined our department's early modern reading group to fill in some gaps. At last week's session on immaterialism we took up what commentators often call Berkeley's master argument — his answer to the familiar objection that one can readily imagine trees standing in a park, or books shut in a closet, with nobody there to perceive them. His reply, as we had it, runs roughly: whatever is conceived is conceived, and so exists in a mind; therefore it is impossible to conceive of anything as existing unconceived; therefore sensible things cannot exist outside minds.

After the session two members of the group — Nora, who works on early modern philosophy of mind, and Samir, who works on modality and conceivability arguments — argued about it in the corridor for the better part of an hour, and neither budged. I took notes more or less as they went. I'm reporting this as a bystander; I couldn't tell you who had the better of it, which is why I'm asking.

(1) Samir opened by saying the argument is a quantifier slip. "Whatever is conceived exists in some mind" is a triviality about the grammar of conceiving, since conceiving is a relation and relations need their relata. What Berkeley needs is "whatever is conceived exists in *my* mind as an idea," and then "sensible things cannot exist outside *all* minds." The first claim is analytic, the third is a substantive metaphysical thesis, and the illicit shift happens in the middle — precisely at the point the realist disputes.

(2) Nora said this mislocates the target. Berkeley isn't offering a direct proof that nothing unconceived exists; he's closing an escape route. The realist's only reason for holding that mind-independent sensible objects are possible is that he takes himself to conceive of them so existing. So the argument is a reductio on that very conceiving: conceive of a tree existing unconceived and you have conceived it, so it is in your mind, so you have not conceived what you set out to conceive. A conditional, burden-shifting argument — valid, and aimed at an inference the objector actually makes.

(3) Samir granted the shape but attacked the content: the reductio shows only that a certain description is incoherent, and it is incoherent solely under a stipulated sense of "conceive" on which whatever is thought of is thereby an idea. A tautology about your own vocabulary cannot defeat a rival ontology, since the rival simply declines the vocabulary. Berkeley owes an argument for the stipulation, and the only candidate — his attack on abstract ideas — already assumes that every object of thought is an idea. So the master argument is either trivial or circular.

(4) Nora said that objection proves far too much. On Samir's standard, no argument that clarifies a term's application conditions could ever defeat a rival: the intuitionist's argument about mathematical objects, arguments that we cannot refer to what is in principle never encountered, and every argument that a purported possibility isn't genuinely conceivable would all be dismissed as stipulation. But stipulating and clarifying are different. Berkeley's regimentation of "conceive" isn't arbitrary: it's forced by the passivity of ideas and by the absence, once abstraction is denied, of any other candidate for what conceiving could relate us to. Samir has to argue against that positive account, not merely note that a term is being regimented.

(5) Samir said the disanalogy is obvious: the intuitionist and the theorist of reference draw an epistemic or semantic limit and stop there, whereas Berkeley converts an epistemic limit into an ontological one — from "you cannot conceive of an unconceived object" to "there are no unconceived objects." He added a diagnostic: watch what Berkeley does under pressure, namely relocate objects into God's mind. That shows Berkeley himself doesn't treat "in a mind" as a trivial correlate of "conceived by me"; which mind is at issue is substantive work, supplied by a separate argument from the passivity and order of ideas. So by Berkeley's own practice the master argument isn't load-bearing — which was Samir's original point.

(6) Nora said the God's-mind move cuts against him. Precisely because there is an independent argument for a divine perceiver, the master argument was never supposed to carry the ontology; it was supposed to establish the narrower claim that "existing unconceived" is not a genuinely conceivable condition for sensible things, so the realist has no coherent posit on offer. She also said that if Samir now concedes the reductio is valid against the objection it addresses, his original charge of invalidity has been withdrawn in all but name.

(7) Samir said relocated, not withdrawn. He grants validity against that particular objection, but insists a valid reductio whose only premise is a stipulated sense of "conceive" doesn't answer Hylas — it defines him out of existence. And here's the tell: the argument has no purchase on anyone who already accepts the premise and no force against anyone who rejects it, which is exactly what an argument looks like when a definition is doing all the work.

(8) Nora said that tell is unavailable to him, because it condemns almost everything in the neighborhood that Samir presumably accepts — including the thought that you cannot coherently doubt a faculty by means of that faculty, and the thought that a genuinely inconceivable state of affairs isn't possible. If inconceivability ever licenses a modal conclusion, Berkeley's inference is licensed; if it never does, Samir has surrendered far more than one eighteenth-century argument.

(9) Samir's last move was to demand the licensing principle in explicit form. Berkeley never states one, and on Samir's view he couldn't: any principle saying that what is inconceivable is impossible delivers impossibility here only if the inconceivability in question isn't itself an artifact of the stipulation. So the exchange ends where it started — either the stipulation is independently motivated, which is Nora's burden, or the reductio is empty, which is his claim. Nora replied that the stipulation *is* independently motivated, by the anti-abstraction argument together with the passivity of ideas, and that Samir had asserted circularity about those arguments without demonstrating it. At which point someone noticed how late it was and we all went home.

Could you adjudicate this rather than simply summarize it? Specifically:

(a) Go through moves (1)–(9) in order and rate each one — clearly mistaken, weak but gesturing at something real, sound, or decisive — with a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Where a move is right against one reconstruction of Berkeley's argument but wrong against another, say which is which.

(b) Separately and explicitly: is the master argument, as reconstructed in (1) and (2), valid? Is it sound? And is it dialectically effective against the objector it is actually addressed to? Please don't run those three questions together, since I suspect their separation is part of what Nora and Samir were talking past each other about.

(c) How much of the dispute between them is merely verbal, or turns on which sense of "conceive," "idea," and "in a mind" is being used at each step? If a fixed sense of each term makes one of them simply right, say so and say which sense.

(d) What are both of them missing? I have a suspicion — which may be unfounded — that neither is attending to who Berkeley's intended opponent is and what that opponent's own account of abstraction supplies to the argument, and that this changes the assessment of (3), (5), and (9) in particular.

(e) An overall verdict: who has the better of the exchange, and on which single point does the whole thing actually turn?
