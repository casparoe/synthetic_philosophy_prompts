I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, and I am drafting a chapter on the normative structure of compelled electoral participation. My supervisor has pointed out, fairly enough, that I have described my central case at some length without ever saying what it assumes. I would be grateful for help with that step. Please write in British English throughout.

The case is the Australian Commonwealth franchise, and the interest lies in the combination of its features rather than in any single one, so let me set them out. Enrolment is compulsory, and so is voting: an elector who fails to attend a federal election receives a penalty notice for a modest sum, and a defence is available on the ground of a valid and sufficient reason, which the electoral authority treats as covering, among other things, a religious obligation to abstain. The duty is nonetheless discharged by attendance. An elector who presents at a booth, is handed a ballot paper and deposits it unmarked — or marked with a drawing, or a protest — has complied; the paper is set aside as informal and tallied as such, and nothing further is asked of them. Yet within the same booth the requirements of formality are strict, since in the House of Representatives every box must be numbered for a paper to count at all. The state thus compels nothing whatever about the content of any vote, while insisting, of those who do want their vote to count, that they express a complete ordering of preferences.

The boundaries of the electorate are then drawn in a manner that looks arbitrary relative to any principle one might extract from the compulsion itself. Persons who were enrolled as British subjects on 25 January 1984 remain on the roll although they are not citizens, cannot opt out, and are obliged to vote. Citizens serving full-time sentences of three years or longer are struck off the roll, while those serving shorter sentences remain obliged to enrol and to vote. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds may enrol but may not vote, and are not penalised for staying away. And persons considered incapable of grasping what enrolment and voting involve, and why they matter, are excluded from the franchise altogether, however able they would be to turn up.

My chapter distinguishes, stipulatively, a duty of attendance from a duty of expression, and I have been arguing that what this arrangement compels is attendance and nothing more. I want that claim tested and sharpened rather than restated. Specifically:

1. What theory of the vote does the arrangement presuppose — one on which voting expresses a considered preference, or something else entirely? Is it plausible that the object of the compulsion is not any aggregate will at all, but the composition of the electorate, or the honesty of the turnout figure? If so, what must be true of legitimacy for that to be the right thing to compel?

2. Does the permission to deposit a blank paper commit the practice to the view that absence is a communicative act and presence is not? I keep circling this. If the state penalises non-attendance but not non-expression, it seems to treat staying away as a refusal to be counted and turning up as the surrender of that very option, and I cannot tell whether that is a coherent implicit theory of political speech or an artefact of enforcement convenience.

3. Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out — the vote as a right that can be waived, the election as conferring a mandate, deliberative or expressivist accounts of legitimacy, the thought that the outcome speaks for the governed? And which of these is the practice merely compatible with, as distinct from requiring?

4. Are the boundary rules consistent with whatever theory the compulsion presupposes? I am most troubled by compelling a long-resident non-citizen while excluding a citizen prisoner, and by keeping the incapable off the roll while demanding no exercise of capacity from anyone on it. Is this a contradiction internal to the implicit theory, or is it explicable on unprincipled grounds — administrative continuity, say, or reluctance to disenfranchise retroactively — that the theory could absorb without damage?

5. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? I suspect several quite different theories would license precisely these rules, and I should like to know which, so that I can stop presenting my favoured reading as the only one available.

6. Finally, suppose the presupposed view were replaced by an explicitly deliberative one, on which the duty is to arrive at a considered judgement and to communicate it. What would have to change institutionally — in the treatment of informal papers, in the defence of valid and sufficient reason, in compulsory preferential numbering, in the composition of the roll — and which of those changes would pull against one another?

I am not after a survey of the literature, and I would rather you did not offer references or a reading list. What I want is the reconstruction: what the practice takes for granted, what it forecloses, and where it forecloses nothing at all.
