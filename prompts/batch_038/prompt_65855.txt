I'm presenting a paper next month at a law-and-philosophy workshop whose attendees are mostly criminal law theorists working on prosecutorial discretion, selective prosecution, and the equitable doctrine of unclean hands, plus two moral philosophers in the Strawsonian reactive-attitudes tradition. My thesis: standing to blame is not a property of the blamer's character or of the reliability of her judgment, but a relational entitlement she holds against a particular wrongdoer. On this view the hypocrite's defect is neither insincerity nor self-deception; it is that her own violation of the very norm she invokes has generated a claim in the wrongdoer against her. Two consequences I mean to defend are that standing is lost norm by norm rather than globally, and that it can be restored only by the wrongdoer's forgiveness — not by the blamer's repentance, apology, or reform.

Give me my question-period prep sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually press, in their own idiom — the criminal-law ones first, then the Strawsonian ones — ordered by how much damage each does to the argument. For each, a reply that fits in two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is thin. Then tell me which single objection, if it lands, takes the thesis down; which concessions I can afford to make early to buy credibility on the rest; and the question I should be hoping nobody asks. Be decisive throughout: I want your assessment of which objections genuinely defeat the view and which merely sound devastating, not a balanced survey that leaves the ranking to me.
