For the past eleven months I have chaired the Student Standards Council at a residential college of roughly 1,100 students, and in September I have one two-hour session in which to prepare sixteen incoming members — eleven undergraduates, five faculty and staff — to begin hearing real cases in October. The room has movable tables, a whiteboard, and no budget. I do not want to spend the session lecturing about moral emotions, and I would like your help designing something that does the work without my doing the talking.

Here is why the question has stopped being abstract for me. Three years of confidential hearings produced a striking regularity: respondents almost always admitted the conduct once the door was closed, and close to half of them proposed a sanction against themselves more severe than the one the council went on to impose. Last spring we broke with that practice and piloted a single open hearing before an audience of about sixty students. The respondent denied matters he had already conceded in the investigative file. Two of the four student witnesses asked to have their statements withdrawn. The complainant transferred out within a month. And yet a substantial number of students in that audience told me afterwards that it was the first time they had understood what the standard actually required, and reports out of that dormitory fell sharply for the following term. Nothing our confidential procedure has ever done produced that. I have been unable to reconcile the two results, and I no longer trust the framework our handbook gives me for thinking about them — it still organizes itself around the old Benedict distinction between guilt cultures and shame cultures, which I now suspect conceals the variables that actually did the work in that room: who was watching, whether the watchers were people whose regard the respondent needed, whether a route back to standing existed and was credible, and whether anyone could simply leave.

I should add the failure that has stayed with me most. I had assumed the answer lay in Braithwaite's idea of reintegrative shaming — disapproval directed at the act rather than at the person, followed by an explicit ceremonial restoration — and we ran the open hearing on that model. The restoration phase backfired. Several students read the welcome-back as proof that the disapproval had been theater all along, and the respondent told me privately that he could no longer tell what any of it had meant.

So: design a two-hour activity for these sixteen people that lets them run into the structural difference themselves and then confronts them with the question I cannot answer — whether a community genuinely chooses between these two economies of moral emotion, or whether the choice is largely settled in advance by its size, its visibility, and the ease with which its members can exit.

The requirements I would ask you to treat as binding:

1. No theory up front. The words guilt and shame should not leave my mouth until the debrief, and participants should be able to reconstruct the distinction from what happened to them rather than from a handout.

2. The session should run two institutional regimes over comparable cases and tally something real: how often the accused admits the conduct, how severe a sanction the accused proposes for themselves, how many witnesses stay with their account under questioning, what the non-participants afterwards believe the rule to mean, and how many people say they would want out of the group. Give me a scoring procedure a non-specialist can run in the room without turning anyone into a specimen.

3. The exposure-based regime must win somewhere, and win in a way participants find unpleasant to concede. If the activity only demonstrates that the private, act-focused, reparative procedure is superior, it has taught them nothing I already believe.

4. Real but bounded stakes. Something participants actually care about must be at risk in the room — standing, a role, an anonymous rating that is later revealed — but the design may not draw on their own conduct histories, may not humiliate anyone, and must let a person step out without that step becoming a matter of record. Explain the mechanism by which you generate felt stakes without harm, and be candid about what the mechanism costs: if the risk attaches to an invented persona rather than to the participant, tell me how much of the effect I am surrendering.

5. Stage the collapse I witnessed. There should be a scripted moment in which the exposure regime offers its restoration phase, and a rule allowing participants to register that the offer retroactively devalued the disapproval that preceded it.

6. The closing phase should be a decision that looks free and is not. Let the group settle policy for their own council — hearing format, whether findings are published, what restoration requires — and only then reveal constraints they did not author: the confidentiality obligations our counsel insists on, the fact that 1,100 people is not a village, the fact that a respondent can withdraw and carry their record somewhere else. They should leave wondering whether choosing was ever the right description.

Please deliver a minute-by-minute run sheet; the role assignments and case materials in a form I can print without further drafting; my facilitation language, close to verbatim, for the three or four moments where one careless sentence would spoil the effect, including what to say if a participant becomes genuinely distressed; a debrief that maps particular moments in the room onto the distinctions described above and says what each moment does and does not establish; a list of what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run with nineteen-year-olds and how to handle each; and a plain statement of which feature of the guilt–shame contrast the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know what I must supply by argument afterwards.

Two final constraints. Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or citations; I will not read anything before September and neither will they. And three of the sixteen have themselves appeared before this council as respondents. Whatever you design has to be survivable for those three.
