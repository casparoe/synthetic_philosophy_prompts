At the end of a six-week adult-education course on Bronze Age Anatolia that I attended, two of the regulars — Alan, a retired structural engineer, and Nadia, who teaches secondary-school history — got into a long argument in the car park about whether it is correct to say that the Trojan War happened. I drove them both home and took notes; they have since agreed that the argument is unwinnable between them and have asked me to get a third-party adjudication. Neither signalled a willingness to concede anything. Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

1. Alan opened by saying that the answer is yes, and not merely as a romantic guess. The mound at Hisarlik contains a major settlement destroyed by fire and violence in the decades around 1200 BCE, and separately we have Hittite state archives — written by a bureaucracy with no interest in Greek poetry — naming a client kingdom in precisely that corner of Anatolia whose name corresponds to the one Homer uses for Troy, whose ruler bears a name that is the local form of the name Homer also gives as Paris's, and which one letter describes as the subject of earlier hostilities between the Hittites and the king of a western power addressed as an equal great king, generally read as the Mycenaean Greeks. Two independent evidential streams converging on the same city and the same century is, he said, about as good as ancient history ever gets.

2. Nadia replied that this gets you a place and some diplomatic friction, not a war of the kind the phrase names. Nothing in those documents mentions a coalition fleet, a decade-long siege, or a conflict caused by an abducted queen. Our literary witness, meanwhile, was composed in performance by singers working in a formulaic oral tradition four or five centuries after the events, for audiences who wanted poetry rather than archives; singers compress, invent, and borrow. The defensible sentence, she said, is: there was a Troy, and there was probably trouble around Troy; the Trojan War is a poem.

3. Alan said that standard would empty the ancient past. Almost everything believed about antiquity rests on sources composed long after the fact and shaped by genre and purpose; if "written centuries later by someone with literary aims" disqualifies a witness, it disqualifies most of what we claim to know about the archaic and classical Greek world too. And the ordinary method is exactly the one he used: discount the supernatural and the formulaic, then retain whatever structural core is corroborated from outside. He added that a conflict over the tolls and trade of the Dardanelles is independently predicted by the geography and the Bronze Age economy, so the tradition's placement of the war there is not arbitrary.

4. Nadia said the discounting method is question-begging. He decides element by element what counts as myth, and the criterion he applies is "whatever fits an independently plausible Late Bronze Age scenario" — so what survives the filter is precisely the generic skeleton he brought to it: a city, a strait, a raid. That skeleton is compatible with dozens of distinct actual events spread over two centuries, and a testimony supports a particular event only if it is more likely on that event than on the alternatives. She then pointed at the epics' own material culture, which is a pastiche of periods: cremation where the Mycenaean practice was inhumation, iron appearing as a rare precious substance, and one piece of equipment described in accurate detail that had been out of use for centuries before the poet was born. That, she said, is a tradition carrying fragments of many times, not a report of one.

5. Alan said that observation cuts his way. If a purely oral tradition transmitted one obsolete piece of panoply accurately for four hundred years, then oral transmission preserves considerably more than she is granting — and it means the tradition carries genuine memory of the Mycenaean world rather than being free composition. He cited the great catalogue of contingents in the second book, which lists places that mattered in the palatial period and were abandoned or trivial by the poet's own day, which he said is difficult to explain if the poet was inventing.

6. Nadia said memory of a world is not memory of an event: a tradition can preserve the geography, the furniture and the place-names of an age while getting its plot entirely wrong. She then offered a comparison. Everyone agrees the Arthurian material has at best a thin kernel, and everyone agrees that the version the phrase calls to mind — the court, the table, the affair, the quest — was substantially manufactured in the twelfth century for a Norman-French audience and elaborated afterwards by a prose cycle. Yet the one near-contemporary British account of the relevant decades records the great siege of the period and never names Arthur at all. If we will not say that Camelot happened, she said, we should not say the Trojan War happened, and for the same reason: the thing named by the phrase is the legend, and the legend is a later construction around a residue too thin to identify.

7. Alan said the two cases are not alike and that the difference is the whole ballgame. For Arthur, the earliest substantial narrative appears more than six centuries after the events, with a silence where the man should be. For Troy, there are Bronze Age archives produced by a third party, placing a Greek-equivalent power in hostile contact with the very city in the very period. If that asymmetry does not change the verdict, he said, then no amount of external corroboration ever could, and "legend" becomes a bucket into which everything ancient is tipped regardless of evidence.

8. Nadia said she would grant the asymmetry and deny that it changes the verdict, because it moves the subject. If the archives are doing the work, then what is attested is a thirteenth-century dispute involving a Hittite vassal in the Troad — no Helen, no Achilles, no horse, no ten years, and possibly not even a military campaign, since the Hittite verb in question ranges from an exchange of strongly worded tablets to a full war and the text does not tell us where on that range it sits. He has, she said, quietly substituted a different object for the one under discussion. The question was never whether something happened near Hisarlik; it was whether the Trojan War happened — and on that question the phrase may simply have no determinate referent for a sentence to be true or false of.

9. Alan said that if the phrase has no determinate referent, then it is equally empty to assert that the Trojan War did not happen, and that is the negative claim she wants to keep. She cannot have both. Either the question is answerable, in which case the answer comes in degrees and the right report is "partly historical," or it is not answerable, in which case her position is not a conclusion but a refusal to engage. His own formulation, he said, is stable and could be shown false: a real but limited conflict in the Troad in the thirteenth century, remembered orally and progressively inflated into epic over four hundred years.
