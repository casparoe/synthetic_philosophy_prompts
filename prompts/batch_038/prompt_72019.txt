So I'm not a philosopher — I do UX research — but a couple months back I picked up one of those fat pop-science books about time at a library sale and I'm about two thirds through. There's a passage in it that I've now read three times and I still can't tell whether I'm being sold something real or something that just sounds real. The book is at my sister's house so I'm working from notes and memory, which means what follows is close paraphrase, not exact wording.

It goes roughly like this. Special relativity killed absolute simultaneity: whether two far-apart events happen at the same time isn't a fact about the world, it depends on your state of motion. Take Andromeda, about 2.5 million light years out. Someone strolling past your front window in Andromeda's direction at a few miles an hour has a "now" out there that's shifted from yours by a matter of days; put them in a ship doing a tenth of light speed and the gap is a couple hundred thousand years. Fine so far. Then the book says Rietdijk and Putnam turned this into an argument. If there's no privileged frame, nothing makes your slice of now more real than the walker's. And if the walker's now out at Andromeda already contains events that are still days or centuries away in your future, then those events are already real. Chain it: the walker's now includes those, and a second observer moving relative to the walker has a now that includes events further along still, and hop by hop you cover any stretch of your future you like. Hence the universe is a four-dimensional block in which past, present and future all exist on the same footing, and the sense that time flows is something our brains manufacture rather than something they detect. The book then draws it's moral: the future is already written, so free will — genuinely being able to do otherwise — is dead. And it stresses this isn't armchair speculation, it falls straight out of confirmed physics: atomic clocks flown on jets, muons surviving the trip down through the atmosphere, GPS satellites having to correct their clocks.

For what it's worth I want this to be wrong. The thought of my next twenty years sitting there complete like a finished film makes me feel physically ill, and I don't want to be reassured just because I'm queasy — but I also don't want to be told the book is fine because I've read two other things that said similar stuff. So: can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on this would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's just wrong? Three things I can't pin down. One: that step where it slides from "real for the walker" to "real," and then hops from walker to second observer — I have a nagging sense something is being smuggled across those hops, but I can't say what, or whether the book is even allowed to do it. Two: whether the block-universe conclusion has anything to do with free will at all, or whether two separate questions got welded together — I keep feeling like "exists tenselessly" and "is causally determined" are being used as if they were the same phrase. Three: the GPS-and-muons bit. Those experiments definitely confirm relativity, but do they confirm the metaphysical premises the argument needs, or is the book borrowing the credibility of the physics for something else entirely? And last thing — tell me one point in that passage that's genuinely right and that I'd be an idiot to wave away, because I suspect I'm heading toward discounting the whole chapter.
