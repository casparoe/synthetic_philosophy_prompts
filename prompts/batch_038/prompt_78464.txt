I direct resident life at a continuing-care retirement community of roughly four hundred residents, spanning independent living, assisted living, and a memory-care unit. Each quarter a programming committee of four staff members and five residents reviews twenty to thirty proposals — some from activities staff, some from residents, some from outside vendors — against an annual activities budget of about $80,000 and a hard cap on staff hours. Somewhat under half are funded. The committee's recommendations go to the executive director, who signs off without change nine times out of ten, and the committee publishes its reasons for every decision in the resident newsletter, in plain English. So the justifications carry as much weight as the verdicts, and the residents who lose a program read them closely.

At present we have no framework whatsoever; decisions are made by whoever argues most persistently. I would like to build one out of Cicero's dialogue on old age, the one that puts the defense of aging well into the mouth of Cato the Elder, and specifically out of its organization around the four charges commonly brought against old age and the answers offered to each. The result has to be usable by staff with no philosophy background: a sequence of questions to run on any proposal, an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure stops with a no, and the points at which it hands the decision back to the committee because what remains is a question of values rather than facts. Twenty minutes per proposal is the most anyone will spend, including me.

Be precise about the translation, since that is the part I cannot do myself. Name the four charges as the dialogue frames them, and for each, separate what Cicero concedes from what he denies — the distinction matters operationally, because a proposal that answers a charge he denies is doing something quite different from one that addresses a charge he grants and then revalues. Then tell me which of his distinctions must survive into the committee's questions, which can be dropped as unusable in a modern residential setting, and why. Be candid about where the procedure will misfire: which sorts of residents, and which sorts of proposals, the Ciceronian framework is simply not built to evaluate, given the kind of life its speaker assumes.

Finally, apply the procedure to three proposals now on our docket, showing the questions and answers in sequence:

(a) A weekly wine-and-film evening, popular with independent-living residents, built around a sommelier-led tasting before the screening.
(b) A paid partnership in which residents tutor local middle-schoolers in reading, four hours a week, with transportation provided.
(c) A sensory music and reminiscence program for the memory-care unit, designed for residents with moderate to advanced dementia, most of whom no longer follow a plot, recall names, or sustain a conversation.

I expect at least one of these verdicts to be one the committee will resent, and at least one to expose a limit of the source rather than of my programming. Say which is which, what the procedure actually outputs in each case, and what I should write to the residents in the two paragraphs we publish.
