Here is the question as it appeared on the exam, worth 30 marks:

*Explain why most Anglo-American jurisdictions recognize two criteria of death — a circulatory-respiratory one and a neurological one — and say what makes them criteria for one and the same thing rather than for two different things. Then assess the following objection: in controlled donation after circulatory death, organs are procured a few minutes after the heart has stopped, on the assumption that the donor is already dead; if that assumption is questionable, the practice seems to violate the dead donor rule. In the course of your assessment, distinguish explicitly between the concepts on which it depends.*

And here is the answer, in full (typos silently corrected):

*The law recognizes two criteria: irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, and irreversible cessation of all functions of the whole brain, brainstem included. Having two criteria does not mean there are two kinds of death. Death is the loss of the organism's capacity to function as an integrated whole, and both the heart-lung system and the brain are indispensable to that integration; each criterion is a way of detecting that loss. In the neurological case the brainstem matters most, because that is where consciousness arises, so once it is destroyed the person is gone even though the body can be kept oxygenated on a ventilator for days. Clinically, death is determined by unresponsive coma of known cause, absence of brainstem reflexes, and a failed apnea test.*

*The dead donor rule has two parts: donors must be dead before organs are procured, and procurement must not be what kills them. In controlled DCD, life-sustaining treatment is withdrawn in a controlled setting, the heart stops, and after a hands-off period — typically two to five minutes — death is declared and procurement begins. The objection says the donor cannot really be dead at that point, because circulation could still be restored: nobody has done anything to the heart that makes its stopping irreversible, and the whole point of the standoff period is that the team is standing by, able to restart it.*

*But the objection confuses things. In DCD, cessation of circulation is irreversible, because a decision has already been made not to attempt resuscitation. Nobody is going to restart the heart, so the cessation will never in fact be reversed; the criterion is therefore satisfied and the donor is dead before procurement. The standoff period is not there to make the cessation irreversible — it is there to rule out autoresuscitation, the spontaneous return of circulation, which is not observed after a few minutes. Since the donor is dead, the dead donor rule is not violated, and there is no tension. What survives of the objection is a concern about optics: procuring organs from a body whose heart could in principle be restarted looks bad and may erode public trust in donation, but that is a problem about appearances, not about whether the practice kills people.*
