You are a philosopher of law and bioethics who has sat on enough dissertation committees to know which projects quietly collapse in the second year, and I would like you to write me a five-page dissertation prospectus. I am a third-year philosophy student with a certificate in law; my committee includes a bioethicist who will keep asking what difference the project makes at a bedside and a metaphysician who suspects the whole subject is a morass of unargued intuitions, so the prospectus has to survive both. The material I want to work on is the dispute over whether an advance directive retains authority once the person who wrote it has changed beyond recognition. The case I keep returning to: a woman writes at fifty-five, after nursing her mother through a long decline, that she is never to be given artificial nutrition or hydration if she reaches advanced dementia; at seventy-nine she has moderate dementia, recognizes almost no one, but plainly enjoys eating, asks for seconds, and resists being fed anything she does not like. Her proxy, who is her daughter, is being pressed from both directions, and the hospital's ethics consultation service says the directive is 'clear' while the daughter says her mother would never have meant this. The philosophical literature, as I understand it, splits into those who defend the directive by appeal to the earlier self's critical interests or settled values, those who attack it on the ground that the interests of the demented patient are all that remain to be served, and a third group that treats the whole problem as an artifact of an overly stringent view of personal identity.

My half-formed idea, which I want you to develop rather than merely polish, is that all three positions argue about the same undifferentiated thing — whether the directive is authoritative — when the practice actually distributes several distinct powers, and the disagreement is largely about which of them is in play. I have in mind separating at least three: the power to fix what counts as a good for the person at all, the power to direct what is done now, and the standing to demand that someone answer for a wrong done to the person. The directive may well retain the first while losing the second, and the third may belong to nobody who is currently competent to exercise it, which is precisely why the case feels intractable. My proposed vehicle for this is not the tired Ulysses-and-the-mast analogy but the trust. In ordinary estate planning a person can declare a trust of her own property, name herself as initial trustee and beneficiary, and name a successor trustee who takes over upon her incapacity, under terms she wrote while competent and cannot now revise; the instrument is revocable, but the power to revoke depends on a capacity she no longer has. That structure is, I think, a better model of an advance directive than the model of a command issued by a prior sovereign, because a trustee's duty runs to the beneficiary's interests as the instrument specifies them while still requiring present judgment about circumstances the settlor could not foresee, and because a beneficiary who lacks capacity cannot consent, release, or complain. I want the prospectus to argue that recasting the question in these fiduciary terms does real philosophical work — it explains why the directive seems both binding and inapplicable, it locates the revocability puzzle precisely, and it makes the identity debate largely beside the point, since a trust does not require that trustee and beneficiary be the same person.

So: write the prospectus, taking the position you actually think is right rather than surveying mineable options. Begin by thinking the framing through — work through at least two rival formulations of the project before you settle on one, including the obvious objection that a fiduciary model is only a redescription of the interest-based view it claims to replace, and tell me in a sentence or two why you set each aside. Then commit to a single, precisely stated research question that has a determinate answer and is small enough to finish; explain why the existing positions leave that question open rather than merely disagreeing about it; lay out the method, saying what I would actually do and in what order across roughly eighteen months of writing, with the sequence expressed in prose rather than as a schedule; specify what a successful result would look like and, just as concretely, what would count as the project failing; and give an honest assessment of the risk that the work turns out too doctrinal for a philosophy committee and too abstract for anyone in clinical ethics, together with what I should cut if it does. Where you think my idea is wrong or merely decorative, say so plainly and propose the repair instead of diplomatically incorporating it. I am not asking for references and will supply them myself, so please do not include a bibliography, a literature review by citation, or any invented titles. And observe one strict formal constraint: the whole thing must be continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter or an email, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables, at roughly two thousand words.
