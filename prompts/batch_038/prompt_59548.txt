I'm in a reading group slowly working through some old criticism and we hit this bit from Coleridge's *Biographia Literaria* (ch. 14, 1817, the part about how *Lyrical Ballads* came about):

"In the one, the incidents and agents were to be, in part at least, supernatural; and the excellence aimed at was to consist in the interesting of the affections by the dramatic truth of such emotions as would naturally accompany such situations, supposing them real. And real in this sense they have been to every human being who, from whatever source of delusion, has at any time believed himself under supernatural agency. ... it was agreed, that my endeavors should be directed to persons and characters supernatural, or at least romantic, yet so as to transfer from our inward nature a human interest and a semblance of truth sufficient to procure for these shadows of imagination that willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith."

Everyone in the group just quotes "the willing suspension of disbelief" and moves on, but the less of the sentence I skip the less sure I am about what it says. Help me actually parse it:

- Who's doing the suspending, and what exactly gets suspended? Our guy says "willing" means the reader is a willing accomplice, that Coleridge is describing a cooperative act on our part. But the sentence's subject looks like Coleridge's own endeavor as a poet, and the suspension looks like something the poem *procures* for the reader rather than something the reader decides to do. Which reading does the grammer support, and does it change what he's claiming?
- Disbelief *of what*, though? And what does it mean to suspend a disbelief rather than a judgment or a belief? If I set my disbelief aside, am I left believing the mariner's story, or merely not-disbelieving it? "for the moment" makes it sound like a temporary belief-state, which seems odd.
- The sentence just before says the aim is the "dramatic truth of such emotions as would naturally accompany such situations, supposing them real." That reads conditional to me — emotions of the sort you'd have *if* it were real — not emotions that require you to take it as real. Is that right? If so, is Coleridge on the side of people who think our responses to fiction are a kind of make-believe, or is he doing something else?
- What's going on with "transfer from our inward nature a human interest and a semblance of truth"? Truth transferred out of *us*? And a "semblance" of truth as opposed to what? Then he calls the same stuff "shadows of imagination" a few words later. Is he being loose, or is there a real difference between the human interest, the semblance of truth, and the shadows?
- Is "poetic faith" a belief state at all, or is "faith" doing different work than "belief" would?

And the bigger thing: my freind keeps saying this passage is Coleridge pre-empting the paradox of fiction, i.e. that he's answering "how can we be moved by people we know don't exist?" I keep thinking the sentence is really a requirement placed on the writer — a claim about what the poet has to accomplish — and not a theory about the audience's mind at all, so that treating it as an answer to the paradox smuggles in a lot. Tell me whether that suspicion holds up, and point to the words in the sentence that would settle it either way.
