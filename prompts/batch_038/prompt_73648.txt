I teach a third-year seminar called Big History and Its Discontents. Twenty-two students, and I'll be honest that maybe six of them are philosophy majors — the rest are data science, environmental studies, a couple of history, and one kid who runs a podcast with about four thousand subscribers and has already announced he's recording his reaction to week nine's reading. For eight weeks we've done the canonical sweep: Harari's four-part arc in Sapiens (cognitive revolution, agricultural revolution, unification, scientific revolution) and the Diamond-style ecological determinism underneath it, which Harari openly credits as an inspiration. They like it. Several have read Sapiens twice and consider themselves fans, which means they've absorbed the specific version where farming was a trap that made most individual lives worse while making the species successful, and where large-scale cooperation is explained by shared fictions. Week nine we pivot to the critics: Graeber and Wengrow's Dawn of Everything, chapters one and two, plus a genuinely hostile review so they don't think I'm running a fan club.

Here's the thing. I don't want to spend the thirty minutes I have before discussion adjudicating who's right, and I especially don't want it to collapse into 'G&W are just motivated reasoning' versus 'Harari is just a TED talk with a bibliography.' What I want them to leave with is one narrow conceptual move: that the question where did inequality come from already has an answer built into it. It presupposes that a society has a single original form, that this form is the kind of thing you could locate at a point in time, and that inequality is a substance that arrives rather than a description of how people are relating to each other. And their replacement question — how did we get stuck — only becomes intelligible once you've also given up the stage-based baseline, because it presupposes that earlier societies were politically self-conscious and could and did change form deliberately: the seasonal swiching back and forth between authoritarian and communal arrangements, the deliberate abandonment of agriculture, schismogenesis as a way of defining yourself against your neighbours, Teotihuacan starting out hierarchical and then reversing course. I want them to feel the dependency rather than just hear both positions read out loud. You can't get to 'stuck' until you've lost 'stage,' and you can't lose 'stage' until something else is in its place. So plan the thirty minutes for me — not the explanation itself, the order the pieces have to go in and what forces each piece to come before the next.

Three more things I need from the plan. First, the carrying case. My instinct is to open with Harari's wheat-domesticated-us line because it's vivid and they all know it, but I suspect that just re-anchors them in the frame I'm trying to break. The alternatives I can see are the seasonal political regimes, or the Kondiaronk material — the claim that the inequality question itself came out of Indigenous American critique of European society reaching Enlightenment readers through travellers' accounts and missionary relations, and that the progress-through-stages story partly worked to dismiss that critique as naive rather than answer it. Tell me which one to build on, and what the case has to have and mustn't have: does it need to be a single society or a contrast pair, does it need to be archaeological rather than textual, does it need to be one they can hold in their heads for twenty minutes. Second, the misconception map. My predictions are that they'll come out thinking G&W deny inequality existed before farming, that they'll hear Kondiaronk as a noble savage in a feather headdress, that the data-science students will say so big history is just storytelling and unfalsifiable, and that somebody will insist the whole thing reduces to we can just choose to be egalitarian again, it's willpower. Tell me where in the sequence each gets headed off, and tell me which one I've got wrong or haven't thought of. Third, one question I can ask in the last two minutes that would show whether the reframing landed rather than whether they can repeat the slogan back to me, plus a short list of what I should refuse to get into at this level and say out loud that I'm refusing — I'm guessing the actual archaeological disputes, but tell me what else. No need to send me readings, the list is already set.
