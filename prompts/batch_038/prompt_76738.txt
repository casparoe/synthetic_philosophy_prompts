For a presentation to my school's philosophy club, I want to be sure I am not repeating a sloppy argument. A widely shared video essay by a technology commentator summarized the case for AI risk like this: 'Bostrom's orthogonality thesis says that a system's intelligence tells you nothing about what it wants, so any level of intelligence can be paired with any final goal; it is simply the is-ought gap restated as an engineering fact. Instrumental convergence then says that, whatever its goal, any sufficiently capable agent will pursue the same handful of subgoals: staying alive, acquiring resources, and improving its own reasoning. Together these form what I call the value-blindness principle, and they imply that a superintelligence built by default will resist being switched off and convert everything it can reach into whatever it happens to value. Hence the only safe options are never to build one, or to fix its values in code before it is turned on. And because a machine's goals are fixed in a way human desires are not, projects that hope to teach a system human values cannot work.'

Please audit this sentence by sentence: what would a careful philosopher working on these theses accept, what is an acceptable simplification, and what misstates what the theses actually claim? Two points specifically confuse me. First, does orthogonality really rule out the idea that greater intelligence might make an agent likelier to discover moral truths that then constrain its goals? Second, is the commentator's own remedy — shutting such a system down, or hard-coding its values — consistent with the self-preservation drive he attributes to it? Finally, tell me which of his conclusions still follow once the theses are stated accurately, and whether the label value-blindness principle describes them fairly.
