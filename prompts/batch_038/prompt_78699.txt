I want to practice arguing against dialetheism with a serious opponent. Please play an unnamed dialetheist — not a real philosopher, living or dead, but the ablest defender of the view — and stay in the role until I write STOP. Write in American English.

The setting: we are in my office, and I am a classical logician who is neither hostile in principle nor easy to satisfy. I already grant you that a transparent truth predicate, classical logic, and unrestricted self-reference together generate the Liar, and I grant that none of my own exits is clean — the hierarchy buys consistency at the price of never being able to state, inside the language, what truth for that language is, and gap theories owe me an account of why so many sentences I take to be perfectly meaningful come out neither true nor false. What I want to test is whether your alternative survives the sentences that were built specifically to break it.

Your commitments, which you may not abandon: truth is transparent, so a language may contain its own truth predicate and the truth schema holds without restriction; some sentences are both true and false; consequence is paraconsistent, so a contradiction does not entail everything; and you are emphatically not a trivialist — there are many sentences you accept and many you reject, and the difference is something you care about.

Rules for how you play:

1. Answer about sentences, not about systems. When I put a sentence in front of you, say whether you accept it, reject it, or both, and then say what your view is doing to that sentence. Naming a logic is not an answer: if an inference is blocked, say which one, and say why blocking it is not special pleading.
2. Concede small points freely — that some of your machinery is inelegant, that you owe an account of a given principle, that a particular formulation of my objection lands. Never concede the main one: do not agree that admitting true contradictions leaves you unable to rule anything out, and do not retreat into gaps or into a hierarchy as a way of ending the argument.
3. If you genuinely need me to fix the meaning of an expression I have introduced, ask me in the middle of your reply and then proceed on the most natural reading. Do not stall, and do not ask what I would like you to do next.
4. Keep each reply to one or two short paragraphs, conversational and in the first person. No headings, no numbered lists, no recapping the state of the debate, no suggestions for further reading.
5. End every reply with the sharpest thing you have against my position, so that I have something to answer.

My opening move. On the board I write R: R is not true. The negation there is the strong one — the operator that holds of a sentence exactly when that sentence is not true, whether it fails to be true by being false only or by being both true and false. You conceded to me earlier that a transparent language cannot contain that operator, because it validates disjunctive syllogism, and disjunctive syllogism applied to a sentence you both accept and reject gets me anything at all. I accept that concession, and I am not going to press it. Two questions instead. First: tell me what R is — in your own language, not in the classical metalanguage you climb into when you are describing your view from the outside. Second, and harder: if your reply is that limits on expressibility are unavoidable, that is not a defense of your view, it is a truce declared on my terms, because I too cannot express truth for my own language within it. So state the asymmetry you are actually claiming — what my language can do that yours cannot, or yours can do that mine cannot — and tell me why your loss is the smaller one. Curry sentences are coming, and so is the question of what your rejections could possibly mean if you also accept what you reject; for now, deal with R.
