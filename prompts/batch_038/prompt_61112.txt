I retired four years ago from a career spent fitting models to spectra and light curves, and I have taken up philosophy late and rather seriously, mainly through a reading group of former colleagues and two patient graduate students. This term we have been working through the attempt to formalize simplicity and confirmation — the view, as I understand it, that the credibility of a hypothesis is governed by the length of the shortest program that would generate the observations given that hypothesis, so that a hypothesis with a shorter description carries a larger share of the prior. I find this attractive and unsatisfying in equal measure, because I have spent forty years making exactly these comparative judgments with considerable confidence and no formalism whatsoever. So I want to do the exercise in the other direction: rather than asking whether the formal account is true in the abstract, I want to lay out my own working verdicts on concrete cases and find out what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the verdicts. First, given a dozen radial-velocity points, I judge a smooth three-parameter fit to be overwhelmingly better supported than a fifteen-term trigonometric sum that passes exactly through every point, even though the latter fits strictly better and the residuals of the former are visibly non-zero. Second, when the same dozen points turn out to be noise, and the shortest program reproducing them is essentially the tabulated values themselves, I refuse to call that program a theory at all — yet I take it that on the formal account it is precisely the leading candidate, which makes me suspect the account is scoring something other than what I mean by an explanation. Third, I care nothing for length measured in English prose: a rival whose informal statement fills a page but whose mathematical statement runs to three lines strikes me as simpler than one whose informal statement is a single sentence, so I must be measuring length in some privileged notation, and I cannot say which, since a physicist's notation and a logician's differ by far more than the handful of symbols I would tolerate as irrelevant. A colleague assures me that the choice of universal machine alters description lengths only by a constant that does not depend on the data; but a constant is exactly the wrong sort of thing to be reassured by when I am ranking two rivals whose lengths differ by less than that constant. Fourth, and this is the pair that troubles me most: the same nineteenth-century celestial mechanician twice patched a failing gravitational calculation by positing an unseen perturbing planet, once from the residuals of Uranus and once from the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion. The moves are structurally identical and, to my ear, differ in descriptive cost by a handful of bits; yet I regard the first as a magnificent confirmation of the theory that licensed it and the second as no confirmation at all, and I cannot locate the difference anywhere in the length of the descriptions. Fifth, in the opposite direction, I consistently prefer a hypothesis that is two pages longer provided it also accounts for a neighbouring class of phenomena I had not originally counted among the data — which suggests that what I am really tracking is not absolute length but the ratio of data compressed to bits spent, or perhaps the total length of the message comprising hypothesis plus data-given-hypothesis. That ratio-flavoured formulation is my own tentative principle, and I would like it tested rather than flattered. Finally, the case I keep going back and forth on: a law containing an empirically determined dimensionless constant, given to twelve significant figures. Those digits are by any measure incompressible bits in the program, so the formal prior should penalize the theory severely for them; and yet I do not feel that a theory is made less simple by containing a constant that had to be measured, and I would happily prefer it to a constant-free rival that fits appreciably worse.

What I am after, then, is a principle — or a short list of competing candidates — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a fresh pair of rivals on Monday morning without first consulting my intuitions about them. For each clause of each candidate, tell me which of my cases motivated it, since a clause that is not bought by a verdict is a clause I should be suspicious of. Then give me the candidates' verdicts on two or three cases I have not listed, including at least one where the deliverance would embarrass a working scientist who had been following it. And be candid about the casualties: which of my verdicts each candidate cannot preserve. I would far rather be told that the ratio principle costs me the fine-tuned constant, or that any principled account costs me the insistence that a table of noise is not a theory, than be handed something that reproduces all six verdicts by accreting clauses until the principle is as long as the cases it was built to cover. Two further questions, if you have room. Does anything change if I stop asking which hypothesis is simpler and ask instead which deserves the higher prior, or are those the same question in different clothes? And what is left of the reassurance about machine-independence once one grants that the constant multiplying two priors can exceed the difference between them?
