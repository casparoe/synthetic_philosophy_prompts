I work in software engineering, with no formal philosophy training, and I have been reading a chapter of a popular book on artificial intelligence that discusses logical uncertainty. It summarises the logical induction result as follows: 'A perfectly rational reasoner would have to assign probability one to every theorem, but no finite machine can check them all. Logical induction fixes this by treating beliefs as market prices: every sentence has a share that pays out a dollar if the sentence is true and nothing otherwise, and the share price is the reasoner's probability. A sequence of such prices counts as a logical inductor provided no computable trading strategy can earn unbounded profit while risking only a bounded amount. Markets of that kind exist and can be computed, and two things follow from this. First, the prices converge to the truth, so any true sentence — including one that no proof will ever reach — is eventually priced near a dollar; the market discovers mathematics without proving it. Second, the reasoner picks up genuine patterns: in the book's example it works out the far-out digits of π, which is only a matter of patience, and which shows that logical uncertainty is really nothing but a shortage of compute.'

I am suspicious of the first conclusion but cannot say why, and the second sounds plausible to me as an engineer. Please go through the summary sentence by sentence: what would someone who works on this accept, what would they regard as an overstatement or a confusion, and what is simply wrong? Then tell me whether either conclusion follows from the parts that are accurate, and whether the restriction on which strategies count as traders is doing substantive work or is a mere technical convenience. Finally, is this market anything more than hedging across predictors with a no-regret guarantee — the thing I would build myself — or is there something in the argument that has no counterpart in that framing?
