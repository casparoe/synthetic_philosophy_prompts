At a reading group I attend, one member defended the Knobe effect — people overwhelmingly judge a foreseen harmful side effect to be brought about intentionally, while withholding that judgment for an equally foreseen helpful one — by saying the asymmetry is like a visual illusion: robust, impossible to switch off even once you understand it, and revealing of the machinery that produces it rather than of the world it purports to represent. From there he argued that, just as the Müller-Lyer arrows tell you nothing about the actual lengths of the lines, the intuitions elicited by the chairman cases tell us nothing about whether such side effects really are intentional, and so any account of intentional action built on them is worthless.

Please audit the analogy. Set out the mapping explicitly — what plays the part of the retinal image, what the illusion proper, and what the independent check that reveals it as an illusion — and then the disanalogies, separating those that genuinely threaten his conclusion from those that are merely decorative. In particular: does calling the effect an illusion already presuppose an independent standard of correctness for "intentional" that no participant in this debate actually has, given that the only access we have to the concept is through the very judgments he wants to discount? Finally, if the analogy fails, is there a better one — does the asymmetry look more like a defect in the instrument, or more like what you would expect from a concept whose content is partly evaluative in the first place?
