You are an expert in formal epistemology and Bayesian updating with substantial experience setting graduate-level examinations, and I would like you to help me design a single problem-set question for the final assessment in my Bayesian Epistemology seminar. The cohort is small and mixed — eighteen students, roughly half from philosophy and half from statistics — and the question is worth 25 marks out of a 90-minute sit-down paper, so it should be substantial but completable. My difficulty is this: every question I have previously set on uncertain evidence can be answered perfectly well by a student who writes down the kinematics formula, remarks that it generalises ordinary conditionalisation, and observes that it therefore accommodates experience that does not take the form of a certain proposition. That is recitation, and I want an item that punishes it.

My half-formed idea is to build the question around a case in which an agent receives two pieces of uncertain evidence that are naturally modelled as shifts over two *cross-cutting* partitions — say, an inspector judging a manufactured tile first for perceived glaze colour (a three-cell partition) and then, under a different light source, for perceived surface relief (a two-cell partition) — and to require the student to carry out both orders of updating, using a rigidity assumption relative to whichever partition is being updated on. The crux I want them to reach is not the arithmetic but what the arithmetic exposes: that successive updates of this kind are generally order-dependent, that this dependence is a consequence of rigidity being imposed relative to a partition that the intervening update has already disturbed, and that a student must therefore say something about what rigidity actually commits the agent to — whether it is a harmless idealisation, a substantive empirical assumption about the agent's perceptual situation, or something that a norm of rationality cannot simply help itself to. I would like the best answers to go further and address what a defender of the rule can say in response (restrictions on admissible sequences of updates, a claim that the two orders encode genuinely different total evidence, or a proposal that the agent should instead have modelled the evidence by a likelihood or sensor model rather than by directly fixing posterior probabilities on the partition cells), and to connect this to the broader question of whether such an updating rule functions as a diachronic norm or merely as a piece of bookkeeping that is silent about what rationality requires. If you can find a way to fold in the observation that on a radical probabilist picture no credence is ever exactly one, and hence that the special case that generates the old-evidence puzzle is supposed to be blocked at the same stroke, I would be delighted, but only if it does not make the question too long for the time available.

Please work carefully and in the following order. First, before drafting anything, sketch three or four candidate cruxes for a question on this material and say in a sentence or two each which you would choose and why, including an honest note on which of them a well-prepared student could still answer by summary. Second, produce the question exactly as the students would see it, with a complete and self-contained numerical setup: give the prior distribution, the two partitions, the posterior distributions the agent is stipulated to adopt on each, and the relevant prior conditionals, choosing the numbers so that all quantities are simple enough to be handled in a few minutes by hand. Please verify the setup yourself before presenting it — confirm that the partitions are exhaustive and mutually exclusive, that the conditionalisation is well defined, and, crucially, that the two orders of updating really do deliver different posteriors for at least one hypothesis of interest; show that verification briefly, and if the numbers you first choose turn out to commute, adjust them and say so. Third, give a model answer of about 700 words that a student at this level could plausibly write in the time, balancing the computation against the interpretive discussion. Fourth, give a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, organised around five named must-hit points with the marks each carries, and then a separate short list of the mistakes students typically make — for instance, treating rigidity as automatically satisfied, conflating the failure of commutativity with an incoherence or Dutch-book result, or concluding from order-dependence that the whole framework is unusable — saying for each how many marks it should cost and what it reveals about the student's understanding. Do not include a bibliography or cite specific works by title or date; refer to positions, arguments, and authors' views in general terms only, and I will supply the attributions myself.
