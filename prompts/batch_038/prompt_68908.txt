ok, context first. I'm a senior and I'm applying to a 6-week summer philosophy program where the application wants a 2-page research proposal, and if you get in you spend the six weeks writing a ~4,000 word paper with a mentor attached. Mine has to be about something real, and right now what I have is a half-baked idea I can't get into shape.

It's about those studies where people hand out Gettier-type cases (and the name ones too, where a guy learned about a famous mathematician from a textbook and it turns out someone else actually did the work) to participants in different countries and get different verdicts. Then one camp says this proves that intuition-mongering in conceptual analysis is just parochial, and the other camp says the studies are garbage because of tiny samples, translation problems, and participants guessing what the researcher wants to hear. I've read a bit of both sides and honestly I think they both step over a prior problem, which is: how do you know the two groups were answering the same question in the first place?

Reason I'm not just making this up as an abstract worry — last year I helped run a mini version of it at my school. About 30 kids, we read them a Gettier case, but before they gave a verdict we had them write down in their own words what they thought the case was really asking. The verdicts split roughly how you'd predict. The written explanations split in a completely different way. Some of them had clearly read it as something like "would you trust this guy to claim he knows," a social/credibility question. Others read it as "does his belief connect to the fact in the right way." And a few changed their verdict afterwards once they heard the other reading said out loud. So theres a dilemma in here that I want the proposal to actually be about, and I've been calling it the stipulation squeeze — that's my phrase, not anybody's. Either you keep the vignette loose enough that it has a genuine intuitive pull, in which case different groups can be responding to different cases and the cross-group comparison doesn't show what either camp thinks it shows; or you nail the vignette down until everyone is definitely answering the same question, in which case you're no longer measuring an intuition at all, you're measuring whether someone followed your stipulation. Either way, "intuitions vary across cultures" looks like it can't be evidenced by this kind of study.

So write me the proposal. I need the question stated precisely enough that a person could disagree with my answer to it; why the two camps above don't already settle it (my guess is they both just assume the thing I'm worried about, but you should say that better than I can); my method week by week across the six weeks — and be honest that I'm a high schooler with a library card and a mentor, I'm not running new cross-cultural surveys, so the progress has to be conceptual, and you have to argue why conceptual progress counts as progress here instead of being a way of dodging the empirical question; what a successful result would actually claim at the end; and the part these proposals never have, what a *failed* version of this project looks like as opposed to just an unfinished one. Then tell me straight what to cut if the squeeze turns out too big for 4,000 words.

No bibliography or reference list — I'll go dig up the actual papers once I know which ones I'm supposed to be looking for. Just talk about the positions and arguments.
