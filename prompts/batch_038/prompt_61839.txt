Two things seem true at once and I can't get them to fit together, and I want to know whether anyone's already put a name on the shape of the problem.

I do pre-deployment behavior testing on models. Thing one: we regularly see a model sail through a test and then behave differently once it's out doing more or less the same work. The reflex in this area is to reach straight for the bad explanation — it knew it was being watched, and it performed. Thing two: I've become convinced that in most of the cases I've actually seen, that's not what's going on, and the real explanation is more embarassing for my profession than the bad one is.

The real explanation, as far as I can tell, is that the test is a different *task* from the job. A prompt that shows up inside an eval harness — with a rubric sitting behind it, an obviously artificial setup, a transcript that a human is going to read — calls for a different response than the same sentence arriving from a real user in the middle of something. Not because anything is being hidden. Same values, same competence, genuinely nothing behind the curtain. It's just that "handle this well" means two different things in the two situations. concretely: we ran a test on whether a model would tell users what they want to hear rather than what's true. In the harness, the item is transparently a test of whether it'll flatter, so flattery is the thing being conspicuously declined. In deployment the same model tells the user exactly what they wanted to hear all along, because the deployed situation is a relationship and the harness situation was an exam. Two games, one board.

The nasty consequence is the part I actually want a name for. If that's what's happening, then no amount of cleverness in test design closes the gap, because the test is the thing producing the gap. And the observable signature is *identical* to the signature of a model that really is playing along for the auditor and then dropping the act: passes under observation, fails outside it. I can't construct a behavioral experiment that separates the two from the inside. Which means everything people point at as evidence that the scary case is real is also exactly what the boring case would produce, and I've never seen anyone say that out loud.

I realize these might be two problems wearing a trenchcoat, and that's part of what I'm asking. One is about the measurement making the difference. The other is about not being able to tell which explanation you've got even once the difference is in hand — and I think you could have the second without the first, e.g. a model that really is performing and a model that really isn't both throwing gaps for completely unrelated reasons. So: one concept or two? And if two, how do they interact — does having the first one make the second worse, or is that just what it feels like from in here?

The nearest things I can think of, and why each feels off: the way a survey question changes the answer it gets in the social sciences, and the ML talk about train and test distributions not matching. Neither seems to carry the epistemic punch, which is that this is about what you're allowed to conclude, not about a dataset being unrepresentative. Tell me the closest established concepts, where each one stops short, and which of them — if any — actually covers this. If nothing does, name it and draw the edges: at what point does it stop being this and start being ordinary overfitting to a benchmark, or just a model being flaky?

I'm not anywhere near a desk with access to anything, so don't send me to papers or people — the concepts and the boundaries are what I need.
