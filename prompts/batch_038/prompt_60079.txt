TA-ing a seminar on philosophy and the good life and next week I'm putting Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life next to Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. I know that's a slightly odd pairing but it worked when I tried it on my roomate, so I'm committed now.

Problem: my students come out of both saying exactly the same thing -- that Stevens's mistake was working too hard and never having any fun, and that the cure is to play more. Which is what Schlick sounds like if you only catch the headline, and my memory of the actual argument is blurrier than I'd like, so tell me where I've mangled it. As I have it: you can't answer "what's the meaning of life?" by naming some purpose the whole thing serves, because that just pushes the question one more link along the means-end chain and there's no final rung to land on, so meaning has to be immanent in the living -- in activities that carry their value in themselves rather than in what they're for. He cashes that out with a work/play contrast, work being what you do for the sake of something beyond it and play what you do for its own sake, but then, crucially, he says the distinction tracks the agent's attitude and not the outward activity, so research can be play for the person absorbed in it and a game can be drudgery for someone playing it to earn a living. And then the youth claim: youth is the meaning of life, youth not as an age but as that capacity for joy in the doing itself, unconcerned with goals. Roughly that.

Read that way the novel is a fable with a one-line moral and Schlick is a self-help pamphlet, and I don't think either is true. So do the reading properly for me:

- Stevens's idea of dignity -- the butler who cannot be shaken out of his professional role by anything external, who wears it like a suit he would never take off in public -- looks to me like Schlick's absorbed player turned inside out: same total involvement, opposite result. Can you develop that, or tell me I'm forcing it? And is there room to say Stevens does value the work intrinsically, that his life is play in Schlick's sense and the tragedy sits somewhere else entirely?

- The ending. The man he talks to on the pier tells him the evening is the best part of the day, that he should stop looking back and learn to banter, and Stevens resolves to practice bantering. Is that Schlick's play arriving at sixty -- late but real -- or is Ishiguro showing a man converting a philosophy of play into one more professional competency, which is work again? Its the second reading I lean toward and I don't trust myself on it.

- Borrowed purposes. Stevens's ends are Lord Darlington's, and Darlington's turn out to be appeasement. Schlick says a life organized as a means to something beyond itself can't be a meaningful one, but I don't see where he has room for the further thought that borrowed purposes can be wicked, and that that's a distinct harm from emptiness. Does the novel push past Schlick here, and would that count as an objection to his account or just as outside what he was doing?

- Where does the reading break, and what does the book know about meaning that the essay can't argue for? I suspect the first person narration matters a lot: we only get at Stevens's values through the gaps, and Schlick's framework might not be able to register that at all. Which scenes would you actually send them to?

Not asking you to quote either of them -- accuracy about what each one is up to matters way more than wording.
