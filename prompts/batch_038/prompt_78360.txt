I am two thirds of the way through a novel set in an invented mill city in the north of England, and I have come to the chapter I have been dreading. The speaker is Vivian Hesketh, sixty-six, who has run the city museum for nineteen years and retires at the end of the month. The occasion is the reopening of the rehung permanent collection: the doors have not yet opened to the public, and she is speaking in the atrium. The collection was made in the 1870s by three mill-owning brothers, and Vivian has spent the last four years rehanging it — putting the brothers' Dutch and Italian pictures on the same walls, in the same light, as work made in the city by artists who are alive now. Several of those artists regard the museum's holdings, and its whole idea of quality, as the aftertaste of empire and mill money. In March one of them was arrested in that atrium for an act of protest I will not spoil for you. She has agreed to come to the reopening on condition that she is not asked to forgive anything. Vivian's own history matters here: at twenty-nine she published one essay that made both her reputation and her enemies, in which she argued that a very great deal of what was then being celebrated was simply bad work, and that saying so was not an exercise of power but a description. She has never retracted it. She will not retract it in this speech, and my novel requires that she be right not to — but she is also, by the end of the chapter, a woman who has come to think she misread the essay she built her life on.

The speech must be built out of David Hume's essay of 1757 on the standard of taste, and it must be built out of it structurally rather than by allusion. Vivian knows that essay the way other people know hymns. She accepts Hume's starting difficulty: that judgments of taste look wildly various across persons and centuries, and that we nonetheless cannot help seeking a rule by which those sentiments may be reconciled; that beauty is not a property sitting in the object but a matter of what the object does to a mind; and that the standard, when it is found at all, is the settled verdict of judges who possess delicacy of imagination, practice, comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense — qualities Hume thought so rarely combined that the true judge is nearly a fiction. She also knows the story Hume borrowed from Cervantes about Sancho's two kinsmen and the hogshead of wine: each pronounces the wine good, one detects a faint taste of iron, the other a trace of leather, both are laughed at by the company until the cask is emptied and a key on a leather thong is found lying in the bottom. In the novel's earlier chapters Vivian retells this story as a triumphant young scholar, and she tells it as a proof that the delicate judge is right and the room is wrong. She cannot tell it that way now, and she cannot tell the room that she used to. She is permitted exactly one quotation, which she will speak without attribution — only one man in the room will catch it, and he will enjoy catching it. It is Hume's line from the opening of the essay: "We are apt to call barbarous whatever departs widely from our own taste and apprehension: But soon find the epithet of reproach retorted on us." Please set that line in the first ninety seconds of the speech, where it will be heard as an ordinary piece of eloquence by most of the room and as a private joke by a visiting professor who wrote a book about the 1757 essay and is standing by the coat check.

The room is as follows, and it is the reason this chapter has beaten me four times: the board and three of its larger donors; two city councillors who want the museum to stop being difficult; the staff, including the conservators and the night security team, whom Vivian likes more than she likes anyone in the art world; the visiting professor; about a dozen living artists whose work is on the walls upstairs, some of whom she thinks excellent and some of whom she thinks merely earnest; and the woman who was arrested in March, standing at the back with her coat on. The speech runs six minutes, so give me eight hundred to a thousand words, and write it to be spoken aloud by a dry, unsentimental woman with a habit of making a joke at her own expense and then refusing to explain it. Short sentences. No uplift. Three things must happen in it, and none of them may be announced as happening. First, the wine anecdote must serve as the hinge on which the whole address turns, not as an illustration dropped in from a lecture: what she does with it in the second half must depend on what she seemed to do with it in the first. Second, there must be a passage addressed unmistakably to the woman at the back — not naming her, not apologising to her, not conceding her argument, and not asking her to be patient — which she and everyone who was present in March will hear as a direct address, and which everyone else will hear as ordinary courtesy. Third, the speech must neither convert Vivian to relativism (that would falsify the novel and let the donors off) nor end in the comfortable conclusion that reasonable people may differ; it must leave the standard of taste intact and unbearably expensive, and it must leave the room with the sense that something has been paid. The word beauty appears exactly once in the whole speech, inside the last two sentences, because this is a woman who has gone forty years without saying it. Do not have her cite or allude to any text other than the one I have quoted, do not name real living artists, real museums, or real scholars, and do not supply a list of sources or a note on further reading — I have those. If it helps you calibrate the register: every draft I have managed so far either lectures the room or surrenders to it, and what I am after is the third thing, which is a woman holding an argument she still believes in the middle of a room it appears to insult.
