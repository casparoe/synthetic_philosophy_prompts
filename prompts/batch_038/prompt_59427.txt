I'm one of six editors on a psychology journal's replication initiative. We commission direct replications and publish the outcomes in a public tally that everyone — journalists, tenure committees, people on twitter — basically treats as a scoreboard. Here's the problem case: we just got back a well-powered direct replication of a published effect. It came in at p = .11, and the Bayes factor is around 1.0, so the data don't favor the original effect over the null at all. Our tally only has two bins, "replicated" and "failed to replicate," and the PI on the replication says that scoring hers as a failure is slander, since a non-significant result isn't evidence of no effect. One of my co-editors wants to move to a three-way scheme (supported / inconclusive / contradicted) driven by Bayes factors; another says thats just handing everyone an escape hatch so nothing ever really fails, which guts the deterrent value of the whole program.

There's also a wrinkle both of them are underweighting: the original study's effect size was almost certainly inflated by the significance filter, so a replication powered to detect the originally reported effect can be underpowered for the truth even when the effect is real.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Friday's meeting. It should recommend a specific scoring scheme — the categories, the thresholds that decide which one a study lands in, and who sets them and when — plus what we do about the tally we've already published, since roughly forty studies currently marked as failures would move under the new scheme. Give reasons the PIs on both sides could actually respond to, and take the escape-hatch objection seriously rather than waving at it.
