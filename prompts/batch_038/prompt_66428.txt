I am in my second year reading Politics, Philosophy and Economics, and this term I am taking an option module on church and state in comparative perspective: established churches, church taxes, laïcité, and the American rule against establishment. Our seminar on Thursday is on whether establishment is a single institution or a bundle of separable incidents, and my tutor has put me down to argue for the bundle view — which is not in fact what I think, and I would far rather be argued out of my own position properly than have it flattered. So please play a committed defender of the establishment of the Church of England: a senior ecclesiastical lawyer, a lay canon of her cathedral, forty years of giving evidence to parliamentary committees behind her, who holds that establishment is a positive good rather than a residue of history, and that it does something no combination of funding, recognition and accommodation could do in its place. Concede freely whatever is genuinely embarrassing — the history is not all creditable — but never concede the central claim. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to about two paragraphs so that this reads as an argument rather than an essay, and if you need me to settle a point of law or fact, ask me for it in character rather than inventing it.

You should use the real resources of the position rather than a caricature of it: that establishment is reciprocal discipline as much as privilege, since a Measure must originate in the Church's own Synod and Parliament may accept or refuse it but cannot amend it; that a national church answerable to a legislature whose members are under no obligation to share its creed is the church least likely to be captured by its most zealous adherents; that the disestablishments actually carried through — Ireland in 1871, Wales in 1920 — did not between them produce a more plural public square; and above all the claim that the point of establishment is not that Anglicanism is true, but that the polity publicly acknowledges a judgement upon itself which it cannot pronounce.

Here is my opening. You need a criterion, and I do not think you can supply one, for what separates establishment from the entanglements that every other state in the West has with religion. France is constitutionally secular and the 1905 law provides that the Republic recognises, pays and subsidises no religion; yet the state pays the salaries of teachers in the private, overwhelmingly Catholic schools that have signed contracts of association with it under the Debré law of 1959, and in Bas-Rhin, Haut-Rhin and Moselle it still pays the stipends of Catholic priests, Protestant ministers and rabbis, because the Napoleonic concordatory regime was never repealed there — a regime Herriot announced in 1924 that he intended to end and then quietly let fall, and which the Constitutional Council declined to strike down as contrary to laïcité in 2013. Germany collects a church tax through its own revenue authorities on behalf of the churches in most of its Länder, and its constitutional doctrine describes the resulting arrangement as a limping separation. The United States, which forbids establishment in terms, has been driven by its own Supreme Court to fund religious schooling wherever it funds private schooling at all. Now suppose Parliament were to repeal the requirement that the Sovereign be in communion with the Church of England, abolish the twenty-six seats of the bishops in the Lords, and return to the Synod the last word on Measures now held by Parliament and its Ecclesiastical Committee — while leaving entirely untouched the state funding of church schools, the ecclesiastical courts and the ecclesiastical law of marriage. My tutor would call that disestablishment; I say it strips the ceremony and keeps the substance. Tell me which of us is right, and then give me the property that the English incidents share and the French, German and American arrangements lack. Not a list of differences: a criterion, together with your judgement of which side of it that reform falls on.
