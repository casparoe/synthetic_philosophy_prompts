I am a high school senior running an independent study in the philosophy of physics with two classmates, and I have been moderating what has turned into four months of unproductive argument about the hole argument in general relativity. One of them is a committed substantivalist: he holds that the spacetime manifold consists of genuine individuals whose identities are fixed prior to and independently of whatever fields happen to be defined on them, that the metric field is itself a physical thing rather than a bookkeeping device, and that the correct response to the hole argument is not to abandon that commitment but to adopt Leibniz equivalence, treating diffeomorphism-related models as different mathematical representations of one and the same physical possibility. The other is a committed relationist: she holds that the manifold's points have no physically meaningful individuality apart from the fields, that the hole argument demonstrates precisely this by showing that point-by-point identity cannot be doing any representational work, and that the substantivalist's "Leibniz equivalence" is an empty concession that quietly grants her whole case while pretending not to. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of physics who is equally at home with the differential-geometric setting of general relativity and with the metaphysics of spacetime, and who has actually supervised adversarial collaborations before rather than merely writing about them. Think carefully before drafting, and begin your answer with a short visible brainstorming section in which you list candidate cruxes and then discard the ones that fail a simple test: a genuine crux must be a sub-question whose settlement would actually move at least one of the two parties, not the entire dispute restated in new vocabulary.

The deliverable is the joint statement these two should have produced months ago and could both put their names to, in three parts. Part one is a numbered list of agreed claims, at least a dozen of them, covering the technical scaffolding both accept: what the drag-along construction yields, namely that given any solution of the generally covariant field equations and any diffeomorphism that acts as the identity outside some matter-free region, the fields pulled back along that diffeomorphism are also solutions, coincide with the originals outside the region and on any Cauchy surface that does not enter it, yet differ from the originals inside the region when the two are compared point by point against the manifold; that diffeomorphism-related models are indistinguishable by any possible observation; that if manifold points are assumed to have their identities independently of the fields, the initial-value problem is left without a unique solution; that Leibniz equivalence is the standard substantivalist reply; and that the relationist reads all of these same formal facts as showing that no manifold point is individuated except by the fields at it and their relations. Crucially, flag any claim on this list that both would sign but would gloss differently, because I suspect that is where the real disagreement is hiding. Part two is a markdown table of the residual disagreements, with columns for an identifier, the dispute stated in the substantivalist's own preferred vocabulary, the same dispute stated in the relationist's own preferred vocabulary, a single neutral sentence that both could sign, a classification of the disagreement as genuinely metaphysical, genuinely physical, about standards of explanation and theoretical virtue, or merely verbal, and what would resolve it. Part three is the cruxes: three or four of them, each with the question stated exactly, an account of why it is a crux rather than a restatement, an argument or case that both parties grant has probative force on it, and each side's prediction about how that argument comes out, written down in advance. Here is the constraint that matters most: since both parties agree that no observation could ever distinguish diffeomorphism-related models, do not offer me experiments, telescope time, or gravitational-wave data as the test. Tell me instead what a non-empirical argument that both sides accept as probative would have to look like, and if you think no such argument exists for some crux, say so explicitly and explain what that implies about whether the dispute is settleable at all.

A few further requirements. Work at least one crux around the analogy to gauge freedom in electromagnetism, asking whether diffeomorphism invariance is the same species of redundancy as the freedom in the vector potential, whether each side is entitled to press that analogy, and whether the analogy breaks at some identifiable point that one side has been exploiting. Address directly whether the relationist can hold on to Leibniz equivalence without conceding the substantivalist's claim that the metric field is a physical thing, and whether the substantivalist can hold on to it without giving up the very commitment to manifold individuals that motivated his position in the first place. Also fold in one toy case that I want both of them forced to answer: a universe whose manifold contains exactly one point particle and no other matter or fields whatsoever. Say what each view is then committed to about whether there is a fact of the matter concerning which manifold point the particle occupies at a given moment, and whether either party ends up with something uncomfortable on their hands. Close with a short section identifying which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, the standard you used in reaching that judgment, what the two of them should agree to say instead, and a list of what each party concedes in advance would count as a reason to abandon their own view. Please do not include a bibliography, a list of citations, or name-dropped references to papers and their authors: I cannot check sources and I do not want padding, though I do want technical vocabulary used precisely so that I can look terms up myself. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words. Finally, keep this a constructive document rather than a dramatized dialogue, a diagnosis of why they are talking past each other, or a verdict on who is right.
