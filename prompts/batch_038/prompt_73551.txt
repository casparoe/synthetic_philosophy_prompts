I am the grants manager of a small charitable trust that funds grassroots projects in one city; in practice I am the only paid member of staff. We make about ten awards a year, between £4,000 and £15,000 each, out of roughly forty-five applications. Three volunteer assessors read the applications with me and two trustees sign off on every award. None of us has any training in economics, statistics, or biology, and no single application receives more than about forty minutes of our combined time.

For four years we have scored applications using a rule I wrote myself, which says in effect that the more an applicant puts at stake of their own money, time, or standing, the more we should believe their promises. I took the idea from reading a little about the handicap principle and about why costly signals are supposed to be hard to fake: a cost that a faker could not afford is what makes the signal trustworthy. I have become convinced that the version I have been running is wrong in some way I cannot articulate, and that the decisions it produces are getting harder to defend. What I want is a procedure my assessors can actually run on an application: an ordered set of questions to ask about any costly thing an applicant offers as evidence of commitment, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a verdict of "this tells us nothing," and where it hands the decision back to the trustees because what remains is a question about our priorities rather than about the applicant.

The difficulties it has to deal with are these.

1. Costs that are perfectly visible to us were sometimes cheap for the person who paid them. A cash match from a well-resourced applicant, or from a friendly funder who wants the project to exist anyway, looks identical on our form to a match scraped together by a group with nothing.
2. I have been toying with a stricter rule of my own: that no cost should count unless it falls on the applicant only if the project fails, so that they are in effect betting against themselves. I want to know whether that is an improvement, a mistake, or right in some cases and wrong in others. It occurs to me that a promise to repay us if things go badly may be worth nothing when the promisor has nothing to repay with, and that a competent applicant might reasonably refuse to sign it because so much of the outcome is outside their control.
3. Our scoring rubric is published and applicants coach one another on it. Whatever we reward, we now receive from everybody, and I cannot tell whether the signal has stopped carrying information or whether I have become paranoid. I would like some simple test we could apply to our own records once a year.
4. Sometimes the most expensive-looking act on the pile is expensive because the applicant is disorganized, desperate, or has no better options, and I suspect we are rewarding the wrong thing.
5. Many of the groups we most want to fund — small, poor, recently formed — have nothing to put at stake and no track record to point to. The procedure has to say what to do in that case, rather than quietly sorting such applicants to the bottom.

Please run the finished procedure on three applications we are considering now.

A. An established charity with a paid development officer, applying for £12,000 to run cooking classes for teenagers. They offer a matched £12,000 from a foundation whose director sits on their board and has funded them twice before, and they additionally offer to repay our grant in full if the classes are not still running twelve months from now. Their budget is immaculate and the trustees are keen.

B. A tenants' association of six people, applying for £6,000 to convert a disused basement into a community room. They have no cash and offer no match. Three of them will sign personal guarantees to repay the grant if any part of it is spent outside the agreed budget; all three are on low incomes and, so far as we can establish, own nothing. What they do have is a room-booking log from the local community centre showing the same four or five people turning up every week for two and a half years to run an existing homework club, and one of them was treasurer of a project that collapsed in 2019 owing money to a supplier.

C. An individual applying for £9,000 to make a documentary about a local housing dispute. Six months ago he left a salaried job to work on the film full time without pay, has run up roughly £14,000 of debt, and has damaged his relationship with his family over it. He offers no guarantees and no match, and there is no organization behind him. I should tell you that this is the application I have personally argued for at every panel this year, so if the procedure comes out against it I want to be told plainly rather than have the conclusion softened.

A few requirements on the answer. It must be usable by a volunteer with no background reading it once, in forty minutes, so please include a one-page version the assessors could hold in their hands. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure. Tell me which distinctions the underlying theory draws that your procedure deliberately drops for the sake of usability, and what that costs us in accuracy. Tell me where it will systematically misfire and in which direction the error will run. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind: I cannot check them and my assessors would not read them. Finally, if you think the whole enterprise is misguided — that trying to read signals out of applications is the wrong use of our forty minutes, and that something else we could do with the same time would produce better information about these groups — say so, and say what that something else is.
