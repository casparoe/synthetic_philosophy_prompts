I sit on a hospital ethics committee and I keep going around in circles on one thing. Maybe you can straighten it out.

During the worst stretch of the pandemic our system paid hazard pay to ICU and ED staff, then cancelled the bonus months later while people were still intubating. Same period, we ran "volunteers only" lists for the aerosol-generating procedures — but nobody who declined got disciplined, they just got scheduled somewhere else. Meanwhile the profession's own ethics code says physicians have an obligation to provide urgent care in a disaster even at greater-than-usual risk to their health, and then in the next breath says they should weigh that risk against needing to be available to treat patients later. And the ER sits under a federal law requiring it to screen and stabilize whoever walks in, insured or not. Then look at the other side of the fence: common law says a bystander who strolls past a drowning stranger has done nothing legally wrong (a handful of states and most of continental Europe say otherwise), and we hand out Good Samaritan immunity precisely to people who had no duty to act in the first place.

My working hypothesis is that hazard pay is what an institution does when it wants to call something a duty without admitting it's one: the moment the risk gets its own seperate line item in the budget, the institution has priced the supererogatory and thereby conceded it wasn't in the job.

So: what theory of moral requirement does this patchwork presuppose? Does paying for the risk tacitly concede it sits outside the role, or can a duty absorb it once compensated? Can you still call someone a hero for what you're paying extra for, or does the payment turn it into an exchange and kill the category? Which strands actually contradict each other, and which are just doing different work? Tell me what the practice strictly presupposes versus what merely fits it nicely, where it's underdetermined — which rival accounts of the optional-but-praiseworthy would license the same policies — and what would concretely have to change in hospital policy under a view with no supererogation at all, where everything is either required or forbidden.

Bottom line in your first sentence, then the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
