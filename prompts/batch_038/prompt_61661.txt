I run a very small philosophy-ish podcast (sub-500 downloads a month, half of them my coworkers) and next month's guest is a metaphysician who defends four-dimensionalism — the view that objects are spacetime worms built out of temporal parts, and nothing is ever wholy present at a moment. I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background. I've read bits and pieces on trains and I've listened to maybe fifteen hours of people arguing about this, which is exactly the amount that makes you dangerous. So I need questions that do the philosophical work for me, because if I ask an open-ended "so, why should we believe that?" he'll give me the analogy where a person is like a road stretched out through spacetime and I'll have nothing to do with the remaining forty minutes.

Can you write me eight to ten questions, and for each one the follow-up I should use against the answer he's most likely to give? The follow-ups need to be real second moves that bite on what he actually says, not "interesting, can you unpack that." If the right follow-up depends on which way he goes, give me both branches.

Things I'd like covered if you can make them work:

- Lewis's problem of temporary intrinsics. My understanding is the standard endurantist comeback is that being bent is a relation to a time, and if that works then change doesn't need temporal parts at all. I want a question that makes him explain why that fails rather than just asserting it does. Follow-up for the case where his answer is that it makes change extrinsic to the object, and a different one for the case where he says the relation-to-a-time move is temporal parts in disguise.
- Relativity. He'll probably wave at special relativity and the end of absolute simultaneity. I want to push on whether that gets him four-dimensionalism or only eternalism, since I think those are different claims and you can hold one without the other. Follow-up for when he says yes, they're different, but relativity plus some further premise does the work — what's the further premise, and does he have an argument for it or a taste?
- The argument from vagueness (Sider's in Four-Dimensionalism, as I recall). Specifically what happens if his opponent just goes epistemicist and says there are sharp cutoffs in persistence that we're ignorant of. Does the argument survive that? Give me a follow-up for if he says it does and one for if he concedes it doesn't and falls back on his other arguments.
- Statue and clay. He'll say his view dissolves the coincidence problem because the two objects just share parts. Press him on the version where the clay outlives the statue, so they don't share all their parts and you still get two things in one place. Also the counting question: how many chairs are in the room right now, and if the answer is something like "depends how you count, there are indefinitely many overlapping worms," is that a feature he's happy to own?
- Worms versus stages. Make him pick one. And then whatever he picks, make him pay: if stages, the sentence about his own childhood is true only via some counterpart relation, which sounds like he's admitting ordinary speech is systematically false; if worms, the problem of the many and where the boundaries of his worm are.
- The objection that four-dimensionalism makes change impossible, since no single thing ever changes — only different parts of it have different properties. I want him to either bite that bullet on the record or explain what he thinks he's actually preserving about change.
- What would move him. Not "what counts as evidence" in the abstract but something concrete: a specific intuition, argument, or result that would send him back to endurantism. If he can't name one, I want a follow-up that makes the absence visible to the audience without me having to say the word unfalsifiable myself.
- One question on whether any of this has consequences outside metaphysics. I'd love something about identity over time in databases or version control, since that's who listens, but only if you think there's a real connection there and not just a cute one — if it's cute, cut it.

One more thing, and it matters more than everything above. For each question, tell me whether the thing it turns on is roughly settled among people who actually work on this, or genuinely contested — as in, is it common ground that the endurantist has the relation-to-a-time reply available and the real fight is over whether it succeeds, or do specialists disagree about what the problem even is? I don't want to phrase a question as though it's background everyone accepts when it's actually a live dispute, and I especially don't want to accidently present his side as the consensus just because most of what I've read was by people who agree with him. And if any of my bullet points above are confused, or rest on a misremembered version of the debate, tell me that before writing the question instead of building on top of it.
