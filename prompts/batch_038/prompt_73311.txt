I am the instructor for an upper-level political philosophy course and I need an independent assessment of one exam answer before I finalize grades. The question was worth 40 of 100 points, written closed-book with 45 minutes allotted.

**Question.** State Condorcet's jury theorem precisely, giving its assumptions and both of its conclusions. Then evaluate this claim: the jury theorem provides a decisive epistemic argument for flat majority rule over any scheme that weights votes by the competence of the voter. In your evaluation, identify which assumptions are doing the work, say what would have to be independently established for the theorem to apply to a real electorate, and say what the theorem does not settle.

**Student answer.** Condorcet's jury theorem concerns a group choosing between two alternatives, one of which is correct. Each voter has a probability p of voting for the correct alternative, all voters have the same p, and their votes are independent. The theorem has two conclusions. First, if p is greater than 1/2, the probability that the majority votes correctly increases as the group grows. Second, in the limit as group size goes to infinity, that probability approaches 1. If p is less than 1/2 the results reverse: the majority becomes more likely to be wrong as the group grows, so the best jury would consist of a single voter.

The theorem is epistemic rather than procedural. It assumes there is a fact of the matter about which alternative is correct independently of what anyone votes, so it applies to questions of truth, such as a jury's verdict, and not to questions where the vote merely expresses preferences. It is also important that the theorem does not establish p > 1/2. That has to be shown separately, and it is a substantive empirical assumption about the electorate rather than something the mathematics supplies.

The theorem does seem to support flat majority rule. Because a larger group converges on the truth with probability approaching one, there is no need to give extra weight to better-informed voters: adding ordinary voters does the work that weighting would otherwise have to do. Weighting also has a cost, since it requires us to identify who is more competent, and that identification is itself a judgment somebody has to make, with no way to check it independently of the voting procedure. So a weighted rule could be preferable only if we already knew the competences, and knowing them is precisely what we rely on the procedure to help us discover.

The crucial assumption is independence. If voters talk to one another or read the same sources, their votes become correlated, and correlation is what makes the theorem fail; it can be better to have three independent voters than thirty who all follow a single source. This has an awkward consequence for deliberative democrats, because the theorem requires voters to make up their minds on their own. Deliberation is thus not merely unnecessary but incompatible with the argument, and anyone defending democracy on Condorcetian grounds has to abandon the idea that citizens should reason together before voting.

Finally, the theorem's second conclusion explains why democracy is self-correcting. If an electorate falls systematically below the threshold, the majority will converge on the wrong answer, and a wrong answer that the whole society converges on is far more visible than a marginal error, so it gets noticed and repaired. That is what makes the theorem reassuring rather than merely conditional: the failure case generates the evidence needed to fix itself.
