For a seminar on visual communication and the philosophy of language, I need a dialogue between a defender and a critic of the claim that a system of pictures can be a language in its own right — not an illustration of sentences already composed in words, but a notation in which substantive claims about the world are actually asserted. The program in question descends from Otto Neurath's picture-language work of the 1930s and its associated encyclopedic ambitions; the dialogue may draw on the resources of that program, but it should not turn into a history lesson.

Setting. Marianne Vogl directs a workshop that designs pictorial charts for a planned international encyclopedia of common knowledge. Tobias Reuter, a logician, has been invited to review a set of exhibition panels before they go to print. They are standing in front of the proof sheets. Two panels are causing the trouble: one must convey a general regularity about a class of machines, and one must convey a negative finding — that no establishment in a given district employed more than a stated number of people in a stated year — alongside a term, the unemployment rate, that names no visible arrangement of things at all.

Marianne argues from these commitments rather than from a general fondness for pictures:

- A physicalist premise: a legitimate scientific statement is ultimately about spatio-temporal arrangements of observable things and their changes. If that is right, the content of any statement is the sort of content a picture can carry, since depicting just is presenting such arrangements.
- The workshop's rules of construction: a fixed inventory of signs, each with stated conditions of use; serial order carrying the order of the matter described; repetition of an identical sign carrying number; and an explicit transformation procedure carrying tabular material into pictorial form. On her account these rules do the work that syntax does in a verbal language, and a caption beneath a panel is a reading aid for the unpracticed, not the place where the content really lives.
- A level playing field about clarity: no notation interprets itself, words are learned by practice too, and in her view ordinary verbal usage is far more variable than her signs. To demand of pictures a transparency never demanded of sentences is a double standard.
- A practical aim she is willing to state plainly: the panels are meant to be readable across linguistic boundaries by people with no specialist training, and that aim shapes what counts as a defect.

Tobias argues from these:

- Expressive gaps. Depictive signs have no negation, no quantifier, no conditional, and no way to mark the difference between asserting something and merely supposing it. He should press Marianne on the panels in front of them: what on the sheet says "not," what says "every" rather than "these," what says "if," and what says "rate," a magnitude that is a ratio of two counts and is visible nowhere.
- A regress about rules. The rules determining how the panels are to be read cannot themselves be laid out in pictures without already presupposing a way of reading them; so the system borrows its determinacy from a verbally stated host language — precisely the dependence Marianne denies.
- Resemblance does no work. What makes a sign stand for what it stands for is settled practice, not likeness; and once that is granted, the system is simply another conventional notation, which must then meet the ordinary requirements on a notation, including that its characters be reliably distinguishable and non-overlapping — something shaded, pictorially continuous signs resist. He should add that both available ways of encoding quantity fail: repeated identical signs handle small counts, collapse at large ones, and hide ratios, while enlarging a sign's area invites the reader to track the wrong dimension.
- He is not an enemy of visualization. He grants that the panels communicate, and quickly, and across languages; his claim is narrower — that this makes them an effective instrument and not a language.

Requirements. Six to eight exchanges. Each speaker must take up the other's immediately preceding move rather than restating a prepared position, and at least twice one of them must be forced to reformulate a claim under pressure. Neither side may be a strawman: Marianne is not naive about likeness carrying meaning, and Tobias is not a partisan of words for words' sake. Include at least one point at which Marianne narrows her claim — retreating, for instance, from everything that can be said to the layer of the encyclopedia she actually cares about — and at least one at which Tobias concedes ground, such as that his determinacy requirement, pressed consistently, would indict a good deal of ordinary scientific prose as well. No references to real philosophers, books, or named doctrines; the arguments must stand on their own. End without resolution, but with a single explicitly stated crux — the question on which the rest of the dispute now turns — plus a sentence or two from each of them saying what an answer in either direction would commit them to.

Prose only, with minimal stage directions, between 1,100 and 1,600 words. Please write in American English.
