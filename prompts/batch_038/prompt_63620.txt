I am a junior at a public high school, and this year I serve on the student senate's budget committee. Last spring the committee adopted a new procedure for handling club funding requests above five hundred dollars, and I have been asked to prepare an evaluation of it for next year's committee. Before I argue about whether it works, I want to know what it assumes, and that is where I am hoping you can help me. Below I describe the procedure exactly as written in our handbook, and then I ask the questions I have not been able to answer on my own.

The procedure:

1. Any club requesting more than five hundred dollars must write its own proposal, and the proposal must end with a single measurable claim about what the money will produce — for example, "average attendance at chess club meetings will exceed twenty-two students per month during the spring semester," or "the robotics team will qualify for the state competition." The club writes this claim itself, since the club is supposed to know best what it is trying to accomplish.

2. Once the claim is registered, a market opens for two weeks. Every student is issued one hundred units of a school currency called rams at the start of the year. Students may buy YES or NO shares in the registered claim; a share pays one ram if the claim turns out to be true and zero if it turns out to be false. Prices float between zero and one on an order book the committee maintains on a spreadsheet, and the price moves only when two students agree to trade.

3. If the YES price closes at or above 0.60, the proposal is funded automatically and the senate takes no vote on it. If the YES price closes below 0.60, the proposal is denied automatically, again with no vote. The 0.60 figure was chosen because the committee wanted a clear line and did not want to fund anything that was a coin flip.

4. At the end of the semester a resolution panel of two students and one faculty advisor determines whether the registered claim came true. Because a denied proposal is never tested — the club never received the money, so there is no fact about what its attendance would have been — the handbook contains a second rule: shares on a claim that was never tested are settled at their own closing price rather than at one or zero. So a student who bought NO at 0.45 in a denied proposal receives 0.45 per share back.

5. Rams can be spent on real things: front-of-line passes at lunch, one excused absence per semester, reserved seats at assembly, and the right to buy tickets to the spring formal before the general student body can. The ten students holding the most rams in May have their names put on a plaque in the main hall, under the heading the committee chose: "Best Forecasters."

The same senate decides certain other questions by ordinary referendum — one student, one vote, simple majority — such as whether to keep the current dress code or whether to move the homecoming dance to a weekday. In a referendum nobody is paid anything for being right, nobody's vote counts for more because of past success, and the wording of the question is drafted by the senate rather than by whichever side has the most to gain from a particular phrasing.

The committee described the funding procedure as a small-scale version of Robin Hanson's futarchy, which as I understand it holds that in such a system "we would vote on values, but bet on beliefs."

My questions, in roughly the order they occurred to me:

First, what theory of belief does step 2 presuppose? It seems to assume that a student has some definite degree of confidence in the chess club's attendance claim, that this confidence is the sort of thing that can be extracted by offering payment, and that the extracted number is comparable across students and additive into a single price. Which rival accounts of belief does that rule out, and which does it merely happen to sit comfortably with? When someone buys YES at 0.70, does the procedure have to assume that person believes the claim to degree 0.70, or only that they are willing to act as if they do — and does it matter to the legitimacy of the outcome which of those is true?

Second, what does the fixed 0.60 threshold in step 3 presuppose about the costs of being wrong? Our rule treats a false YES and a false NO as though they were equally bad, and it applies the same threshold to a two-hundred-dollar art club request and a nine-hundred-dollar robotics request. Is that a substantive claim about how decisions should be made under uncertainty, or is it just an administrative convenience that quietly imports an assumption about symmetry?

Third, what theory of value does step 1 presuppose, given that the club chooses its own measure of success? The procedure converts every question of the form "should we support this activity?" into a question of the form "will this measurable thing happen?" — and it lets the interested party pick the measurable thing. What has to be true about goals, about the relation between a goal and a proxy for it, and about who is entitled to define the relevant good, for that substitution to be harmless rather than a way of winning the argument before it starts?

Fourth, step 4 is the one I find hardest to make sense of. What view of truth and of counterfactuals does it presuppose to settle an untested claim at the market's own closing price? Is the committee treating the market's estimate as though it made the claim true, or as though the claim had no truth value at all and the estimate is the best available substitute? And is that consistent with also having a resolution panel of three human beings exercise judgment about snow days and transfer students in the tested cases — a panel whose discretion seems to reintroduce exactly the kind of politics the market was supposed to replace?

Fifth, the two-track system puzzles me most of all. We let a market weighted by accumulated rams decide funding questions, and we let a strictly equal vote decide dress code questions. What distinction between the two kinds of question does that practice presuppose? Is it that some questions are about what will happen and others are about what we want, or is it that some questions are technical and others are contested, or something else? And is the line we have drawn the line the distinction actually requires — note that students can and do disagree about whether chess club deserves support at all, independently of any prediction about its attendance.

Sixth, what view of desert does the plaque in step 5 presuppose, given that rams buy perks, perks free up time and money, and time and money buy more rams? Is rewarding forecast accuracy a claim about merit, about accuracy being a skill, or simply an incentive device that we have dressed up as an honor? Is there anything in the practice that presupposes a single year of being right is evidence of skill rather than luck, and if so, what would have to change to make that presupposition defensible?

What I would like back is a reconstruction of the implicit theory or theories, organized around the six questions but not obliged to follow my order. Please be careful to separate three things that I keep running together: what the procedure strictly cannot function without assuming, what it merely happens to fit well with while being compatible with something else, and where it is genuinely underdetermined, so that two or three rival views would license exactly the same handbook language. Where the parts of the procedure presuppose inconsistent things, say so plainly and identify which part would have to give. Finally, tell me what would have to change — in the actual rules, not only in the philosophy — if we replaced the presupposed view with the view that a funding dispute is a disagreement about what is worth doing rather than a disagreement about what will happen.

A few practical requests about the answer: aim for roughly twelve to fifteen hundred words, in prose rather than bullet points, and write it so that a committee of high school students and one faculty advisor could follow it without having studied philosophy. You do not need to cite or recommend any sources, and please do not invent any — I have no way to check them, and I would rather you give me names for the positions in plain language than references I cannot verify. If you think one of my six questions rests on a confusion, say that instead of answering it.
