In the forecasting seminar I attend, two senior analysts have published conflicting probability estimates for the same near-term geopolitical event four months running. They exchange numbers in writing every week, both are competent Bayesians, and they consult largely different sources. A third colleague closed the discussion by remarking that Aumann proved rational people cannot agree to disagree, so at least one of the two must be self-deceived or acting in bad faith, and their estimates can accordingly be discounted.

Could you take that remark apart? I want the distinct precise claims the slogan might be standing for — the theorem itself and exactly what it assumes; a claim about what idealized agents converge to once they trade opinions; a normative claim about what we should do with our degrees of belief; a descriptive claim about how real disagreement behaves; a claim that no persistent disagreement can be justified once each party's opinion is known to the other — and for each, what would have to be true for it to hold and whether anything that strong is available in my colleague's situation.

Please also treat separately the assumptions I suspect are doing the quiet work: that two people cannot have different priors without one of them being irrational; that the two analysts are answering the same question over the same state space rather than merely uttering the same sentence; that a weekly written exchange of numbers amounts to common knowledge rather than something much weaker; and that nothing changes if the event's outcome partly depends on which policy gets adopted, as it may here. Deliver it as a menu with a verdict beside each reading — platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or irrelevant to the use it was put to — and close with the strongest conclusion about those two analysts that the slogan, on its best reading, actually supports.
