I'm on a small regional committee that votes on which animal and plant populations get legal protection here, which means roughly twice a year I have to write the word "species" next to a name, and I've become uncomfortably aware that my votes dont hang together. Here's what I actually think, case by case, not what I think I should think.

- Those salamanders that form a ring around the Central Valley in California: every neighbouring pair along the ring interbreeds, but the two ends meet again down south and behave like strangers. I want to say that's one species *and* that the two ends are two species, and I don't want to give up either half of that.
- Neanderthals and us. We interbred; plenty of people alive today carry a percent or two of their DNA. Separate species, still, no hesitation.
- Two little forest birds, look identical, sing different songs, share the same valleys, never hybridize where they overlap. Two species, obviously, and this one feels like the easiest call I ever make.
- Two lake fish, same shape and colour, would breed readily in the lab and the offspring are fine, but their lakes have been cut off for a couple million years. I say two species here too.
- Two duck populations that hybridize in the contact zone at something like one pairing in seven, and stay clean everywhere else: two species. Another duck pair that hybridizes at maybe one in forty, where the difference amounts to a slightly paler head: one species. So gene flow rate clearly isn't what I'm keying on, and I can't tell you what is.
- Two strains of gut bacteria, 98-odd percent identical across the core genome, swapping plasmids with half the rest of the gut, but forming a tight cluster of thier own. I voted yes, it's a species, and afterwards realized I'd meant something totally different by "species" than I meant with the birds.
- A marsh grass that came into being as a hybrid maybe a century and a half ago, doubled its chromosomes, breeds true, doesn't cross back with either parent, and has since taken over the estuaries. Way younger than anything else on my list, and I'd call it a real species without blinking.
- And two where I flipped. Genetics came back showing the two island lizards I'd split were each other's closest relatives and still exchanging migrants, and I lumped them without argument. Genetics came back showing that my one widespread beetle is actually four deep old clades, and I refused to split, because they look the same and still hybridize where the ranges touch.

Every time I try to explain this I end up saying "it depends on the group, you have to be a pluralist," which is useless to me, because the committee publishes one list, my name is on the votes, and a landowner whose creek gets regulated deserves a better answer than "the word does different work in different taxa."

So: is there a principle behind my verdicts? What I want from you is

1. The principle (or at most two competing candidates), stated precisely enough to apply — clause by clause, with a line saying which of my cases each clause got dragged in to handle. If a clause only exists to save one case, say so.
2. Run it on four cases I deliberately left off my list: (a) a mountain butterfly fixed for a different wing pattern from the valley population, meeting it in a narrow hybrid belt that's been stable and hasn't shifted in decades, hybrids fertile but apparently doing badly; (b) a dozen distinct-looking lineages of freshwater snail that don't have sex at all, ever; (c) an aquarium lineage of a common fish, sixty years of captive breeding, visibly changed, still breeds fine with wild stock when you force them together; (d) a thing I only have bones of, from three million years back, where no amount of future evidence will ever tell me who could breed with whom.
3. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle can't save and I should drop, and pick one — the one you'd actually overturn if you were in my chair, with the reasoning. I'd rather lose a case I care about than keep an incoherent rule.
4. Say whether your principle is a claim that species are real chunks of the world or a claim about good bookkeeping, and tell me whether that difference should change anything about how I vote.

Don't lay out the menu and leave me holding it. I know the standard options exist and roughly what each one costs. Take a position, argue for it, and if you think the honest conclusion is that no single principle fits and I should say that out loud to the committee, argue for *that* as a real position with consequences rather than a shrug. And flag anywhere you suspect one of my case descriptions is doing sneaky work — e.g. where I've quietly built the answer into how I described the case.
