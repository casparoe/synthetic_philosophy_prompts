I sit on a hospital allocation working group -- ventilators, transplant lists, that sort of thing -- and one of our members has been leaning hard on a 1977 philosophy paper by John Taurek, the one whose title is a question about whether the numbers count. He hasn't read it. He's getting it from a trade-press book about algorithms and scarce resources, and the book's paragraph goes like this:

"Taurek showed that five deaths are not worse than one death. Value doesn't add up across people, because there is no such thing as the five -- there are only five separate losses, each of them a loss to one person, and nobody is around to suffer the bigger number. It follows that whenever a rescuer, or a machine, has to choose between a greater and a lesser number, it should randomize: any other rule treats the extra people as a bonus that no one actually experiences. This is why the whole edifice of cost-effectiveness in medicine -- QALYs, disability weights, the arithmetic of quality-adjusted life years -- rests on a straightforward logical mistake. Note also that on Taurek's view saving the many is supererogatory: praiseworthy, never required. So a self-driving car that swerves toward the bigger group does nothing better than one that swerves at random, and neither can be faulted for its choice."

I got hold of the actual paper and its way more hedged than that -- he keeps saying he isn't persuaded of things rather than denying them outright, and he spends a lot of time on what a rescuer may do, not on how good outcomes are. Can you audit that paragraph for me, sentence by sentence? Three buckets: what Taurek would recognize as his own claim, what's a harmless simplification, and what's a real misrepresentation that changes the conclusion. And then a few things I couldn't work out on my own:

- Does he actually assert that five deaths aren't worse than one, or is the move weaker? If it's weaker, does anything else in the paragraph survive the weakening?
- Is the coin toss supposed to follow from that? It looks to me like you need an extra premise about caring for each of the people equally, otherwise why not just save whoever you happen to feel like saving -- which he seems perfectly happy to allow in some cases.
- Does his reasoning get any purchase on mismatched cases -- one person's life against five people losing an arm, say -- or is the book's slogan just not addressed to those, and if so does that matter for us?
- The QALY bit, mainly. Our member thinks this paragraph settles it that we should be lottery-ing our transplant list. Does anything Taurek actually argues support randomizing an institutional queue, or is that the book's own move? I keep feeling like a coin flip run by a policy is a different animal from a coin flip run by a person standing there who cares about everyone involved, but I can't say why.

For what its worth I lean toward thinking the numbers do count, so flag it if you think I'm reading the paragraph uncharitably just because I want it to be wrong.
