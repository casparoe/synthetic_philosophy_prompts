I sit in on a reading group at my university that's working through classical Indian epistemology — I'm a linguist, not a philosopher, so I mostly listen. Last week two of the regulars, Deepa and Marcus, got into a long argument about whether śabda (verbal testimony) really deserves to be counted as its own pramāṇa, or whether it's just perception and inference wearing a costume. For background as I understood it: Nyāya counts four knowledge sources — perception, inference, upamāna (learning what a word means by being told "that's the thing like the one you saw"), and testimony; the Buddhists count only the first two; the Mīmāṃsakas go the other way and add postulation and non-apprehension to get six. Here's roughly how it went, in order:

1. Deepa: testimony isn't separate. When someone tells me the 6 train is delayed, what happens is that I perceive the sounds, I have background beliefs that this person is generally reliable about trains, and I infer the train is delayed. So it's pratyakṣa plus anumāna all the way down. The Naiyāyikas' own test — a candidate source has to deliver knowledge you can't get otherwise — is exactly the test testimony fails.

2. Marcus: the test is right, your application is wrong. Look at what your inference concludes. On your story I first land on something about the speaker and only then on the train. That's not what happens. My mother says the stove is hot and I come to know the stove is hot; I form no belief about my mother's reliability. Worse for you: a child learning a first language can't have those background beliefs, since it doesn't yet know what "hot" picks out. If awareness of reliability were a precondition, nobody would ever learn a language. That's basically the later Nyāya line — Gaṅgeśa holds that the hearer's cognition of the speaker's trustworthiness isn't among the causes of testimonial knowledge.

3. Deepa: the child case proves too much. Either the child gets knowledge from words with no reliability filter at all, in which case a liar transmits knowledge too, which is absurd; or something filters, and the only candidates are features of the utterance and the speaker, which are perceived and reasoned from. Also your own school defines testimony as the statement of a trustworthy speaker, so reliability is doing work — and tracking reliability just is inference.

4. Marcus: neither horn. The filter is causal, not inferential. A knowledge source is a cause that produces a cognition which doesn't misrepresent; a liar's sentence simply fails to be that kind of cause. The hearer doesn't have to spot the defect, any more than you have to notice your eyes are jaundiced in order to fail to see white as white. Same shape as perception, which is reliable without your ever reasoning about it.

5. Deepa: then you've lost any principled stopping point. If a distinct reliable causal route makes a pramāṇa, you get memory, telescopes, calculators, a good map, a dog's nose. The Buddhists' reason for exactly two is that they individuate by object — particulars to perception, general features and relations to inference — and testimony has no object of its own. A sentence about the stove is about the stove.

6. Marcus: two replies. The object criterion begs the question: it assumes everything knowable is either a particular in front of you or a generalization over such, which rules out precisely what Nyāya thinks testimony is needed for — dharma, the specific efficacy of a ritual act, what liberation amounts to. None of those are perceptible and none admit of a pervasion you could infer from, so there's nothing left but the word of an expert. And "too many sources" cuts both ways: you'd call telescope-seeing perception because it's the same faculty, and that's exactly the Nyāya move — testimony is the same kind of cause each time, an utterance, so it's one source.

7. Deepa: so the whole fourth pramāṇa now rests on the Vedic cases, which is the shakiest part of the view. Strip those out and every remaining testimonial belief — trains, birthdays, what the treaty said — is about the public world that somebody could in principle perceive or infer. Secular testimony is a shortcut, not a source. And notice you've also just made your account hostage to a claim about authorless scripture. Also, if a speaker can transmit knowledge while the hearer tracks nothing at all about the speaker, you might as well say a parrot reciting a true sentence transmits knowledge.

8. Marcus: two things. First, you're relying on the very thing you're denying — you learned your Sanskrit from people, and your reading of the sūtras comes from a commentary tradition, so you can't coherently treat all of that as inference while you're using it to argue. Second, even in the secular cases testimony has its own defeat conditions: if I saw smoke and inferred fire, finding out the speaker was lying changes nothing; if I was told, it destroys my knowledge. Different defeaters, different epistemic kind.

They went another twenty minutes and I stopped following. Can you go through the eight moves and rate each one — clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, neither, mostly right, clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification, rather than just telling me who won overall? Then: which single move, if any, actually decides something, and which ones are just the same disagreement said louder? I kept feeling like they were both invoking the "can't get it otherwise" test but reading it three different ways — something about irreducible content, something about an irreducible causal route, something about irreducible defeaters. Sort out how many readings there really are, say which one each of them is using at each step, and tell me whether the argument survives once you pin the reading down.

Two cases neither of them raised, and I'd like a verdict on both under each side's account: someone who fully intends to deceive me but happens to say something true, and a tape recording of a long-dead but scrupulous expert playing in an empty room.

Also flag anything both of them missed — including whether the Nyāya four and the Buddhist two are even answers to the same question. And please be definite where you think an argument genuinely settles a move, but explicitly mark where you're reconstructing rather than reporting: I do not want you inventing confident positions for Gaṅgeśa or Kumārila just to make someone's move look stronger than it was.
