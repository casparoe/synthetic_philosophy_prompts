You are a professional historian of collective memory who also coaches moderators for adversarial-but-fair public interviews. Think carefully before drafting, and treat this as an argument-design problem rather than a writing exercise: I want questions that a well-briefed guest could not dissolve with a slogan. The booking is a ninety-minute recorded conversation on my history-of-memory series. My guest is a comparative historian of national memory whose new book advances a specific mechanism for why some lost-cause myths endure and others do not. As I understand her argument:

1. Durable lost-cause myths are organized around what she calls a *pivot counterfactual*: one nameable decision or betrayal at a single moment, such that had it gone the other way, the defeat would have been averted. Her stock cases are the tardy Confederate attack on the second day at Gettysburg; Vuk Branković's supposed withdrawal from the field at Kosovo in 1389; the German halt order of 24 May 1940 outside Dunkirk; and the post-1918 stab-in-the-back legend. She deliberately excludes Masada, on the ground that its central act is a chosen catastrophe rather than a foreclosed alternative, and so is not a lost-cause myth in her sense.
2. Such myths are resilient because of *pivot migration*: refuting a given pivot does not weaken the narrative, it relocates it. If the late attack on day two falls away, the myth moves to the absent cavalry, then to the failure to hold the high ground taken on day one, then to the decision to accept battle at all.
3. Therefore corrective work is not merely ineffective but productive: each published refutation supplies raw material for the next pivot.
4. Her prescription is what she calls *conceding the pivot and contesting the payoff*: stop litigating whether Gettysburg was winnable, and argue instead that winning it would not have changed the war or justified the cause.
5. Her comparative claim is that the density of pivots available in a defeat predicts a myth's longevity better than the scale of the defeat or the moral charge of the cause.

Deliverable: a moderator's question set plus the reasoning behind it. Please use exactly these sections, in this order:

4. **The questions** — eight to ten numbered items. For each: the question as I would actually say it out loud (one or two sentences, no throat-clearing); a bracketed line naming what it tests; and one follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, with a short note on what that likely answer is.

The questions must collectively cover all of the following, and each coverage point should be load-bearing rather than a courtesy nod:

- Falsifiability. If every refutation triggers migration, what possible observation distinguishes "pivot migration" from the trivially true claim that flexible narratives flex? Push her for a case where migration *stops* and the myth dies, and ask what the stopping condition is.
- Her own Kosovo case. The myth's center of gravity is Lazar's deliberate preference for a heavenly kingdom over an earthly victory, and the betrayal attached to Branković is treated by most scholars as a later accretion, with the epic form consolidated only in the nineteenth century — yet Branković is a pivot and Lazar's choice is exactly the "chosen catastrophe" she uses to exclude Masada. Ask which element her theory says carries the myth, and, if a pivot can be retrofitted onto an already-successful narrative, whether the pivot is a cause of the myth's durability or a symptom of it.
- The category boundary. Dunkirk is a defeat narrated as a moral triumph by a country that went on to win: roughly 338,000 men lifted between 26 May and 4 June 1940, with the national story built on the small civilian craft and on the halt order. Is that a lost-cause myth on her account, or has she selected her sample on the dependent variable? If it is not one, ask what her framework says about *victorious* nations that nonetheless cultivate pivot counterfactuals.
- The institutional alternative. In the American case the monuments, the textbook campaigns, the veterans' and ladies' organizations, the film industry, and the postwar political bargain around racial order plausibly did the causal work, with counterfactuals as decoration. Ask how she distinguishes a narrative-structure explanation from an interest-and-institution explanation, and press for a case where a pivot spread without sponsorship, or where heavy sponsorship failed to sustain one.
- Pivots of error versus pivots of treason. The halt order and the late attack blame a decision; Branković and the stab-in-the-back legend blame a *group*. Ask whether "concede the pivot, contest the payoff" has a different moral profile when the pivot names a traitor class, and what happens under migration when the nearest available pivot points at a minority rather than at a general.
- Measurement. How does she operationalize the density of available pivots without already knowing which pivots became popular? If availability is scored after the fact from the myths that survived, the comparative claim is circular; ask her to show the coding done prospectively on a defeat whose myth-formation she did not study.
- A forward prediction, on the record. Preferably a non-military defeat — a lost independence referendum, a collapsed currency peg, an abandoned expedition — where she will commit now to the direction and target of the next migration, and say what would count as her being wrong within five years.
- The cost of her prescription. Conceding the pivot concedes viability: that the cause could have won. Ask why a movement that has secured viability should care that the payoff is contested, and whether her advice works only for myths whose payoff is moral rather than territorial.

5. **Traps** — roughly 150 words. Three questions that sound impressive in planning but that I should not ask, with one line each on how they would backfire.
6. **Success criteria** — roughly 100 words. What she would have to concede, clarify, or predict for me to consider the hour a success rather than a pleasant survey of her book.

Tone throughout: fair, not prosecutorial. Every question should leave her a real answer available; the interest lies in which way she turns, not in cornering her into an admission.
