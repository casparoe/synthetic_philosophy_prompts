I have been working through the foundations of statistical mechanics on my own, and I have assembled an argument whose premises I cannot bring myself to reject and whose conclusion I find unacceptable. I would like to know where it goes wrong; if it does not go wrong anywhere, I would like to know which premise is cheapest to give up and what the giving up costs.

(1) The microscopic laws governing a gas in a laboratory apparatus are, to every precision anyone has measured in that domain, invariant under time reversal: if a trajectory of microstates is dynamically possible, so is its reverse.

(2) Statistical mechanics derives its predictive power from a single inferential license. Given a system's present macrostate, assign equal weight to every microstate compatible with it, and expect the system to evolve toward macrostates that occupy more of that compatible volume. That license is the whole engine; everything downstream of it is mechanics and counting.

(3) Nothing in the license refers to a direction of time. So apply it to the present macrostate of my study — the distribution of ink across my notebooks, the configuration of my own brain — and ask what the immediate past was probably like. Almost every compatible microstate sits in a larger-volume region a moment ago as well as a moment hence, since larger-volume regions are overwhelmingly larger. The subset of compatible microstates on which my notebooks were written down yesterday, rather than having assembled themselves this morning in a configuration that merely looks written, is negligible.

(4) Therefore the probability, conditional only on the present macrostate together with the laws, is that my apparent records and memories are the residue of a fluctuation, and that entropy was higher in my immediate past than it is now.

(5) If (4) holds, then the very considerations that recommend statistical mechanics to me — remembered successes of its predictions, preserved laboratory data, the trustworthiness of the reasoning by which I put this argument together — are considerations I am no longer entitled to trust. A hypothesis which, were it true, would destroy my warrant for believing it cannot be rationally believed.

(6) Hence at least one of (1), (2), and (3) is false.

I want to keep (1) badly. I have no appetite for retrocausality, nor for smuggling a fundamental temporal asymmetry into the dynamics in order to protect my diary. I am equally unwilling to reject (6) merely because it is uncomfortable to live with. So tell me which step fails, and tell me why that step looked innocent to me when I wrote it down — I want to understand the temptation, not only the error. Price each candidate repair rather than merely naming it. And if your repair amounts to adding a further postulate about the initial condition of the universe, say explicitly what that postulate's content is, and whether it earns its place or merely encodes the conclusion I was hoping for: a postulate whose sole function is to make my records come out true strikes me as indistinguishable from simply stipulating that they are true.

Please answer in continuous prose, as one would write a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicized emphasis, no tables. State your diagnosis in your opening sentences, before you develop it.
