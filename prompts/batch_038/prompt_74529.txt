Doing my quals reading on pain asymbolia and I'm stuck between two readings of what the dissociation is supposed to show, and I'd rather have them put head to head than summarized one after the other.

Reading A, roughly Grahek's line: a pain experience has a sensory-discriminative part and an affective-motivational part, and asymbolics have lost the second. They still feel it — location, intensity, quality, they'll even use ordinary pain vocabulary — but it doesn't hurt in the way that matters. Unpleasantness is a genuine ingredient of the experience, seperable at least in principle, and it's the ingredient that does the motivating.

Reading B, the evaluativist pushback, Aydede-style: there aren't two ingredients, there's one experience whose content is that some part of your body is in trouble and that this is bad for you. Asymbolics have lost the evaluative uptake, so their state is still a pain but not an unpleasant one. On this reading asymbolia doesn't show unpleasantness is a separable felt quality, it shows unpleasantness is an evaluation.

Both sides agree she's in pain and that she doesn't mind it. So: are these actually rival theories, or is part of the disagreement verbal — a fight over whether "unpleasantness" names a quality or a content? And is there anything an asymbolic could say or do that would break the tie, or is the clinical data too thin to decide anything? I keep feeing like both camps treat the same handful of case descriptions as decisive for their own side, which is suspicious.

Here's the wrinkle I actually can't work out. Suppose you got a clean-ish case where the patient says the pain is bad, says she'd rather it stopped, would take an analgesic if you offered one — and still doesn't move the limb or get away from the thing when she easily could. Which reading takes more damage? My instinct is B, since B says the badness-representation is gone and here it looks intact, but then A is stuck with a component that's supposed to supply the motivation and demonstrably isn't, which starts to look like a component doing no work at all. Tell me if that instinct is wrong.

Also, while you're in there: is asymbolia just pain's version of the wanting/liking split from the reward literature — incentive salience coming apart from hedonic impact, double dissociable in animals? Does importing that framework help, or does it quietly assume that the badness of pain just is hedonic impact, which is the thing in question?

And end by telling me what the winner, whichever you pick, implies about whether pain is bad because of how it feels or because of what it disposes you to do — and whether the asymbolic's pain is bad for her at all. No bibliography needed, I've got the papers, I just want the argument.
