Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of two hours about Solomonoff induction, and neither of us is equipped to settle it, so I would like an adjudication from someone who knows the technical literature. His position came almost entirely from a chapter of a popular book on the mathematics of rationality that he had just finished, and he read me the central passage aloud:

"Solomonoff induction is the first account of inference that turns Occam's razor from a rule of thumb into a proof. Every hypothesis a computer could so much as express is assigned a prior of two to the power of minus its Kolmogorov complexity — the length of the shortest program that prints it — so simplicity ceases to be a matter of taste and becomes a number you can compute. Because that prior is positive for every such hypothesis, nothing is excluded in advance; and yet Solomonoff showed that as evidence accumulates, the predictions of the mixture converge to the truth, which means the problem of induction is no longer a philosophical puzzle but a theorem. The catch is that the procedure cannot actually be carried out, since it would require knowing which programs halt, but that is an engineering difficulty rather than a conceptual one, and the approximations people actually use inherit the same guarantees. One sometimes hears that the whole edifice rests on an arbitrary choice of programming language; but any two universal languages differ only by a fixed constant, and a constant is swamped once the data start coming in, so the objection proves nothing. The upshot for practice is that when two theories account for the evidence equally well, you are not merely permitted to prefer the shorter one — you are obliged to believe it."

My friend's own view is that the machine-dependence remark gives the game away: since the choice of reference language is genuinely arbitrary and the discrepancy can be astronomically large, there is no fact of the matter about which theory is shorter, and the whole construction reduces to a decorative restatement of "prefer what you already liked." I am inclined to think that is a cheap shot and that the book is broadly right, but when he pressed me I found I could not defend either half of that response.

Could you take the passage sentence by sentence and say what a specialist in algorithmic information theory would accept, what she would tolerate as a simplification, and what is simply false? Four points matter most to me. First, what exactly does the convergence theorem assume, and what does it deliver — is "converges to the truth" a fair rendering, and what work is being done by the assumption that the environment is itself computable? Second, does giving positive prior to every computable hypothesis help or hurt when it comes to underdetermination and Goodman-style predicates? Third, do the tractable approximations really inherit the guarantees, or does the uncomputability bite deeper than the passage suggests? Fourth, is the constant-factor reply enough to dispose of the machine-dependence worry, or does it only dispose of an asymptotic version of it while leaving my friend's finite-data point standing — and are the constants involved even the same constant? Finally, tell me whether the closing claim, that equal fit obliges belief in the shorter theory, follows from any part of the passage that survives correction.
