I run a political philosophy reading group — 16 people, mixed grad students plus a few auditors who work full time, and I've got the room booked for two 90-minute sessions back to back, so you can spread the thing over both if you want. I want an activity that gets them to feel the case for choosing representatives by lottery instead of by election, but I don't want to say the word sortition, or even mention Athens, until the very end of the debrief. And more specifically I want it to land a point I think always gets skipped in these discussions: random selection looks clean on paper right up until you let the drawn people decline, at which point the willingness to decline turns out not to be random at all. Nobody in my group will have thought about that before hand and I want them to walk into it.

Materials I actually have: one room, a whiteboard, a printer, a hat, phones, and about $40 I'm willing to spend. I'll buy coffee-shop gift cards and cut them up, or run everything on paper tokens that convert to gift cards at the end, whichever you think is cleaner — but the payoff scheme has to be simple enough to explain in three minutes, and the group has to be deciding something they genuinely care about rather than a toy case, because that's the only way the stakes will bite. My plan is that the group allocates the $40, and whatever allocation comes out actually gets executed. If they decide one person takes all of it, that person takes all of it. Say that out loud at the start, it changes behaviour.

The structure I want you to build: run the same allocation task twice under identical conditions — same pot, same number of rounds, same information, same clock — except for how the allocator is picked. First arm, majority vote after open campaigning. Second arm, names out of a hat.

Then two twists. In the election arm I want an incumbency problem to emerge on its own: let the allocator set their own share, and let the group re-elect them next round, so someone who is good at talking their way to the front keeps the job three rounds running and the class realises they've manufactured a career politician without ever deciding to. In the lottery arm I want the opt-out: anyone drawn may decline, no reason required, but declining has to cost something visible — put a price on it that you design, because that price is what will skew the resulting body toward the loud and the unencumbered and away from everybody else, and I want them to see the skew in the numbers on the whiteboard rather than be told about it.

Give me: full rules, a materials list, a minute-by-minute timeline for both sessions, the debrief with an explicit mapping of which moment in the room corresponds to which claim in the argument, and what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I've never facilitated anything like this and I'll be doing it alone, so assume someone sulks, someone dominates, and someone tries to make a side deal in the hallway.

Last thing: be honest about the one feature of the real debate a 16-person room can't reproduce. My guess is the scale problem — that a room this small can't make you feel the difference between a body that looks like the population and a body that merely claims to speak for it — but tell me if I'm wrong and it's something else, and tell me what I have to supply verbally to cover the gap.
