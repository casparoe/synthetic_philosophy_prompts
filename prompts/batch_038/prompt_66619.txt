I'm in an evening reading group on the American pragmatists and we've just started The Public and Its Problems (1927). I'm not a philosophy student — I work in local government, which is half the reason I picked the book up — and I've hit a wall on the very first page. Before he gets anywhere near publics, or the state, or anything you'd expect from the title, Dewey opens chapter one like this:

"If one wishes to realize the distance which may lie between 'facts' and the meaning of facts, let one go to the field of social discussion. Many persons seem to suppose that facts carry their meaning along with themselves on their face. Accumulate enough of them, and their interpretation stares out at you. The development of physical science is thought to confirm the idea. But the power of physical facts to coerce belief does not reside in the bare phenomena. It proceeds from method, from the technique of research and calculation."

Then he wanders off into the state and I've completely lost the thread of why that opening was there at all. One person in the group reckons it's throat-clearing, a generic bit about bias, and that the real argument starts a page later; another says it's the whole book in miniature. Can you unpack it for me? What exactly is he denying when he says facts don't carry their meaning on their face — is this just the familiar point that data need interpreting, which nobody disputes, or is he committed to something stronger and more controversial about what a fact even is? And what's the force of the physics comparison? He seems to be saying that science convinces us not because of what's out there but because of how it's investigated, which makes the authority of science depend on procedure rather than on reality. Is that a fair reading, and if it is, doesn't it undercut him, given that he also wants social inquiry to be scientific?

Mostly though I want the payoff. Given this is a book about democracy and why the public has gone missing, why on earth would you begin with a claim about method? What would the case for my second friend look like — in what sense is that paragraph the book in miniature rather than a preamble? And coming at it from my day job: we commission a report, publish the numbers, and then act suprised when different groups read wildly different things into exactly the same figures. Is that a communications failure we could fix with better summaries, or is it something that opening paragraph is already diagnosing as structural? I'd rather not be told to go and read more Dewey — I want this passage thought through with me.
