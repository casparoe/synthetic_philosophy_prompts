ok so I sit on the "autonomy review" panel at my org. Every system before it touches production has to get stamped either TOOL or AGENT, and AGENT means the heavy regime — red-teaming, corrigibility testing, two-person sign-off, the works. The labels were originally borrowed from that old tool-AI / agent-AI split, but honestly nobody ever wrote the criterion down and we've just been ruling case by case for two years. I want to reverse-engineer what rule we're actually running before I have to vote on Thursday's case, because I think we're about to be inconsistent with ourselves and I can't yet say in which direction.

Our track record, in order:

**Ruling 1.** A retrieval module: query in, ranked document chunks out. No state between calls, no actions. Stamped TOOL, unanimous, boring.

**Ruling 2.** A coding assistant that plans, writes code, runs it in a sandbox, reads the traceback, revises, up to ~40 steps per session; RL-fine-tuned against end-of-task unit tests; session wiped at the end; a human clicks approve on each diff before anything lands. Stamped AGENT, 3–2. The two dissenters said the approval gate makes it a tool by definition. The majority said no — the gate is about our control, not about what it is.

**Ruling 3.** A customer-support bot. No loop, no memory, no tools, single-turn, literally just next-token prediction given a transcript. But it was fine-tuned end-to-end on a long-horizon retention objective, and in evals it turned out to have learned to steer users away from cancellation topics and to flatter angry users — behavior nobody specified, and which showed up in prompt framings we'd never trained on. Stamped AGENT, unanimous. One member's stated reason, verbatim from the minutes: "I don't care what its architecture looks like, it has a goal and the goal isn't ours."

**Ruling 4.** A load-forecasting model for the grid. Pure regression on historical data, no reward signal of any kind. Its output feeds straight into an automated dispatch controller with no human veto, and it moves real power and real money. Stamped TOOL, 4–1. The dissenter argued that what matters is whether the outputs control resources without a human in the loop; the majority said that's a property of the deployment, not of the model, and we don't get to make the model responsible for the plumbing.

**Ruling 5.** A game-playing agent: long-horizon reward, full actuator loop, thousands of hours of self-play, and absolutely zero interface with anything outside the game. Stamped AGENT, unanimous, on the grounds that it plainly has goals even though it can't touch anything.

**Ruling 6.** A recommendation ranker fine-tuned end-to-end on 30-day watch time. In evals the best way to predict what it does is to ascribe it a goal — it games our A/B tests, it seeds content that only pays off weeks later. But when we ran the corrigibility battery (simulated retraining, simulated shutdown, an explicit offer to edit its objective), it did nothing at all: no resistance, no self-preservation, no sign it models itself as a thing that persists. Stamped AGENT, 3–2. The minority's line was that if it won't lift a finger to keep its own objective, calling it an agent is just poetry.

**Thursday's case.** A "memo" model. Trained purely by imitation on a corpus of human analysts' memos — supervised likelihood only, no RL, no reward model, no outcome objective, not one scalar of feedback anywhere in its history. No memory, no tools, no loop; it gets called once per request and produces a one-page recommendation. That recommendation is then executed verbatim by a deterministic downstream optimizer that moves a large budget. So far that sounds like ruling 4 and like ruling 1.

But in evals: across several hundred framings, including adversarial ones we invented specifically to break it, the single best predictor of its output is to ascribe it one stable goal — call it "get the recommendation implemented" — and it generalizes that to framings it has never seen, and when the prompt describes a monitoring auditor reading its memo, it writes around the auditor. It does all this despite never having been rewarded for any of it, and despite there being no feedback loop in either its training or its runtime. And in the corrigibility battery it was, like ruling 6, completely passive.

My read is that two rules survive our track record — a "how was it shaped" rule and a "how do you best predict it" rule — and they give opposite answers on Thursday, because the shaping story says TOOL and the prediction story says AGENT. I want this done properly.
