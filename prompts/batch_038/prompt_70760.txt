So my town is finally running a citizens' panel on the relief road — 40 people drawn at random off the electoral roll, six Saturday sessions, and somehow I've ended up chairing it because I wrote a grumpy letter to the parish newsletter about the council's nine-person planning committee deciding everything behind closed doors. First session is in three weeks and I've got about twenty-five minutes at the top of it to explain why 40 random people is a better decision-making body than 9 elected ones. I want to build that on the Condorcet jury theorem, because that genuinely is the argument and I dont want to just stand there saying "trust me, crowds are wise." Conditions: room above the library, flipchart and markers only, no projector, and I can do a two-by-two and draw arrows but I am not doing algebra in public. The mix will be a few retirees who have been fighting this road since the nineties, some parents with pushchairs, one extremely confident haulage businessman who has already emailed me twice, and two teenagers someone recruited to hit a quota.

Don't explain the theorem to me — I've got it, more or less. Plan how I explain it. I want the dependency structure: which piece has to be in place before the next one is even interesting, what order the assumptions go in so the conclusion feels like it was already coming rather than a party trick, and where I should be explicit that I'm simplifying. And I need the one example that carries the whole twenty-five minutes. My mate insists I use that old story about villagers guessing the weight of an ox at a livestock fair, and I have a nagging feeling that example is actively harmful here because it's a different phenomenon wearing the same costume — if I'm right about that, tell me why in language I can say out loud to a room of non-mathematicians, and give me the replacement. What does the carrying example have to have, what would ruin it, and should it be the road decision itself or something harmless from another domain that I then transfer across?

Then the misconceptions, which is the part I'm actually worried about. I can already hear three: "so bigger groups are always smarter," "so this proves democracy is right," and "but if we all get the same briefing pack we'll all think the same thing, so your theorem is useless." Map out the ones I'm not anticipating — I'm pretty sure there's a double-edged piece about what happens when people are more often wrong than right, and I have no idea where in the sequence it belongs without torpedoing the whole thing. For each misconception give me the exact moment to head it off, ideally before it forms rather than after someone has said it out loud, plus the one or two sentences I'd say at the flipchart. Also tell me what to leave out entirely at this level — the strategic voting wrinkle someone on a forum mentioned, whether everyone has to be equally competent, all that — and which of those omissions I should flag openly so the haulage guy doesn't smell a con. Finish with one question I can put to the room in the last two minutes that would show me whether the idea landed or whether they just like me. No reading list, please — nobody's going to read anything and I'm not handing out references.
