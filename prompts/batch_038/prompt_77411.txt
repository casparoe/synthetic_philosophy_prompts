I'm in a welfare-economics reading group and I've drawn the short straw of defending what I actually think is a pretty strong claim: that Harsanyi's agreggation theorem more or less settles the utilitarianism question, and that the surviving debate is just about which axiom people are willing to give up. Let me state it as I'd defend it, so you can attack the right thing. Take a common state space and a set of prospects over it. Suppose each individual's preference ordering satisfies the expected-utility axioms, the social ordering satisfies them too, and the social ordering respects weak Pareto. Then any expected-utility representation of the social ordering is a positive affine combination of representations of the individual orderings; add a symmetry condition treating people as interchangeable and the weights go equal, and you have average utilitarianism. So my position is: if you apply decision theory to individuals, apply it to the social ranking, and accept Pareto, you are a utilitarian whether you like it or not — and a prioritarian or an egalitarian who says otherwise is owed an account of exactly which premise they're dropping.

I think I've already handled the three objections I keep hearing, and I'd like you to tell me whether those handles actually hold or whether I'm fooling myself. First, the calibration worry: since each person's von Neumann-Morgenstern utility function is only unique up to positive affine transformation, the weighted sum doesn't pin down a social ordering until you fix everybody's zero and scale, so the theorem gives a family of rules rather than utilitarianism. My answer is that the impartial observer story does the fixing — equal chance of being each person, with that person's utility function — so there's no residual freedom. Second, the ex ante / ex post worry: the objector says the theorem ranks prospects, not outcomes, and so licenses nothing about how to evaluate realized states of the world. My answer is that this is a change of subject, not a counterexample: social evaluation just is over prospects, and anyone who insists otherwise is refusing to accept that the object of the theory is a lottery. Third, the claim that the theorem smuggles in interpersonal comparability. My answer is that it doesn't need it: the weights are numbers that fall out of the axioms plus symmetry, and the whole point is to get a weighted sum without ever comparing levels of wellbeing across people.

So: what would count against the position, including against those three defenses? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want them sorted by kind. On the formal side, what results or constructions let someone keep weak Pareto and still land somewhere other than additive aggregation — where does seperability do the real work, what happens if the social ordering is allowed to violate independence even though the individuals don't, and is rank-dependent or otherwise non-additive aggregation a live route to prioritarianism rather than a curiosity? On the beliefs side, what happens when the Pareto condition is applied to acts and people have different subjective probabilities — if the social ranking ends up weighting both tastes and beliefs, so that the social probability is a mixture of the individual ones, is that genuine consensus or something spurious, and does that undermine the theorem or just reinterpret it? On the auxiliary-hypothesis side, what would have to be true for the individuals' utility functions to be wellbeing measures at all, and is there any way to check that rather than assume it? On the empirical side, would documented ambiguity aversion and violations of the independence axiom actually hurt the position, given that I'm offering it as a normative argument and not as psychology — or does the position quietly depend on people being roughly expected-utility maximizers? And then the meta-question I most want an honest answer to: does the position predict anything its rivals don't? Specifically, it predicts that every prominent non-utilitarian in the literature is committed to rejecting weak Pareto, or the social expected-utility axioms, or the shared-state-space setup — so go check that against how those views are actually held, and tell me if the prediction comes out false, because if the prioritarians and egalitarians get away with keeping all of it by shifting the domain over which social evaluation happens, that's a real problem for me. Finally, tell me whether the claim has been hedged into something that risks nothing — a taxonomy saying any anti-utilitarian must reject some axiom, true no matter what — and whether that's a fatal weakness for a view advertised as settling a substantive debate, or a legitimate reframing. And be clear throughout about which of these a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against them and which they'd dismiss as a misreading, since I'd rather know which hills I'm actually standing on.
