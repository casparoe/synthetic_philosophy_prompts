I spent forty years in condensed-matter physics and have taken up seventeenth-century natural philosophy in retirement. I am working through Hobbes's *De Corpore* (1655), in the English translation of 1656, Part I, chapter 1. Since I cannot reproduce his wording from memory, what follows is my own close paraphrase of the stretch where he sorts out which branches of philosophy admit of demonstration; please treat it as mine rather than his, and tell me frankly where you suspect the paraphrase has flattened something that matters.

The substance, as I read it: philosophy is knowledge of effects acquired by reasoning from the manner in which a thing is generated, or, conversely, of generation reasoned back from its effects. Science is possible only where causes are known, and causes are known only where the generation of the thing is known. It follows, he says, that geometry admits of demonstration, because we ourselves draw the lines and describe the figures from which we reason; and civil philosophy likewise, because we ourselves make the commonwealth. Natural philosophy does not admit of demonstration: the natural bodies we investigate we do not make, and from their effects we can reason only to *possible* causes — we can show that such-and-such a motion would produce the appearances, not that it does. He makes a closely parallel point somewhere in the last part of *Leviathan*, in his attack on the universities.

Three questions, which I should like answered by argument rather than by referral to commentators.

First, what work is the "because we make it" clause actually doing? Is it a claim about causes (only an artificer knows the generation of what he produces), about definitions (a geometrical definition must be generative, showing how the figure arises from motion, whereas no definition of gold or fire can be), or about epistemic access generally? He holds in the same chapter that truth and falsity are attributes of speech and not of things, and that we conceive nothing beyond what is in us. If reasoning is nothing but the consequences of names we have ourselves imposed, then we seem able to stipulate a definition of a natural body exactly as we stipulate one of a triangle. On his own terms, what prevents this? Is anything left for construction to do once the nominalist account of definition has done its work?

Second, does the concession about natural philosophy leave physics anything that answers to his definition of philosophy? He defines philosophy as knowledge of causes and then admits that in the study of nature we never attain causes, only possibilities. Is this a modest fallibilism within a science that remains genuinely causal, or does it reduce natural philosophy to the saving of appearances by hypotheses of the very kind he elsewhere derides? Say which reading the passage supports and why.

Third, a working scientist's objection: since Hilbert, no one supposes that a geometric proof depends on anyone's having drawn anything; the demonstration runs from axioms, and constructions are heuristic at best. Does this simply refute his rationale, or is the rationale immune, because what matters to him is not the diagram but the availability of a generative definition? If the latter, does his criterion not become empty, since generative definitions can be written for objects nobody has ever made?

Please reason from the passage and from Hobbes's own doctrines elsewhere in the work. Do not send me a bibliography; I will find the commentators for myself.
