A colleague of mine who works on reasoning capabilities in large models has a slide he returns to whenever someone proposes to repair a model's logical failures by writing the relevant rules into its prompt. The slide says that the tortoise in Carroll's 1895 dialogue with Achilles is exactly such a model: put the premises A and B in its context, add the hypothetical that if A and B are true then Z must be true, and it will accept that too, as just more text to condition on, and still decline to emit Z; so Achilles must add a hypothetical about the first three, and then another about those four, and the notebook grows to thousands of pages while the tortoise is still writing. From this he draws a strong conclusion: a rule supplied in context is inert, because any stated rule is only another premise requiring a further rule to be put to use, so a model that can recite modus ponens and then fail to apply it is a tortoise by nature rather than by prompting, and inference has to be disposed in the weights rather than handed over at test time. He uses the slide to dismiss chain-of-thought scaffolds and system-prompt rulebooks as Achilles's notebook writ large — voluminous, and no nearer to Z. I'm coming at this from the philosophy side and have to respond to the talk in a fortnight, and I don't want to object that the dialogue is a century old or that models aren't tortoises; I want to know how far the comparison actually reaches.

So please audit it rather than summarize either side. Give the correspondence explicitly — what in the dialogue plays the tortoise, what plays Achilles, what is the notebook, what is Z, what corresponds to accepting a hypothetical, and what plays the role of a rule of inference as distinct from a premise — and then say where each pairing strains. I'm most interested in features of Carroll's setup that the analogy quietly imports into the model case and that the model case may lack: the tortoise's challenge is normative, since Achilles is asked to compel assent by logic rather than to cause an output; the notebook has no capacity limit, whereas a context window ends the regress for reasons Carroll's point doesn't care about; the tortoise is a stipulated creature who grasps every hypothetical perfectly and merely refuses, unlike a system that may not represent the rule at all; and the dialogue is about the justification of deduction, not about the reliability of an inference mechanism. Tell me which of these disanalogies genuinely threaten his conclusion and which are harmless, since a generic list of differences is not what I'm after. It would also help to know whether the analogy behaves differently for rules that are demonstrated rather than stated — a few-shot example that the model reliably continues may be showing the tortoise something instead of dictating to it.

Then answer three things. First, does the conclusion survive: can the analogy carry rules in context are inert and therefore inference must be trained in, or does it license only the weaker claim that stating a rule is not the same as using one, which is compatible with a good deal of prompt engineering working? Second, on the familiar readings of Carroll's moral — that a rule of inference cannot be reduced to a further premise, and so that logical knowledge must be practical or dispositional rather than propositional — does the analogy even pick out the right target, or does it mistake a regress about justification for a story about where a capability is stored? Third, if the tortoise is the wrong figure for the model, is there a better one: it has struck me that the tortoise might map more naturally onto the human interrogator who keeps demanding that a system articulate its rule before he will accept the inference, or onto a system whose dispositions are sound but whose self-report is a regress, and I'd like to know whether either reframing is defensible or merely clever. A table of correspondences is welcome, and a recommendation about what, if anything, my colleague is entitled to keep from the slide.
