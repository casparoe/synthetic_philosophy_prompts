I am assembling a problem set for an advanced undergraduate seminar on the ethics of war, and I want one item to be a constructed case rather than a summary of familiar positions. Here is what I need built: a realistic, present-day scenario involving a single professional soldier fighting for a state whose war is clearly unjust, engineered so that the traditional Walzer-style doctrine of the moral equality of combatants and the revisionist, liability-based account associated with McMahan deliver the *same* verdict on whether that soldier may permissibly be killed by the enemy combatants opposing him, but *opposite* verdicts on whether he may permissibly kill them. To keep the target fixed: the traditionalist holds that combatants on both sides enjoy an equal permission to attack enemy combatants regardless of the justice of their respective causes, while the revisionist holds that permissibility tracks individual liability, which in turn depends on whether what a person threatens is serious enough to be worth averting by killing him.

The construction has to clear several hurdles, since the obvious versions fail. First, excuse must be structurally unavailable: the soldier has decisive first-person evidence that his side's cause is unjust, so he is not merely reasonable-but-mistaken, and he faces no duress — no conscription, nothing beyond ordinary career costs for requesting a transfer or resigning, no leverage against his family. Second, the unjust war's aim must be grave enough that revisionist liability plainly attaches to him, so that the agreement about his being killable is not an artifact of a trivial threat. Third, the opposing soldiers must not themselves be committing wrongs in bello; if a bad actor is smuggled in on the other side, the asymmetry I care about could be generated by that instead of by the theories. Fourth, keep it within ordinary contemporary geopolitics — no omniscient narrators, no exotic technology, no science fiction — and keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph of roughly two hundred words.

After the scenario, do the work rather than just announcing labels. Give each theory's verdict on both questions together with the reasoning that produces it, and be explicit throughout about the difference between justification and excuse, because a colleague insists that a traditionalist who concedes the soldier's full knowledge can only be offering an excuse dressed up as a permission; tell me whether he is right about that. Then identify the single feature of the case that is doing the most work, and run at least two variation tests — conscript him, or downgrade the war aim from conquest to seizing a mining concession — saying for each whether the verdicts still come apart in the way I specified or the case collapses back into an ordinary dispute. Close with how a convinced revisionist would attack the case instead of accepting it: which premise they would deny, and whether the traditionalist has a reply that does not merely restate the doctrine. Finally, flag anywhere your scenario stipulates a conclusion rather than generating it from the theories, since a case that builds the verdicts in by assumption is useless to me as an exam item.
