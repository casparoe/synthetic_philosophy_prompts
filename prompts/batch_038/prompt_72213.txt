I am a novelist, four years into a book set in and around the burning of the General Slocum — the chartered parish excursion that caught fire in the East River on 15 June 1904 and killed something over a thousand of the thirteen hundred people aboard, a great many of them women and children belonging to a single German congregation on the Lower East Side. Most of my characters are invented. Three are not; they are people who existed, two of them named nowhere but on a list of the dead and in the minutes of the relief committee. An editor at a literary quarterly has commissioned an essay from me on what the historical novelist owes the dead, and before I commit anything to print I would like the position examined rather than admired, because I fear it was assembled at different times for different reasons and has never been audited as a whole. The commission is not entirely academic. When my second book was optioned, the screenwriter placed a line of invented cruelty in the mouth of a real man who occupies four pages of the novel, and I wrote to the producer in terms I could not now defend on any of the principles I am about to state.

Here is the package:

1. I hold that the dead cannot be harmed. There is no one there to be injured by a false portrait, and I do not think it coherent to say that a misrepresentation reaches backward to a person who no longer exists. Whatever obligations obtain here run to the living and to the truth, not to the dead.
2. Nevertheless I hold that the strictest obligation attaches to the most obscure among them — those who survive as a name and a date and nothing else. I have come to think in terms of two debts: the debt of the record, owed to a well-documented life defended by letters, photographs, and institutions; and the debt of the name, owed to a life with no other champion. In my practice the second is by a wide margin the stricter.
3. I hold that readers of historical fiction are not entitled to treat any particular in it as fact; the genre issues no warrant. I also research to the point of establishing the weather and the state of the tide for the morning of the fire, which I have always assumed sits perfectly well with the foregoing.
4. I hold that an afterword specifying my principal inventions discharges whatever residue of obligation remains, and I have included one in every book I have published.
5. I hold that invented vice is a wrong and invented virtue is not. I may make a real person braver, kinder, or more prescient than the evidence warrants; I may not make them meaner. Flattering a dead stranger strikes me as a gift rather than a trespass.
6. I hold that selection is the whole of the novelist's art and cannot itself be a wrong: no book contains a life, and nothing was promised about the parts I leave out.
7. And yet the failure I find least defensible in the work of my contemporaries is rehabilitation by omission — the shaped and sympathetic figure delivered to readers who will never check him against a footnote, achieved entirely by what the author declined to include. In my own manuscript I have dropped a documented act of petty self-dealing by one of my three real people because it slowed a chapter, and I have been more troubled by that omission than by anything I have invented.
8. I hold that I may imagine the interior life of a real person without limit, on the grounds that interiority is precisely what no archive preserves, and that I may never put in a real person's mouth a sentence the record does not support. For me the licence ends at the quotation mark.
9. I hold that dramatization is heavier business than prose — that a face does what a sentence cannot, and that performance installs belief by a route a paragraph does not possess. This is why I objected to the screenwriter's line and would not have objected to the same line in a novel of my own.

Please audit this rather than expound it. Which of these genuinely pull against one another, which only appear to, and for each real tension, what is the cheapest way to resolve it and what does that resolution cost — in the essay, and in the manuscript, since several of these commitments are load-bearing for decisions already taken. Rank the tensions by severity; do not present them to me as equally grave. My own suspicion falls on 3 and 8, but I would not trust my suspicion for a moment, and I should rather you told me the trouble lies where I have not thought to look. Two further questions I would like answered squarely: whether any single revision would dissolve several of the tensions at once, and whether 9 states a principled difference between media or merely dresses up my irritation at having lost control of a book. I have no attachment to any item on the list that I would ask you to spare.
