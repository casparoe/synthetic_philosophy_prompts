For several years I have kept a private log of my own aesthetic verdicts in cases involving fakes, copies, and attribution, on the theory that if I write them down before theorizing I will at least end up with honest data. I have reached the point where I want the theory. Here are the entries, in roughly the order I made them.

1. A fake Vermeer praised by the best experts of its day and exposed decades later. Before the exposure I would have said I loved the picture; after, I find I love it less. I cannot point to a single perceptible difference, and I do not believe the object changed. Verdict: diminished — and diminished in the thing I look at, not merely in the deed that produced it.

2. The same picture in a world where it is never caught. Verdict: also diminished; I am inclined to say the value was simply never there. But this leaves nothing in the world corresponding to the difference in value between entry 1 and entry 2, which bothers me more than I expected.

3. A gifted student's open copy, made as an exercise, never intended to pass for anything, hung where everyone knows what it is. Verdict: not diminished in the slightest, and something close to admirable.

4. A machine-generated image in the manner of a dead master, plainly labeled as machine-made, which I find genuinely lovely. Verdict: diminished — more than entry 3, though like entry 3 it deceives nobody.

5. The master's own replica: a successful composition the artist paints a second time, with his own hand, for a patron who wanted a pair. No deception, no borrowed reputation, full authorship. Verdict: worth considerably less than the first version. A copy with permission.

6. The reverse forgery: a dealer passes off a superb original by an unknown painter as a lost work by a famous master; years later the unknown painter is identified. Verdict: the picture goes up in my estimation when the truth comes out, because the achievement is larger — there was nothing to lean on. I also think the painter was seriously wronged by the dealer, and I do not want to say the wrong makes the picture better.

7. Overpaint and restoration. Given the choice between a damaged original and the same original beautifully completed by a later hand, I find I would rather look at the honest ruin. Separately: watching the news cycle around the panel sold at auction in 2017 as a Leonardo — long catalogued as a copy, heavily overpainted, then stripped and reattributed — I notice my estimate of it has swung several times without my having seen it any differently.

8. Cross-domain controls. A counterfeit watch that keeps perfect time and looks perfect: nearly as good as the real thing, with the loss legal and reputational rather than in the object. A replica violin acoustically indistinguishable from the old master's: worth a small fraction, and I cannot say why, since the sound is the entire point of a violin.

9. A forgery that is, to my eyes, better composed than the work it imitates. I prefer looking at it and would pay more for it. I also insist it is worth less. I am flagging this one as a flat contradiction rather than a puzzle.

The entry I keep returning to: my father, an excellent amateur, painted a careful copy of a work we both loved and gave it to me. It moves me more than the original does, which I have now seen in person. I cannot tell whether that is a correct perception of something in the copy, a confusion about what I am valuing, or evidence that I have miscategorized entries 3 and 4 all along.

My own candidate principle, offered to be broken: the value of an object is proportional to the achievement it is evidence of, discounted by the fraction of that achievement the maker did not have to supply himself — borrowed composition, borrowed style, borrowed reputation, borrowed risk. It seems to handle entries 5 and 6. It predicts that 3 and 4 should score identically, and it gives no reason whatever for 1 and 2 to differ.

I have read the standard moves — Goodman's split between arts in which forgery is possible and arts in which it is not, and Lessing's contention that what a forgery lacks is originality, construed as an achievement located in a historical situation rather than a perceptible property — and neither seems to me to reach entries 4, 7, or 8. Tell me if that is wrong.

What I would like back:

(a) One or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a new case without consulting you again, with each clause tied to the entry that motivated it.

(b) For whichever principle you favor, verdicts on three cases I have not logged: an aging artist who reproduces his own celebrated early work exactly, with his own hand, and sells it as a new piece — forgery, diminished, or neither; plus two cases of your choosing, selected specifically because your rival candidate principles disagree about them.

(c) An explicit list of the entries above that your principle cannot save, ranked in the order you would most want me to abandon them, with the reason for each. Please do not spare entry 9 or the father's copy; I would rather be told that entries 3 and 4 are the same case and that one of my verdicts is motivated reasoning than be given a principle with eleven clauses.

(d) A judgment on the meta-question: is the pattern that survives best read as tracking something in the objects, or as status and signaling intuition wearing aesthetic clothing? If the latter is live, I want a test I could run on myself, not a reassurance.

No need for citations or a reading list; I will fill those in myself.
