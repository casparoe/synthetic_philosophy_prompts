I'm working on a chapter for an edited volume on moral responsibility and literature and I keep going back and forth on wether the spine of it actually works, so I want a second brain on it before I commit. Act as a philosopher who works on moral responsibility — the Strawsonian line especially — but who also writes real literary criticism and doesn't treat novels as data mines. Think hard before you write: I'd like a rough brainstorm first, just scratch notes to yourself on what's actually at stake, which of my claims are risky, and what the obvious objections are — then the finished piece.

The chapter reads Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go through Strawson's Freedom and Resentment. Assume your reader knows the novel well and read Strawson once, in a survey course, and hasn't thought about him since.

Here's my spine. Push on it, don't just execute it.

Strawson's move is that holding someone responsible isn't settled by a metaphysical discovery about free will; it's settled by whether you take up the participant stance toward them or the objective one, and you switch to the objective one for reasons — he didn't mean it, he was coerced, he's a child, he's badly disturbed — reasons that either show no ill will was aimed at you or show the person isn't a proper object of the attitudes at all. Hailsham looks to me like a machine for producing that switch at scale. The guardians run the students objectively: things to be soothed, kept healthy, kept half-informed, never told straight. Miss Lucy loses her job for breaking that convention, and I want to read her as refusing the objective stance — treating the kids as people entitled to the truth rather than as patients of a policy. And Madame crying in the doorway while Kathy dances around the dorm with her tape on, hugging a pillow, is the opposite gesture: a recognition of someone as a participant.

Then the Gallery. Miss Emily's project is empirical — collect the art, show the world the students have souls. A Strawsonian ought to say that project was misconceived from the start, because being a proper object of resentment and gratitude isn't an empirical question you settle with evidence; it's a stance you take up or refuse. I want that objection developed rigorously, with the machinery shown. But I also want you to say whether the objection is too cheap. The novel seems to think the proof did political work, and that the world's refusal wasn't a bare refusal but a cultivated one — manufactured, funded, taught. Does Strawson have any resources for a stance that's collectively engineered, or does his picture quietly assume individual agents weighing individual reasons?

Second strand, and I think this is the interesting one. Strawson's reactive attitudes seem to need an addressee: resentment answers someone's ill will toward me, indignation answers someone's ill will toward another. The wrong in this novel is structural — nobody's ill will is aimed at Kathy. The test case is Tommy's rage after they leave Miss Emily's house, the bit where he makes Kathy pull over and screams in a field. It looks exactly like moral rage and it has no target. I've been calling this the addressee problem, but that's my coinage, not a term of art, and I dont want you writing as though it's established vocabulary in the literature — define it in your own words as something the essay is proposing. Then: does the novel expose a gap in Strawson's scheme, an apt reactive attitude with nowhere to land, or does Strawson have an answer — that Tommy's rage does have objects, just dispersed ones; or that it's grief wearing rage's clothes; or that the addressee requirement was never what Strawson thought it was? Argue for one. Don't hand me a menu of options.

Third, briefly: Kathy's narration is almost preternaturally free of resentment, while her guilt over Ruth — and Ruth's attempt at amends from her recovery bed, handing over the address and pushing them to go ask for a deferral — is the most intact moral relationship in the book. Is Kathy a case of reactive attitudes atrophying when they're never met halfway? Strawson thinks a wholesale switch to the objective stance is practically inconceivable, but I suspect that argument assumes reciprocity. Does the novel describe a state his argument is supposed to rule out?
