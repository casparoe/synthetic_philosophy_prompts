Long before he was famous as an economist Keynes wrote a book on probability, and philosophically its the most important thing he ever did. His view was that a probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis — a real objective thing you can in principle discover, like a mathematical truth. So for Keynes, either a claim had a definite probability or it was just a feeling people had. He also introduced the idea of the weight of evidence: the thinner your data, the closer your probability should sit to fifty-fifty, and it only becomes a real number as evidence piles up. That's why he thought so many everyday probabilities can't be compared to each other or turned into numbers at all. Then a young Cambridge philosopher, Frank Ramsey, wrote a paper showing this whole approach was hopeless — if probability is going to guide action you have to define it by what people will bet — and Keynes basically conceded the point in the introduction he wrote for the posthumous collection of Ramsey's papers. Everything Keynes later says about uncertainty and animal spirits in the General Theory is leftover confusion from a book he'd already given up on. Animal spirits just means business decisions get made on gut feeling instead of calculation, and his example about the newspaper contest where readers pick the prettiest faces shows he thought share prices are pure psychology with no connection to the actual value of the underlying business. The upshot is that Keynes thought models and numbers are worse than useless when it comes to the real economy, so you go with instinct and let the government do the spending.
