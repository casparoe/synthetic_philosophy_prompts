I have just taken a seat on the board of a small employee-owned cooperative — roughly two hundred member-owners — which self-insures its members' health and disability costs out of a common pool. Last month a proposal came before us to levy a supplementary contribution on members who take up what the drafting committee called elective risk: continuing to smoke, free-solo climbing, amateur motorsport. The proposal tried to be clever about it. Rather than excluding or surcharging anyone outright, it would offer each member an actuarially adjusted premium that could be purchased to restore full cover, so that a member who declines to buy it is treated as having chosen the lower contribution together with the thinner coverage that goes with it. The meeting went badly, and not because anyone was rude. It went badly because afterward I could not tell you what we had actually been disagreeing about. Some of us objected to the premium on the ground that it would fall hardest on people whose taste for risk is not really theirs to begin with; others objected that refusing it would mean the cautious were subsidising the reckless; others again said the whole thing was degrading regardless of who paid what, because it required the cooperative to sort its members into careful and careless kinds. Those are not the same complaint, and I do not think we yet know how they relate to each other.

So I would like you to build me a classification instrument — eight to ten concrete cases, each with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions in the debate about equality, responsibility, and distribution — which I can hand to the other eleven board members without telling them which positions are in play. Three constraints on its design. First, the cases must be miniature self-contained situations rather than questions about the surcharge itself; I want to know what people say when the stakes are described at a distance from our own dispute. Second, no question may use the words luck, responsibility, or desert, and none may name a position or a theorist; the instrument should sort people by what they say about cases, not by which vocabulary they reach for. Third, the leaves must include at least the following, and you should say if any of them collapses into another under questioning: a Dworkinian resource view on which hypothetical insurance markets do the work of separating chosen from unchosen disadvantage; a view on which the operative corrective is a shared ethos rather than any mechanism; Anderson's democratic-equality alternative, which denies the distributive framing altogether; a prioritarian leaf and a luck-prioritarian leaf, separated if my question set can separate them; a position that gives up on equality of distribution in favour of something about respect and non-stigmatising treatment; and a responsibility-insensitive strict egalitarian leaf. Any pattern of answers that my questions make possible but that no live position occupies should be listed separately as an empty cell, with a line on whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has thought to defend it.

I want to specify which distinctions matter for my purpose, since I suspect the familiar brute-luck/option-luck cut is not where this board is actually split. Build the tree, so far as you can, on these axes instead: whether what is to be equalised is resources, welfare, or the social relations among members; whether holding people to their choices is a constraint on the distribution or part of its point; whether the objection to leaving the imprudent to bear their own costs is about the severity of the outcome or about something else entirely — the expressive meaning of the transfer, the knowledge a scheme would need in order to administer it, the classification it imposes on people; and whether compensation runs all the way or only to a threshold. Please include at least three cases that stress the edges of the framework rather than its centre: a pair who make identical choices on different information, one of them having handled insulation for thirty years without being told what it was; a person whose modest wants are the sediment of a life in which wanting more was punished; and a person who is imprudent but would take the same risk again in the same position, so that what they endorse is the gamble rather than regretting it. Add the case I have failed twice to make my colleagues take seriously — the prudent member whose prudence is a temperament they did nothing to acquire. For each leaf, give me the one commitment the board member sorted there is stuck with and would probably deny in the room. And be candid about where the instrument is weaker than it looks: which pairs of leaves it cannot distinguish and why, which leaves you have shoehorned in because I asked for them, and where you suspect two positions I have listed are one position wearing two hats. If you think the premise is confused — that a boardroom fight over a premium does not map onto these theoretical positions, or that a quiz of this kind would give us false precision about ourselves rather than clarity — say so plainly and tell me what you would do instead. I would rather hear that now than run the exercise and misread the results.
