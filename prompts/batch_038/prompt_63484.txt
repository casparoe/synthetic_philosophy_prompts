Every autumn our college holds a reception for the benefactors of the humanities division — donors, trustees, a couple of deans, their spouses, faculty from across the division, and the work-study students who pour the wine and stand by the doors. This year the chair has asked me to speak for about eight minutes on what philosophy has actually achieved, and the reason is not celebratory. At last year's reception one of our trustees — a man who built a logistics company and has been quietly generous to us for two decades — asked me, entirely without malice, whether we were still working on the same problems the Greeks had, and if so, what exactly we had been doing with our time all these years. He is sincere, he has an undergraduate degree in chemical engineering, and he measures disciplines by whether their experts converge. He will be standing six feet from the lectern. So will a colleague from physics who has argued in print that our department is distinguished mainly by the number of questions it has failed to answer, and who will enjoy watching me work. Also in the room: essentially the whole philosophy faculty, including one colleague who knows the current literature on this exact question cold — she has read Stoljar's 2017 book defending a reasonable optimism about philosophical progress, and Chalmers's argument that the interesting question is not whether philosophy progresses but why there is not more progress — and she will notice immediately if I garble either. And at the student table sits my best senior of the last five years, who wrote an excellent thesis on personal identity and is going to law school in the fall instead of applying to PhD programs, and who asked me privately in April whether her four years with us had been an expensive mistake. I want a speech she could take seriously.

Here is what I have so far, and I want you to build the speech around it rather than around whatever the standard answer to this question is. First, I want to concede the trustee's point in its strongest form — stronger than he put it — and then not wriggle out of the concession. On the metric he cares about, convergence of expert belief over time, philosophy has not progressed, and there is no particular reason to expect it to: whether our choices are determined is as live now as it was in antiquity, and we are no closer to agreeing about what makes an action right. Second, I want the case that this is the wrong metric for any discipline whose output is partly exported. Physics keeps its results; when a part of philosophy gets results, that part stops being philosophy and becomes physics, or psychology, or economics, or computer science, and the books that record the achievement are reshelved in another library. A discipline that scores itself by what remains inside its own borders will look permanently unsuccessful in exact proportion to its success. But I do not want that argument handed to me as a get-out-of-jail card. It is obviously exposed to the charge of unfalsifiable self-immunization — succeed and we keep it, fail and we exported it — and the speech has to turn and face that charge, and either answer it or state plainly how much weight it can bear. Third, my own private measure, which I want named and used explicitly, because I think it is the actual content of the answer: call it foreclosure. Progress in philosophy shows up not as agreement about which position is true but as a steady shrinking of the set of positions an informed person can still occupy. Certain views are still held, by cranks and by first-years, but they now carry an argumentative debt that compounds: anyone who wants to hold them must answer to distinctions that did not exist when the view was first proposed, and the price of remaining informed while holding them keeps rising. That, not convergence, is what I would point to. You choose the illustration, but I want exactly one; it must be followable by a chemical engineer in a single sentence, and it must be a case where the foreclosure is genuinely owed to philosophical work rather than to a scientific discovery that philosophy merely stood around reporting.

The craft constraints matter more to me than they might sound. Roughly nine hundred words, speakable in eight minutes without notes at the lectern, no blackboard, no diagrams. No quotation from any philosopher and no title of any work: at a donor reception a slightly mangled famous line is worse than none, and I refuse to be the person who misremembers one in front of a physicist. Name at most two philosophers in total, and only if the sentence cannot be built without them. The phrases critical thinking and asking the right questions are banned, as is any version of the claim that philosophy's value lies in the journey, or that what it really sells is learning to think — that is the answer my colleagues find insulting and the answer the trustee would correctly hear as a surrender. The speech must not end with an appeal for money, must not end with an exhortation, and must contain no rhetorical question in its final paragraph; it should end so that the trustee feels answered rather than managed. It has to survive the six people in the room who could object on technical grounds without turning into a seminar for them: two or three sentences should be precise enough that my colleagues recognize the exact position being stated, while everything around those sentences stays ordinary English. I have read rationalist blogs for most of my adult life and I still think in those categories, so write for someone who flinches at applause lights and cached thoughts, who would notice if a paragraph existed only to sound resolute, and who has spent too long on the internet to believe that a room of smart people can be moved by an argument with no failure modes. If a sentence is doing no work, cut it. Then, after the speech, give me a separate note of four or five sentences saying where it is most exposed — the two places the physicist will push, and what I can say from the lectern, without notes, that is both true and short.
