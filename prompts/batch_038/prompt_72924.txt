My mom has been a staffer on our state legislature's joint resolutions committee for like nine years and I spent part of the summer sorting thier files, so I actually got to read how they've handled all the apology resolutions for old state wrongs. Their record so far:

- A state training school that beat and underfed kids in the 60s. Agency still exists, same name on the door, survivors alive and organized, they asked for the apology, state doesn't gain anything from it today, practice ended decades ago. Passed a full formal apology, no money attached.
- A jail program run by a county department that got dissolved and folded into a regional one in the 90s. No identifiable survivors, just advocacy groups, state still owns the building. Declined the apology, passed a "resolution of remembrance" that pointedly never uses the word apologize.
- A 1950s sterilization program authorized by the state health board. Living victims. Approved, but conditioned on the state paying for counseling and on the board releasing everything it had withheld.
- A wrong where the practice being apologized for is still legally authorized today, just rarer. Declined. The chair's line was that you don't apologize with the engine still running.
- A wrong done by a private company that held a state license; the state never ordered it but inspectors knew. Declined the apology, approved a statement admitting failure to protect people.
- A case where the victims' own organizations disagreed, one wanting words and one wanting money. Approved both, on the reasoning that the state doesn't get to pick which one the victims should want.

Here's the one on the agenda next month and my mom's curious what someone with no stake would say. In the mid-70s our state health department gave an experimental drug to patients in state psychiatric facilities without consent. Same department, same name, still exists. Most of those patients are dead; about thirty are alive and nearly all of them personally want the apology. The state doesn't profit off it anymore, but two current treatment protocols still cite the study's data, and there's an active lawsuit from the survivors. The program itself shut down in '79, except the statute that let the department medicate wards over their objection is still on the books and still gets used a few times a year with a court order. One coalition of advocates backs the apology, another says apologizing while the state is still citing the data is worse than saying nothing.

So: what should they decide if they want to be consistent with their own record? I keep flip-flopping on which old case is the closest analog, the training school one or the engine-still-running one, and I want you to argue it rather than just pick. If the precedents genuinely don't settle it, tell me what principle they'd be signing onto with each option, because I think that's the part nobody at the meeting is saying out loud. Also, which rules actually fit their whole track record? It looks to me like at least four could explain everything they've decided so far, something about the name on the door, something about the harm being finished, something about still holding the fruits of it, something about whether the victims want it. Give me a best guess at what they'll actually do versus what they should do, and then give me one made-up hypothetical case I could bring up that would force those rules apart, since right now I'm pretty sure they all give the same answer on everything real that's come through.
