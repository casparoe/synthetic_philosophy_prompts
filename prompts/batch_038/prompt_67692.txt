You are a professional philosopher who works on the metaphysics of artifacts, kinds, and aesthetic properties, and who is comfortable enough with typography and software standards to reason about them precisely. I am a software engineer on a document-rendering team. I have no formal philosophy training, so please define any technical vocabulary you introduce and do not assume I know the literature. Think carefully before answering: rather than settling on the first plausible reconstruction, begin by brainstorming several candidate readings of the practice I describe — including readings that look implausible at first glance — and only then commit to an account and defend it.

Here is the practice. Our stack resolves a requested typeface name against whatever fonts happen to be installed locally. When the requested family is missing, we substitute a metric-compatible stand-in — Liberation Sans for Arial, Carlito for Calibri — chosen so that advance widths, sidebearings, x-height, and cap-height match, which keeps line breaking, pagination, and table geometry identical. The same logic appears one level up: CSS fallback stacks and fontconfig alias tables map a requested name to an ordered list of acceptable substitutes terminating in a generic class such as sans-serif. Meanwhile variable fonts expose weight, width, slant, and optical size as continuous axes *within* a single design, while the boundary between two designs is treated as absolute. In ordinary PDF, a document may name one of the standard PostScript families — Times, Helvetica, Courier — without embedding any font program at all, and the viewer supplies whatever it has; the archival profile PDF/A forbids exactly this and requires every font to be embedded, including those standard ones. At the encoding layer, the standard encodes characters and leaves appearance to the font, so the same code point for a lowercase "a" is rendered double-story in Helvetica and Arial and single-story in Futura and Gill Sans, and both are uncontroversially the letter "a" — nothing in our pipeline warns about the difference. Yet Helvetica and Arial are treated as strictly distinct designs: different names, different licenses, never conflated, despite differences (the leg of the R, the spur on the G, whether terminals are cut square or at an angle) that are invisible to most users.

The specific artifact I want examined is a substitution spec my team is drafting. It defines an equivalence class over glyph instances using only measurable predicates: same character mapping, same advance widths and sidebearings, same baseline/x-height/cap-height bands, bounding box within tolerance. Any member of a class is declared an acceptable realization of the request. "Style" enters only as a scalar score used to *rank* candidates within the eligible set — never as a gate on eligibility. We dropped the idea of gating on style because nobody could define a style metric that reviewers agreed on. Separately, our design team talks about a family having a "spirit": a coherence across all twenty-six lowercase letters that is not present in any single glyph, and that our substitution engine has no representation for whatsoever.
