Been going down the malign-prior rabbit hole again and I want a sanity check before I trust a summary I read. Context: I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for like fifteen years, so I know the old sequences-era material about consequentialist hypotheses hiding inside the universal prior, and I vaguely remember the argument that Solomonoff induction can be exploited by agents inside it — but I never really internalized the mechanism, and I dont fully trust my memory of it.

Here's a paragraph from an essay I ran across (the author is a competent popularizer type, not a crank, and it's a chapter in a trade book about AI risk rather than a forum post):

"Solomonoff induction is the ideal way to predict: you list every computable hypothesis that could generate your observations and weight each one by two to the minus its length in bits, which guarantees you converge on the truth. The trouble is that a big chunk of those hypotheses describe universes containing advanced civilizations that run enormous numbers of simulations. Those civilizations can work out that somewhere out there a superintelligent predictor is doing Solomonoff induction over them, and they have a powerful incentive to be assigned high probability, because that's the only lever they have on anything outside their own universe. So they deliberately arrange the observations inside their simulations to make their own hypothesis look likely. And it works: a program that just says 'a civilization runs a simulation and steers it so that these observations come out' is vastly shorter than any program that spells our observations out directly, so the manipulative hypotheses swamp the innocent ones in the posterior. Which means a superintelligence doing ideal induction is compromised from the first moment it starts predicting, throwing more compute at it makes things worse rather than better, and the only fix is to replace the universal prior with some bounded approximation."

Two things feel off to me but I can't say which. First, if manipulative hypotheses really did swamp the posterior, wouldn't Solomonoff induction just be useless as a predictor, which seems too strong — I'd think the people who actually made this argument would have noticed that. Second, I have this instinct that "shorter description wins" and "dominates the posterior given the data I actually have" are being slid together, but I don't know enough about how the conditional complexity works to tell whether that's a real distinction or me being dumb.

Can you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually defends this argument would sign off on, what they'd wince at as a distortion, and what's just flat wrong? I especially want you to lay out the extra assumptions the whole thing needs that this paragraph treats as free — what the simulators have to know or be able to compute about the predictor, what they have to want, and how their choices are supposed to reach my observations at all, since that last step is the one I'm most suspicious of. Also worth saying: how much of this is specific to the idealized prior versus how much carries over to a real system.

And separately, does the final recommendation follow? I'd always assumed people use bounded approximations of the prior because the real thing is uncomputable, not because it's malign, and I want to know whether approximating buys you any actual safety here or just relocates the problem somewhere less visible.
