I'm a backend engineer on a small team that supports clients in the UK, and during a long on-call week in 2019 I ended up reading the summary and press materials around the Supreme Court's ruling in the prorogation cases (the joint Miller and Cherry appeals). I have no philosophy or legal training, but the episode has stayed with me, and I've since read enough Berlin and enough Pettit to have a hypothesis I can't test on my own. I'd like you to analyze that episode — the August 2019 advice to the Queen, the prorogation that took effect in early September and was set to run to 14 October, the two lower-court rulings pointing in opposite directions, and the unanimous eleven-justice judgment on 24 September holding the advice and the resulting Order in Council to be of no effect — through the lens of the three-way distinction between negative liberty as non-interference, positive liberty as self-government or self-realization, and republican liberty as non-domination. Treat the case name and the constitutional details as I've given them; if you think a detail matters that I've left out, say so rather than inventing it.

Here's the mapping work I actually want. First, fix the cast: who is the relevant agent, who is interfered with, and what counts as the interference — noting that individual MPs were not arrested, fined, or blocked from anything they had a legal right to do as private citizens, that what was switched off was the institution's capacity to sit, legislate, and scrutinize, and that the Queen granted the request under a convention that arguably left her no real choice. Second, give me each theory's verdict on the same facts. On my reading, negative liberty has an awkward time here, since the standard case is a paradigm of unobstructed power being exercised through lawful forms rather than a paradigm of coercion; positive liberty seems to fit the grievance almost too well, which raises Berlin's own worry about that concept; and non-domination appears to be the one theory built for this shape of complaint, because what it objects to is a power held on terms that need not track anyone's interests, whether or not it is ever used. I want you to press on that last point rather than just endorse it: the Court's test as I understand it was whether the prorogation had the effect of frustrating Parliament's constitutional functions without reasonable justification, which sounds like a judgment about the actual consequences of an actual exercise, not about a standing capacity. So tell me whether the judgment is best read as a legal enactment of non-domination, or whether it is really a proportionality-style inquiry into effects that happens to leave the prerogative power itself, and its unconditioned character, entirely in place — the power survived the case, merely with a new outer limit.

Third, there's an angle I can't evaluate myself. In my day job I think about authorization in terms of capabilities: the security problem is usually not the one observed misuse but ambient authority — a long-lived, broadly scoped capability that sits in a process unneeded, with no revocation path and no approval trail, where the correct fix is to shorten its life and scope it down rather than to police invocations after the fact. A colleague and I disagree about whether this is a good way to think about the 2019 case. He says it is exactly right: the ruling, on his view, is the judiciary discovering that a capability had been left unrevoked and unlogged for centuries and refusing to let it be invoked in the one circumstance that would disable the only component capable of auditing it. I said the analogy is doing too much work, because in the capability case the principal is fixed and known and revocation is essentially free, whereas here the identity of the principal is precisely what's in dispute, and revoking a prerogative power redistributes constitutional authority in a way no sandboxing does. Please adjudicate that disagreement move by move — his claim, my two objections — and say who is right about which specific point, if either of us is right overall. Then turn the episode back on the theories: what does it do to non-domination that the supposed dominator here was a democratic government with a majority in the Commons, a fresh leadership mandate, and a referendum result behind it, so that the standard republican reassurance (a properly constituted state is the guarantor of non-domination, not its threat) points the wrong way; what does the remedy — eleven unelected justices defining Parliament's constitutional functions as a matter of common law — do to Pettit's claim that courts can interfere without dominating, and does the case expose a regress about who controls the controllers; is this an instance of Berlin's warning about positive liberty being deployed to assert a collective real will, or a refutation of it, given that the assertion here protected a deliberative body rather than overriding one; and what is the status of the monarch's own role, which none of the three frameworks seems to have a natural place for. Finally, be explicit about the silence: what these theories cannot tell you — how long a prorogation is too long, whether the justification offered was sincere, what a legislature should do about a convention that makes the head of state a cipher — and where you'd expect a careful answer to say the frameworks are simply not addressing the question rather than straining them until they produce one.
