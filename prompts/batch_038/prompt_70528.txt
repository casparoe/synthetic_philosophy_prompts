I manage a mid-sized regional warehouse — we distribute specialty glassware and laboratory supplies across four states, roughly 180 people spread over three shifts — and eighteen months ago we installed a system that records, automatically and continuously, where each picker's scanner is, how long they dwell at each location, and how their throughput compares with the shift average. Nothing is enforced. Nobody has been disciplined on the basis of it: in the entire period two supervisors have logged into the dashboard more than a handful of times, and our written policy forbids using the data in performance reviews or pay decisions. I have said all of this to my team repeatedly, and I mean it. Yet in the first quarter after installation, units per hour rose by eleven percent and recordable injuries fell by a third, with no change in targets, staffing, or incentives whatsoever. Since then three further things have happened that I cannot account for. The informal practice by which senior pickers used to spend twenty minutes at the end of a shift walking a newcomer through the awkward aisles has essentially vanished. People have begun taking conspicuously long dwells at a small number of checkpoints that the system reads as productive work and that are in fact convenient places to stand and rest. And several workers have told me, unprompted, that they feel permanently observed; when I ask by whom, they cannot say.

My operations director holds that none of this raises a moral question. His argument is that getting someone to do something is a matter of threats, commands, and sanctions, and that since we issue no commands, impose no sanctions, and use the data to punish no one, there is nothing there to object to; the unease people report is a private feeling rather than an injustice, and in any case the numbers are excellent. Something about this feels wrong to me, and I suspect it is wrong in a specific way — that he is working with a picture of how one person gets another to act that is simply too narrow to describe what has happened on my floor, where the largest changes came about without anyone ordering anything. I also dimly recall, from a sociology course fifteen years ago, an argument to the effect that arrangements which make conduct permanently recordable can change it even when no one is actually looking, and that what such arrangements do is not mainly to forbid things. I could not reconstruct that argument then and I cannot now.

Please take the role of a political philosopher who has thought hard about how institutions shape the people inside them, and think carefully rather than reaching for the obvious conclusions about workplace monitoring. Begin by laying out, in your own words, what is actually going on in the situation above and what hinges on getting it right. Then address the following. Is my operations director's argument sound, and if not, precisely where does it break down? Is there a framework — name it and explain it — that accounts for all four observations at once: the productivity gain, the disappearance of the mentoring, the strategic dwells at rest-friendly checkpoints, and the sense of being watched by no one in particular? And does that framework show that I am doing something wrong, or does it merely redescribe an efficient arrangement in ominous vocabulary? I have to make a decision within six weeks: keep the system, dismantle it, or modify it in some specified way. Give me your recommendation and the reasoning behind it, together with what I should be measuring or asking between now and then, since I would rather have a diagnosis that commits to a position than a balanced survey. I am not asking for references or a reading list, and please do not pad the answer with generalities about transparency and trust.
