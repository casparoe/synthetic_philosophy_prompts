I work in software, not philosophy, and I came to Integrated Information Theory from the outside during the 2023 episode in which a large group of researchers published an open letter describing the theory as pseudoscience and a group of IIT researchers answered with a signed defence. What strikes me is that the shape of the dispute has not changed at all since: the same axioms and matching postulates, the same Φ formalism, the same TMS-EEG complexity measures the theory inspired for use on unresponsive patients, and the same two complaints traded back and forth — that Φ makes no testable difference to anything measurable, and that the critics are presupposing a third-person, function-first methodology the theory explicitly declines to adopt.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not disagreeing about consciousness but about what a theory of consciousness must do to be scientific. One side requires that Φ track reportable, measurable differences between systems; the other holds that Φ is fixed by an identity claim about experience itself, so a failure to measure it is a limitation of the instruments rather than evidence against the theory. I have a second, more technical suspicion: Φ is defined exactly, but the search over partitions makes it intractable for any realistic system, so every number actually reported is an approximation with no stated error bound — which would mean the defenders are defending a defined object while the critics are attacking a measured one.

Diagnose the disagreement rather than adjudicating it. Is it substantive, methodological, merely verbal, or two different questions carried under one name? Which of my two hypotheses does the real work, if either, and is there something the dispute actually turns on that I have missed? Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a computational result, a change of question — or argue that none should be expected, and why.
