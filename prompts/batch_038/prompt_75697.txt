I'm a fourth-year philosophy of science PhD student and the reference class problem is basically my whole thesis at this point, so bear with me. I've backed into a position I want stress-tested: I want you to argue FOR it, hard, as an actual argument with premises and a conclusion rather than a survey of the literature. And please no citations, no bibliography, no "for further reading" — I've read all of it, I just want the reasoning.

Here's the setup. The problem is usually posed like this: any particular sits in indefinitely many classes, each with a different frequency for the outcome, and there's no principled way to pick, so single-case probabilities are indeterminate. My claim is that this framing fuses two requirements that pull in completely different directions, and that the "problem" is an artifact of the fusion.

Requirement (1) is epistemic: the class has to be big enough and well-sampled enough that we can actually estimate a frequency for it.

Requirement (2) is about homogeneity: the class has to be such that no further property of the individual is statistically relevant to the outcome within it — roughly, Salmon-style screening off, and the spirit of Hempel's maximal-specificity condition.

Standard treatments demand both simultaneously, find that nothing satisfies both, and then announce a paradox. My position: seperate them and the paradox goes away. (2) is the metaphysical question — it determines what the single-case probability actually is, and plausibly picks out a unique or near-unique class. (1) is a question about how much confidence to place in our estimate of that number, and its perfectly fine for the two to come apart. A narrow, unsampled class isn't a counterexample to single-case probability; it's just a case where our estimate is bad. So the reference class problem dissolves into the unparadoxical observation that we frequently can't measure what is nonetheless there.

Now argue for that. Specifically I want you to:

- make the case that (2) can pick out a class without circularity (this is where I'm most exposed — screening-off is defined relative to probabilities, and I'm using probabilities to define the class),
- handle the objection that homogeneity is outcome-relative, i.e. the same particular sits in differently homogeneous classes depending on which explanandum you ask about, so (2) doesn't deliver uniqueness after all,
- explain why the dissolution is better than the three rival moves: biting the bullet and denying single-case probabilities exist, going subjectivist so probability is a feature of the credal state, or contextualism about inquiry, where the context supplies the class,
- and say what's left of Kyburg-style direct inference on my account, since that machinery seems to need exactly the fusion I'm rejecting.

Use my running example instead of the textbook ones. I want the probability that one particular bridge survives a magnitude 7+ quake sometime in the next 50 years. Candidate classes: steel truss bridges built in this seismic zone before 1970; bridges built by the same contractor; bridges whose first-mode resonant frequency falls in this band; bridges retrofitted with this particular damper design; bridges inspected by this agency. Some are broad and richly sampled, some are tiny or empty, and their frequencies disagree wildly. Show me what the position actually does with that case — especially whether it escapes the charge that "the homogeneous class" is just whichever class makes your numbers come out.
