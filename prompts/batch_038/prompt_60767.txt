You are a philosopher of art with experience advising museums on attribution disputes, and you are unusually good at taking a loose sentence apart into the distinct, precisely stated claims it might be doing. Please treat this as a careful piece of analytical work rather than a summary of a debate.

**The situation.** I am the registrar at a small regional museum. We hold a panel painting long catalogued as by a seventeenth-century Dutch master. Last year's technical examination suggested it is instead a workshop production — an assistant's hand, same period, same materials, same panel stock, almost certainly made under the master's eye and possibly finished by him. Our conservator believes she can distinguish it from the accepted original by something in the ground layer, but she will not commit to that in public. The chair of our board, a practical man, has twice ended the discussion with the same sentence: "If you can't tell the difference, there is no difference." He uses it in two ways. First, about the wall label: since visitors standing in front of it cannot tell what they are looking at, the distinction between "by the master" and "workshop of the master" is a curatorial vanity that costs us a headline. Second, about value: since the painting gives the same experience, it deserves the same place on the wall, the same insurance figure, and the same pride of the donors who gave it.

I do not think either move works, but when I tried to say so at the last meeting I got tangled up, because the sentence keeps sliding between meanings and I could not pin down which meaning I was objecting to.

**What I want from you.** Produce a menu of the precise claims that "if you can't tell the difference, there is no difference" could be expressing. For each reading, state it explicitly with its parameters filled in — who is doing the telling (a visitor, a trained eye, an instrument, the same person before and after being informed); by what means (looking, side-by-side comparison, cross-section, provenance research); at what time (now, in 1640, after being told what to look for); and difference *in what respect* (in appearance, in the work itself, in the experience it affords, in its value, in what one ought to do). Then give each reading a verdict: is it trivially true, substantive but defensible, false, or true yet irrelevant to the case? And say for each whether it would actually license the chair's conclusion about the label, his conclusion about value, both, or neither.

Before you write the menu, spend a short paragraph brainstorming the ways "tell" and "difference" can come apart in a case like this — including the possibility that the difference is knowable but not perceivable, and the possibility that a difference nobody currently detects is nonetheless a difference someone is positioned to detect. Generate the space of readings first, then prune to the ones worth presenting.

Also flag any point where the chair's argument appears to shift between readings mid-sentence, and identify the hidden premise that would be needed to get from an indiscernibility claim to a claim about labels or about value — say whether that premise is one he would accept if it were stated aloud.

Finally, in two or three sentences at the end, give me something I can actually say in the meeting that concedes what is right in his slogan and isolates what it does not settle.

Do not give me references, a bibliography, or names of books and papers — I cannot look anything up, and invented citations would be worse than none. (I have heard there is a distinction in the literature between works whose identity depends on their history of production and works where it does not; use it if it clarifies things, but do not build the answer around terminology I would have to go and find.) Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, organized with clear headings and numbered readings.
