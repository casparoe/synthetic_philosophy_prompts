I cover science and ideas for a mid-size outlet — the beat where a lab, a courthouse, or a records room turns out to be the actual story — and for the last month I've been sitting in a county archive in the Midwest going through the surviving books of a poor farm that ran from roughly the 1870s to the 1930s. What got me isn't the misery, it's the vocabulary. An entire life enters those pages as a handful of words that recur down a ruled column: admitted, discharged, died, and then the numbers hanging off them — cost per day, rations, "keeping." The books survived because the county had to account for money to the state, not because anybody wanted to remember anybody. There's no trace of what the people in them ever said, and the one volume that might have held the matron's notes is listed in the finding aid as missing since around 1954.

I want to run a short sidebar with the piece, and I've decided the honest form for it is a sestina, because of the rotation. The thing I keep circling is a argument I half-read a while back — a Haitian historian's book about how silence isn't just an absence but gets manufactured at four separate points: when the record is made, when the records are gathered into a collection, when they're pulled back out, and when somebody finally narrates them as history — and the sestina performs that mechanically. Six end-words, and the form guarantees the poem can only ever hand you those same six words back in a new order; whatever isn't on the list can never appear, no matter how hard the poem strains. That is precisely what an administrative ledger does to a person. So: write me a strict sestina, six six-line stanzas plus the three-line envoy, 39 lines total, with the last end-word of each stanza becoming the first end-word of the next and the standard rotation from there. Choose the six end-words yourself out of the ledger's own vocabulary — they should be words an institution would use about a person, never words a person would use about herself, and at least one of them should be a unit of account or a number-word. Then map the stages onto the stanzas: the first two are the clerk filling in the form, the third is the statute that decided which books got kept and which got burned, the fourth is the finding aid that renders a life as a fraction of a linear foot, the fifth is me narrating it in the present tense like it's evidence, and the envoy is where the missing volume lands.

Don't moralize, and don't use the word silence anywhere in the poem — I want the gaps to be structural rather than announced. Someone who's worked in an archive should read it and wince at how right it is; someone who hasn't should still get a good poem. American English, please. Hard cap: 39 lines and a title, and then stop — no note on your choices, no gloss, no alternate draft.
