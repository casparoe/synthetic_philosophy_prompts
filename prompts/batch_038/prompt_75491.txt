ok, so I've been building my own tiny analogy domain — deliberately not the usual letter-string one, and not the alphabet, so that neither I nor anyone else can coast on familiarity. Whole spec, it's really small:

- symbols are 1 2 3 4 5, in that order. Every symbol has a successor except 5, and a predecessor except 1. No wraparound, 5 is a hard ceiling and 1 a hard floor.
- a "run" is a maximal ascending contiguous chunk (in 2345 the whole string is one run); a "repeat" is a maximal block of identical digits (44).
- an item is always: if X changes to Y, how should Z change in the same way?

Three items I actually run on people:

(i) 123 → 124 :: 2345 → ?
(ii) 12 → 13 :: 44 → ?
(iii) 45 → 44 :: 2345 → ?

Note that (iii) is meant as a control: the letterwise reading and the "stepped back off the ceiling" reading agree there, so there shouldn't be any contest. But I keep seeing people's answers to (iii) shift depending on whether they got (i) first, which is part of what's bugging me.

Here's the position I want stress-tested. I think slippage in a microdomain like this is fully accounted for by a single ordered salience scale over the concepts in play, rescaled by local context: wherever the most salient reading is blocked, you slip to the next concept down that's still compatible, and the spread of answers you get across people is just that ordering plus random temperature. Nothing else is needed — no global "does this whole mapping hang together" check, no essence-finding step, no pragmatic reading of what the exercise is for, no seperate typology of kinds of slip. And when the data misbehave, I say the salience values were mis-set for that context and I re-estimate them.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say which ones a committed defender of the single-scalar view would himself concede as genuinely damaging, versus which he'd wave off as just re-fitting. Specific things I'd like you to weigh, though don't limit yourself to them: a stable bimodal split on one item (say 45 vs 55 on (ii)) that doesn't move when you vary wording or add filler; order/priming effects that survive repeated exposure; individual differences that are consistent across items, i.e. people have styles rather than noise; the fact that some people answer 2346 on (i) — an out-of-domain answer — which suggests legality isn't a filter on candidates at all but a post-hoc rationalization; and whether a single scalar can even represent a contest like the one in (ii), where there's arguably no ambiguity to resolve.

Then: is the re-estimation move insulating the view into unfalsifiability? If it is, is that a problem here specifically — this is supposed to be a computational account of fluid concepts, not just a description of choice — or is it defensible in the way re-deriving utilities from observed choice is defensible? Be willing to tell me the view is well insulated and that this is fine.

Finally, and this is the part I most want: using only my five symbols and the run/repeat rules, design two or three new items that force the single-scalar view and its main rivals (a global coherence / structure-mapping account, and a two-process account where a fast salience-driven answer competes with a slow checked one) to diverge. Say what each view predicts on each item and what observed pattern would kill which. Also tell me straight whether my view predicts anything at all that its rivals don't, or whether it's just a redescription of whatever comes out.
