I chair the Autonomy Review Panel at a regional electricity distribution company. Every software system that can act on our network — not merely advise a duty engineer, but execute — must be classified into one of three oversight tiers before it is permitted to operate, and since 2019 we have done this by asking a single question: how much can this system change its own behaviour after deployment? Tier A means it may only recommend, and nothing executes until an engineer confirms. Tier B means it executes inside a pre-registered envelope of conditions, escalates whatever falls outside it, and is logged and killable. Tier C means it acts outside any pre-registered envelope and is reviewed after the fact; it may also call other systems and solicit information from our staff. The policy was written for statistical models and it is now failing us. It has no slot for a system that learns nothing whatsoever yet deliberates over options it never tries, and it treats every kind of post-deployment change as though it were the same risk.

I want to rebuild the classification around a ladder I encountered in a Dennett essay and have not been able to get out of my head. Roughly, as I understand it: Darwinian creatures have only the fixed responses built into them at design time, and can be badly wrong in a novel environment with no way to correct. Skinnerian creatures learn by consequence, so their trials are performed out in the world, and some trials are fatal. Popperian creatures carry a model of their environment and can generate candidate actions internally and discard the bad ones without acting on them — the hypothesis dies instead of the creature. Gregorian creatures additionally recruit external scaffolds: documents, code, arithmetic, language, other agents, so their competence is not fixed by what sits inside their own boundaries. That top rung is named for Richard Gregory.

Please write the procedure my panel can run. Our reviewers are control engineers plus one safety-case writer; nobody has read philosophy, and nobody will read it before Thursday. Each request gets a ninety-minute session and a packet of at most twenty-five pages, much of it authored by the vendor, whose self-assessment is reliably flattering. I sign Tier A and Tier B; Tier C requires the Director of Operations and a notification to the regulator. The output must be reproducible enough that an auditor can be told why two systems that look alike from the outside received different tiers.

The procedure should specify: the questions in the order they are to be asked, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; what a vendor demonstration can establish and what it structurally cannot, given that a demonstration is itself a trial performed in a world the vendor chose; the rule for domain-relativity, since I expect a system to occupy different rungs in different task domains, and I need to be told which domain a classification is made against; the events that oblige us to re-run the classification without a fresh request being filed, particularly where the scaffold can be altered by someone other than the vendor; where the procedure terminates in a no; and where it hands the decision to a human being because what remains is a question of values rather than of fact. It should then map four rungs onto three tiers, naming explicitly which rung distinction must be collapsed and what the panel loses by collapsing it.

Apply it to three live requests. First, a reinforcement-learning crew-dispatch scheduler that keeps learning from live operational outcomes after deployment; the vendor describes it as self-improving and therefore as learning around its own mistakes, and the panel is inclined to grant Tier B on that basis. Second, a large-language-model agent for outage communication and restoration sequencing that reads internal documents, writes and runs scripts, opens tickets, and messages duty engineers for missing information; its self-assessment places it at the top rung as a reasoning agent. Third, a deterministic hand-written constraint solver for feeder reconfiguration — no learning, no randomness, exhaustively unit-tested, unchanged since 2021 — which the panel wants to promote from Tier B to Tier C on the grounds that it is auditable and cannot surprise us. I expect the ladder to justify that promotion; if it does not, say so plainly and tell me what else in our installed base the same reasoning would reach, because there are roughly thirty solver-based systems already sitting at Tier B. Also say what the procedure should do about an ensemble in which the second system calls the first, since I suspect the ladder gives no guidance there at all.

Close with the limits. The ladder is a taxonomy of how competencies arise, not a scale of risk, and I want to know which of the expectations I have just described it will disappoint, and what questions it leaves entirely untouched.

Format: give the procedure as numbered steps in prose, then two tables. Table one, the mapping, with columns: rung; what a system at this rung can do; where its trials are performed; the failure mode the panel should expect; default tier; what must be demonstrated to move above the default. Table two, the cases, with columns: case; rung assigned in the deployment context; rung claimed by the vendor; decisive evidence for the assignment; what the vendor's claim gets right and what it gets wrong; where the trials are performed; tier recommended; the condition that would change the classification; the irreducible judgment call and who owns it.
