I teach an upper-level undergraduate course on famine and global justice, and a friend and I spent most of last night arguing over a point I now want to build an assessment around. The dispute is whether the entitlement analysis of the 1943 Bengal catastrophe really supports describing it as a political crime rather than a natural disaster. My friend's position: on that account's own terms the proximate drivers were wartime inflation, a price-control regime that pushed grain into black markets, and withholding by traders responding sensibly to expectations of shortage; since nobody need have violated any right for all of that to happen, calling the outcome a crime requires a criterion separating culpable withholding from ordinary commerce, and the analysis never supplies one. My counter: the wartime measures that stripped coastal districts of boats and rice stocks, together with the authorities' refusal for months to acknowledge a famine at all, show a state actively destroying entitlements and then declining relief, so the attribution stands. His rejoinder, which I could not answer: that only works if one already has a principle specifying who owes famine prevention to whom, and a framework explaining who starves is not a theory of obligation.

Write one exam question, to be answered in 45 minutes and worth 25 marks, that requires students to adjudicate this dispute rather than summarise either side. It should make them state and defend some criterion for when a famine is properly attributable as a political crime, and confront whether that criterion can be formulated without either implicating every market participant in a shortage or leaving famine blameless by default. Then supply a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, with marks allocated to each and one line on how students most commonly fail each point.
