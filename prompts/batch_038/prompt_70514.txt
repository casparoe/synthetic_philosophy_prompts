I am specifying requirements for a formal theory of choice under ambiguity — not to explain the Ellsberg choices away as mistakes, but to give an implementable standard for an agent that must act where it cannot pin down the process generating its evidence. Write the desiderata list any adequate theory has to meet: the choice patterns it must reproduce (the two-urn reversal in both directions, so the known urn is preferred whichever colour is bet on; the single-urn pattern in which people take the red bet over the black one yet the black-or-yellow bet over red-or-yellow; and the further cases in which the unknown urn is actually preferred), the structural features of belief it must represent without collapsing them into one another (attitude toward unknown odds as distinct from attitude toward known odds; the fact that some events support comparative likelihood judgements even when others do not), the dynamic requirements it must survive (sequential choice, ambiguity resolved partway through, and whether an agent who commits to a plan can be exploited by a series of trades it accepted one at a time), and the normative burden it must discharge (an account of why the sure-thing principle fails here rather than counting as a requirement of rationality). For each item, state the consideration behind it and mark it as non-negotiable or as the signature commitment of one camp — maxmin expected utility, non-additive or rank-dependent approaches, multiple-priors views with an explicit updating rule, or views that place a distribution over distributions. Then say which subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, so that the list is itself an impossibility result, and which single requirement eliminates the most candidates. Score the contenders against the list only briefly. Finally, say what would change your mind: which consideration would move you to rank a different requirement as most exclusionary, or where you think this reasoning is most likely to break down. End with a line of the form Verdict: <the single most exclusionary requirement>.
