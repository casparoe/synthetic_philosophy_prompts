I've been a hospital medical interpreter (Spanish) for about four years, so I know a lot of the vocabulary even though I have zero ethics training. Last week I interpreted a family meeting about withdrawing support on a guy in his thirties — massive bleed after a car wreck, no meaningful recovery expected, and he was a registered donor. The family decided to withdraw, and he became a candidate for donation after circulatory death. Nothing went wrong and the donation went fine. But afterward in the family lounge I got an earful of an arguement between two of the doctors who'd been in the room — Marcus, one of the intensivists, and Ruth, who does palliative. I was sort of trapped there with my coffee and they kept turning to me like I was a scoreboard. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) Marcus opened by saying he's never been comfortable with the DCD declaration. We watch the monitor for five minutes of no pulse and no spontaneous breath, we call the death, and then we start the operation. But if we wanted to we could bring him back — the drugs and the machine are literally in the room. So what we have isn't an irreversible cessation of circulation, it's a cessation we've decided not to reverse. Which means the declaration is resting on our decision and not on a fact about the body. And the rule that's supposed to keep us clean — you may not cause someone's death in order to take their organs — isn't buying anything here, because the thing that makes him dead is precisely that we chose not to save him.

(2) Ruth said he's right about the diagnosis and wrong that it's a problem, and that the distinction he's fumbling is irreversible versus permanent. Irreversible means no available intervention could restart it. Permanent means it won't be restarted, because nobody's going to restart it. She said the declaration has only ever needed permanence, and that if his objection works it works everywhere: when we stop compressions after forty minutes on an ordinary arrest, we don't know the patient is irreversible, we know we're not going to keep going. So the decision is baked into every death called in that unit, not just the donation ones.

(3) Marcus said that proves too much, and that's his objection rather than an answer to it. If deciding-not-to-reverse is enough for death, then practically anything we choose to leave alone is dead, and the criterion isn't tracking anything. You need a constraint, and the only one anybody offers is something like irreversible-by-any-intervention-it-would-be-ethical-to-provide, which he says is just our own decision wearing a collar and tie.

(4) Ruth said fine, keep permanence, and pair it with the two things that make it non-arbitrary: the decision to withdraw has to come first, from the patient or the family, on grounds that have nothing to do with donation; and the wait is there to rule out autoresuscitation. She said nobody in that building could name a documented case of circulation returning on its own after five minutes of untreated asystole, so the permanence isn't fragile the way he's implying.

(5) Marcus said the five minutes isn't set by metaphysics, it's set by the organs. Wait as long as you'd have to for real certainty and the liver and kidneys are garbage from warm ischemia. So the interval we describe as long enough to know he's dead is calibrated by the transplant clock, and then we narrate the result as if the length had been chosen for epistemic reasons.

(6) Ruth said the short interval shows the opposite of what he thinks: we're not trying to be certain that nothing could ever bring him back, we're trying to be certain nothing will bring him back on its own and that we won't be the ones who let it.

(7) Then he brought up the perfusion thing, which is where I lost both of them a little. He said in some programs, after the declaration, they clamp the vessels going to the head and put the body on a pump that reperfuses the abdomen so the organs get oxygenated blood again. So we declare death on the grounds that circulation stopped, and then we restart circulation in the same body, and the only thing that makes the restart compatible with the declaration is that we clamped off the brain first. That's not discovering a fact, he said, that's manufacturing the conditions under which the fact stays true.

(8) Ruth said the declaration was made before any of that, on circulatory grounds, and that reperfusing abdominal organs is no more a resurrection than a pump running during a transplant; what would actually undo the declaration is perfusing the brain, which is exactly what the clamp prevents. Marcus said that's his point — we're doing surgery on the truth conditions.

(9) Then he zoomed out and said the same shape of problem is in the neurological standard, and this is the part I'd most like someone to check, because I couldn't tell if he was showing off or right. A brain-dead body on a ventilator digests, heals incisions, fights infection, can mount a stress response when you cut it, keeps spinal reflexes including the one where the arms come up, and there are reported cases of pregnant women declared brain dead who were maintained for weeks until delivery. So the claim that the body as a whole has stopped integrating itself doesn't look like something you read off the patient either — the integration is being supplied by the ICU. And he noted that the UK declares on brainstem criteria while the US uses the whole brain, and both think they're tracking the same thing; if this were a biological fact of that kind, he said, you wouldn't expect the criteria to be fixed by statute. (He also said something about a state or two letting families raise religious objections to the neurological standard, which I think is true but wouldn't swear to.)

(10) Ruth said conventions can track joints, and two different instruments can measure the same quantity: the two standards agree on every patient either of them actually meets, because a destroyed brainstem takes the rest of the brain with it, and the disagreement only surfaces in hypotheticals like anencephaly. And she said Marcus is running together two claims — that a body can be made to do X, and that the body is doing X. Every function on his list is being held up from outside; the question isn't whether the machinery can sustain them but whether the patient is still the kind of thing that can sustain itself, and he isn't.

(11) Marcus ended by saying the honest move is to stop pretending and drop the dead-donor rule altogether: get consent in advance, anesthetize, take the organs, let the person die, and say that out loud. Ruth said that would torch public trust in donation, which costs far more lives than the squeamishness it saves, and that it changes what a family is being asked to agree to — the whole system depends on a mother being able to believe her son was dead before anybody cut into him.

They would have kept going if I hadn't stood up. Please go through this move by move and rate each one — clearly mistaken, partly right, clearly right, or right conclusion for bad reasons — with a couple sentences of justification per rating. Specifically: does (1) identify a problem unique to donation, or does (2) dissolve it into ordinary practice? Does proves-too-much in (3) actually land against permanence, or is he just restating his discomfort with a new label? Is (7) as damning as he thinks, or does Ruth's before-and-after reply work? Is (9) a genuine problem for the whole-brain standard or a fallacy about who's doing the integrating? And on (11), is the dead-donor rule moral honesty or PR? Then tell me who was more right overall, and what both of them are missing. My own suspicion, which I couldn't have defended in that room, is that they're arguing as if death has one job when it's doing three or four seperate ones at once — I'd like to know whether that's a real insight or just a comfortable way of refusing to pick a side.
