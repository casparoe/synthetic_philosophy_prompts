I help run a monthly fiction workshop, and for two seasons now the same piece keeps arriving: a character faces a lever, a switch, a door, and the whole thing turns on a final reveal about what they chose. I would like to commission something that goes the other way, into territory our group handles badly — the metaphysics of causation by omission, and specifically whether someone who merely fails to act can properly be counted among the causes of what happens next. Write me a short story that treats that as a subject for fiction rather than a seminar wearing a costume.

The shape I want, with details left entirely to you: a remote river valley with one paved road out, a volunteer radio net that carries weather, road-closure and welfare-check traffic between the villages along it, and a November storm with the freezing level dropping all night. A young technician for the regional water board drives up-valley alone to inspect an intake screen; a ford comes up fast at a place you should name; nobody hears from him for two days. The net controller on duty half-hears something broken under the weather noise, marks a question against a timestamp in her log, and does not call it out. Populate the story only with people who did nothing. I want the reader to notice that no one in it strikes, opens, spills, releases or pushes anything at all, and that the entire harm arrives by way of what was never transmitted, never checked, never said aloud.

Please build in the following, and let each emerge from scene and dialogue rather than explanation:

- The list problem. If the controller's silence caused his death, so did the silence of the shepherd two ridges over who logged the same garbled call and forgot it by morning, the haulage driver who saw a vehicle parked at the wrong angle on the wrong side of the ford and kept going, the technician's sister who didn't ring when he missed Sunday dinner, the county office that had cut the overnight watch to three nights a week, and whoever it was that had unplugged the repeater hut heater to run a kettle. Give me at least five such people in a line or two each, and let the length of the list be the disturbing thing. Nobody resolves it.
- Omitting versus merely not doing. Separate them by roster, key, promise, sleep and proximity rather than by anyone giving an account of duty: who was signed on, who could have reached the hut, who was awake, who had said they would listen out.
- A broken backup. Include a second channel that ought to have saved him — a ridge repeater, a neighbour with a handheld, a scheduled check-in at a fixed hour — and disclose, late and in passing, that it would not have worked that night anyway. Let the reader feel the counterfactual get much less tidy than the inquest assumes, without anyone saying why.
- The physics of absence. At least one scene should dwell on a thing that never happened in fully concrete terms: a carrier wave never keyed, a heater never switched on, a knock never landed on a door, described with as much specificity as any action.
- An official setting where the question is asked badly. A coroner's hearing, an insurer's file, a county review panel: someone asks in flat administrative language whether the failure to relay that message caused the death, and the answer that goes on the record is a formality the reader knows is not the end of it.

Format: exactly five numbered scenes, no more and no fewer. Each takes a one-line heading giving time and place, runs between 180 and 260 words, and closes on an ordinary detail rather than a turn. Nothing before scene one and nothing after scene five — no title, no preface, no note on sources of inspiration, no closing paragraph telling me what it meant. No character may deliver anything like "you can't be blamed for what you never did" as a thesis; if the argument surfaces at all, it should surface as an interruption, a form field, a sentence somebody abandons halfway. And no twist ending: let the fifth scene be purely procedural — a signature, a stamped time, a box being ticked — and then simply stop.
