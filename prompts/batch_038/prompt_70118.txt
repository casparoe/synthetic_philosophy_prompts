I've been sitting in on a history-of-economic-thought reading group and we've now burned three sessions going in circles about Berkeley — not the idealism, the money stuff. The Querist, mostly. Half the group thinks he's a genuine and badly underrated monetary theorist: money isn't valuable because of the silver in it, it's a ticket or a counter whose whole point is to get people moving and working, which is why a poor country with no mines can still be rich if it has the right paper and the right bank behind it, and why he spent so much energy pushing paper credit and a national bank on Ireland. On that reading he got to something like the chartalist line, and to the credit theory of money, a century and a half early, and he got there out of his own philosophy — no abstract ideas, signs don't have to carry determinate ideas to do their work, so money is a sign whose entire being is in its use. The other half thinks that's charity shading into fabrication. Their Berkeley is a bishop writing depressed-economy pamphlets who keeps sliding between the quantity of money mattering enormously and the quantity not mattering at all so long as it circulates, who never says what determines prices or the exchanges, and whose ticket-and-counter business is a slogan doing zero explanatory work. On that reading he's a mercantilist with a nice turn of phrase, and hauling him into modern arguments about state money is anachronism of the worst kind — reading Knapp and the currency-versus-banking fights backwards into a 1730s Irish pamphlet. Then there's a third faction saying both sides are missing the point, because the prior question is whether the money writing is even continuous with the metaphysics or just a seperate practical hobby, and until you settle that you can't settle anything else.

Nobody has moved an inch. My hunch, which I can't yet defend, is that this isn't actually a disagreement about Berkeley: it's a disagreement about what something has to do to count as a monetary theory, and each side is grading him against a rubric the other side doesn't accept, so every passage one camp cites as decisive the other camp scores as beside the point.

Is that right? Diagnose the impasse rather than picking a Berkeley. Is it verbal — over theory, over chartalist, over what counts as an explanation — is it a clash of interpretive standards about how much coherence you're allowed to impose on an occasional pamphlet, is it a live economics dispute wearing period costume, or is there a determinate textual answer that people are too invested to concede? And given whatever diagnosis you land on, what would count as progress here concretely — a distinction, a principled way of weighing the passages where he contradicts himself, a change of question, some piece of context that would settle it — or should we just expect the group to keep circling? One more thing: please mark clearly which parts of what you say are settled among people who actually work on this material and which parts are contested readings, and don't hand me a minority interpretation dressed up as the consensus.
