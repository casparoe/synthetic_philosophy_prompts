I'm writing something on Moore's paradox and I need a case built to order rather than pulled off the shelf — none of the raining, none of the exam results, none of the examples that are already in print.

My thesis is that what makes a Moorean conjunction absurd isn't anything in the content of the two conjuncts; it's the particular way asserting P is supposed to hook into believing P. So I need a scenario that keeps the content in exactly the same shape but changes the hook.

Target: an ordinary person in an ordinary contemporary setting who sincerely says something with the form P but I don't believe that P, where both conjuncts are true, they know both are true, and saying it is the right move — the thing they should say, not a slip or a performance. I want someone who thinks every sincere Moorean utterance is either absurd or self-deceived to have to bite a bullet.

To be clear I'm not asking for a perfectly rational agent. Their doxastic state can be unfortunate, impaired, stuck — that's fine, maybe even useful. The claim I need to land is narrower: the utterance itself isn't irrational, and it doesn't put them in contradictory commitments.

Constraints:
- one paragraph of scenario, realistic, no sci-fi, no split brains, no neurology. Ordinary psychology is allowed — grief, anxiety, conditioned fear, a habit of doubt, whatever you need.
- P is a mundane factual claim about the world, not about the speaker's own mind, and not a moral or aesthetic judgement.
- none of the usual escapes: the speaker isn't changing their mind partway through the sentence, isn't equivocating on believe (no sliding between believe and feel and trust), isn't being ironic, isn't lying, isn't a philosopher demonstrating assertion norms for an audience.
- no self-deception and no unconscious belief. Don't solve it by saying well, deep down they do believe it. The absence of belief has to be the real thing and visible in how they act, not a verbal disavowal their behaviour contradicts.
- and don't make P something the speaker knows, because knowledge entails belief and your second conjunct would come out false. Overwhelming evidence is what I want, not knowledge.

Then the demonstration:

1. Which account of assertion — and of the link between asserting and beleiving — makes this utterance fine, and why it makes it fine here rather than everywhere.
2. Walk through the standard diagnosis of Moorean absurdity (roughly: asserting P expresses or commits you to believing P, so the whole utterance commits you to a pair that can't both be held) and point at the exact step that fails in your case. Don't just say the case is an exception to the diagnosis; show the step.
3. Tell me which feature of the scenario is doing the work, and what happens to the verdict if I strip that feature out.
4. Give me the strongest attack a defender of the standard diagnosis can make on the case, and say honestly whether it's an actual refutation or just my scenario redescribed in their vocabulary.

And if you think these constraints can't all be satisfied at once, say so and tell me which one you'd drop and what the case loses by dropping it. I'd rather be told that then handed something that quietly breaks one of them.
