A colleague of mine convenes an informal reading group on value theory. She is not a specialist — she works on something else entirely — but she is unusually even-handed, she does not change her mind under pressure, and over the past year I have been logging her verdicts on pairwise comparisons because I suspect a single rule is generating them and I want to test that suspicion before I say so in front of her.

Her verdicts to date:

1. One person tortured for two years versus one person with a hangnail for a day: the torture is worse, decisively, and she will not entertain the comparison as close.
2. One person tortured for two years versus a billion people each with a hangnail for a day: the second outcome is worse. All things considered, the torture is the better of the two.
3. Walking from case 1 to case 2 by successive doublings of the number of hangnail-sufferers: she grants that each doubling is worse than the one before it, and she will not retract any individual step. Pressed on whether something must give, she says only this: if you force the issue, then either some doubling is not worse than the one before it, or a thousand worse-thans do not add up to a worse-than — she does not know which, and she has never needed to know.
4. Mozart's mature string quintets versus a competent, agreeable, wholly unambitious piece of light background music: on a par. She insists this is a genuine third relation — not a polite way of saying equally good, and not a confession that she has failed to compare them.
5. The same piece of background music after a skilled editor's modest improvements (a better close, less repetition): still on a par. Likewise after the next modest improvement, and the next. But she denies this licenses an endless chain, and she says the inference from one modest improvement not settling the comparison to no quantity of modest improvements settling it is precisely the move she refused in verdict 3.
6. Eighty years of rich, absorbed, deeply worthwhile living versus a trillion years of a life that is barely worth living but never miserable: the second is worse. She calls this the same case as verdict 2 with the numbers turned up, and refuses to treat it as raising anything new.
7. When a member of the group constructed a sequence of paid swaps that, given her verdicts, returns her to her starting position minus a fee at each turn, she conceded that accepting the sequence would be foolish, but denied that this indicts her comparisons: the pump, she said, gets at her willingness to trade, not at her ranking.
8. She holds that better than and better than all things considered come apart routinely — two outcomes may each be better than the other relative to different ideals, and the overall comparison need not inherit the structure of the piecemeal ones.

The new case, on which she has agreed to rule publicly next week, is this. There is a spectrum of a thousand outcomes, O1 through O1000. In O1, one hundred thousand people each endure a serious week of influenza: bedridden, feverish, wretched, but leaving nothing permanent behind. At each successive step the harm per person becomes very slightly less bad and the number of people affected very slightly larger, so that O1000 involves an astronomically greater population each enduring a very slightly less bad week — and at every point on the spectrum the week remains an unmistakably bad week by any ordinary standard, never a mere nuisance. She has been told in advance, and has accepted, that no adjacent pair on this spectrum differs in kind: nobody in the room, she included, would describe any single step as involving a different category of harm. She has also been offered, for each adjacent pair, a swap — she may move the whole population one step down the spectrum in exchange for a fee, and she may accept or refuse each swap in turn. She must rule on four things: each adjacent pair; O1 against O1000; which of the swaps she should accept, if not all of them; and whether her answers to the first three are jointly consistent, and if so, by what account of consistency.

What I want from you:

First, predict her four rulings, and be specific rather than hedged — a prediction that she will "say something nuanced" is worthless to me.

Second, and this is the main task, lay out at least four distinct principles that would account for verdicts 1 through 8 equally well. For each, state what it commits her to on the new case, where it diverges from the others, and what it costs her elsewhere. I expect the candidates to include some version of the claim that the comparative relation simply is not transitive, some version of the claim that there is a threshold somewhere in every such chain, some version of the claim that the comparative is vague or imprecise at the margins in a way that absorbs small changes without any failing step, and some version of the claim that the adjacent comparisons and the endpoint comparison are not answers to the same question. Do not treat that list as exhaustive, and do not treat my labels as neutral: if one of them is a confusion rather than a candidate, say so.

Third, tell me which principle you would bet on, and why — and identify the single one of verdicts 1 through 8 that does the most work in narrowing the field. Also tell me whether verdict 8 is load-bearing at all, or whether it is doing no more than restating verdict 3 in different vocabulary.

Fourth, design one further comparison — one, cheap to state, introducing no new machinery and no new kinds of value — that would discriminate between your top two candidates. Say exactly what she would have to say about it on each, and why the two answers cannot be reconciled.

One warning. I keep sliding into the assumption that she must be a straightforward intransitivist, because verdicts 2, 3, and 7 seem to hand that to me. Tell me whether that reading is forced by what she has said, or whether everything in verdicts 1 through 8 admits of a fully transitive reconstruction. If a transitive reconstruction exists, give it, and be honest about what it obliges her to give up — in particular, whether it can survive verdict 3's refusal to locate a failing step alongside the stipulation, in the new case, that no step differs in kind.
