I am opening next week's graduate seminar on the philosophy of spacetime with the hole argument, and I should like to begin it with a joke — one that rewards the students who have done the reading and quietly exposes those who have not. A seminar on radical indeterminism goes better if it starts by laughing at something, but only if the laughter is aimed at the right thing.

Please compose the joke as a dialogue in exactly five numbered exchanges between two anthropomorphized points of a differentiable manifold — you may call them p and q — both of which happen to sit inside the hole. Between them, the two should manage to convey the actual architecture of the argument: that a diffeomorphism which is the identity everywhere outside the hole is free to redistribute the metric field inside it; that if the points are individuals in their own right, the redistributed arrangement and the original one are two genuinely distinct physical possibilities, both compatible with exactly the same data specified outside the hole, so that the theory is left wildly indeterministic; and that the standard escape is Leibniz equivalence, which declares the two arrangements to be one and the same physical situation rather than rivals.

The punchline must land on the price of that escape, not on the indeterminism itself: the points now owe their entire individuality to a field distribution that was supposed to be individuated by them. Do not allow either character to win the exchange outright, and do not dissolve the circularity — let the last line sit in it.

Some constraints, which I would ask you to treat as binding. No puns on the word hole in any of its senses: not on gaps or gaps in an argument, not on cavities or potholes, not on whole versus hole. No material about coordinate systems being merely a matter of convention, no physicist-against-philosopher business about whose discipline is more pointless, and no joke whose payload would survive having the physics replaced by, say, a set of interchangeable filing cabinets. The comedy must be generated by the metaphysics and by nothing else; a reader who has followed Earman and Norton should laugh at the argument's own shape, and a reader who has not should be able to tell that something is being said over their head.

Format: exactly five numbered exchanges, each one or two sentences long, attributed to p or to q. No title, no preamble, no stage directions, no gloss afterwards explaining the joke, and no note about why it works.
