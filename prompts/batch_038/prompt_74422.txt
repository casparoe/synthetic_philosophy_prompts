Ran a sensory neurophysiology lab for thirty-four years, mostly vision, some single-unit work in macaque, and I retired in the spring. Now I read philosophy full time, which my wife thinks is a hilarious way to spend a pension. Lately I keep running into the same metaphor from two directions and I cant decide whether it is one idea wearing two hats or two ideas that only sound alike.

The metaphor: what you experience is a control panel, not the machinery. Fine. But there seem to be two very different claims hiding inside it.

Claim one — the illusion is of the user. There is no little central viewer; the self is a simplified model the system builds so that it can monitor and control its own processing. Roughly Dennett's user-illusion line and his self as a center of narrative gravity, and Graziano's attention schema theory, where the brain's model of its own attention is deliberately low-resolution and silent about the actual mechanism, and that is exactly why awareness feels non-physical and ineffable when you turn it on itself.

Claim two — the illusion is of the world. Hoffman's interface theory of perception: space, time and objects are the desktop, icons need not resemble what they stand for, and selection tunes perception toward fitness payoff rather than toward truth, so a lineage that saw reality as it is would get out-competed by one that sees a handy interface.

Compare these two for me properly, not in the pop-science way where both just get waved at as your brain making things up. What I want:

1. State precisely what is being called non-veridical in each case. In the first, the simplified model is still a model of a real process going on inside the same skull — attention, narrative self-organization — and it is low-res but arguably pointing at the right machinery. In the second the icon is supposed to bear no resemblance to its referent at all. Is that difference load-bearing, or is it just a difference in what each author happened to aim the metaphor at? Are the two views rivals, or orthogonal? Could somebody hold both without strain, and if so, what would the conjunction actually commit them to?

2. Do a thought experiment with me. Suppose you could de-iconize a subject: widen the interface, add channels, increase resolution, and hand the system a more truthful readout of the underlying process, all while leaving its ability to report intact. Which theory predicts the subject's experience gets richer and better? Which predicts it gets worse, noisier, or collapses into unusable detail? Design me the cleanest version of this as a real experiment — animal or human, whatever you need — and tell me which pattern of results would count against which view. I want to know if either of them is actually falsifiable or if they are both just unfalsifiable commentary on the same data.

3. The self-application problem. If the interface view is right, then the interface view is itself an icon on the desktop, which looks like it saws off the branch it is sitting on. Does the user-illusion view have an equivalent problem — a self-model reporting on the self-model — and if so, why does the reflexive bite feel sharper in one case than the other? Is there a principled reason, or am I just being squeamish?

4. Bluntly: which of the two, if either, touches the question of why there is something it is like to be the system, and which one has quietly swapped in a different, easier question — and then claimed credit for the original one. I have a suspicion that one of them explains the feel of introspection and the other explains the shape of perception, and that neither is a theory of consciousness at all. Convince me I am wrong, or sharpen the suspicion.

Call it a thousand to fourteen hundred words. Argue, don't survey — I don't want a history of the debate or a list of who said what when, and please don't send me a reading list, I have a library card and a wife who polices my nightstand. Flag every spot where the dashboard picture is doing work the argument can't support on its own; that is the part I care about most.
