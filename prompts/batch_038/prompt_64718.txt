Write a short story of roughly 1,500 words that dramatizes the position W. E. B. Du Bois defended in his 1926 address "Criteria of Negro Art" — that art made by a despised people can never be merely decorative, and that beauty, truth, and justice come as one demand. Do not name Du Bois, and do not use the word propaganda anywhere in the narration; let the view emerge from what the characters do and argue.

Set it in the present. A muralist has won a commission from a city transit authority for the concourse wall of a new light-rail station in a neighborhood that was rezoned eighteen months earlier, and the contract's community-benefit clause requires the work to celebrate the area's resilience and heritage. Build the story around three pressures that cannot all be satisfied: a tenants' organizer who wants the wall to carry the street addresses of the buildings whose residents were priced out; a retired schoolteacher who insists the neighborhood has a right to see itself rendered beautifully, and warns that a wall of grievances will be buffed or ignored; and the artist's own conviction that the piece has to work as a picture before it can work as anything else. The story should turn on a specific decision about what actually goes on the wall, and should show the finished piece doing something in the world that the artist did not intend and partly disowns.

Give the beauty-first position its strongest case rather than treating it as a foil, and end with the artist's own account of whether the work succeeded — an account the reader has reason to distrust.
