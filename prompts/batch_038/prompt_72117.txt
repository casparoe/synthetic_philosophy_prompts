> Hacking separates kinds that are indifferent to how they are classified from kinds that interact with their classifications. Quarks, electrons and planets are indifferent: they do not read the journals in which they are described, and nothing in the object changes when our taxonomy of it changes. Human beings are interactive: we learn the labels applied to us, we come to describe ourselves in their terms, and we organize our conduct around them. Because the classified change in response, the classifier has to change the classification, which changes the classified again. Hacking calls this back-and-forth the looping effect, and it is why he thinks the human sciences are chasing a moving target in a way that particle physics is not.
>
> He also stresses that a looping kind does not appear out of nowhere. It needs what he calls a matrix: clinics that can receive the patient, a legal and administrative apparatus that has a use for the label, media that make the description available to the public, and a diagnostic manual that gives it an official home. In his account of fugue the ecological niche had to be filled on several sides at once — the disorder had to fit an existing taxonomy, sitting between hysteria and epilepsy; it had to lie between two cultural poles, romantic travel and criminal vagrancy; it had to be observable through the passport and policing arrangements of the period; and it had to offer something to the person who had it.
>
> His two preferred examples are multiple personality disorder and fugue. MPD was diagnosed a handful of times in the middle of the twentieth century and then, in the 1970s and 80s, the number of diagnosed cases went from roughly a dozen to several thousand. Fugue appeared in France in the late 1880s, was diagnosed across continental Europe for about twenty years, and then essentially disappeared; the decision to drop homosexuality from the DSM in the 1970s worked the same loop in the opposite direction. Hacking chooses these because both the emergence and the disappearance of the diagnosis are documented. He is careful to say that the people involved genuinely suffered and that he is not accusing them of acting; his question is not whether the disorder is real but what it is real *as*.
>
> On the claim in (c): the looping-effects argument does support it, though only in a particular way. Hacking's looping effect is essentially Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy generalized from beliefs to classifications. A self-fulfilling prophecy is a definition of a situation that begins false and, by being acted on, becomes true. In the psychiatric case the label starts with nothing corresponding to it — nobody in 1955 was a multiple — and once the label exists people take it up and the diagnosis becomes true of them. The loop is what makes the kind true. It follows that an interactive kind is not real in the way an indifferent kind is real: its instances exist as instances only after the classification, whereas quarks were quarks before anyone thought of them. The DSM entry is therefore not a discovery but a prescription, and the quoted student's conclusion is the right one.
>
> Two consequences follow. First, when the DSM is revised, the revision cannot be closer to the truth than the edition it replaces; it can only be differently entrenched, since there is no standing target for it to approximate. Second, this is why psychiatry cannot accumulate results as physics does: each generation is classifying a kind that its own classifications have already altered. Hacking is no relativist about the natural sciences — electrons are electrons — but for human kinds the looping is a reason to deny that our classifications track anything independent of them.
