I produce a podcast in which a historical author is cross-examined about one of his own books, and next month's episode puts Cicero in the chair over his dialogue on old age, the one in which Cato the Elder, at eighty-four, explains to young Scipio and Laelius why his years are no burden. I need the question sequence drafted in advance rather than improvised on the day.

Two constraints shape everything. First, the programme is carried by a network of continuing-care communities, so most listeners are between sixty-eight and eighty-five, in mixed health and mixed circumstances: retired machinists, clerks, and nurses, a good many widows, several caring for a spouse who no longer recognises them, and almost nobody who ever held office or owned an estate. A question that lands only on a Roman senator will not land at all. Second, the format permits one answer and exactly one follow-up per question, and Cicero has two replies ready that can absorb nearly any challenge: he produces another admirable old man, or he insists that the complaint arises from the character rather than from the age. Every follow-up you draft must be one that neither reply can discharge; it has to require him to say something the dialogue does not already say.

Draft ten questions in running order, plus a closing question. For each, give me three things: the pressure point in a sentence or two of plain prose, described by content rather than by chapter and section numbers, since neither I nor the audience will have the text open; the answer he is most likely to give, stated in his terms and not in mine; and the follow-up keyed precisely to that answer.

Between them the ten must cover the following, and you should tell me where each is handled:

1. The first of his four charges — that age withdraws us from active affairs — and his reply that the old do the more important half of the work, supplying counsel, judgement, and authority rather than muscle. Press the institutional premise: that reply assumes a deliberative body with seats reserved for old men. What is left of it for a listener whose judgement now has no forum at all?

2. The thesis that a well-spent life makes old age tolerable and that every complaint against old age is really a confession about the complainer. Ask him what would count, on his own view, as a grievance genuinely attributable to age rather than to character, and whether a claim immune to that distinction can be tested by anyone.

3. The method of exempla itself: the blind censor who harangued the senate against making peace with Pyrrhus, the playwright who read a new tragedy aloud to defeat his sons' attempt to have him declared incapable, the farmer who plants for a generation he will not see. Is the list offered as evidence or as illustration, and what does he think explains why these men are exceptions rather than samples?

4. The argument on pleasure, which appears to run two ways at once: the old are released from the tyranny of appetite, and this release is a gain rather than a loss, while the pleasures that remain — the table without excess, conversation, cultivation of the land — are finer. Force him to choose which prong is doing the work, and ask what he says to someone for whom the loss is experienced as loss and not as liberation.

5. His confidence about the mind: that memory fails only where it is not exercised, and that no old man has ever forgotten where he buried his money. Ask whether the comparison of the mind to a muscle he strengthens by use is something he holds as a fact about the world or as a rule of conduct, and what the argument becomes if decline arrives regardless of exercise.

6. The two horns on death — either the soul perishes, in which case nothing is felt and nothing is bad, or it survives, in which case the change is for the better. Ask whether he has excluded the middle possibilities: survival in a worse condition, and non-existence as the loss of what one had rather than as a state one occupies.

7. His own philosophical method. He writes as an Academic who weighs probabilities and claims certainty about nothing, yet the old Cato is made to speak with confidence about the soul's divine nature and about conversing with the great dead. Is that confidence Cato's or Cicero's, and does the consolation survive for a reader who, following his own procedure, finds the opposing branch better supported? Relatedly: the year before this dialogue he had argued at length that death is no evil even on the assumption that the soul perishes, whereas here the weight falls on survival. Ask him why the two books lean differently, and which argument he would want held up to him at the end.

8. The standpoint of the consolations he offers — estates, clients, honours, learned company, senatorial authority — all of which belong to a propertied Roman male. Ask whether the four charges he sets out to answer are the four charges of an old human being or of an old senator, and what he would have to change in the argument if he answered them for a person with none of those resources.

For the closing question, go to the occasion of the book rather than to its argument: it was written in the year after his daughter's death, addressed to the friend with whom he said the gift could be shared in common, and prefaced with the remark that composing it had wiped away the annoyances of his own old age and made that old age pleasant. Ask whether a work composed as self-consolation can also function as an argument, and what a listener should make of a defence of old age whose author was sixty-two, politically sidelined, and grieving. Advise me whether that question should come before or after the ten, and what it costs either way.

Finally, mark each question as one he can answer while staying inside the dialogue, speaking as Cato, or one that forces him to answer as Cicero the author; flag the two where that distinction does the most work, and explain why. Leave out anything of the what-drew-you-to-philosophy kind, anything about his reputation or afterlife, and any question whose force depends on modern demography, medicine, or actuarial tables, which he could dismiss as anachronism rather than answer. If the recording runs short I will have to cut two questions: tell me which two and why they are the most expendable.
