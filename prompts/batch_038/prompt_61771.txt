So I'm a junior and I sit on our student council, and this year we picked the president with ranked choice voting, four candidates. Afterwards my friend Dev (math club, insufferable about this stuff) showed me that three of us could have burried our actual favorite a little lower in the ranking, knocked out the guy who was going to win in the first rounds, and gotten somebody we liked second-best elected instead. He said that's exactly what the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem predicts, and then he wrote out a chain that I assumed was going to end with "so democracy is doomed," but instead it ends with the theorem itself being bogus. I've read it like ten times and I can't tell whether he's onto something or cheating, and he won't say which.

Here's the chain, basically in his words: (1) A voting rule is strategy-proof if no voter can ever get a better outcome for themselves by handing in a ballot that isn't their true ranking, no matter what anyone else does. (2) Two-candidate majority rule is strategy-proof, and so is any rule that just picks the same fixed winner no matter what the ballots say. (3) Gibbard-Satterthwaite: with three or more possible winners, a rule that's onto — every candidate can win for some set of ballots — and isn't a dictatorship can't be strategy-proof. (4) So with three or more candidates the only ways to get strategy-proofness are to shrink the range of possible winners or to give one person total control, which means either making everyone's ballots matter less or making them matter for exactly one person. (5) And in general, the less your ballot can change the outcome, the less you can manipulate it, so strategy-proofness is basically just a measure of how much a rule ignores people. (6) Which means strategy-proofness goes up as responsiveness goes down, and the fixed-winner rule is obviosly the strategy-proofest rule there is — it beats instant-runoff, plurality, everything, on this one criterion. (7) But a criterion that's maximized by a rule that ignores literally everyone can't be tracking anything we care about, so being manipulable isn't actually a defect in a voting rule. (8) Therefore Gibbard-Satterthwaite isn't a discovery about voting, it's an artifact of a bogus criterion, and the honest lesson is to stop caring about strategy-proofness.

Which step do you reject, and why did it look fine to me when I read it — because (5) still feels really intuitive, like if your ballot can't change anything then you can't manipulate anything, end of story? And what does giving that step up cost, because I don't want to lose it for nothing? For what it's worth I'm not willing to give up (1), I checked and I'm pretty sure that's the actual definition, and I really don't want (7) or (8) to come out true, because in our election the manipulation would have flipped the winner and that felt like a genuine wrong, not a technicality — Dev's whole framing makes it sound like there was nothing to complain about. If the chain breaks somewhere in the middle, does the theorem still say something interesting once you patch it, or is (8) half right? Last thing, and sorry for piling on: Dev also mentioned two escapes, one where everyone's preferences have to line up along a single left-to-right spectrum and you take the median voter's pick, and one where the rule randomizes between candidates instead of choosing one. Which hypothesis of the theorem does each of those give up, and are they real escapes or are they just the fixed-winner trick from (2) wearing a costume?
