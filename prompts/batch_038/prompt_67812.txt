I teach a fifteen-student upper-level undergraduate seminar in social epistemology that meets once a week for two hours and fifteen minutes, in a room with movable tables and a whiteboard on each of two walls. We finished testimony and epistemic peer disagreement last week, and in two weeks we move on to opinion pooling and aggregation, so this session is the hinge between them: I want to introduce the common prior assumption as the structural device behind Aumann's result that agents who share a prior and have common knowledge of one another's posteriors cannot rationally agree to disagree, and behind the companion no-trade result that speculative trade should be impossible under those conditions. I emphatically do not want to open with the theorem, its name, or a proof sketch. I want the students to spend an hour doing something in which they are, by construction, all reasoning from a prior they were handed in common, and then to discover either that their market refuses to clear at all, or that it clears when it should not — and to feel how strange that is before anyone tells them what they have stumbled onto. Two of my students are economics majors who would otherwise solve the game aloud in the first five minutes and flatten everyone else's experience of it, and the remaining thirteen have had one term of decision theory: they can count outcomes and take ratios, but they are rusty on conditionalization and I do not want any arithmetic harder than two-digit division, so please design the signal structure so that every posterior is obtainable by counting cards rather than by formula.

Here is the rough architecture I have in mind, which you should refine, repair, or replace if you see a better way to do it. Phase one is a genuine common-prior baseline: a state of the world is determined by a deck whose composition I announce publicly and in writing, each student privately receives one signal card, and then a short trading pit opens in which they may buy and sell a claim paying off on the state, using tokens that are redeemable at the end of the session for chocolate coins. My prediction is that no mutually acceptable trade exists, and I want the rules built so that this deadness is palpable and slightly frustrating rather than merely noted. Phase two introduces disagreement, but the crucial design constraint is that the heterogeneity must be smuggled in by a device the facilitator never announces and cannot plausibly be accused of having announced — for instance, an unreconciled anchor belief seeded quietly before the prior was stated, or a subset of students whose tokens convert at a different rate, or whose claim is indexed to a correlated but distinct event. I would like you to think carefully about which of these confounds is most productive, because the diagnosis in the debrief should not have a single clean answer: I want some students to insist the premises of the theorem failed at the prior, others to insist it failed at common knowledge, and a third group to notice that what actually differs is the payoff or act structure rather than any belief at all. Phase three is a public-announcement round in which students state posteriors aloud in sequence, possibly more than once around the circle, and observe whether or not they converge; please tell me whether to run this before or after the smuggling device is introduced, since the order seems to change what the students can learn from it.

Please deliver the whole thing as a runnable package: the exact deck and signal specifications with worked integers, a materials list, the token economy and payoff schedule, the pairing rule for an odd number of traders, what the economics majors are doing instead of trading (a market-maker or auditor role would be welcome if you can make it substantive rather than a holding pen), a minute-by-minute timeline for the full two hours and fifteen minutes, and the scripted instructions I read aloud. The facilitator must not say the words prior, posterior, common knowledge, or Aumann until the debrief, and the ordinary phrase agree to disagree should be formally banned in the room as a forbidden expression, which I expect will be both funny and revealing when someone eventually blurts it. The debrief should map specific moments of the session onto specific premises of the theorem, including the idealizations the activity quietly exploits — logical omniscience, perfectly public announcements approximating common knowledge, a shared act space — and it should contain one required task in which the students attempt to re-describe the phase-two behaviour as a common prior over an enlarged state space, and then argue about whether such a re-description, if always available, shows the assumption has no empirical content at all; please tell me how to run that task in twenty minutes without it collapsing into me lecturing Stephen Morris's argument at them. Give me at least four things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with a fix for each, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to supply in words — I suspect the answer has something to do with the difference between a genuine prior difference and a student who has merely botched the conditionalization, and with the fact that a finite announcement round is not common knowledge. Finally, if there is a clean way to connect the session backward to last week's material on whether one should conciliate toward a peer, tell me where it goes and roughly how long it should take, but do not let it swallow the debrief.
