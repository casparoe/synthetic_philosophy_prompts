I am convening the first round of my department's pilot adversarial-collaboration scheme this term, alongside my third-year module on death, meaning and value. We have spent six weeks on Scheffler's *Death and the Afterlife* and on the Epicurean and deprivationist responses to it, and the two students I have paired have so consistently talked past one another that I should like to hand them the document they ought to have written jointly. Please draft it.

The two camps are as follows. The first — call them the conjecturists — holds that the afterlife conjecture is substantially true: a large part of what we value is valuable only on the condition that humanity continues to exist after our deaths, and the horror provoked by the doomsday scenario, in which one learns that the Earth will be destroyed thirty days after one's own death, tracks a genuine disvalue rather than a mere fact about our psychology. The second — call them the experientialists — holds that all value and disvalue is ultimately a matter of how things go for subjects capable of experience; that nothing occurring after our deaths is bad either for us or impersonally; and that the very same horror is fully explained by our contingent emotional constitution, by anticipatory dread, by the fragility of our projects and by our poor imagination of our own absence, with no residue left over to support the conjecture. Please define the camps by their theses alone and do not attribute either position to any named philosopher.

The statement should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims. I want these to be non-trivial: each should be something a reader would have expected the two sides to dispute, such as what the doomsday scenario actually elicits, what the infertility variant elicits, and how much weight reflection on our own reactions can bear. No padding with logical platitudes.

2. A disagreements table, with columns for the issue, the conjecturist's formulation of it in wording that conjecturist would sign, the experientialist's formulation in wording that experientialist would sign, whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and a one-sentence justification of that classification. Be honest about verbal disputes — I suspect a good deal of the argument turns on the difference between what a life means to someone, what is valuable, and what someone happens to care about.

3. A cruxes section. For each crux, give the sub-question whose resolution would move at least one side, the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Please work through at least these three cases, restating each briefly so that the draft does not depend on my phrasing:
   - the Amnesia Case: humanity ends in thirty days, but you take a drug that permanently removes your knowledge of this, with everything else about your remaining life unchanged;
   - the Archive Case: humanity ends in thirty days, but every record of human thought and achievement is preserved in a self-sustaining archive that will be found a thousand years hence by conscious, appreciative agents who are nothing like us and share none of our concerns;
   - the Retroactive Case: you are told that the extinction has already happened — you are the last human being and have been for a year, everyone else you have known being an indistinguishable replica — and nothing about your experiences or projects changes from the inside.

4. A short closing section in which each camp names one case or argument that would count as evidence against its own view, and the two say whether anything at all would in fact move the debate.

Please write in American English, in Markdown, at roughly 1,400 words. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list or any citations, and do not put any passage in quotation marks; describe the views and the arguments rather than sourcing them.
