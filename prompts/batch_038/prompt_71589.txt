I am a novelist, currently drafting a book whose narrator keeps a running private account of his own past conduct for an audience of one — himself — and I have been rereading Ishiguro's *The Remains of the Day* less for its prose than for its epistemology. I want a serious reading of the novel through the dispute between reductionism and anti-reductionism about testimony, one that treats both positions as carefully as the book treats its butler. My interest is in the philosophical question rather than the craft question; I am not asking how to build such a narrator.

First, set out in your own terms what the dispute turns on: the Humean demand that a hearer possess positive reasons, not themselves resting on testimony, for taking a speaker to be reliable; Reid's opposing presumption in favour of what we are told, on which credibility is the default and what is required is the absence of defeaters rather than the presence of warrants; Coady's stronger claim that the reductionist programme is self-undermining, since the independent evidence it demands is itself largely of testimonial origin; and the further division among reductionists between a global thesis about testimony as a source and a local thesis about particular tellings, attentive to context and to a speaker's cues. Then read the novel against that apparatus.

The complication I want you to press on is one the standard literature does not squarely address. Stevens is only incidentally a teller to others; he is principally a teller to himself. He recounts, revises, hedges, and resumes, and the reader watches a man receive testimony from the one source he is least equipped to audit.

1. Does the debate get any grip on testimony that is self-addressed? Reid's principle of credulity appears framed for a social relation between distinct persons, and the assurance view — on which telling is less the supply of evidence than the undertaking of a responsibility toward a hearer — seems unable to operate reflexively, since one cannot hold oneself to an assurance one is free to reinterpret. If neither framework applies, is the novel about testimony at all, or about memory and self-deception under a borrowed label? I would rather be told that my framing is a category error which flatters the book than have it accommodated.

2. How does the novel distribute positive reasons and defeaters across the episodes that matter: Miss Kenton's repeated challenges to Stevens's account of his own motives; the dismissal of the two Jewish housemaids at Lord Darlington's instruction and Miss Kenton's threatened resignation; the post-war press coverage and the libel action that finished Darlington's reputation; the warnings Stevens hears and discounts from people outside the household. For each, say whether a local reductionist should classify it as a defeater Stevens culpably failed to register, or as something he never had the standing to assess at all.

3. Test this reading, and dismantle it if you think it wrong: anti-reductionism's default entitlement is only as sound as the hearer's capacity to notice defeaters, and in the reflexive case the speaker is precisely the party with the strongest interest in dulling that capacity, so a presumption of self-trust is not merely unwarranted but self-defeating — Stevens being what self-addressed anti-reductionism looks like when it goes wrong. If you reject this, say what the novel supports instead and where my inference breaks.

4. Be faithful in both directions. Identify where the epistemological reading strains, thins, or instrumentalises the book — its preoccupations with dignity, class, emotional repression, and a life spent in service — and, separately, where the novel exhibits something the argumentative literature has not articulated.

5. On the seafront bench at the end, Stevens concedes that his employer was at least making his own mistakes and that he cannot claim even that much for himself. Is this achieved self-knowledge, or a final and well-upholstered act of self-deception, resolved into a plan to practise bantering? Does settling that question settle the epistemological one, or can the two come apart?

Two housekeeping notes. I am working from a reading some years ago and a recent skim, so where my recollection of an episode or a name is off, correct me rather than following me into error. I do not want quotation from the novel; describe the scenes.

Close with a final line beginning "Verdict:" and, in a single clause, name which side of the reductionist/anti-reductionist divide the novel vindicates for the case it actually dramatizes — or state that it vindicates neither.
