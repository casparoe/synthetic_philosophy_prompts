I'm a grad student in a philosophy of biology seminar, and my department runs a tiny podcast that mostly just gets listened to by people in our building. Next week's guest is a philosopher of biology who defends a pretty specific position on the units of selection debate, and I've been asked to put together the question list. Roughly: she thinks "individual" and "unit of selection" name roles that entities play to a degree rather than kinds of thing you either are or aren't, and she cashes out the degree in terms of screening off — an entity counts as a real interactor to the extent that knowing its traits screens off information about the traits of its parts when you're predicting reproductive success. She's fond of saying that biofilms and slime molds are the paradigm cases and that everybody talks about ant colonies and beehives because they're photogenic. She also treats the gene's-eye view as a notational convenience rather than an ontology, and when someone asks her flat out whether group selection is real she tends to answer that the questioner hasn't yet said what counts as reproduction in the system. It's a 40 minute conversation so I need eight to ten questions, and I want them to turn on the actual content of that position rather than on generic philosophy-of-science boilerplate.

The audience has read some Dawkins and roughly knows what Hamilton's rule is, but almost nobody has heard of the MLS1/MLS2 distinction, so a couple of the questions need to force her to explain that in plain language — but please don't make them softball questions. I definitely want at least one question on each of the following: (a) the circularity worry, that screening off is supposed to be defined in terms of selection while selection is supposed to be defined at the level of the interactor, so isn't she just expaining individuality by means of individuality; (b) the major transitions — when a colony of cells becomes an organism with a germ-soma split, does her degree-based account say individuality shows up gradually, and if so what does it predict about the intermediate cases where the split is only partial; (c) a gene that gets copied faithfully but whose contribution to success depends entirely on the rest of the genome it sits in — is that still a replicator on her account, and if not, what is; (d) clonal reproduction, e.g. one of those huge quaking aspen clones covering dozens of acres — who is the interactor there, and does her answer actually differ from the standard one; (e) bacteria and horizontal gene transfer, where genes hop between lineages, and whether her framework has to say the gene stops being a unit of selection altogether; and (f) evidence, i.e. what observation would count against her view, preferably something a field biologist could actually go and collect. For each question, also give me the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — she has two habits I want to be ready for, which are telling the questioner that they're conflating replicators with interactors, and conceding something "in the limit" and then changing the subject — so the follow-ups should be second moves that don't let either habit work. And please include one genuinely hostile question about whether her framework is just a redescription that no experiment could distinguish from a trait-group model of the Sober-and-Wilson sort.

Write the whole thing in American English. Before each question add a one-line note to me about what I'm actually trying to extract from her, so I can recover if she wanders off. No "what drew you to this topic?" filler of any kind.
