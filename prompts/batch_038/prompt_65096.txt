I serve on the seven-member board of a regional land trust, and this spring we must choose between two courses of action: commit our reserve now to buy a 400-acre parcel at a price that is fixed only until June, or hold the reserve in case a state acquisition grant is approved in the fall, which would let us buy a far larger and more ecologically valuable property but would leave us with nothing if the grant fails.

Because our bylaws require that the board's decision reflect the preferences of all its members rather than a simple tally, a trustee who has studied some economics proposed the following procedure. Each of us privately assigns every possible outcome a number representing how much we would value it. We then average the seven sets of numbers and use the averages to rank the two courses of action. He assured us this is the right way to decide jointly: if all seven of us prefer one option to another, the group's numbers will show it, no member's view is discarded, and no single member can impose a result on the rest. I accepted the proposal at the time, but three difficulties have emerged, and I no longer know whether the procedure is sound.

First, we turned out to agree almost exactly about what each outcome would be worth, and to disagree violently about the likelihood of the grant. One trustee puts it at four in five, another at one in seven. Because each of us folded our own estimate into our own numbers, the two optimists' scores carried the day and the average favored waiting — even though four of the seven told me afterward that, had they shared the pessimist's estimate, they would have scored buying now higher.

Second, a seventh trustee declines to participate at all, on the grounds that there is no fact of the matter about whether one person's 70 is worth more or less than another person's 45, so the average of our scores is a number without a meaning.

Third, after we reported our pooled estimate of the grant's likelihood to a contact at the state agency, she sent us material that all seven of us read. When one trustee revised our original pooled figure in light of that material, the result pointed toward buying now; when the rest of us each revised privately and then averaged our revised figures, the result pointed toward waiting. Half the board says the trust has changed its mind and half says it has not, and we cannot determine which of the two routes is the correct one.

Is this procedure defensible? I want to understand what, if anything, has gone wrong here; whether the three difficulties are three separate problems or one problem surfacing three times; and what a board in our position should do instead — together with an honest account of whether the alternative you recommend is better justified than our current rule, or is simply a different arbitrary convention.
