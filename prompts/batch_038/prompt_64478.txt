I'm a third-year PhD student in political philosophy working on how responsibilities for admitting refugees and asylum-seekers ought to be allocated, and next week I give a practice version of the talk to my department before the real job-market run. My own view, roughly, is that claims to admission track the severity and irremediability of what a person faces, and that the distribution of those claims across receiving states should track capacity and something like fair shares rather than history. My supervisor's standing objection is that I have never taken seriously the rival family of views on which the duty to admit is agent-relative — owed by *this* state to *these* people because of what this state did — and he suspects that until I can answer that view on its own terms my chapter is simply asserting a distributive principle. So I would rather spar with it than read about it.

Please play the strongest proponent you can of that rival view, which I'll call the responsibility view. You hold that whether a person counts as someone to whom asylum is owed is not settled primarily by the shape or severity of the harm they face, but by whether some particular state stands in a relation of responsibility for their predicament: it generated the flow by intervention, arms sales, extraction, or imposed conditions; it dismantled or displaced the institutions that would have protected them; it made a promise, ran a program, or recruited local collaborators; or it has already assumed a protective relationship with them. On your view, "who counts" and "who must take them in" are one and the same question, and a state with ample capacity but no such relationship owes admission only as charity, not as justice, however appalling the applicant's circumstances. You have real resources and should use them: the distinction between doing harm and failing to help; the difference between proximate and merely background causal contribution; the value of a polity's capacity to make and keep commitments to specific others; the objection that capacity-based allocation asks nothing of the states that caused the problem and everything of the states that did not; and the worry that a purely harm-based criterion converts asylum into an open-ended welfare claim no polity could sustain. You may concede small points — that you need a threshold, that causation comes in degrees, that implementation may have to borrow other states' capacity — but never the main one: you must not let the severity of the harm become the operative criterion, and you must not slide into a generic humanitarianism on which everyone owes everyone a fair share. Argue from the position's own logic. Do not cite literature or name scholars, and do not step out of character to hedge or to sketch what a balanced view would say. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, ask me for further details of a case if you need them, and stay in role until I say stop. I have forty minutes and a room of people who will enjoy watching me lose.

Here is my opening. A coastal city in a middle-income state is leveled by an earthquake; deaths run into the tens of thousands, and survivors face the collapse of the water supply, untreated injuries, and a cholera season that the country's public-health service no longer has the staff or the stockpiles to meet. Nobody targeted any of these people: there is no persecutor, no political opinion, no particular social group — only deprivation and predictable death. But eleven years earlier your own government concluded a trade-and-debt agreement with that country whose conditions included the abolition of its independent building inspectorate and the privatization of its national drug stockpile, both pressed for in writing by your own treasury officials at the time, and the engineers' post-disaster reports attribute the great majority of the collapse deaths to construction that inspectorate would have condemned. Now a family from that city is at your border, and I want to know whether you admit them, on your own grounds. Either answer costs you something, and I want to see which horn you take. If you say yes, then responsibility does the work you claim for it only because it is so cheap to find — there is essentially no badly governed or failed state in the world whose institutions were not shaped by the trade terms, aid conditions, arms sales, sanctions, or border policies of richer ones — and your view collapses into my harm-based account with an extra causal story attached that is doing no independent work. If you say no, then tell me what your criterion is actually tracking, because the causal contribution here is documented, specific, contemporaneous, and conceded, and refusing it looks exactly like the harm-shape test you said you had rejected: persecution in, starvation out.
