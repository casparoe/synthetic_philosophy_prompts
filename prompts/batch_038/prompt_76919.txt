Next Thursday my university's Student Conduct Review Panel has to decide an appeal that I do not think our own record settles, and I want to walk in knowing precisely which of our past decisions is doing the work. I am a third-year, one of five voting members, and this term I am also in a seminar on political obligation, so let me say at the outset what I do not want: no survey of the literature on civil disobedience, no reading list, no citations, and no reference to any source I could look up. The theory matters to me only where it changes the outcome on these facts. Everything you need is below.

The standard we apply is a single sentence in the student code. The panel may reduce or waive a sanction where it finds that the conduct was a public act of conscience, nonviolent, addressed to the community's sense of justice, undertaken after ordinary channels of redress had been tried and had failed, and for which the student accepts responsibility and whatever sanction results. Our written reasons gloss that sentence differently from case to case, and I am not confident the glosses are compatible.

The six prior appeals that bear on it:

1. Twelve students blocked the registrar's entrance for four hours during the first week of enrollment, protesting the registrar's new enrollment-hold policy. They announced it in the campus newspaper two days ahead, named themselves in the announcement, stayed when asked to move, and did not resist removal. They had petitioned the registrar once, unanswered; a faculty senate hearing on the policy was scheduled for three weeks later and they did not wait for it. Reduced to a written warning. The reasons stressed that the action fell on the office that had made the policy, that the students invited identification, and that the conduct was legible to the people it was meant to persuade. The pending senate hearing is never mentioned.

2. A group blockaded the loading dock of the university's conference hotel and cut power to the ballroom for a day, disrupting a donor weekend and a symposium hosted by an outside academic society, in protest of a contract the university had signed with a private security firm. Public, self-identified, sanctions accepted in advance, two petitions to the provost rebuffed. Not reduced. The reasons said that a protest which bills people who had no voice in the decision it sought to reverse is not an appeal, and that the disrupted event had nothing to do with the security contract.

3. A work-study student in the development office anonymously released internal correspondence showing that the office had solicited a gift from a company the student body had voted to oppose. The student came forward only after an audit identified them, then accepted a two-semester suspension without contesting it. Reduced to one semester. The reasons said the disclosure was aimed at the office that made the decision, and that the panel gives weight to what a student actually pays rather than only to what a student says they would pay.

4. A coalition blocked the campus kitchen operated by an outside catering contractor for one meal service, over that contractor's treatment of its campus dining workers. Public, self-identified, prior petitions, sanctions accepted. Reduced. The reasons said the contractor was the hand by which the university did the thing complained of, so an action aimed at the hand was aimed at the decision.

5. A coalition occupied the undergraduate admissions office over the trustees' legacy-preference policy. Fully public, self-identified, three petitions, a senate resolution, and a meeting with the president beforehand; sanctions accepted. Their statement also said that a rival student organization, named, would deserve expulsion without a hearing for the same tactics, since the tactics are legitimate only in the hands of people who already know which side is right. Not reduced. The reasons said the code requires an appeal to the community's sense of justice, and that a group claiming a licence it would deny its opponents is not making an appeal but announcing a preference for itself. A recorded dissent argued that the majority was objecting to the group's contempt rather than to any clause of the code, and noted that the admissions office had said publicly that it only implements trustee policy and could not have reversed it.

6. Two near-identical encampments on the same quad in the same semester over the same question. The Office of Student Conduct charged the second coalition and, for staffing reasons, never charged the first. The panel reduced the second to a warning, saying it is not in the business of grading one group's conscience against another's, and that where the university declined to treat identical conduct as sanctionable once it cannot treat it as sanctionable twice.

The appeal in front of us. A coalition occupied the loading dock of the mixed-use building that houses the investment office and three academic departments and cut power to one floor for a day, preventing a scheduled investment-committee vote on divestment.

- Announced four days ahead on their own channels and by flyer, not in the campus newspaper; eleven participants named themselves in the announcement and identified themselves to the dean on the spot.
- Two petitions to the provost rebuffed. A faculty senate hearing on the same question was offered three weeks out; they declined the slot, saying the senate has no authority over the endowment.
- The power cut cancelled four hours of instruction in two unrelated departments and delayed one pay cycle for roughly four hundred hourly staff. The sanction includes restitution for that delay.
- The investment office is where the vote was to be taken, but its staff told the campus paper last month that they execute the investment committee's mandate and have no discretion over holdings.
- Their statement says they will accept and serve whatever sanction results. It also says that if the committee votes against divestment they will occupy the same dock the following week, with or without a sanction outstanding.
- The same statement says the rival organization that staged last year's sit-in over an unrelated question would have deserved worse than they get, because that cause was not worth disrupting anything for.
- Last spring a different coalition ran an almost identical dock blockade over this same endowment question. It was never charged.

Sanction imposed: two-semester suspension for each of the eleven, plus restitution.

Two of the five members sat on cases 2 and 5 and have told me they will vote the same way, so whatever I argue has to run through those two decisions rather than around them.

What I want from you is a working memo, not an essay. Specifically: the readings of the code's sentence that are each compatible with all six prior outcomes, stated as operative tests rather than as themes; what each of those readings yields on the new appeal, and where a reading has to strain to accommodate one of the six; your recommended holding, with the closest analog identified and an account of why the strongest competing analog does not control; a sentence the panel could actually put in its written reasons that states the principle it is adopting, together with what that sentence forecloses for the next appeal; one hypothetical future appeal, realistic for a campus panel, that would separate the two strongest readings, with each reading's predicted outcome; which of the stated reasons in the six decisions look like post-hoc glosses rather than the operative ground, and what you are inferring from that; and which facts about the new appeal will carry the most rhetorical weight in the room while being, on our own record, beside the point. Where the record genuinely does not decide the case, say so plainly and tell me what the panel is choosing when it chooses.
