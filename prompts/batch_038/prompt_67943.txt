Third-year philosophy PhD student here, working on causation and explanation, and I co-run an interdisciplinary reading group with a couple of people from the history department — this term's theme is individuals versus structures in historical explanation. I've talked them into assigning chunks of War and Peace: the Borodino sequence, and the second epilogue where Tolstoy stops telling the story and lectures at us about power and cause. My worry is that the group will treat the novel as a big illustrated argument against Carlyle, who opens his On Heroes lectures by claiming that universal history is at bottom "the History of the Great Men who have worked here", and then congratulate themselves for having refuted him. I'd like a genuinely critical reading before I write up discussion questions, not a plot summary with some epistemology sprinkled on.

The thing I want held onto throughout is that great men make history isn't one claim, and neither is its denial. There's the counterfactual reading (the events wouldn't have happened without this person), the magnitude reading (this person contributed more causal weight than anybody else and the rest is noise), the explanatory-priority reading (the best account of what happened cites the person's agency rather than the structural conditions — Versailles, the Depression, the conservative elites who thought they could use Hitler and then couldn't), and the narrative reading (history is best *told* agent-centred regardless of what the underlying causal facts are). Which of those is Tolstoy actually attacking? Does he slide between them, and does the slide matter? His argument that a command only has an effect when it happens to coincide with a chain of events already unfolding feels like it targets the magnitude reading, but the integration-of-infinitesimals business — history should give up hunting for causes and start summing infinitely small contributions the way the other sciences do — sounds like something much stronger, maybe a claim about what a cause even is at that scale. Is that an argument, a promissory note, or a metaphor doing all the work? And what would have to be true of historical explanation for it to be more than decoration?

Then the scenes, because this is where the novel should get to push back on the taxonomy rather than just illustrate it. Napoleon at Borodino issuing orders that are overtaken by events before his aide reaches the line; Kutuzov at Fili handing over Moscow and then mostly declining to direct anything at all — isn't the man wise enough to read the swarm and therefore refrain from commanding still a great man in a structuralist costume? And Tolstoy's contempt for Napoleon's vanity only makes sense if individuals are the right objects of praise and blame, which is precisely what the epilogue says they aren't. I want you to say where the novel undercuts its own thesis, where it undercuts the analytic categories I've imported, and where I'm forcing the fit tho — the interiority stuff, Pierre and Andrei and that oak tree, is doing something the epilogue has no room for, and I'd rather be told that than have it smoothed over. Finally, bring it back to the question one of the historians is guaranteed to raise in week three: would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler? I don't want a verdict so much as a diagnosis of what the question is even asking and whether Tolstoy gives us tools for pulling it apart — the gap between no Hitler no seizure of power, no Hitler no war of annihilation in the east, and no Hitler no genocide looks like the counterfactual/magnitude gap to me, and the intentionalist versus functionalist fight looks like the same distinction prosecuted with seperate archives instead of arguments. Tell me what the novel adds to that debate that an argument wouldn't, and where you think the analytic version gets it right and the novelist just doesn't notice.
