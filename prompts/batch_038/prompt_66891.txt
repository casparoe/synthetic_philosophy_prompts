Eleven weeks ago an infarct took most of my left insula and the white matter beneath it. I was sixty-two and had spent thirty-four years as a structural engineer, which is why I have been keeping notes rather than a diary. I am writing to you because the notes have stopped telling me anything, and because my daughter has asked me a question four times now that I cannot answer from the inside.

The notes record events like these. I set my right palm flat against the side of a kettle that had just boiled; I felt the heat at once, knew its exact outline on the skin, and could have ranked it seven out of ten without hesitation. I did not move my hand. I stood and watched the skin redden and thought, with genuine interest, that this was going to blister. When the physiotherapist pinches the web of my thumb I smile at her, and she has to tell me to stop smiling so that she can score the response. The pain clinic asks for two numbers — how intense, and how unpleasant — and I have never once been able to supply the second. I now inspect my hands and the soles of my feet twice a day, the way I used to walk a site and read gauges, because nothing in me reports damage unless I go and look for it.

I have read enough of the arguments about cases like mine to know that two readings are on offer, and that my notes cannot decide between them. On one reading, the sensation was never the bad part: the badness lay in the minding, in the recoil and the suffering, and I have kept the report and lost the minding — so what reaches me now is not bad, and was only ever bad by courtesy of something I no longer have. On the other reading, what reaches me now is not pain at all, but a smoke alarm going off in an empty house: I would be wrong to say I still feel it, and the word has simply stopped applying. There is a third thing the notes keep forcing on me, which the arguments mostly skip: the recoil that is gone was also a protection, so the loss of the badness is not only a loss. And I have begun to suspect, though I cannot verify it, that the same disconnection may be spreading — that I feel the events of my life and no longer mind them, and that this is precisely the sort of thing one could not detect by introspection.

What I want is a poem to hand my daughter instead of an explanation: a ballade in the traditional English form — three eight-line stanzas rhyming ababbcbC over three rhyme sounds, in more or less iambic tetrameter, with the same refrain line closing each stanza, and a four-line envoi rhyming bcbC. In place of the prince the envoi is traditionally addressed to, address her.

The refrain must be my answer to the question she keeps asking — does it hurt — and it must be a single sentence of ten words or fewer that says both things at once: that I feel it exactly as before, and that it does not bother me. It must be word-for-word identical in all four of its appearances; no substitution, no changed punctuation, no added capital. Everything that changes about what it means must come from the lines newly built around it, and that is the point of using this form at all: the sentence is a constant, and its valence is not.

Give each stanza one scene, drawn from the material above or invented in the same register, and make the refrain mean something different in each — nearly a mercy in one, nearly a horror in another, and in the third neither, simply true, which is the hardest of the three. The envoi must not choose between the two readings I have set out, and must not console: no freedom, no diminishment, no peace. It should end by putting the question to her, since she is the only one of us positioned to answer it.

Two limits on register. The words stroke, lesion, insula, syndrome, asymbolia, numb, hollow, and dissociation may not appear; the diagnosis is not the subject. And do not make me admirable or pitiable — I am a man describing instruments, and I would like the poem to sound like one. Someone who knows this literature should be able to tell from the poem alone that the localization and the intensity are intact, that the aversion is gone, and that these are two different losses with a protective function caught between them. Someone who has never heard of the condition should get only a father answering his daughter.
