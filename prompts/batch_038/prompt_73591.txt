so I'm running an informal reading group on philosophies of history and I wrote this up as the provocation for our second session, sat on it for a day, and now I don't think I can actually defend it — but I also can't find the bad step, which is annoying.

(1) Anyone who writes a theory about the large-scale shape of history is writing from inside some particular political order, at some particular point in that order's life.
(2) The cyclical guys make this point themselves, harder than anyone else does: for Polybius every constitution decays into its corrupt twin and each phase comes with its own self-description; for Ibn Khaldun a dynasty's asabiyyah — its group solidariy — conquers, then gets used up over roughly three generations of city life, and what that society can believe about itself moves along with it.
(3) So "history runs in one direction and we've arrived at the point where the cycles stop" isn't a discovery about history, it's the self-description of the current phase, no different in kind from the confidence of the Romans Polybius was embedded in, or the courtly optimism Ibn Khaldun spent twenty years serving before he quit.
(4) But (2) and (3) apply to the cyclical theory too, and its own biography is the best evidence of that. Polybius worked out the cycle as a deported Greek living in the Scipionic circle in Rome, at what he took to be the peak of the mixed constitution. Ibn Khaldun drafted his in four years of seclusion at Qal'at ibn Salama in 1377 and then spent his last twenty-odd years teaching and sitting as a Maliki judge in Cairo — the soft late phase of exactly the pattern he's describing. Spengler, as I read him, says outright that a culture's thought-forms don't travel, which lets him file his own book as a Faustian achievement rather than a finding about cultures in general. Toynbee's the interesting one: he insists on comparison across civilizations and then spends the back volumes handing the payoff to the universal churches, and the standard complaint is that this puts a direction back into a scheme that was only ever supposed to be comparison.
(5) So the cyclical theory explains why its rivals get believed and why it gets believed by the same mechanism, and it isn't in a position to complain about that.
(6) And if a belief's acceptance is fully explained by where its author sits in a cycle, then acceptance is no evidence for truth — wherever in the cycle I happen to sit, I'd wind up believing whatever that position produces, so my believing this particular thing tells me nothing.
(7) So there's no reason to prefer the cyclical theory to its rivals.
(8) And by (3), no reason to prefer any linear one either.
(9) So philosophy of history is a museum of symptoms, not a competition of theories.

(9) is the part I can't take. Not because I can refute it — I just don't believe it, and I wrote the thing, and everyone I showed it too said "sure, but" and couldn't get past that. The pieces I most want to keep are (2) and (3): I do think where you sit distorts what you can see, and I do want the hit on triumphalist progress talk. Which step do I cut? And for whatever you cut, say why it looked innocent when I wrote it, because it definitely did, then price the alternatives — specifically, if I keep (6) I seem to owe an account of how anybody ever climbs out of their own phase, and if I drop (6) then (3) looks like it was never licensed and I've basically written an advertisement for the cyclical view. Also flag anywhere I've been unfair to the actual texts, since two people in the group have read more Polybius and more Ibn Khaldun than I have.
