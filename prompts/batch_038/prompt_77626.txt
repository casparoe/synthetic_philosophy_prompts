I am preparing a session for an upper-level seminar in population ethics. We will already have worked through the argument for the repugnant conclusion and Parfit's reasons for finding it repugnant, and I want to pair that material with Aldous Huxley's *Brave New World*, on the working assumption that the novel is doing philosophical work the formal argument cannot do. I would like a critical reading that tests that assumption rather than simply asserting it, and that is prepared to conclude the pairing does not work.

Start with the obvious reading: the World State is Parfit's Z rendered as a society — a vast number of lives that are, on the book's own evidence, not merely worth living but genuinely pleasant, and yet drained of everything that made other lives worth writing about. Tell me how far that reading actually holds. My difficulty is that the novel's horror seems driven by quality rather than quantity. Reproduction is centrally planned at the Hatchery; the Bokanovsky process multiplies a single lower-caste embryo into dozens of identical persons; and the resulting society is described as stable precisely because its numbers are managed. Nothing in the book suggests runaway population growth. If that is right, then the World State is not Z at all but something different: a fixed, carefully regulated headcount living at a deliberately lowered level of excellence, which makes the book an objection to leveling down rather than to mere addition. Say whether you accept this, and if you do, what the novel is then an argument against — an averaging view, a perfectionist complaint about drabness, or a claim about how a society's optimum gets chosen rather than how many people are in it.

Second, I want the reader's revulsion separated from the philosophical case. Stipulate a variant; call it Bokanovsky-plus. In it, the conditioning involves no deception, nobody is misinformed about their own situation, and the pharmacology removes pains that would otherwise genuinely be suffered. The resulting citizens lead lives that are, by any standard we can articulate, good: warm, secure, sociable, undemanding, and untroubled. They are simply shallow. Does Huxley retain a case against Bokanovsky-plus, or was the novel's force parasitic all along on the manipulation, so that it is really a book about consent and engineering rather than about population value? If the case survives, identify the value carrying it — autonomy, authenticity, achievement, agency-dependent welfare — and say whether that value is welfarist or whether the objection has left welfare behind. If the case does not survive, say so plainly; the seminar gains more from that than from a comfortable answer.

Third, the person-affecting problem. The Epsilons would not otherwise have existed, and their contentment is produced in them by design. Is there anyone the World State has wronged, or is Huxley's objection irreducibly impersonal? And is hypnopaedic contentment a case of adaptive preference formation that disqualifies the welfare data altogether, or is it, on an informed-desire account, positive evidence that these people are badly off? I want the two diagnoses kept distinct, since they imply different things about whether the World State is worse for its inhabitants or only worse in itself.

Fourth, the ending. At the lighthouse, John's self-punishment is taken up by the crowd and converted into a shared spectacle of pleasure. Does that sequence carry an aggregative point — one person's agony traded into many people's delight — and does it belong to the same family as the result sometimes discussed under the name the sadistic conclusion, or is it merely theatrical and doing no argumentative work at all?

Finally, where does the novel resist this entire apparatus? John is a fanatic and a bigot, the Reservation is not romanticized, and Huxley supplies no positive alternative to the World State, which means the book may be unable to show that anything better was available. Tell me which two or three scenes you would have the seminar discuss, what you would warn them against overreading, and what, if anything, *Brave New World* contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot supply — in particular, whether it earns the thought that Z is not a trade-off anyone chooses but the fixed point of an administrative system optimizing for contentment.
