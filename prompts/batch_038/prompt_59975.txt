I sit on the Recruitment Appeals Panel of a territorial police force in England. Our terms of reference require the panel, when it hears a complaint about an annual selection round, to report separately on two questions: whether the selection process itself treated candidates of one sex less favourably than candidates of the other; and whether, independently of that, the round produced a disparity for which the force is responsible and ought to remedy. Our powers differ between the two limbs. We may dismiss, and we may make recommendations to the Chief Constable under either limb, but only a finding under the first can support quashing the round or directing a re-run.

A complaint has been lodged by the force's Gender Equality Network about the 2024–25 police constable intake. In total, 1,650 candidates applied for 779 posts. Each candidate stated a first-choice unit and was assessed only by that unit's board, the three units having different additional assessments. Vacancies were fixed in advance by the resourcing board: 437 to Neighbourhood Response, 208 to Custody & Detention and 134 to Specialist Operations. All 779 posts have now been filled; the successful candidates began their training in February and are due to be attested in July.

The workforce analyst closed her monitoring report with the following table.

| Unit | Men applied | Men selected | Rate | Women applied | Women selected | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Neighbourhood Response | 600 | 372 | 62.0% | 100 | 65 | 65.0% |
| Custody & Detention | 200 | 82 | 41.0% | 300 | 126 | 42.0% |
| Specialist Operations | 50 | 9 | 18.0% | 400 | 125 | 31.3% |

She concluded that women fared better in every unit in which a comparison was actually made, and recommended dismissal. The Network's representative replies that the panel should look at the round as a whole: of 850 male applicants, 463 were selected (54.5 per cent), and of 800 female applicants, 316 (39.5 per cent). She says that no board ever set a woman against a man from another unit, so the unit table describes decisions nobody took, whereas the fifteen-point gap describes the outcome of a single force-wide exercise for which the force is answerable.

There is a complication inside Specialist Operations. Candidates there also stated a rota preference, between the fixed-days rota and the rotating-nights rota.

| Rota | Men applied | Men selected | Rate | Women applied | Women selected | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed days | 10 | 4 | 40.0% | 340 | 119 | 35.0% |
| Rotating nights | 40 | 5 | 12.5% | 60 | 6 | 10.0% |

The Network argues that the unit-level advantage in the first table is itself an illusion: on the finest-grained comparison available, women lose out on both rotas, and the panel should therefore treat the second table as the one that reveals what actually happened. The analyst says the rota split is a further artefact of where candidates applied, of exactly the same kind as the unit split.

Two features of the background will bear on whichever reading the panel prefers. Specialist Operations is the only unit in the force offering a fixed-days rota; every rota elsewhere involves rotating night shifts. And three years ago the force ran a recruitment campaign addressed specifically to women, encouraging applications to Specialist Operations on the ground that it is the recognised pipeline to the senior ranks; the campaign material said nothing about the number of vacancies in that unit or about its selection rates.

Please draft the written opinion I shall circulate to the panel before Thursday's meeting. It should reach a clear recommendation on each limb, state which of our powers the panel should exercise and over what period, and give reasons the Network could actually answer. It must explain, in terms a lay member of the panel can follow, why the same body of data can be made to point in opposite directions at different levels of disaggregation, and what should decide which partition is probative of which question; simply preferring the finer table to the coarser one, or the reverse, will not do. Include a short section directing what the analyst should report in future rounds, together with the criterion governing that choice, so that this disagreement does not recur. Deal at length and fairly with the strongest considerations on the side you reject — including the Network's argument that a rule whose verdict reverses whenever a further variable is introduced cannot be the rule the panel relies on.

No references, case citations or reading list are needed: the panel wants reasoning it can act on. Write in British English, in about 1,200 words.
