I audited a two-day workshop on the political economy of food crises last month, and in the closing session two of the participants got into a long argument that I have not been able to settle in my own head. I was not involved and have no stake in either side; I am reporting what they said as literally as I can reconstruct it, with their moves numbered. I would like a careful, move-by-move adjudication.

**Setup.** Nadia, a postdoc working at the intersection of climate science and economic history, proposed that the familiar dispute over whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes should be resolved by borrowing the machinery of climate event attribution. Her scheme, sketched on a whiteboard: for a given famine, construct a counterfactual world in which the physical shock is held fixed — same monsoon failure, same blight, same drought — but institutions are set to what she called a competent-response baseline (working price signals, no politically motivated procurement or trade policy, relief deployed about as fast as a well-run administration of that period could have managed). Model both worlds, estimate excess mortality in each, and report an attribution fraction: the share of the death toll owing to the shock and the share owing to political failure. Her pitch was that this converts a rhetorical stalemate into an estimate with error bars, and that the fraction is precisely the quantity Sen's claim about democracies and free presses is implicitly about, so it would also make that claim testable rather than creedal.

**1.** Desmond, an economic historian, said the competent-response baseline is not a parameter one can set but a normative commitment one has to assume. The officials in London and Dublin in the 1840s considered themselves competent and were largely operating within the administrative science of their day; so whichever norms Nadia picks as competent, the resulting fraction measures her standards, not the event.

**2.** Nadia replied that every baseline is normative, including the one embedded in calling deaths preventable, and that hers is at least explicit, arguable, and open to sensitivity analysis. She added that competence should be fixed by what was administratively feasible at the time, not by what the actors believed: the same imperial state ran codified famine relief with defined triggers in India a few decades later, and food was in fact imported into Ireland during the famine years, so a competent-response world is not a fantasy.

**3.** Desmond's second objection was nonseparability. In climate attribution you can hold the forcing fixed and vary the baseline because the atmosphere is indifferent to your modeling choices; in a famine the shock is already political. Bengal in 1943 involved the loss of Burmese rice imports to a war, wartime procurement, and inflation caused by monetary and spending policy; China around 1960 involved procurement quotas, diverted labour, and a reporting system that suppressed the news. Holding "the shock" fixed is not a well-posed operation.

**4.** Nadia refined: hold fixed only exogenous biophysical inputs — rainfall, temperature, the crop pathogen — and count everything downstream, including the economic consequences of the war, as political. She noted this yields a small natural share in all three cases, and said she takes that to be the finding rather than an artifact.

**5.** Desmond said that is exactly the problem: if everything downstream of the weather is political by definition, the fraction is not estimated but assumed, and a scheme that returns "overwhelmingly political" for every possible case cannot fail. He said the same immunity infects Sen's thesis, which survives any apparent counterexample by tightening what counts as substantial, democratic, or a relatively free press.

**6.** Nadia answered that it can fail: a harvest failure deep enough that even an optimal distribution leaves a physical food deficit would drive the natural share up, and the test is to apply the scheme to a case with a catastrophic shock and a decent response. She granted that Ireland is partly such a case — the potato was close to the exclusive calorie base for something like a third of the population — so the natural share there is not negligible, even though grain and livestock continued to leave Irish ports throughout. She also said that if the framework never once returned a high natural share, she would regard it as doing no work.

**7.** Desmond accepted the concession but said it changes the object. If the question is how many would have died under an optimal distribution, the quantity of interest is a food deficit and a distribution problem, not a fraction of a death toll — which is the entitlement way of putting it — and that can be read off the actual record: exports, stocks, prices, wages, relief rolls. No model of a world that never happened is required. He added that the appearance of precision is dangerous for public memory, since a number like "the political component was seventy per cent" would be quoted for fifty years with its assumptions stripped away.

**8.** Nadia's last word was that the entitlement evidence is what her baseline is built from, so the two approaches are not rivals; and that historians who decline to state a baseline have not avoided numbers, they have hidden them inside adjectives. Desmond replied that "preventable" is at least honestly normative, whereas a fraction dresses a contested normative baseline as a measurement.

Please adjudicate this exchange. For each of moves 1 through 8, say whether the move succeeds, succeeds only against a weaker version of the opponent's position, or fails, and what would have to be added to repair it. Then address four questions directly: (i) Is the nonseparability objection in move 3 fatal to the proposal, or does the refinement in move 4 genuinely answer it, and if so at what cost to the proposal's claims? (ii) After move 6, does the attribution-fraction framework still risk anything — state what finding would oblige Nadia to abandon it, or explain why no such finding exists and whether that is a defect in a framework of this kind? (iii) Is Desmond right in move 7 that a moral question has been disguised as a measurement question, and if he is, does that make the proposal worse or better than ordinary historical talk of preventable deaths? (iv) What are both of them missing — in particular, is the analogy to climate event attribution, which Nadia treats as a warrant and Desmond as a category error, doing more work than either of them admits? Close with a verdict on who is more right overall and which parts of the proposal, if any, survive the exchange.
