For close on sixty years the argument that special relativity compels the block universe has been met with the same handful of replies, and neither side has shifted a degree. I am drafting the framing section of a collection on the metaphysics of time, and what I need is a diagnosis of that impasse rather than a verdict on it, and rather than another survey of presentism, the growing block and four-dimensionalism.

Begin from what I take to be the shared ground. The argument starts with a piece of physics both camps accept: two inertial observers in relative motion carve Minkowski spacetime into different spacelike hypersurfaces of simultaneity, so there is no observer-independent answer to which distant events are happening now. To that it adds two premises which are not theorems of the theory: that no observer's slicing is privileged, and that whatever is real relative to an observer is real, full stop — whence the coexistence relation is an equivalence relation whose transitive closure sweeps up the past, present and future alike. The familiar popular gloss, in which two passers-by walking in opposite directions disagree about whether a decision two and a half million light-years away has already been taken, is just this structure dressed up.

Then the standing replies, none of which has dislodged anything. The neo-Lorentzian holds that a privileged foliation can be empirically undetectable without being incoherent, and that the equivalence of inertial frames is a claim about the form of the laws rather than about what there is; so premise two, on this view, is metaphysics smuggled in as physics. In a related vein, it has been urged that definiteness attaches only to an observer's past light cone, so that the transitivity premise illicitly iterates a local relation across observers who can never be in causal contact — the reply being that this is a reductio of the argument's formulation, not of the presentist's position. A third family makes the present point-relative rather than hyperplane-relative, reading it off light-cone structure at a location. A fourth is deflationary: spacetime theories specify metrical and causal structure and say nothing whatever about which parts of that structure exist, so the presentist simply declines the question. The eternalist's rejoinder to all four is that the presentist has thereby either abandoned relativity, or abandoned a global present, or abandoned content altogether; and that if the argument fails to refute presentism it at least puts a price on it.

My working hypothesis, which I want tested rather than adopted, is that the two sides agree on every item of physics and are disagreeing about something else entirely: about the ideology in which an ontological theory may be stated. The eternalist takes "there exists" to be a single unrelativised, joint-carving quantifier whose domain is fixed by what the world is like, and reads the argument's second premise as a constraint on any candidate ontology — if existence were relative to a frame, existence would not be a feature of the world at all. The presentist, on this reading, is not asserting that fewer things exist; they are denying that existence is the kind of notion the eternalist assumes, holding that "what is there?" is not well posed until one says from when it is asked. If that is right, the argument is not unsound but addressed to a thesis no presentist holds, and the subsequent literature consists largely of two sides restating metaontological commitments in the vocabulary of spacetime geometry.

Assess that hypothesis against at least three alternatives. First, that this is a verbal dispute about "exists" or "real", closable by stipulation. Second, that two different questions are being answered under one heading — the eternalist answering "what is there?" and the presentist answering "what is settled?" or "what is determinate?" — in which case the light-cone objection would not be a premise-denial at all but a change of subject, and the debate would have been talking past itself since the start. Third, that the disagreement is substantive with a hidden formal component: whether a relativistic dynamical theory can be written down that reproduces Lorentz-invariant predictions while containing a structure privileging one foliation, or one light-cone-relative present. If such theories exist — hidden-variable schemes that build a preferred foliation into the dynamics, and relativistic spontaneous-collapse models that manage without one at some cost in generality — then the coherence of a physical home for presentism is not in question and the residue is purely metaphysical; if they cannot be constructed, the presentist owes an outright rejection of relativity. Say which of these you think is closest to the truth, and say whether the frame in which the cosmic microwave background is isotropic does any real work here or is merely a rhetorical device deployed by both camps, given that it is distinguished by contingent matter distribution rather than by the laws.

Finally, given the diagnosis you settle on, tell me what would count as progress. Is there a formulation to be had — one formal theory in a shared vocabulary, with the two positions as genuinely non-equivalent readings of it — such that writing it down would either end the dispute or locate exactly where the disagreement lives? Or should we expect none, on the grounds that the argument's function in the literature is to discipline a choice of ideology rather than to establish a conclusion, so that the stalemate is the correct steady state and the honest recommendation to my contributors is to stop treating it as a live refutation?
