I'm putting together a diagnostic for the first meeting of a graduate seminar on population ethics, and I need your help building the instrument. You should write this as a working population ethicist would — someone who's spent a decade in this literature and knows exactly where the standard labels mislead people. Please start by brainstorming the whole answer space out loud before you commit to any tree structure: I want to see you list the underlying yes/no commitments that actually generate the positions (does "better than" have to be transitive across populations, is there an exchange rate between adding lives and improving lives, do person-affecting restrictions bind, is there a welfare level below which adding a life is worse than not adding it, and whatever else you think belongs) and only then arrange them into branches. The reason I want this: every year the same thing happens, students announce in week one that they're total utilitarians or that they're "person-affecting", and by week five they discover they can't hold on to what they said. I want a handout that catches that in advance, so the deliverable is a decision tree of seven or eight questions, each one a concrete case rather than a claim about a principle, plus a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named leaves.

The cases have to be answerable by a smart first-year who has never heard any of this, and no question may contain the name of a position or of a paradox — if a question reads like it's fishing for a particular answer, rewrite it. Here are the ones I've drafted, feel free to sharpen them or replace any that turn out to be double-barreled: (a) Second Earth — we get one shot at either founding an off-world society of a billion people whose lives are good but noticeably less good than ours, with nothing changing back home, or staying put and using the same resources to make everyone already here somewhat better off; (b) The Dimmer Switch — you can add a thousand people at very high welfare, or raise a thousand existing people's welfare by some fixed amount, and I want the question to force an answer about where the tradeoff point sits rather than letting them dodge; (c) The Borderline Life — a life that is worth living but only barely, worth creating or not; (d) The Long Dusk — ten billion people with rich, full lives versus a hundred trillion lives each containing nothing but a warm room, a good meal, and an hour of mild contentment, and no third option; (e) The Rescue — you can relieve terrible existing suffering only by giving up the chance to bring happy people into existence. Two structural requirements. First, don't open the tree with anything about whether future or non-existent people's welfare counts at all — that's the boring first split and it wastes a question, since almost every live position says yes in some form. Second, the leaves have to include total and average views, critical-level views, Hurka-style variable value views, the asymmetry/anti-natalist option, suffering-priority views, and at least one leaf you mark "unnamed" because nobody defends that cell.

Then give me the part I actually care most about: a table of the logical space your questions carve out, with the unoccupied cells flagged and each one diagnosed in a sentence as incoherent, unattractive-but-coherent, or merely unexplored by anyone so far — don't just say "nobody holds this." Also flag any pair of leaves your tree genuinely cannot separate, and say why (my suspicion is that several positions agree on every finite case and only come apart in limit or infinite cases, which no handout of this length can sort out). Each leaf should carry a one-sentence note on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defenders get asked about at conferences. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, please; I want the instrument, not the syllabus. Think carefully about whether your branch questions are really independent, because if two of them are secretly the same question the tree will overfit to whichever intuition the student happens to state first.
