My uncle did his service in Finland, still turns up for refresher exercises at fifty, and closes every argument about the draft with 'if you want peace, prepare for war.' Last week he aimed it at a friend of mine who'd rather scrap conscription for a small professional force — Sweden suspended thier draft and then brought it back a few years ago, so it's not a crazy idea — and treated the proverb as settling the thing: a conscript army deters, a professional one wouldn't.

I'm pretty sure a proverb can't settle that, but I can't say what it's even asserting. Can you pull apart the distinct claims hiding inside the one sentence — what is preparing what, whose preparation counts, preparation of what kind (troops? resolve? industry? a whole society already used to being mobilised?), and 'peace' in which sense — and sort them into the near-tautologies, the substantive-but-defensible, and the ones that are false or run backwards, since I have a nagging sense that arming up can make an attack likelier rather than less likely. Then tell me which readings, if any, get him all the way to conscription rather than stopping at defence spending in general, and which of them the Swedish reversal actually speaks to. Numbered list would be great, he doesn't read prose.
