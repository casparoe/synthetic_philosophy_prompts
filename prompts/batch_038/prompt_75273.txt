I teach philosophy of mind, and a colleague in the history of science has asked me to read a chapter of her book-in-progress on what she calls the mid-century revolt against inner states across psychology, linguistics, and economics. She is a careful historian, but the economics section lies outside my competence, and I would like an accuracy audit before I return my comments to her. Her passage on revealed preference runs as follows:

"Samuelson did for the theory of demand what Watson had done for psychology: he abolished the inner. On his account a preference is not a mental state that choices express; it is the choice, redescribed. Utility is therefore not merely unmeasurable but meaningless — a term with no observational content at all — and revealed preference theory amounts to verificationism applied to consumer behaviour. The cost of this was slow to be recognized. If 'x is preferred to y' means nothing more than 'x is chosen when y is affordable', then no behaviour can contradict the theory, since whatever an agent does may be redescribed as her preference; Afriat's theorem later gave this a technical form, showing that for any finite body of choice data one can construct a utility function that rationalizes it. Little's critique then finished the job from the other direction, demonstrating that consistency of choice cannot support any welfare judgment without covert value assumptions. The theory was thus at once too empty to be refuted and too austere to be normative. What remains is a bookkeeping device for checking the internal coherence of choice data — useful, but not a theory of anything."

Three things trouble me, and I cannot yet tell which are signs of my ignorance and which of her errors.

First, the identification of preference with choice. My impression, possibly naive, is that this is one contested reading of Samuelson rather than a settled fact about his programme: that other readers treat the link between preference and choice as a substantive hypothesis carrying tacit conditions rather than as a definition, and that which reading is right makes an enormous difference to whether the emptiness charge has any purchase at all. Is that so, and what exactly does it turn on?

Second, and more particularly, the invocation of Afriat. I retain enough philosophy of science to distinguish two claims her sentence seems to fuse: that a term is stipulated in observational vocabulary, and that a theory is compatible with every possible observation. A dispositional definition — solubility defined by what happens on immersion — is a stipulation, and yet the claims it licenses constrain what may be observed. Might not the same hold here, so that even a definitional reading of preference leaves the consistency axioms as genuine restrictions on choice data rather than as tautologies? If so, what does Afriat's theorem in fact establish, and does it support or subvert her sentence? I should also like to know whether the tacit conditions she would need — tastes unchanged, no coercion, no ignorance — can be deployed ad hoc to rescue the theory from any recalcitrant data, in which case perhaps her conclusion survives, though by a route she has not taken.

Third, Little. Does showing that a theory cannot do normative work without value judgments amount to showing that it is empty or false? And does the final dismissal — bookkeeping rather than theory — sit well with what I understand to be the practice, namely that these axioms are tested against real household data and that the results are used to bound welfare measures and price indices?

Please separate what in the passage is accurate, what is a defensible but disputed interpretation presented as settled, and what is simply wrong; tell me where her framing would make an economist or a philosopher of economics wince; and say whether her conclusion about what survives follows from those parts of the passage that are true. I would also like your view on whether the analogy with behaviourism holds once one distinguishes denying that inner states exist from excluding them from a particular theory.
