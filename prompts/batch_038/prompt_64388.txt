I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in history and philosophy of science. For our department's spring methods series I have been asked to produce a model of the document two long-arguing camps ought to have written and never did: the joint statement that would follow a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a defender of Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes and a methodological anarchist of the Feyerabendian type. The second party holds that any set of universal methodological rules can be shown either to have been violated at some point in the history of successful science or to be dispensable without loss; and that Lakatos's framework, precisely because it permits tenacity in a degenerating programme and refuses to dictate any particular decision at any particular moment, licenses anything while continuing to speak in the vocabulary of rationality. Please draft that statement.

Begin with a numbered list of claims both parties can sign without qualification. I expect these to include at least the following, though you should judge each and add or drop as you see fit: that single theories are not separately testable in isolation from their auxiliaries; that modifications to a protective belt differ in quality rather than being uniformly legitimate or uniformly illegitimate; that methodological appraisal attaches to series of theories rather than to individual theories; and that the anarchist's slogan is a thesis about universal rules, not a nihilistic denial that scientists ever have reasons for what they do.

Then give a table of the disagreements that survive the agreed section. State each one twice: once in wording the Lakatosian would accept as a description of her own position, and once in wording the anarchist would accept as a description of his. Neither phrasing should smuggle in the other party's vocabulary as a pejorative.

Beyond the table I want the cruxes: three or four sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of the parties. My own suspicion, which I would rather you tested than adopted, is that the first crux is temporal — whether "this programme is degenerating" can ever function as a claim an agent may act on now, or is only ever a historian's retrospective verdict — and that the second is operational: whether the criteria of theoretical and empirical progress could be converted into a coding scheme that independent raters could apply to a documented sequence of theories with acceptable reliability, where ad hocness is scored not merely by the empirical content of a modification but by whether the modification was generated by the programme's positive heuristic or was drawn directly from the anomaly it was introduced to absorb. For each crux, name a case, an argument, or a study design that both parties would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about the outcome in advance, before the evidence is in. Two pieces of testing material that I think both would accept are the prediction of Neptune from the perturbations of Uranus and the history of Prout's hypothesis concerning whole-number atomic weights; describe each only as briefly as you need in order to make the crux concrete.

Finally, state plainly what each party would accept as evidence or argument against their own view, and say whether you judge any part of the dispute to be verbal — in particular, whether the two use "rational" to mark different things — and if so, which part is verbal and which part is substantive.

Keep the document to roughly fifteen hundred words. Do not include a bibliography or a literature review by citation; refer to positions and arguments, and I will supply the references myself. I would also ask that you not introduce the sociology of scientific knowledge as a third party: I want the two camps defined by what they say about rationality, not by what they say about social explanation.
