My paper argues that al-Ghazālī's seventeenth discussion in the Tahāfut al-falāsifa is not a piece of occasionalist metaphysics at all, and that reading it as one — the fire doesn't burn the cotton, God does — mistakes what he's actually denying. What he denies, on my reading, is that the Avicennans can demonstrate the necessity of the causal connection; the chapter is an attack on a proof, not an assertion about who the real agents turn out to be. The weight sits on the ʿinda/bi move: observation shows the effect occurring *at* its usual antecedant, and that licenses no inference to its occurring *by* it — but that's a claim about warrant, not about ontology. On my reading he can cheerfully grant that fire has a nature and that the ʿāda is real and stable, since God's habit is precisely what makes natural enquiry possible, which is why the Iḥyāʾ and the Iqtiṣād are full of perfectly ordinary causal talk without any embarrassment. So he isn't Malebranche with a different theology: he's an anti-demonstrationist about necessitarianism, and the miracle possibility is a corollary rather than the engine.
