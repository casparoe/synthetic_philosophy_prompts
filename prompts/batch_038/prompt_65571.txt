Six weeks ago the governor appointed me to our state's Sentencing Reform Commission. Before that I spent fourteen years as a clinical psychologist inside the state's prisons, running risk-and-needs assessments and treatment programs, so I arrived already suspicious of the word "deserts": I have watched it do work in parole hearings that nobody involved is willing to describe out loud. The vote I now have to cast in three weeks has turned what was an academic interest into something I have to put my name to, and I would rather be argued out of my inclinations than confirm them.

Here is the matter. Section 2 of our sentencing code states the purposes of punishment. The current text provides that a sentence must be proportionate to the gravity of the offense and to the offender's just deserts, with public safety, deterrence, and rehabilitation pursued as further aims within that limit. Two commissioners have proposed striking the desert language and replacing the section with the following: "The sole legitimate ground for depriving a person of liberty is the reduction of the risk that the person will cause serious harm to others. Any such deprivation shall be no more restrictive in degree or duration than is necessary to that end." Their supporting memorandum invokes the published exchange between Daniel Dennett and Gregg Caruso on free will and punishment, and presents the proposal as taking Caruso's side: no basic desert, punishment displaced by a public-health framework in which incapacitation is justified as self-protection and constrained by the least restrictive alternative.

Two cases are attached to the docket as tests of the language, and both involve people whose hearings fall in the next quarter.

Docket 118: a man sentenced at nineteen to fifteen years for an aggravated assault that left the victim permanently disabled. He has served eleven years, completed every program offered to him, and the instruments we use score him low risk on every static and dynamic measure. Under the proposed section there would be no remaining lawful basis to hold him; under current law he serves four more years.

Docket 207: a woman convicted of second-degree arson for setting a fire in a building she believed to be unoccupied; she has a serious and only partially treated psychotic illness, no prior record, and low culpability on any ordinary reckoning. Our instruments nonetheless score her high risk, chiefly on static factors that treatment cannot move. Under current law her ceiling is three years. Under the proposed section there is no ceiling at all — only a necessity test she may never satisfy.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to the commission before the vote: roughly a thousand words, bottom line first, recommending one of three options — retain the existing section, adopt the proposed one, or adopt a hybrid in which culpability sets an upper bound and assessed risk sets the conditions and the release date. I want reasoning that commissioners on the losing side could actually argue with, not a survey of the debate. Specifically, the memorandum should:

- state what the commission would and would not be committing itself to by adopting the proposed language, including whether a state that rejects basic desert can still blame, demand acknowledgment of harm, or expect apology, given that skeptics about desert generally preserve other senses of responsibility;
- take Dennett's side of the argument seriously: his contention that a purely protective framework ends up needing a punitive enforcement mechanism standing behind it, and his broader worry that discarding desert-talk removes a constraint on state power rather than a license for it;
- show how the recommendation resolves both docketed cases, and whether it can do so without special pleading;
- separate the philosophical questions from the empirical ones, flagging every point at which the recommendation depends on the accuracy, stability, and known demographic biases of actuarial risk instruments rather than on argument;
- include one paragraph stating the strongest case against your recommendation in a form its proponents would recognize.

No footnotes, no reading list, and no quotations I would have to verify — I want the argument carried by the reasoning itself. Where you rely on how Dennett or Caruso frames a point, paraphrase rather than quote, and tell me plainly if the commissioners' memorandum, as I have described it, mischaracterizes either of them.
