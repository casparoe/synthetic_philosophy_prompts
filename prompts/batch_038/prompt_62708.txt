You are a philosopher of law with specialist expertise in mid-twentieth-century debates over legal moralism and the proper limits of the criminal law. Think the problem through carefully before answering, and begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that could generate the track record below rather than settling on the first principle that happens to fit.

I sit in a jurisprudence seminar whose tutor defends the criminal enforcement of morality in a broadly Devlin-like vein: on her account, the law may reach conduct that the reasonable member of the community regards with revulsion rather than mere dislike, subject to respecting privacy as far as the cohesion of society allows. Her rulings so far this term:

1. Repealing the offence covering consensual homosexual acts between adults in private — opposed; the repeal would be a mistake.
2. Creating a new offence of adultery — opposed; the conduct is condemned, but it belongs to the divorce courts, and enforcing it would mean searching bedrooms.
3. Creating a new offence of sexual contact with an animal, where the animal is uninjured and there are no witnesses — supported.
4. Extending the obscenity offence to private adult possession — opposed; she would confine it to material liable to reach children or to corrupt the young.
5. Creating a new offence of desecrating a grave, committed alone at night and noticed by no one — supported.
6. Decriminalising the private use of a currently prohibited narcotic by adults — opposed.
7. Repealing the offence of bigamy — opposed.
8. Criminalising a publication that a bare majority of the public finds merely distasteful — opposed; she insists the test is not a head-count, and that distaste falls short of the reaction the law should wait for.
9. Amending an existing offence simply because recent polling shows the moral climate has moved — opposed; she holds that the criminal law should change slowly and that a shift in opinion is not by itself a reason to change it.

The legislature is now weighing a bill to repeal a long-standing offence concerning the treatment of human remains. The sponsors describe the conduct as follows: it takes place entirely in private; every body involved was released under the deceased's written consent given while alive, together with the consent of the family; no living person is distressed by it; and three surveys over the last five years show that the public now regards the practice with curiosity and mild queasiness rather than horror.

Predict her verdict on this bill, and then:

(a) Lay out the distinct principles that are each consistent with all nine earlier rulings — I expect at least three to fit — and say which familiar candidates are already ruled out by particular verdicts, naming the verdict that does the ruling out.
(b) State what each surviving principle implies about the repeal bill, and mark precisely where they diverge.
(c) Give your best single prediction with reasons. Be explicit about an ambiguity in her notion of the reasonable member of the community: is that figure a proxy for actual public feeling, or a normative ideal whose reactions may outrun or lag the polls? Show how each reading changes the prediction, and which reading her verdicts in 8 and 9 support.
(d) Propose one or two further hypothetical bills that would discriminate among the principles that survive her verdict on this one, and give the verdict each principle predicts for them.
(e) Close with two or three sentences on where the Hart-style objections to enforcing morality bite hardest on this bill, and whether any surviving principle can absorb them.

Aim for roughly 800 words. Argue from the structure of her verdicts rather than from anything she may have said in print; you may invoke the general shape of the debate over legal moralism, but do not supply quotations, citations, or a reading list.
