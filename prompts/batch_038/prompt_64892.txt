So I'm giving the speech at the ribbon-cutting for a restored 1840s textil mill in my town — it's a local history museum now — and the board asked me because I taught history at the high school here for thirty years and grew up two streets from the mill yard. Five minutes, and the room will be townspeople plus maybe forty out-of-state donors.

The restoration money came from one guy, a fund manager, and he's hoping for a nice bit about the visionary founder whose grit built the place. I can't do that. I've taught Marx for three decades and I genuinely think the founder was mostly the lucky product of everything already sitting there when he showed up: the drop in the river, the Boston credit, what cotton was going for that decade, and four hundred farm daughters with no other wage work to walk to. He didn't make those conditions, he walked into them.

But I also refuse to get up there and imply the man was a puppet of the economic base. That's rude, it's a cartoon of what I believe, and it would burn the only chance this town gets at saying out loud the names of the women who worked the looms.

So: write the speech. It should be warm to the donors, say the founder's name with respect, and get a room of non-specialists to feel that the real story is bigger and stranger than one great man — without ever saying determinism, base and superstructure, or mode of production. And if you can pull that off without making the donor look like the villain in a story about capital, do it, because he'll be standing ten feet away.
