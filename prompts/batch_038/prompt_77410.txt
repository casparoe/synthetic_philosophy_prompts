I have been part of a small reading group for some years now — the sort that grew out of prediction-tournament threads and calibration exercises, though we have drifted considerably towards foundations lately — and last Thursday we got into an argument I have not been able to put down. We were discussing a member's forecast of 25% that a particular treaty would be signed before a stated deadline, and the familiar question arose: is that number a feature of the world or a feature of my friend's head? One of the group, who is doing a doctorate in philosophy of physics and whom I shall call R., was having none of the subjectivist answer. His move was an analogy, and it is the analogy I want audited. A chance, he said, is a disposition: the coin's propensity to land heads belongs to it in exactly the way that fragility belongs to a glass. The glass is fragile even if it is never struck and never breaks; likewise the atom has its chance of decaying within the hour whether or not anyone holds a scrap of evidence about it, and whether or not it ever decays. Nobody supposes that fragility is secretly a report on the observer's expectations, so nobody should suppose that chance is either. R. then drew two conclusions from this: first, that single-case chances are perfectly respectable and that the frequentist's insistence on a long-run collective or reference class is a pseudo-requirement; and second, that the subjectivist has the direction of explanation backwards — chances are out there in the world, and credences are correct insofar as they track them.

I want to know how far the analogy actually holds, because I suspect R. is borrowing more from the source domain than he is entitled to. Please give me the correspondence table first: what plays the role of the glass, of the striking, of the breaking, of the underlying structure that grounds fragility and makes it a real property rather than a redescription of its manifestations, of the ceteris paribus clause everyone quietly appends to dispositional claims, and of the test that reveals a disposition without consuming it. Then the disanalogies, but sorted — which ones bite against the first conclusion, which against the second, and which are merely aesthetic complaints R. could shrug off. Several pressure points are already nagging at me and I should like them taken up explicitly. (a) Fragility has a categorical base we know something about independently; what is the categorical base of a chance, and if the only candidate is the pattern of outcomes the chance was invoked to explain, has the analogy smuggled the frequencies back in at ground level? (b) Dispositions can be finked and masked — the protected glass that does not break when struck — and if chances can be masked too, what survives of any reliable connection between chance and frequency, which is the one thing the analogy was supposed to secure? (c) Fragility belongs to an object, whereas a propensity seems to belong to a whole experimental arrangement, what Popper's tradition calls the generating conditions; but arrangements are not objects, they are descriptions carved out of the world, and I worry that deciding which arrangement is the right one is the reference-class problem wearing a new hat. (d) The originator of the propensity programme, if I have read him correctly, glossed the propensity of a set of generating conditions in terms of the outcome frequencies that would obtain were those exact conditions repeated indefinitely — uncomfortably like a limiting frequency in disguise, which would mean the dispositional analogy is being enlisted to defend a single-case view its own author did not hold. (e) In the classical case the analogy looks weakest: fix the microstate of coin, air and table and deterministic mechanics settles the outcome outright, so the half-and-half number is doing nothing except reporting ignorance about initial conditions — and yet this is precisely where we are most confident that there is a stable, projectable value. Is the analogy salvageable only in the quantum case, and if so, is R. entitled to deploy it about coins and treaties as well? (f) Finally, even if it works, the analogy seems to deliver a scalar tendency rather than a structured measure: nothing in it yields additivity across disjunctions or any account of conditional chances, and it says nothing at all about why credences ought to align with chances — a bridge the subjectivist never needs, since for him the two are the same thing. That missing bridge strikes me as the real bill the analogy leaves unpaid.

So: which of R.'s two conclusions, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what does the analogy quietly import in each case — intrinsicality, causal efficacy, degree structure, context-independence? I should also like your view on whether a better source domain exists. If dispositions are the wrong vehicle for objective single-case chance, is there another — a force, a law-like constraint, a measure over possible initial conditions, a magnitude defined purely by the theoretical role it plays, as charge is defined by what it does — that carries the objectivity R. wants without importing the frequencies he is trying to escape? Say what each alternative would cost, and where it too would break. I should add that I am not trying to rescue subjectivism by any means: I have spent years internalising the discipline of putting numbers on my beliefs, and I would happily concede that chance is objective if the argument is sound. I simply want to know whether the fragility comparison is doing the argumentative work here, or merely decorating it.
