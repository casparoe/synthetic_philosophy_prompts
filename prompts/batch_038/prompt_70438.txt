I'm on a heritage committee in a small town and we've got a proper mess on our hands. There's a plaque and an annual wreath-laying tied to a story everyone here grew up with — during the war a local family hid a downed airman, and the people who tell it are the ones whose grandparents were in the room when it happened, so it's thier story in a way none of us can match. An archivist has now been through the deposited estate papers and the parish records and come out with a version that doesn't line up: a date wrong, a name wrong, and one bit of local heroism that reads more like a piece of opportunism. The chair has a line he reaches for at every meeting, and it always lands: a community's memory is eyewitness testimony, the documentary record is forensic evidence, and everyone knows eyewitnesses are unreliable while the lab results aren't — so memory gives way, end of story. Two or three of us feel that's too quick but we can't say why, and he sounds so decisive that we look like we're just defending the nice version.

You're a philosopher who has worked on memory, testimony and historiography and has heard this arguement dressed up a hundred different ways. Think it through properly first — brainstorm in your head, build the strongest case for the chair as well as against him, and don't send me any of that — then give me only the finished audit, in British English: exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no summing-up paragraph at the end.

1. What maps onto what (bare pairs are fine here).
2. The disanalogies that genuinely bite for our decision.
3. The ones that look like disanalogies but don't actually do any work.
4. Whether the chair's conclusion survives 2 and 3 — and if only partly, which part of it survives.
5. A better analogy for the memory-versus-record relation, if there is one, and what it lets you conclude that his doesn't.
