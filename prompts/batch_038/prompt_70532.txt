For several years I have been assembling a view about when it is legitimate to say that natural selection is operating on one level of biological organization rather than another, and I have accumulated a set of verdicts about particular cases that I cannot reconcile with one another. What follows are my considered reactions, stated as plainly as I can manage; some are confident, two of them pull in opposite directions, and I am aware that at least one may simply be wrong.

1. A mouse carrying a driving haplotype that kills the sperm bearing its rival allele and thereby spreads even though carriers sire fewer offspring and are prone to sterility: here I am confident that the haplotype, not the mouse, is the object of selection. What happens to the mouse population is a side effect that no organism-level account can render adaptive.

2. A sterile worker insect that devotes herself to rearing sisters, in a colony where reproduction is monopolized by one or a few females: I am willing to say the colony is a unit of selection, and I am willing to say this despite knowing that workers police one another and occasionally lay male eggs. The policing, to my ear, does not show that talk of the colony is a façon de parler; it shows the colony is held together by work.

3. A mixed bacterial biofilm in which a non-producing lineage withholds the public good it benefits from, and which therefore outcompetes producers within the same patch: here I say there is no group-level unit at all. Even when the patch structure temporarily favors groups of cooperators, I regard any claim that the group is the beneficiary as bookkeeping, not biology.

4. A cell lineage inside a mammalian body that acquires a mutation releasing it from contact inhibition, out-prolifering its neighbors at the eventual cost of the host: I say selection is real here, that the cell lineage is the unit, and that the organism has ceased to function as one.

5. A population of free-living bacteria, structured into clonal patches, in which a lineage has arisen that resists a locally abundant phage at a measurable growth cost, and which spreads only because patches containing it survive exposure that eliminates patches without it: structurally this looks to me like the same situation as the previous case, yet I say that here the population is a genuine higher-level unit and the individual cell is not. I cannot say what makes the two come out differently for me.

6. An obligate intracellular symbiont with a radically reduced genome, no recombination, an extreme sex ratio bias, and transmission exclusively through the female germline: I say that neither the symbiont nor the host is any longer a unit in its own right, that there is a single fused unit, and that this is not a redescription of the facts but the correct statement of them.

7. A dioecious population in which recombination between the sex chromosomes is suppressed across most of their length, so that a locus on the non-recombining chromosome is permanently associated with one sex and can spread alleles that benefit that sex at a cost to the whole: I say the chromosome has become a unit with interests of its own, even though nothing about its population-genetic treatment differs from an ordinary low-recombination region, where I would say no such thing.

8. Two lineages that have recently entered a mutualism, each still separately reproducing, whose fitnesses have become so correlated that neither can be assigned a stable independent contribution: I say that in such a case there is a fact about which, if either, is the unit, and that selection will settle the question on a timescale too long for anyone to measure. I have no armchair verdict, and I am not embarrassed by that.

What I would like is the following. First, state the principle, or the small number of candidate principles, that could generate these verdicts, in terms precise enough to be applied to a case I have not described: I want conditions on variance in reproductive success, on the transmission of fitness from one generation of candidate units to the next, on the partitioning of that variance into within-unit and between-unit components, and on the degree to which the fates of lower-level replicators are correlated rather than opposed. A principle that merely assigns labels to my eight cases in the order I gave them is not what I am after. Second, tell me what distinguishes, or whether anything does distinguish, the tumor cell lineage from the phage-resistant bacterial population, and likewise the driving haplotype from the suppressed sex chromosome, since each pair contains one case I call a genuine unit and one I do not. Third, describe three cases of your own that I have not listed, on which the recommended principle makes a definite prediction, and say plainly where you expect my intuition to resist that prediction. Fourth, identify which of my verdicts any reasonably simple principle must overturn, and argue for overturning that one rather than another: if the choice is between a principle that saves all eight at the price of a clause for each, and a principle that saves six with two conditions, I want to know which verdict is the cheapest to give up and why. Finally, I would like an explicit statement of whether my pattern of reactions is best read as tracking the amount of recombination and shared transmission between the candidate units, or as tracking the extent to which their fitnesses have been made to coincide, since I suspect I have been sliding between those two ideas without noticing it, and I suspect that one of my eight verdicts is a symptom of the slide.
