I convene a funded seminar series on the methodology of the quest for the historical Jesus — five workshops over two years, mixed company of New Testament scholars, historians of Second Temple Judaism, and a couple of philosophers of history. The methodological argument at the centre of the series has not moved in the two years we have been running it, and I now have to decide whether to extend it for a further cycle or to reframe it. I would like a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on the merits, and I would like the diagnosis to bear directly on that decision.

Here is the shape of the impasse as I see it. One camp holds that the traditional criteria — dissimilarity in its double form, embarrassment, multiple attestation, coherence with what has already been established, and the linguistic and environmental tests involving Aramaic features and Palestinian setting — remain the only way to move from the tradition to the man behind it, and that the standard objections call for refinement rather than abandonment. The other camp holds that the criteria are not merely imperfect but jointly incoherent: that dissimilarity is self-defeating, since it filters out precisely what Jesus would have shared with his Jewish environment and what the early movement would have preserved from him; that it cannot be combined with coherence without contradiction; that multiple attestation is a claim about the independence of sources rather than about the historicity of an item; and that the whole apparatus presupposes a model of the tradition as a series of stratified, recoverable layers which form criticism never established and which work on oral tradition and social memory has since undermined. On this second view, memory was fluid and interpretive from the outset, so there is no pre-communal residue to peel back to.

What puzzles me is that both camps work from the same material: the synoptic gospels, Paul's letters, Josephus, and the same comparative corpus of Second Temple Jewish literature. They agree about the texts, they agree about the dating and the general character of the tradition, and they use the same vocabulary. Every argument on both sides is decades old and universally known. Nobody budges, and the disagreements never get sharper — they simply recur in the same form at each meeting.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test and to reject if it is wrong, is that the two sides are not disagreeing about Jesus at all, but about what the output of an argument of this kind would be: one side is trying to produce a binary verdict of authenticity for individual items, the other is trying to describe the conditions under which any item could bear warrant at all, and the word authentic is doing different work in each case. On this reading the dispute is a question in the epistemology of historical testimony that has been conducted in the vocabulary of first-century Galilee.

Please tell me whether that hypothesis is right, or whether the more accurate diagnosis is something else — a purely verbal dispute about the term, a difference in evidential standards, a difference about which items even count as data for the inquiry, two distinct questions pursued under a single heading, or a question that is ill-posed rather than merely hard. Ground the diagnosis in what each side actually argues, not in a summary of their labels.

Then give me three things. First, a controlled test of your diagnosis. Consider two schematic items: a logion unlike anything in Second Temple Judaism and unlike anything in the early churches, singly attested, with no linguistic or environmental marks; and a logion thoroughly at home in the movement's preaching, attested across independent streams, carrying Semitic syntactic features and Palestinian detail. Does your diagnosis predict where the two camps part company on these cases, and does the case dissolve the disagreement or merely relocate it? Second, what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a probabilistic reconstruction of the inference from a tradition's properties to an event's properties, a reframing of the target of inquiry, a concession that the question is misdescribed, or something else — and why would that count as progress rather than as one side winning by redefinition? Third, an honest answer about whether any progress should be expected at all, and what you would tell me about the seminar: extend it as it stands, redirect it toward a question the two camps can actually share, or close it.

Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not attempt to reconstruct particular authors' positions by name; work from the arguments as I have set them out together with what you know of the debate in general terms.
