I co-host a small podcast aimed at working software engineers. The audience is technical but has no training in physics or philosophy; we record 35-minute episodes and we handle the first two minutes of setup ourselves, so a guest never has to explain the basics.

Next month's guest is a philosopher of physics who defends the decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule in the Everett interpretation: the program that starts from premises about the preferences of a rational agent facing quantum bets and concludes that such an agent must weight outcomes by squared amplitude. I have read enough secondary discussion to follow the shape of the argument, but I will not be able to check his mathematics live, so I need questions whose answers I can evaluate as an interviewer without faking comprehension.

One feature of his particular version of the view, which I want the interview to exploit rather than tiptoe around: when pressed with the branch-counting objection — the thought that if every branch is equally real, an agent should weight branches equally rather than by amplitude — he does not argue that counting yields the wrong numbers. He argues that there is no well-defined number of branches to count, because branch individuation is vague and depends on a choice of coarse-graining, so the objection has no determinate content. He also has a habit of defusing hard questions by relocating the dispute: he says the disagreement is really about metaphysical bookkeeping, not about the physics, and that no working physicist is troubled by any of it.

Please write me ten interview questions, ordered so that difficulty rises across the episode and the hardest one comes last. For each question give me four things:

1. a one-sentence plain-language setup I can read aloud to orient the audience;
2. the question itself, no longer than about sixty words, phrased the way I would actually speak it;
3. a short note telling me the answer I should expect from him, in a phrase or two, so I can recognize it when I hear it;
4. a follow-up keyed to exactly that expected answer — the specific next pressure point, not a generic request to elaborate.

Between them, the ten questions must cover the following. Two of them should work the branch-counting thread described above, and one of those two should ask whether the same vagueness he appeals to also undermines the amplitude weights he wants to keep. One should pursue the circularity charge — whether the premises already encode the Born rule — and ask him to name the single premise doing the most work and to say what survives if it is dropped; I have read that a group of quantum-information researchers produced a joint critique to roughly that effect, and I could not follow the details, so I need a version of this question I can ask without misstating their argument. One should target the gap between what an agent should bet and what an observer should expect to see: the derivation is about decisions, but what physicists care about is that recorded frequencies come out right, and I want to know whether he has earned that. One should be about confirmation — if every outcome occurs, what could count as evidence for or against the theory, and on his account can a long run of Born-rule-violating observations ever be more than merely unusual in this branch. One should put to him the rival route that treats probability as self-locating uncertainty in the interval between a measurement happening and the observer looking at the result, and ask whether that makes decision theory unnecessary or whether the two are combinable. One should ask about the competing derivation that gets Born weights from symmetries of entangled states rather than from rational preference — I have seen it called envariance — and whether it is a rival, a special case, or something that quietly relies on premises he would reject. One should test robustness: what happens to the derivation if the agent does not maximize expected utility, for instance because she is ambiguity-averse, or if her preferences over rewards are not the ones the proof assumes. One should close the escape route mentioned above, so that relocating the dispute to metaphysics does not end the exchange. And one should be the closer, asking what would have to happen for him to abandon the derivation.

Finally, an analogy from my own work that I would like turned into one of the questions. In my day job I run deterministic simulations replicated across many identical machines, each seeded differently, with every run recorded. If someone asked me why the outcomes have the frequencies they do, the honest answer is that there is a distribution over the seeds and the frequencies follow from it. In the Everett case, as I understand it, there is no seed distribution: the amplitudes are just sitting in the deterministic dynamics. Build a question that puts this comparison to him and asks what, in his picture, plays the role that the seed distribution plays in mine. Separately, outside the question list, tell me in a few sentences where that analogy breaks down, so that I am not corrected on air for having offered a bad one.

Constraints: no biographical questions and nothing he has certainly been asked on every previous appearance; do not ask him to recommend papers, books, or further reading; do not build any question on a claim about what another researcher or the physics community at large believes unless I have stated that claim above; if a question cannot be phrased without a technical term, keep the term but give me a one-line gloss to read immediately before it. At the end, tell me which two questions to cut if we run long.
