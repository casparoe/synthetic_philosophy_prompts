I'm writing a seminar paper on the modal argument for property dualism, and I have grown suspicious of a slogan my professor leans on constantly: "conceivability entails possibility." He has now deployed it twice, in ways I cannot reconcile.

First use. A student objected that Kripkean a posteriori necessities wreck the inference — one can coherently suppose that water is not H2O, or that Hesperus is not Phosphorus, and yet those situations are impossible — and my professor waved the objection away on the grounds that it presupposes a vulgar notion of conceivability, since on the right notion conceivability does entail possibility.

Second use, two weeks later, on an unrelated topic. A student argued that it is metaphysically possible for someone to have been born to different parents, and my professor rejected it with: you cannot even conceive of that, so it is not possible.

Note that the second use is not the first one run backwards. From "if conceivable, then possible" nothing follows about what is ruled out by inconceivability, so either he is invoking two different principles or one of the two uses is simply invalid.

Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise principles that this slogan could be expressing, ordered from the trivial to the radical. For each one, tell me: what sort of conceivability is in play (vivid mental imagery, absence of any contradiction one can detect, coherence of a complete description, what survives idealized rational reflection with unbounded cognitive resources); what sort of possibility is the target (epistemic, conceptual, metaphysical, or something indexed to the a priori truths); whether the principle is one-way or also licenses the step from inconceivability to impossibility; and whether it is true, false, or true only because it fails to say anything at all. Then answer my actual questions: which reading does the first use require if it is to survive the Kripkean counterexample, is there any such reading that does not beg the question against the objection to dualism, which reading does the second use require, and is there a single principle that can do both jobs? Finally, say which of these readings people usually have in mind when they repeat the slogan casually and without argument.

Do not hedge or leave the selection to me. Commit to a verdict on each reading, commit to an overall judgment about whether my professor is entitled to the slogan in either place, and give your reasons. I am not looking for a reading list or pointers to the literature — I want the analysis itself. Please write in American English.
