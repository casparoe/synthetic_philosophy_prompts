I'm about halfway through a popular book on corruption — the trade-press kind, by someone who's done years of fieldwork rather than an academic monograph — and there's a passage early on that tries to settle what corruption even is. I typed it out because I keep going back to it:

'A bribe is just a gift you have to hide. That is the entire test. If a transfer of value can be written down, published, and looked at by everybody without embarrassment, then it is business, or politics, or hospitality; if it has to be handed over in an envelope, it is corruption. The anthropologists who studied the great gift economies — societies with no state apparatus and no bureaucracy to speak of — found whole worlds organised by the obligation to give, to receive and to reciprocate, and nobody in them supposed they were doing anything corrupt. There was no word for corruption because there was nothing there to name. The concept arrives only with the modern state, which insists on a wall between an official's public role and his private life and then declares that anything crossing the wall is a scandal. So when the big anti-corruption agencies define corruption as the misuse of a position of trust for private gain, and rank every country in the world against it, they are not reporting a universal moral fact. They are exporting a historically recent and culturally specific line and calling it human nature. The honest conclusion is not that some countries are corrupt and others clean. It is that they draw the line in different places.'

How much of that survives contact with someone who actually works on this? Go through it claim by claim and tell me what a specialist would accept, what they'd shrug at as tolerable popularization, and what is just wrong. Three things I especially want pushed on:

1. Is secrecy really the line between a gift and a bribe, or a heuristic that breaks exactly where it matters? I'm thinking of campaign donations, lobbying, the revolving door, and the case where a capture happens by rewriting the rules so the whole thing is legal and on the record — all public, all disclosed, and still called corrupt by plenty of people.
2. Is the gift-exchange material being used fairly, or has the book turned Mauss into something he wouldn't recognise? And what should I make of the move from a society lacking a word for something to there being nothing there to name?
3. Does the last sentence follow from the true parts? Is there a middle position between "universal moral fact" and "they just draw the line differently" that the book is skipping, and if so what is it?

Related: the passage seems to want corruption to be a purely descriptive thing it can locate historically, but then it slides into saying the ranking agencies are smuggling in morality. Can the concept be defined without any moral loading at all, or is it essentially evaluative — does the book get to have both?

I'm asking because I'm inclinded to agree with the ending and I'd like to know whether I'm agreeing for a good reason or just because it's rhetorically satisfying. Flag anything in there that's a stock move in this literature being presented as a discovery. No reading list or citations needed — I just want to know what to believe.
