Please take on the role of a philosopher of causation who has also spent serious time on how historians argue about causes, and think this through carefully before answering: begin by listing the candidate concepts you would consider, rule each one in or out, and only then give me your verdict. No reading list or citations, please — I want the conceptual carving, not the bibliography.

Last night a friend and I argued for three hours without getting anywhere, and I now suspect we were fighting over a structure neither of us could name. The pattern is this. He says that if the assassin's car had not stalled on that particular corner in Sarajevo in 1914, there would have been no world war. I say that gets the causation wrong, because within a year or two some other incident would have lit the same fuse; the great powers were already aimed at one another and any of a dozen sparks would have done it. He replies that the war did in fact follow the stalled car, so the car caused it. I reply that by the same standard I could name twenty other events, any one of which would have produced the war, so on his account nothing caused it. We repeat the identical exchange about Waterloo — if the marshal who was supposed to come up in time had not been delayed, and so on. Note what we are *not* disagreeing about: neither of us thinks the outcome was forced, and we both accept that the alternative routes existed and were never taken.

So — is there an established concept for this configuration: an actual link in a historical chain that was the only route operated, whose removal would nevertheless have left the end point unchanged, because enough independent alternatives were available? I have read enough to know there are terms for two causes each being sufficient on their own, for cases where a backup cause exists but is blocked, and for the difference between something that brings an event about and something that merely sets the stage. None of those seems to fit, since here the route taken was genuinely the one that operated and yet was, in some sense, arbitrary. What I want is: (a) the nearest established concepts and exactly how my case departs from each; (b) whether any single term covers the departure; and (c) if not, the definition you would propose, together with boundary cases — at what point does an outcome stop being robust in this way and become genuinely fragile, so that the stalled car really was the difference-maker? A bare label will not satisfy me. I want to know where the edges of the phenomenon lie, and whether the argument I described was substantive or merely verbal.
