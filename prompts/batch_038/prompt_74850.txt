The background is a running dispute in my friend group about population decline. Several of us are broadly pronatalist, and one of them insists that the case for having more children, or for policies that produce more people, rests on the simple claim that more happy people means a better world. I tried to work out that simple claim and ended up somewhere I cannot stay. I built an argument intended to show that adding happy people never makes things better, expecting to arrive at a modest conclusion I could live with. Instead it appears to prove far too much: it seems to show that a flourishing far future is worth exactly nothing, so that nothing is lost if it never happens. I will call the crucial move the Neutrality Ledger, because it treats each new person as an entry of zero.

(1) The only way for the world to get better is for things to get better for somebody: if a change is neither better nor worse for anyone, it is not an improvement.
(2) Creating a person whose life will be worth living is not better for that person than not creating them, since the comparison would have to be made against a situation they are not in, and there is no subject for whom never existing could be a benefit or a deprivation.
(3) By the same token, creating them is not worse for them.
(4) Suppose the addition satisfies what I will call the Isolation Clause: the new person shares no land, food, time, or attention with anyone already alive, displaces nobody who would otherwise have existed, and has no effect on anyone else's projects, relationships, or prospects. I secure this by stipulating hermits in separate valleys who never meet, trade, or hear of one another, and I do not think I give anything up by that stipulation, since my friend's claim was about happy people as such.
(5) So adding one such flourishing person is neither better nor worse for anyone: not for them, by (2) and (3), and not for anyone else, by (4). By (1), therefore, it is not an improvement. It is a neutral addition, worth zero.
(6) A ledger of zeros sums to zero. If I make such additions one after another and each leaves the world's value exactly where it was, then there is no point in the sequence at which that value rises, and if it rises at no point it cannot have risen overall. This strikes me as arithmetic rather than ethics.
(7) Hence a world containing one flourishing person is exactly as good as a world containing a trillion flourishing persons, all isolated in the sense of (4).
(8) But then the flourishing trillion-person future is no better than its never occurring, so there is nothing to regret in extinction and no reason, on these grounds, to prefer that humanity continue. I cannot accept this.
(9) Therefore (1) is false: a world can be improved without anything getting better for anybody.

I have already attempted one repair and had to withdraw it. I tried saying at (6) that value comes in lumps: that adding a thousand people all at once is an improvement even though adding them one at a time is neutral at every step. But the endpoint of the two processes is the very same world, with the same people living the same isolated lives, so if the lump route ends up better than the stepwise route ends up, then how good a world is depends on the order in which it was assembled. That seems worse than the conclusion I was trying to escape, because I had assumed that goodness is a property of a world and not of a route to it.
