So I'm in an informal reading group with a couple of people from my department (history of political thought, mostly early modern but we decided to go backwards for once) and we've been slogging through book 6 of Polybius's Histories in one of those Victorian English translations. We got through the six-constitutions business fine, but we hit a wall in chapters 9 and 10 that we could not resolve after an hour and a half of arguing, and our group includes a guy who insists Polybius is basically a Stoic determinist in disguise, which I don't buy but can't really refute either. Here's the first bit, the end of ch. 9, right after he's described democracy rotting into mob rule and the mob finding itself a new master:

"This is the regular cycle of constitutional revolutions, and the natural order in which constitutions change, are transformed, and return again to their original stage. If a man have a clear grasp of these principles he may perhaps make a mistake as to the dates at which this or that will happen to a particular constitution; but he will rarely be entirely mistaken as to the stage of growth or decay at which it has arrived, or as to the point at which it will undergo some revolutionary change. However, it is in the case of the Roman constitution that this method of inquiry will most fully teach us its formation, its growth, and zenith, as well as the changes awaiting it in the future; for this, if any constitution ever did, owed, as I said just now, its original foundation and growth to natural causes, and to natural causes will owe its decay."

Then immediately in ch. 10 he brings in Lycurgus like this:

"That statesman was fully aware that all those changes which I have enumerated come about by an undeviating law of nature; and reflected that every form of government that was unmixed, and rested on one species of power, was unstable; because it was swiftly perverted into that particular form of evil peculiar to it and inherent in its nature. For just as rust is the natural dissolvent of iron, wood-worms and grubs to timber, by which they are destroyed without any external injury, but by that which is engendered in themselves; so in each constitution there is naturally engendered a particular vice inseparable from it [...] Lycurgus, I say, saw all this, and accordingly combined together all the excellences and distinctive features of the best constitutions, that no part should become unduly predominant, and be perverted into its kindred vice; and that, each power being checked by the others, no one part should turn the scale or decisively out-balance the others; but that, by being accurately adjusted and in exact equilibrium, the whole might remain long steady like a ship sailing close to the wind. [...] The result of this combination has been that the Lacedaemonians retained their freedom for the longest period of any people with which we are acquainted."

Can you help me figure out what's actually going on here, because the two chapters seem to be pulling in opposite directions and I can't tell if it's Polybius being confused or me. If constitutional change happens by an "undeviating law of nature," in what sense is Lycurgus's foresight supposed to be an escape from it rather than just another instance of it — is he saying a clever legislator can genuinely step outside the cycle, or only that the mixed constitution runs the same cycle very slowly, with the rust/woodworm analogy doing the work of showing the decay is internal and therefore only postponable? And what is the status of the "law of nature" claim anyway: a metaphysical necessity, or a diagnostic heuristic (note that he immediately cashes it out as a claim about prediction, that you'll rarely be wrong about the stage of growth or decay)? I keep tripping on the fact that he applies the naturalist framework to Rome too — Rome's constitution "owed its original foundation and growth to natural causes, and to natural causes will owe its decay" — which seems to say the mixed constitution doesn't get immunity at all, and if so what exactly is the payoff of Sparta's balance of powers beyond buying time. I also have a vague memory that later in book 6 he says something about the Roman constitution having reached its peak and starting to slide toward mob rule in his own lifetime, though I could be misremembering or mangling where that shows up, so tell me if that helps or makes it worse. I'm not after references to scholarship on this, I mostly can't chase them down anyway — I want your reading of the text in front of us, and I'd rather you tell me straight if the tension is only apparent and there's a clean way to hold both chapters together.
