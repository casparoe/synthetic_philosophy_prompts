I cover science and ideas for a weekly and I've got a piece due Friday that started life as a story about simultaneous invention — the fact that calculus, oxygen, the telephone, the transformer, and arguably the last two big leaps in machine learning all turned up in two or more places inside a few months of each other, which makes the singular-genius framing my editors always want feel pretty dodgy. Somewhere in the reporting I attributed the "great man theory of history" to Carlyle, and my fact-checker, bless him, said I should probably actually read Carlyle instead of quoting him from other people's summaries. So I did. Or I started to. Now I'm stuck on the opening of the first lecture of *On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History* (delivered May 1840, published 1841) and I can't get past it, because every gloss I read treats it as a flat claim that great men make history, and the sentence itself keeps not looking like that. Here's the first paragraph:

> We have undertaken to discourse here for a little on Great Men, their manner of appearance in our world's business, how they have shaped themselves in the world's history, what ideas men formed of them, what work they did;—on Heroes, namely, and on their reception and performance; what I call Hero-worship and the Heroic in human affairs. Too evidently this is a large topic; deserving quite other treatment than we can expect to give it at present. A large topic; indeed, an illimitable one; wide as Universal History itself. For, as I take it, Universal History, the history of what man has accomplished in this world, is at bottom the History of the Great Men who have worked here. They were the leaders of men, these great ones; the modellers, patterns, and in a wide sense creators, of whatsoever the general mass of men contrived to do or to attain; all things that we see standing accomplished in the world are properly the outer material result, the practical realisation and embodiment, of Thoughts that dwelt in the Great Men sent into the world: the soul of the whole world's history, it may justly be considered, were the history of these. Too clearly it is a topic we shall do no justice to in this place!

and a few sentences later, in the next paragraph, there's this about the same figure:

> He is the living light-fountain, which it is good and pleasant to be near. The light which enlightens, which has enlightened the darkness of the world; and this not as a kindled lamp only, but rather as a natural luminary shining by the gift of Heaven; a flowing light-fountain, as I say, of native original insight, of manhood and heroic nobleness;—in whose radiance all souls feel that it is well with them.

So here's what I need, and please be blunt if I've misparsed things. What is "at bottom" doing in that central sentence — is Carlyle making a causal claim about what produces events, or a claim about what the true subject matter of history is? Because if it's the second, the multiple-discovery cases I'm reporting don't actually refute him and I need to know that before I write a sentence implying they do. Relatedly, what is he conceding with "in a wide sense creators" — is that hedge quietly admitting that the hero isn't the efficient cause, with the "general mass of men" doing the actual contriving while the hero only supplies the thought? If the mass executes and the hero thinks, is this even a theory of power, or is it a theory of how ideas get transmitted — and does that leave the hero substitutable, i.e. would Carlyle have to accept that someone else could have had the thought so long as somebody had it? Then there's the grammar I can't crack: "the soul of the whole world's history, it may justly be considered, were the history of these" — why the subjunctive "were," and is "it may justly be considered" Carlyle hedging or Carlyle being mock-modest about something he takes to be certain? That distinction decides whether I describe him in print as a metaphysician about history or as a guy making a methodological recommendation to historians, and I genuinely can't tell. Also the lamp-versus-luminary line: I suspect "not as a kindled lamp only" is aimed at some account of genius as assembled or lit-from-outside rather than shining on its own, but I may be reading way too much into a bit of Victorian decoration, so tell me what that contrast is actually doing if anything.

Two more things, then I'll let you go. My editor wants me to fold in the familiar counterfactual about whether there'd have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler, and my instinct is that that's a different question from the multiples one — one is about a discovery that several people were converging on anyway, the other is about a single political decision point that nobody else was positioned to make — and I want to know whether anything in this passage gives Carlyle a grip on the second kind of case or only on the first. And finally, give me the one-sentence paraphrase I can fairly print: specifically, am I allowed to say Carlyle was committed to the counterfactual that without the great man the achievement wouldn't have happened, since that's the version of the claim my evidence needs in order to bite? If he isn't committed to it, say so plainly and tell me what my story's real target is instead. No reading lists, please — I've got about six hours and the fact-checker only needs to know I haven't mangled the text.
