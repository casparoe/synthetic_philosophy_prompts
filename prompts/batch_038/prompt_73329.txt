I'm a staff software engineer at a company that builds implantable neural prosthetics. What we ship today is unglamorous: when a small subregion has been damaged by stroke or injury, we learn that region's input-output mapping from months of recordings and replace its function with a synthetic module, so the signals arriving produce approximately the signals that used to come out. The roadmap I'm working on is more aggressive, and it is the part that has gotten us into arguments. Elective, incremental replacement of healthy tissue, one module per month, with the patient awake, talking, and reporting throughout a multi-year schedule. Our counsel has asked me to help draft the informed-consent language, and there is a sentence I cannot write: what the patient is guaranteed to survive. My training is in distributed systems, so I can specify exactly what the device does, but I have no idea what would count as a correct answer to the question it raises, and I would rather know that before I start reading theories of personal identity, which I assume is the name of the relevant literature.

Our device has features that I suspect the standard thought experiments were not built to handle, and I want requirements that a theory cannot dodge by idealizing them away. First, fidelity is statistical rather than exact: the module reproduces the tissue's behavior within a measured tolerance, and the tolerance that counts as good enough plausibly differs by region, since matching a visual area closely may be a different matter from matching something involved in autobiographical memory. Second, modules are patchable after implant, so a patient's functioning can change with a firmware update and no surgery; because patches are written once and shipped to everyone, two patients' synthetic parts can drift toward being more similar to each other than to their own earlier tissue. Third, the implant takes periodic state snapshots and can restore one, and restore-into-a-second-body is at least conceivable, which gives us a branching case in which one branch is the original tissue and the other a restored copy, rather than two equal halves. Fourth, the synthetic internals are opaque: we can verify outputs but never confirm that the internal states resemble the biological ones they replaced, since the same function can be realized in a completely different format. Fifth, partial states are permanent. Plenty of patients will sit at something like thirty percent synthetic for the rest of their lives, and some will die mid-schedule, so whatever an account says has to apply to intermediate fractions and not only to the endpoints.

So: write the desiderata, the specification any account of whether the patient survives this procedure must meet for me to be able to use it in a consent document and in a regulatory submission about what restoring a snapshot does to the person who was snapshotted. Exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two: the requirement, the case or consideration that forces it (yours or mine), and a tag saying whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of one particular camp. Make sure the set covers the distinctions a theory must not collapse, including the difference between being the same person and whatever actually makes survival worth wanting, between a relation that can branch and one that cannot, between the gradual schedule and installing every module at once under anesthesia, and between the patient and the organism. Include at least one demand that looks reasonable but is really just one side's prejudice, such as insisting there must always be a determinate answer, and say why it is a prejudice. For at most one item, state outright that it cannot be jointly satisfied with another item on the list and name the other; that is the part I most want to see. No introduction, no conclusion, and no scorecard of existing views. I will read the contenders afterward and score them myself against whatever list you give me.
