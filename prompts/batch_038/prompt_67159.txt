I run a year-long seminar on transitional justice and my two strongest students have landed in camps that keep talking past each other, and now I've talked them into a good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want you to write the document they should be able to put their names to.

One of them is a hardline retributivist. She thinks a state that trades prosecution for amnesty does something that no amount of later good outcomes can undo, because the duty to prosecute is owed to victims as a matter of their standing and not as an instrument for deterrence or peacebuilding. A truth commission that records suffering but punishes nobody, on her view, is not a partial satisfaction of that duty — it's a substitution of the state's interest in closure for the victim's right to see the wrong adjudicated.

The other is what he calls a tragic-choice pragmatist. He thinks the only honest comparison at a transition point is between the outcomes that were realistically on the table, and that in the cases everybody cites the alternative to a negotiated amnesty was not a clean set of indictments but several more years of war. Refusing to bargain is, on his view, itself a way of killing people, and the retributivist's clean hands are paid for by other people's bodies.

Give me:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign — about what amnesty actually bought and cost in transitions, about what victims are owed, about what a truth commission is and is not capable of doing, and about which of their disagreements turn out to be about the word justice rather than about substance. Flag those verbal ones explicitly and say why.
2. A disagreements table with columns for the issue, the retributivist's own preferred wording of her position, the pragmatist's own preferred wording of his, and whether the gap is substantive or merely expressive. Neither wording should be a strawman the other side would reject.
3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling the matter would actually move at least one of them. For each, name the case, argument, or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, before the answer.

Then I want the two of them to fight over one specific design, because that's where I think the real content is. Not blanket amnesty and not blanket prosecution, but individualized conditional amnesty: granted case by case only on full disclosure of the politically motivated act, void if the disclosure turns out to be false, and paired with what I'll call a standing condition — the amnesty fails for any crime where an identifiable victim objects, and that objection can't be outvoted by a commission or by a majority of the country. Have them say whether the standing condition is a genuine third option that dissolves part of the fight, or a fig leaf that just relocates it. He thinks it makes any deal unsignable, since no armed group disarms into a veto held by the people it harmed. She thinks its a concession that lets the worst offenders buy their way out with a sufficiently detailed confession.

I don't want citations or a reading list, and don't invent sources. Where a claim is empirical and contested, just say so and give each side's prior.
