ok so i run my school's ethics club — like 12 of us, mostly juniors — and every couple of weeks we get into a fight about something. This term it's whether the numbers count. You can save five strangers or one stranger, can't do both: do you have to save the five? Some of us have read Taurek, who says no, the five don't automatically win, you owe everyone an equal chance, maybe flip a coin. Some of us think that's insane and some of us think it's obviously right. The problem is that when we argue, it keeps turning out that half the club doesn't actually disagree about the numbers at all — they disagree about something upstream, and then everyone just yells labels at each other.

So I want to build a quiz: "which numbers-person are you, actually." It should sort us by what we say about cases, not by what we call ourselves. 9 or 10 questions, each one a short concrete scenario with 2 or 3 options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto a leaf. Nobody should need to know any terminology to answer it — no words like aggregation, contractualism, or "separateness of persons" anywhere in the questions themselves, and no question that just asks "do you think more people is better?" We meet in a classroom for 40 minutes so keep the scenarios short enough to read out loud.

Here's the angle I want, please build the tree around these instead of around the usual textbook map:

- Several questions have to be ones where "save the many" and "flip a coin" come apart for reasons that have nothing to do with how many people there are. Like: five people each lose a finger versus one person dies. Or: five bad headaches cured versus one life saved. Or: the five are the people who set the fire. Or: the one is your little sister and the five are strangers. I want to find out which of these my clubmates are treating as the same question and which ones they're splitting apart.
- At least one has to be a gamble rather than a sure thing — like a 50% chance of saving five versus a certain saving of one, or whether you'd accept a lottery weighted toward the bigger group — because I strongly suspect half of us are consistent in the certain cases and a total mess in the risk cases.
- At least one should be many versus many where it's close, like 5 on one roof and 4 on another. I think the coin flip gets weird there and I want a question that exposes whether people actually hold the view or just like the vibe of it.
- One should compare inside a person to across people: one person who'd lose five fingers versus five people who'd each lose one finger.

The leaves have to include at least: Taurek's equal-chance/coin-flip view; the straight "save the many, more good is more good" view; a view where numbers only matter when the harms are comparable in kind, so the headache case comes out differently from the finger case; a Kamm-style view where numbers matter because of what each of the five would individually be denied rather than because five is a bigger total; a Scanlon-style view where each of the five has an equally strong complaint so the five win, but a million headaches still don't outweigh one death; the weighted-lottery view; and at least one cell where you think nobody actually is. Mark that one unoccupied and say why — is it incoherent, just unattractive, or has nobody bothered to think of it? If my one-line glosses on Kamm or Scanlon are wrong, correct me and write the leaf with a description that's accurate instead of using my phrasing.

Also flag which two leaves your quiz genuinely can't seperate with these questions, rather than pretending it sorts everybody cleanly. I'd rather know where the instrument is blind.

Last thing, and I mean it: don't just hand me the instrument and walk away. Answer your own quiz honestly and tell me which leaf you land on and why — pick one, commit to it, give me your reasons, including the specific case that's hardest for your leaf and what you say about it. No "well it depends on your intuitions" hedging. We'll read your answer out loud at the next meeting and argue with it, so give us something to argue with.
