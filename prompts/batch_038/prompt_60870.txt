Im working through the famine debates — the "natural disaster or political crime" argument over Bengal, Ireland, the Great Leap — and I keep getting pushed back to this bit of Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations (1776), Book IV ch. V, the digression on the corn trade. Its 250 years old and the prose is doing something I cant quite pin down:

> "The interest of the inland dealer, and that of the great body of the people, how opposite soever they may at first sight appear, are, even in years of the greatest scarcity, exactly the same. It is his interest to raise the price of his corn as high as the real scarcity of the season requires, and it can never be his interest to raise it higher. By raising the price he discourages the consumption, and puts everybody more or less, but particularly the inferior ranks of people, upon thrift and good management. If, by raising it too high, he discourages the consumption so much that the supply of the season is likely to go beyond the consumption of the season ... he runs the hazard, not only of losing a considerable part of his corn by natural causes, but of being obliged to sell what remains of it for much less than what he might have had for it several months before. If by not raising the price high enough he discourages the consumption so little that the supply of the season is likely to fall short of the consumption of the season, he not only loses a part of the profit which he might otherwise have made, but he exposes the people to suffer before the end of the season, instead of the hardships of a dearth, the dreadful horrors of a famine. ... Without intending the interest of the people, he is necessarily led, by a regard to his own interest, to treat them, even in years of scarcity, pretty much in the same manner as the prudent master of a vessel is sometimes obliged to treat his crew. When he foresees that provisions are likely to run short, he puts them upon short allowance. Though from excess of caution he should sometimes do this without any real necessity, yet all the inconveniences which his crew can thereby suffer are inconsiderable in comparison of the danger, misery, and ruin to which they might sometimes be exposed by a less provident conduct. Though from excess of avarice, in the same manner, the inland corn merchant should sometimes raise the price of his corn somewhat higher than the scarcity of the season requires, yet all the inconveniences which the people can suffer from this conduct, which effectually secures them from a famine in the end of the season, are inconsiderable in comparison of what they might have been exposed to by a more liberal way of dealing in the beginning of it."

A few things Im stuck on. Id rather you work from the passage itself than gesture at what the secondary literature says:

1. Walk me through Smith's causal chain step by step. In this passage what actually *causes* a famine? I keep coming out with the strange answer "prices being too low" — i.e. the villain is cheap bread and excessive liberality, not greed and not the weather. Is that right? And if so, where did the weather go: how does the bad harvest enter the story, and how much explanatory work is it actually doing once it's in?

2. What is a "dearth" for Smith as against a "famine"? Two magnitudes of one thing, or two different kinds of thing — one natural, one produced? He writes as though there's a threshold you can fall off partway through the season, and I cant tell whether he takes that threshold to be a fact about the harvest or a fact about behaviour.

3. The captain-and-short-allowance analogy is carrying a lot of weight and I suspect its smuggling things in. Spell out what has to be true for one price to operate like a captain rationing a crew — does it require that everyone faces the same price *and* has the same capacity to meet it? What breaks in the analogy if the crew isnt homogeneous, if a good part of it earns wages that don't move when the price of provisions moves?

4. Someone I know claims this is the entitlement insight two centuries early, on the grounds that Smith has people starving through a failure to command food at a workable price rather than through there being no food to command. I think thats a misreading, because everything here seems to presuppose a fixed aggregate supply for the season that merely has to be spread out across time — a rationing problem, not an access problem. Adjudicate between us. Is one of us right, or is the passage genuinely doing both at once?

5. The part I actually care about: does anything in this passage leave room for a famine that is *nobody's* fault? Smith puts famine on one side of a ledger and dismisses the other side's costs as "inconsiderable in comparison." Tell me who he's implicitly addressing with that weighing — a magistrate, a mob, a parliament tempted by price controls — and whether his model can even represent the case where the state is what makes food unavailable, as opposed to the case where the state merely fails to stop a shortage from turning lethal.

Plain-English paraphrase of anything opaque is welcome. I mostly want the argument taken apart and put back together so I can see the joints.
