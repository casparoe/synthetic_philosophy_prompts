I am in Year 13 and this is the central chapter of my EPQ dissertation, which asks whether an obligation can bind one person absolutely and yet be incapable of being explained to anybody else. My supervisor teaches Classics and has no philosophy background, so the chapter has to explain its own framework as it goes. My two texts are Shakespeare's Hamlet and Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, published in 1843 under the pseudonym Johannes de Silentio.

I want you to develop and then stress-test a reading I have drafted, rather than simply agree with it. My contention is that Kierkegaard's Abraham treats as one problem what Hamlet treats as three, and that this is the real reason for the famous difference between them: that Abraham does not delay and Hamlet does. The three problems, as I have sorted them, are these. First, provenance: how does the recipient know that the voice is divine rather than demonic or merely his own? Second, scope: whether the command binds only the one who hears it, or can be relayed, shared and obeyed by others — and whether it concerns something the recipient is asked to give up, or a third party he is asked to destroy. Third, revocability: whether faith includes the expectation that the demand will be withdrawn and the thing given back in this life, which seems to me to be the engine of Abraham's confidence and to have no counterpart in Hamlet at all.

Please apply that triad to particular moments and tell me where it illuminates them and where I have forced it. I have in mind: the revelation on the battlements in Act 1 Scene 5, where the Ghost is seen by Horatio, Marcellus and Bernardo but speaks its business to Hamlet alone; the soliloquy at the end of Act 2 Scene 2, where Hamlet says that the spirit he has seen may be a devil, that the devil has power to assume a pleasing shape, and that he will have grounds more relative than this before he acts; the play-within-the-play in Act 3 Scene 2, which I think is precisely the thing Abraham is structurally unable to do — manufacturing evidence that a whole court can witness; the closet scene in Act 3 Scene 4, where the Ghost appears to Hamlet alone and he anticipates that his mother may take it for his madness speaking rather than her trespass; and Act 5 Scene 2, with the divinity that shapes our ends however we rough-hew them and the claim that the readiness is all. I want a straight answer on that last one: is the readiness infinite resignation dressed up as faith, and would Johannes de Silentio notice the difference?

Two further things. I want the foils handled: Laertes acts at once and Fortinbras acts publicly, and I suspect the play's tripartite structure of alternatives to Hamlet is doing the same classificatory work as the three tragic heroes Kierkegaard sets beside Abraham — Agamemnon, Jephthah and Brutus — men who remain inside the ethical, sacrifice the particular for the universal, and can therefore be wept for. Say whether that parallel holds or whether I am tidying the play to fit. And I want the analogy broken where it deserves to break: the Ghost commands the killing of a third party rather than the surrender of what is dearest, Claudius is not what Hamlet loves most, and Hamlet's command is not issued by a being whose promise it contradicts.

Practical constraints. No bibliography, no list of secondary literature and no invented page references or quotations — I will be checking anything you attribute, and you do not have either text in front of you, so please cite scenes by act and scene and describe what happens rather than quoting. Assume the reader knows the plot of Hamlet but nothing of Kierkegaard beyond the Binding of Isaac. Aim for around 1,500 words. Close with a short section stating what the play contributes to the philosophical problem that argument alone does not reach: specifically, whether Hamlet's insistence on public justification is a failure of faith or a condition of sanity, and whether Fear and Trembling's silence on the provenance question is a deliberate exclusion or a gap it cannot fill. If you think my three-way division is not a distinction Kierkegaard would accept at all, say so and explain what he would say instead — I would rather be corrected in the chapter than in the viva.
