I write a weekly-ish column about science and ideas for a mid-sized magazine, and every other Friday our newsroom does this thing we call nerd hour where somebody presents whatever they've been chewing on — normally it's a chart or a records-request trick, but I've got the slot next Friday and 45 minutes including questions, and I want to do Death in Damascus, the decision-theory puzzle where Death is startled to run into the man in one city and tells him he'll come for him tomorrow, so the man has the night to decide whether to stay put or ride for the other city. I've spent the past year covering prediction markets, superforecasting, and self-fulfilling panics — toilet paper, the regional bank run — and I keep watching genuinely smart reporters write sentences that slide from "what would happen if we did X" to "what does it tell us that somebody's doing X" without noticing they changed gears halfway through. My pitch to myself is that the whole puzzle is that gear-change, and if I can make eighteen people feel it in their chest on a Friday afternoon I'll have done something. Room is a whiteboard and no slides (projector broke in March, its still broken), nobody's done philosophy since high school, and the cast includes our data guy who's had a stats course and will want to write probabilities on the board, two interns, a photographer, a health reporter who is deeply skeptical of anything that sounds like fate, and a markets reporter who read a lot of rationalist blogs in college, already knows Newcomb's problem cold, and will 100% say "oh, this is just Newcomb backwards" inside the first two minutes.

So: plan the session for me, don't write the talk. What I need is the dependency structure — what has to be in place in their heads before the story registers as a puzzle instead of a spooky anecdote, in what order, and why each piece has to come before the next (I think the load-bearing idea is that your own decision can be evidence about something you don't cause, and I have no idea how to land that without sounding like I'm pitching destiny). Then the carrying example: should I tell the man-and-Death story straight, or build a mundane version first — a parking enforcement officer, a friend who always guesses which bar you'll end up at, a bookie — and only then reveal that the fairy tale has the same skeleton? Tell me what the example absolutely must have and what it must not have, because I can already see it going wrong if people decide Death is lying, or chasing him, or that there's time travel involved. And give me the misconception map in the order they'll actually surface, with the beat in the sequence where I head each one off: "Death's just bluffing," "it's a self-fulfilling prophecy, that's all this is," "just flip a coin" (our data guy will definately say randomize, and I genuinely don't know whether that's a good answer, so tell me how to handle it if it is), "the answer is obviously stay put, why are we still talking," "this is Newcomb again," and the one I'm most scared of, which is the room drifting into "so nothing you decide matters, you can't beat fate." Which of those should I let them say out loud first because it does work for me? Also — when do I name the two camps, if at all? My instinct is to keep the labels out until the last two minutes or skip them entirely, because naming them turns it into a team to join rather than a distinction to see, but tell me if that's cowardly and what it costs.

Finally: one comprehension check I can put to the room at the end where the answers seperate people who actually got the distinction from people who just caught a vibe — ideally a variant of the case with one knob turned that they'd argue about rather than a quiz. And tell me what to leave out and what to say out loud while cutting it: I know there's a whole literature about whether the man can even settle on a decision, plus ratifiability and mixed strategies and the fact that the case was cooked up in the late seventies to make a narrow technical point, and I don't want to lie to the room, but I also can't open five doors I can't close in 45 minutes. Rough minutes per beat would help. No readings or bibliography please — I'll find my own way back to the source stuff, and nobody in that room would read it anyway. And if you think my framing above is wrong somewhere — e.g. if the reporter's gear-change isn't really the same knot as the puzzle — flag that before I build a talk on top of it.
