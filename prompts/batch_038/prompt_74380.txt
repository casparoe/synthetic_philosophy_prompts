For thirty-nine years I worked in atmospheric chemistry; since retiring I have read almost nothing but philosophy, and I have been asked to propose the toast at next month's valedictory dinner for a radio astronomer who is an old friend. She directed a listening-only observatory for three decades, and in her last years argued publicly, and effectively, against deliberately transmitting our presence to nearby stars. The room will hold her former staff and engineers, a contingent of planetary radar scientists who think she was wrong and would like to say so, her family including two grandchildren, and a science journalist who will quote whatever we say. She has asked that nobody use her farewell to settle the question, and I intend to respect that.

Please write the toast: five or six minutes, to be spoken aloud, in British English. It must praise a life of deliberate silence without asserting that the dark-forest reasoning is sound and without implying it is mere fiction, since either move would insult her in front of her colleagues. She was fond of distinguishing two readings of the silence — that the forest is empty, and that it is merely quiet — and she held that no observation available to us separates them, and further that this makes no difference to what we ought to do. I think the first claim was hers to make and the second was not. That must not surface as a correction, only, if you can manage it, as affection. Include one moment of humour that lands with both halves of the room, and find something for the praise to rest on that holds whichever way the evidence falls.
