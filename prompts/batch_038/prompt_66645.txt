Small town, about 4,000 people. Our main street has been named for the doctor who founded the hospital here since 1901 — call him Dr. Vance, I'd rather not type the real one. A couple of high school kids digging around in the county archive found out that he also served as registrar for most of that time and adminstered the state's literacy tests, and by their count nobody from the Black neighborhoods on the east side got registered between 1912 and 1944. Council voted in the spring to change the name. New sign goes up Saturday at ten, I've somehow been asked to say a few words at the corner. Three minutes, maybe eighty people standing around: Vance's great-granddaughter, who says she's coming to listen and not to argue; four families who've been on the street longer than I've been alive and are mostly furious about re-registering their cars; the kids who did the research; and one local historian who'll know if I fudge anything.

The commitee worked with a distinction I want the remarks to actually run on without ever stating it: a statue in a square asks people to look up and agree with something, but a street name gets used every day by people who have no view about it whatsoever — in 911 calls, on envelopes, in the directions you give a friend — so it does its work whether or not anybody endorses it. That's why we ended up thinking pulling down a statue and renaming a street were different decisions with different costs. And for the same reason we picked a new namesake the record has almost nothing on: a woman who ran the ferry landing here for thirty years, born 1868, no speeches, no surviving letters, four lines in the census. We didn't want a counter-monument. We wanted a name that isn't asking anyone to agree to anything.

So write it. It has to be sayable out loud in front of the great-granddaughter without turning into a eulogy for Vance, it has to make the ferry woman worth having a street named after her using nothing but how little we know about her, and it can't contain "legacy," "on the right side of history," or any other phrase that sounds like it came off a plaque.

Then, after the speech: tell me how confident you actually are that the statue/street-name distinction is doing structural work here rather than just dressing up a decision we'd already made, and point at the one line you'd be most nervous reading aloud to that particular crowd.
