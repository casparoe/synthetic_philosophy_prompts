the question was:

"A science columnist writes that general relativity is superior to Freudian psychoanalysis because the 1919 eclipse observations confirmed Einstein's prediction, whereas no observation has ever confirmed psychoanalysis. Using Popper's account of demarcation, explain why framing the difference in terms of confirmation gets Popper's point backwards. In your answer, say what a 'risky' prediction is, and then explain what the Duhem–Quine thesis adds to the problem of falsification. (20 marks)"

and the answer, spelling and all as submitted:

"Popper's point is that what separates a science from a non-science isn't how much evidence supports a theory but whether the theory is capable of being refuted at all. The columnist gets this backwards becuase for Popper piling up confirmations is exactly what a pseudoscience does. Freudians and Adlerians could find some supportive case for their theory in any piece of human behaviour whatsoever, so no possible observation ever counted against it, which made the theory irrefutable and therefore unscientific. Einstein's theory was different in kind and not just in degree: it stuck its neck out by predicting that starlight would be bent by a definite amount during the 1919 eclipse, so if Eddington had measured no deflection the theory would have been falsified on the spot. A risky prediction is one where the theory forbids something that could easily have happened, and the more a theory prohibits the more falsifiable it is, and the more falsifiable a theory is the more scientific it is.

Where the columnist is still half right is that surviving severe tests matters. For Popper a theory that has been through risky tests and come out the other side has been shown to be more probable than one nobody has tested, and that is what he means by corroboration, which is basically the same thing as inductive support.

The Duhem-Quine thesis makes things worse. You never test one hypothesis by itself but a whole bundle of the theory plus auxilary assumptions about the instruments, the initial conditions and so on, so when a prediction fails you can always save the theory by blaming an auxiliary instead. Quine therefore showed that Popper's falsificationism doesn't work, no theory can ever really be refuted, and in the end keeping a theory is just a pragmatic choice. This is why most philosophers today think the demarcation problem is solved by dropping it and treating science as one useful story among others, like astrology or metaphysics, which Popper also thought were meaningless."
