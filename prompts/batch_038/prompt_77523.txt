I am two-thirds through a novel whose second strand is set at Cirey in the closing years of Émilie du Châtelet's life: the country house she ran with Voltaire, the French translation of the Principia still unfinished on her desk, the young officer Saint-Lambert, the childbirth that killed her in September 1749. The first strand is contemporary, in a converted priory that sells residential resilience weeks. Its house philosophy is a soft Stoicism dressed in clinical language — manage your expectations, detach from outcomes, journal your gratitude, treat happiness as a competency for which you are personally accountable if you fail to acquire it. My device is that the retreat's marketing people have licensed a posthumous text and adapted it, and that one chapter opens with the insert.

So I would like you to write the insert: a sponsored feature in a glossy wellness magazine, in the Marquise's own voice, composed as though she had been commissioned to deliver her account of happiness in their format. Headline, deck, five numbered practices, each with a one-line italic pull-quote; one sidebar that promises evidence and contains none; an advice box with two readers' questions, the second from a woman whose circumstances leave her none of the ordinary outlets. Eight hundred to nine hundred words. After it, a hundred-word editor's note in the magazine's voice, congratulating her on having finally made her peace with the passions. Invent the publication's name and the sponsor.

The comedy must come from doctrine, not from costume. The joke is emphatically not that a dead Frenchwoman is bewildered by modernity: she should be entirely at home in the genre and simply refuse its premises. The practices should each be traceable to something she actually holds — that the passions are the machinery of happiness and a life without them is not worth the trouble of preserving; that illusions are a resource to be husbanded rather than errors to be corrected, so that the demand to see clearly is very often a demand to become unhappy; that the sage who has achieved impassibility has achieved something nearer death than wisdom; that one ought not observe oneself too closely, because happiness inspected at that range goes out; that self-love is an engine and not a stain; that virtue is the most durable support across a whole life yet is not the same thing as happiness and cannot be substituted for it; that the available sources of happiness change with age, and study, taste, and friendship are what remain once the passions cool, which is precisely why cultivating the mind matters more and not less to those, women above all, whom the world has locked out of every other avenue; that the opinion of others should be neither courted nor despised but priced.

Two formal constraints. She may not slip into the retreat's Stoicism anywhere — no detachment from outcomes, no happiness-as-a-choice, no self-surveillance that survives her refusal of it — unless that lapse is itself the point, and if it is, the piece must let the reader see it. And one section should be a place where the format demands a metric, a tracker, a streak, and her content breaks the instrument; I want that the quietest and sharpest moment in the piece, with no wink from the author.

Register: polished modern English with an eighteenth-century cadence and a mathematician's exactness, never mock-archaic. Do not quote verbatim from any real text; every line in quotation marks should be invented for her. No bibliography, no citations, no page references, no named editions or letters, and no speculation about which real correspondent the discourse was addressed to. Keep Leibniz and Newton to at most a passing mention as a habit of mind rather than a subject. A reader who has never heard of her should enjoy it as a magazine piece; a reader who has should be able to name the thesis standing behind every practice.
