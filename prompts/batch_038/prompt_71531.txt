A friend and I argued until two in the morning about the Sleeping Beauty problem and exhausted the standard moves in both directions. She is a thirder; I am a halfer. Each of us accused the other of equivocating on what a credence in a self-locating proposition even is, and we both noticed that we were re-running familiar arguments rather than making contact. We have agreed to convert the quarrel into an adversarial collaboration, but neither of us trusts the other to draft the first version, so we have commissioned a neutral one. Please write that document.

The set-up, stated once so that nothing depends on either of our retellings: on Sunday a fair coin is tossed. Heads: Beauty is woken on Monday only. Tails: she is woken on Monday and again on Tuesday, and after each awakening she is given a drug that erases all memory of it. Every awakening is subjectively indistinguishable from every other, and she knows the protocol in full. The disputed quantity is her credence in Heads upon awakening. Neither of us is an eliminativist about the puzzle, and neither of us wants to retreat to the view that the answer simply depends on what the credence is to be used for — though if you think that retreat is where the truth lies, you must show precisely which numerical answers remain genuinely disputed after it is granted, rather than using it to dissolve the exercise.

Please produce the following, in this order.

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. Include the points on which the original set-up is already uncontroversial — what the credence should be once Beauty is told it is Monday, what it should be once she is told it is Tuesday, what it was on Sunday — plus agreements about what the puzzle is and is not about, and about the connection between credence and betting behaviour. Anything either of us would have to sign under protest belongs further down, not here.

2. A disagreements table with the columns: issue | formulation the thirder would accept as a fair statement of her own view | formulation the halfer would accept as fair | substantive or merely verbal | reason. Audit your own wordings: if a formulation quietly presupposes one side's answer, say so in the reason column instead of leaving it buried in the phrasing.

3. Three stipulated cases, on each of which both parties record a definite answer in advance, before seeing the other's.

Case A, vouchers: at every awakening Beauty may buy, at price p, any number of vouchers each paying one dollar at the end of the experiment if the coin landed Heads; purchases made at different awakenings are all honoured. What is the highest p at which she should buy a voucher, and does that number settle the question about her credence? Both parties must also say what she should have accepted had the offer instead been made once, on Sunday, to a precommiting agent who knows the protocol and whose choice binds every later awakening.

Case B, sequential copies: the Sunday person is scanned and destroyed. Heads: one instance is instantiated on Monday. Tails: one instance is instantiated on Monday and a second, qualitatively identical instance on Tuesday, neither retaining any memory of the other. Do the answers from the original puzzle carry over? If they change, which premise does that expose as doing the work?

Case C, simultaneous copies: as in Case B, except that on Tails both instances are instantiated at the same time in separate rooms. Same questions.

If one party's view yields no determinate answer to a case, record that as the answer: "no fact of the matter here" is itself a substantive commitment and should appear in the table as such.

4. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of us. For each, state what argument or case both parties would accept as bearing on it, and record both parties' predictions. Likely candidates include whether the bare fact of being awake now constitutes evidence, whether any norm of self-locating belief can be stated that does not lean on the amnesia, and whether the halfer's appeal to the coin's objective chance survives the appeal being made by a centered agent. Rank the cruxes by how much of the remaining disagreement each would close.

5. Falsifiability: for each side, one argument that would make it abandon its position, and one strong objection to which it currently has no answer it considers adequate.

6. A closing assessment, as neutral as you can make it, of which portions of this dispute you judge to be about words, and what is left standing once those are set aside. If you conclude that nothing is left, say so explicitly and defend it; that is an admissible conclusion but not a default one.

No citations, no reading list, no tour of the literature — we have read it. Every entry should be either something both of us could sign or an explicitly flagged disagreement.
