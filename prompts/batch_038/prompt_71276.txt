I'm in a third-year philosophy of mathematics seminar that has just spent three weeks on the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems and Skolem's paradox, and I am drafting the term paper. A framing has taken hold in the room — our instructor offered it twice, and a classmate repeated it in a response paper — that runs roughly as follows. A countable model of set theory is a village that possesses, and uses correctly, a word meaning 'too many to be counted.' The villagers apply it to their grain stores and are right by their own lights; a visitor from the city can enumerate those same stores on her fingers. The paradox then dissolves into a lesson about linguistic reach: 'uncountable' as it functions inside the model behaves like 'beyond the horizon' or 'more than we can number,' the appearance of contradiction is a trick of where one is standing, and nothing in set theory's claim to describe a determinate reality is thereby threatened. The village, the framing stresses, is not wrong; it is merely situated.

Before I build an essay on that framing I would like it audited. I want the mapping spelled out term by term — which feature of the village stands in for the model, for its language, for the bijections it contains, for the bijection it does not contain, for the predicate 'too many to be counted,' and for the external standpoint — and then the places where the story stops fitting sorted into those that actually undercut the moral drawn from it and those that are merely picturesque. Three pressure points especially interest me. First, whether a village of grain stores has any analogue for the fact that the bijection witnessing countability from outside is not itself among the model's sets, and so is unavailable to the model in principle rather than merely not yet carried out; a visitor who could count the stores tomorrow is a different thing from one whose counting could never be replicated in the village. Second, whether the visitor's standpoint, which the story simply treats as the truth, has any counterpart at all when the question at issue is whether there is an absolutely uncountable collection for her to be right about. Third, whether the story can absorb the fact that the model is not a community of speakers, so that whatever work 'believes' and 'is situated' are doing in the retelling is not what the analogy invites us to hear.

Then give me your verdict: does the deflationary moral — our words outrun their objects, but set theory's objectivity survives unharmed — hold once those costs are counted, or was the village doing the reassuring work rather than the theorem? If the analogy fails, tell me what would serve better, and if you think every analogy in this family settles the dispute between the realist and the Skolem-skeptic in advance, say so and show me exactly where in the story the settlement happens. Finally, note whether anything in the audit shifts if the discussion moves to a second-order set theory under full semantics, where the countable village is not obviously on offer.
