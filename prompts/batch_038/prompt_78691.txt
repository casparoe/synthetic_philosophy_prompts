Week five of my sophomore metaphysics module is on individuation, and the centerpiece is the identity of indiscernibles together with Max Black's 1952 *Mind* counterexample: a universe containing nothing but two exactly similar iron spheres. I have eighteen students, seventy-five minutes, movable desks, two whiteboards, no projector, and about ten dollars to spend on index cards and pens. I lectured this material last year and it failed in an instructive way. When I put the case on a slide, roughly half the class said at once that the spheres differ by being in different places, and the remaining forty minutes went into arguing about that rather than about the principle. What I want this year is an activity in which the students run into the difficulty themselves before anyone names it, and in which the two habitual escapes — spatial location, and simply attaching names or labels — are blocked by the rules of the exercise rather than by me arguing against them. The principle, the word "haecceity," Black's name, and Leibniz's should not surface until the debrief.

I have a rough architecture, but I want you to work out the mechanics and tell me where the architecture breaks. Phase one: in trios, students receive a constrained vocabulary — a printed card listing a fixed set of monadic and relational predicates, with no names, no numerals, no indexicals, and no spatial terms — and are asked to write a complete description of a world. Phase two: two trios who were handed different world cards discover that the descriptions they produced are word-for-word identical, although one card specified a single sphere and the other specified two; each student then commits, on the record and for small real stakes (I plan to convert the pool into a vote on which optional reading goes on the week-six list), to a claim about how many objects the world they described contains. Phase three: a rotating Auditor role polices every proposed difference, under a rule I want you to state precisely: an escape counts only if it is expressible in the vocabulary on the card and is not asymmetric merely in virtue of mentioning one of the spheres. My hope is that identity properties, naming, and left/right talk are all eliminated mechanically, and that the one move that survives — an irreflexive relation holding between the spheres, the resource usually called weak discernibility — emerges as the only non-circular thing left on the table. Two things I need from you here. First, there is a puzzle in phase two that I have not solved: a description mentioning a one-mile distance between spheres already entails plurality, so it is far from obvious how two world cards can have literally identical complete descriptions while differing in how many objects they contain. Either show me how to construct such a pair within the vocabulary restriction, or explain why the device has to go and give me a replacement that is equally vivid and does not feel like a conjuring trick once the students notice how it works. Second, I want a built-in inconsistency: at some point the same students should be relying on the principle unreflectively in an ordinary setting — I was thinking of something with identical banknotes, or mass-produced parts that came off the same line — so that their confidence in bare numerical difference in the sphere case collides with their practice elsewhere and they catch the collision themselves rather than being shown it.

Deliverables: a minute-by-minute timeline for the seventy-five minutes; the exact contents of the vocabulary card and of each world card; the rules for the Auditor role, including how to adjudicate a student who proposes a property the vocabulary does not contain; a payoff scheme for the committed bet that requires no money beyond the stakes I described; and a debrief that maps particular moments of the activity onto particular parts of the dispute — the demand for qualitative rather than merely numerical difference, the worry that a version of the principle stated in terms of all properties is trivially satisfied by identity properties, the haecceitist reply, weak discernibility, and the prior question of whether spatiotemporal relations should count as properties at all for the purposes of the principle. Two of my students are physics majors and will raise identical quantum particles inside the first ten minutes; tell me whether to fold that in as an optional extension or to defuse it, and if the former, what the extension consists of and how long it costs. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — including whether the phase-two reveal will read as a gotcha and cost me the room, and what to do at that moment — and be frank about which feature of Black's case and of the wider debate the activity cannot reproduce, so that I know exactly what I have to supply by hand afterwards.
