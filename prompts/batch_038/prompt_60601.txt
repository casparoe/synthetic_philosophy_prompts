For eleven years I have been the director of a mid-sized public housing authority, and about two years ago I made a decision I have not stopped turning over since: we evicted a family — a mother and two children, with no other violations anywhere in their record — because the older son had repeatedly brought real danger into the building, and every alternative I could construct either exposed our other residents to harm or exposed the agency to litigation that would have jeopardized the funding keeping the remaining two hundred units habitable. I still cannot settle whether that was simply the correct decision under bad circumstances or something worse than that. A friend of mine who reads more political theory than I do and works in state government recently told me that what I am describing has a name, and gave me what he presented as the standard account of it. His summary ran roughly like this: 'Politics sometimes forces people in authority to do things that are morally wrong — lie, break a promise, wreck an innocent family's life — and the dirty hands idea says a decent official does them anyway rather than keeping his own conscience clean at other people's expense. Walzer picked the phrase up from Sartre's novel about a Communist assassin. But the dirt is only metaphorical: once you have shown that the act was the right one given the stakes, the wrongness has been used up, so there is nothing left over to feel guilty about, and anyone who insists on guilt is just being sentimental. What the concept really amounts to is that ordinary morality does not govern the exercise of public power — Machiavelli, reason of state, that whole tradition — which is why we tolerate in officials what would ruin anyone else. It is the trolley problem, except that the person at the lever then has to live as the sort of person who pulls it. The practical upshot is that you are clean if you made the call the numbers supported; the real danger is handing authority to the squeamish, who refuse to make the call and thereby cause more harm.'

Parts of this match how the job actually feels and parts of it make me uneasy, but I do not have the background to say which is which, and I would rather not find out by guessing. Could you go through his account claim by claim and sort it for me: what a political theorist working in this literature would accept as a fair characterization, what is a simplification that quietly drops something load-bearing, and what is simply the reverse of what the view says? I am especially unsure about the middle of it. Is it right that establishing an act to be the correct one under the circumstances discharges the wrongness entirely, so that nothing is left to feel guilty about — or is the whole point of the concept that an act can be both what the official ought to do and genuinely wrong, with those two claims held together rather than one cancelling the other? If it is the latter, I would like to understand what is actually doing the work there, because "both justified and guilty" starts to sound like double-talk to me, and I want to know whether it is or whether there is a real distinction being preserved. I would also like you to check whether the genealogy he offers holds up — the attribution to Sartre, including what kind of work it is, and whether "ordinary morality does not govern public power" is the thesis of dirty hands or rather the rival position that the concept was formulated to resist.

Finally, I care about what survives the correction, since that is the part I can use. If my friend's version is repaired, does his practical conclusion still stand — is the official who made the call the numbers supported thereby clean, and does his warning about the squeamish follow from anything true in the account, or is it a separate claim he has smuggled in? And does any of this reach someone in my position at all, or is the concept reserved for people wielding the coercive power of the state, such that a housing authority director making a ruinous-but-defensible call is doing something categorically different? Please give me an audit I can act on rather than a list of things to go and read.
