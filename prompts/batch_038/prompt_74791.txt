Since April I have held the post of lead curator for the reinterpretation of the permanent galleries at a mid-sized museum of science and medicine, and the appointment carries with it a twelve-month research secondment whose output is meant to be a methodological report that the other institutions in our group could genuinely use. I should like you to draft that report's research proposal — approximately 1,500 words, in British English throughout, and in a register formal enough to go before a board.

The difficulty that has become urgent for me is that our brief consists of a single instruction, 'stop telling the history of science as progress towards the present', and the longer I work under it the less confident I am that it names one vice rather than a bundle of them. My working hypothesis, which the project would test and probably have to reshape, is that the label 'whig' is doing at least four distinguishable jobs in our draft texts, and that only three of them are vices:

(i) evaluative anachronism — assessing past actors against standards they could not have recognised as standards;
(ii) retrospective salience — choosing which episodes to narrate by asking which of them led to us;
(iii) narrative teleology — allowing the shape of the telling, a story with a destination, to imply that the destination was already on its way;
(iv) survivorship — narrating only the lineages that persisted, so that once-live rival programmes get recast as mistakes rather than as options.

My suspicion is that (ii) is not a defect but a condition of there being any museum narrative at all, since what makes the past matter to a visitor is that she is standing in the present; and that if (ii) is licensed, then the familiar corrective — attending to the past on its own terms — is not a remedy but a way of concealing (ii) rather than disciplining it. What therefore needs investigating is whether a discipline can be placed on retrospective selection that renders it answerable rather than merely inevitable.

The specific device I should like the proposal to develop, pressure-test, and if necessary replace with something better is a test I have provisionally been calling the counterfactual-vantage test: re-run one's stated selection rule from a different present — a visitor in 1900, say, or one in 1830 — and ask whether it would have singled out the same episodes and made them intelligible without knowing what came next. Selections that pass look like claims about significance at the time; selections that fail look like whig selection in disguise.

The proposal should contain: a precise statement of the question, formulated so that it has a determinate answer; an account of why the existing positions leave it open, on both sides — the mid-century critique of present-oriented historiography descending from Butterfield's 1931 polemic, and the defences of writing history of science from where we now stand; the method, staged across the twelve months, including what I would concretely do with our own gallery texts and what a small controlled exercise would look like (for instance, having several curators select episodes under differently worded selection rules and comparing the results); a defence of this approach against the two obvious alternatives, a purely conceptual analysis of the notion of progress and a straightforward audit of current curatorial practice; a statement of what would count as a result and what would count as failure, including the live possibility that the test collapses into triviality or proves unusable by people under deadline; a description of a deliverable that a curator with no philosophical training could apply on a Monday morning; and an honest assessment of what makes the project too large, or too small, for a year, together with what I should cut first.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reference list, or citation placeholders — I will supply those myself. Refer to positions and arguments by description where you need to.

One further thing, since it bears on the framing: a panel currently scheduled for removal in the second gallery is one I wrote myself early in my career, and I am not confident that the reason I am reluctant to lose it is a good one. If the proposal can find a principled way to treat that kind of attachment as evidence rather than as noise, so much the better.
