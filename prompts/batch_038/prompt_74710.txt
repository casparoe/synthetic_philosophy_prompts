I run a two-person relocation shop — about 40 clients a year, mostly Europeans taking 2–3 year work postings in the US, and Americans going the other direction. Half the job is logistics (visas, leases, schools) but the other half is fielding panicked questions about local norms: how much to tip, whether you cross against the light, whether you line up when nobody else is lining up. Neither I nor my colleague studied philosophy, but I've noticed I keep answering from two intuitions that don't fit together. Sometimes I say "it's only a convention, it carries no moral weight, do whatever's efficient and harmless" — and sometimes I say "you're a guest, you don't get to opt out of a system you're personally benefiting from every day." Same me, different day, opposite verdicts. I want to stop making it up as I go.

So: write me a procedure my team can actually run on any local convention a client asks about, in about five minutes, sometimes on the phone while the client is standing on the street corner wondering what to do. Numbered steps in order, and for each step tell me the question to ask and what counts as a sufficient answer — plus where the procedure terminates in "you ought to follow it," "you're free not to," "your call, but here's the cost you'd be imposing," and where it has to hand off to the client because what's left is a values question rather than a facts question. Ground it in whatever the real thinking about conventions-as-conventions gives you: the difference between a rule it'd be nuts to break because identifiable people are relying on your compliance, and a rule that's just locally-approved behavior whose violation merely looks bad. Plain language, no reading list and no citations, names of philosophers optional — the mechanism is what I need, not the pedigree. And flag every step that is secretly a judgment call wearing a procedure costume.

Four things I especially need it to handle, because they're where I currently waffle. Does the length of stay change the answer — a client here for three weeks is a one-shot player who'll never see these people again, and I can't tell whether that dissolves the obligation or just hides the free-riding. Cases where the convention has zero coordination payoff in the specific instance — a red pedestrian light at 11pm on an empty street. Cases where locals themselves defect, say, forty percent of the time, so "the local norm" isn't one thing. And cases where the visitor is riding on a practice they think is unjust and would like to protest — the tipping norm as a de facto wage subsidy is exactly this.

Then run it on four real-ish clients. (1) Swede posted to a city where the bus stop is a scrum and whoever pushes to the door gets on; he insists on forming a one-man queue and has missed three buses this week. (2) Australian in Chicago who calls 20% tipping extortion and wants to leave 8% plus a seperate note explaining why, every single time, as a small protest. (3) American teenager we placed with a host family in Berlin, told that people wait at the red light even with no traffic for a hundred meters in either direction, and asking whether it matters that his host family's eight-year-old is watching him. (4) The one I'm braced for: a client somewhere where the residence permit stalls unless you slip the clerk a small "facilitation fee," which everybody calls a normal local practice. My gut says the procedure should output "no," but I want to see whether it actually does, or whether it quietly licenses the payment because the fee is reliable, expected, and everyone complies — and if it does license it, tell me which step I should have written differently. Also tell me who eats the cost when the client refuses.

Format: numbered steps first, then a compact table for the four cases (case | verdict | which step decided it | how confident the procedure is entitled to be), then three or four sentences on where this thing will misfire and what it structurally cannot settle. Hard cap of 800 words for the whole response, and please dont go over — I need something I can pin above a desk, not a chapter.
