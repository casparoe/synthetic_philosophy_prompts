I'm a second-year in a philosophy of mind program and my reading group (half of us are over in cog sci) has been banging on about Molyneux's problem for about two months now, and we're going nowhere. Here's the standoff as I see it. One camp says the cross-modal matching work with people whose congenital cataracts got removed in their teens basically settles the thing: transfer fails, so the answer is no, and Molyneux's own reasoning — that the newly sighted man has no experience of what a cube does to the eye — was right all along. A second camp says those results are beside the point entirely, because the subjects aren't seeing in the relevant sense: their acuities are bad, they've had a decade and a half of no visual learning, they never picked up the sensorimotor know-how that sighted people have, so a negative result is exactly what you'd predict on any theory and licenses nothing. And a third group says everyone is talking past everyone, because the request to identify the cube and the sphere by sight alone hides at least three questions — whether shape is phenomonally present in the visual field at all, whether the subject can make a correct visual judgement about shape, and whether they can visually recognize an object as being of the same kind as something they know by touch — and once you split those apart the famous question just dissolves. I'm supposed to give a talk on this next week and I really don't want to just rehearse the survey.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to stress-test rather than accept, is that the actual fault line isn't any of those three and isn't empirical at all: it's about what possessing the concept CUBE amounts to. If concept possession is a recognitional capacity, then failing to pick out the cube by sight just is lacking a visual recognitional capacity, and the negative result is substantive. If concepts are individuated by representational format — the amodal-symbols picture — then the concept could be sitting right there, fully deployed in touch, with vision simply not hooked into it yet, and the same result is uninformative. So the very same experiment reads as decisive to one side and as noise to the other, and no amount of extra data will fix that. Is that right? I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement — verbal or substantive, different questions hiding under one heading, a hidden empirical question doing the work, a question that's ill-posed versus one that's merely hard — and I want it grounded in what each side actually claims, not in the history. I know the Berkeley/Leibniz/Diderot literature, please don't summarize it at me.

Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on: what would count as progress? Concretely — a distinction somebody needs to draw, an experimental design whose outcome would come out differently on the two readings of concept possession, a formal result about what cross-modal transfer actually requires, a reframing of the question, something else. And if you think no progress should be expected, say so and tell me why the impasse is stable rather than just unlucky. One more thing I'm curious about, and feel free to tell me it's a bad question: is there any sense left in which Molyneux's original problem has a determinate answer, or is the name now just a label for a pile of seperate questions that happen to share a three-hundred-year pedigree?
