I hold the following methodological thesis about evolutionary psychology, and I have come to think it is right:

No body of evidence that the discipline can in principle obtain confirms the claim that a given psychological trait is an adaptation shaped by selection for a particular ancestral function. Universality and cross-cultural stability of a trait are equally well expected on non-adaptive accounts — phylogenetic inheritance from a shared ancestor, general-purpose learning operating on near-universal environments, convergent cultural responses to convergent ecological constraints, or by-product status relative to some other trait that was itself selected. The ancestral conditions invoked to fix the function are reconstructed largely from the very traits they are supposed to explain, and in any case describe a shifting ensemble of environments rather than a single stable selection regime, so the adaptive problem the trait supposedly solved cannot be specified independently of the trait. And the property practitioners point to as the mark of an adaptation — economy of means, precision of fit, special-purpose structure, robustness across development and across cultures — is a profile that any mechanism which reliably does its job will display, whatever its history. The inference is therefore abductive at best, and the abduction is never forced: for every proposed function there is a rival account that accommodates the same data at least as well, and no test discriminates between them.

I do not want a defense of this thesis. I want a map of its exposure. Please address the following.

1. What would have to be true for the thesis to be false? Identify the kinds of evidence that would genuinely discriminate an adaptation hypothesis from its non-adaptive rivals, and say for each kind whether it is merely absent, practically difficult, or conceptually unavailable.

2. Assess the strongest real candidates against the thesis: predictions of previously unobserved phenomena made in advance and then borne out; natural experiments in developmental chronology, such as the finding that sexual aversion tracks the duration and timing of close early association between children, and the demographic profiles of arrangements in which spouses are reared together from childhood; content-specific patterns in reasoning that appear only for some domains and not structurally equivalent ones; behavioral-genetic partitioning that locates variation where an adaptation account expects it and not where rival accounts expect it; convergent evidence from species with different mating and rearing systems. Rank these by how much pressure each actually puts on the thesis, and say which are routinely overclaimed by both sides.

3. Distinguish which of three separable claims each candidate defeater threatens: that the mind is massively modular; that specific traits have specific selected functions; and that this body of work constitutes a unified research program rather than a loose collection of independent hypotheses. A result that damages the second claim in one domain need not touch the general thesis, and I want the distinction kept sharp rather than blurred.

4. Which of my premises would working researchers in the field concede, and which would they reject — not as caricatured by their critics, but as they argue among themselves? Where the field is internally divided about method, say which side's position, if adopted, would come closest to granting my case, and which would come closest to refuting it.

5. Reflexively: my thesis appears compatible with any future result, since every successful prediction can be absorbed by noting that a rival account fit the data equally well. Give an honest assessment of whether it has thereby been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a genuine defect for a claim of this kind or an acceptable feature of a thesis about the limits of inference.

6. Finally, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and name the single kind of evidence you judge most likely to change my mind.
