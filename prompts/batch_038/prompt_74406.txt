ok so here's a case that I think breaks the standard story about lying vs. misleading, and I want you to actually argue one side of it rather than lay out both sides.

Start with the usual setup. A drug rep wants a physician to believe her product is safe when the rep knows it isn't. Two routes. Route L: she just says "it's safe," flat out, knowing that's false. Route M: she says only true things — "in our 12-week trial no patient reported liver toxicity" — which is literally accurate, because the trial screened out everyone with preexisting liver conditions and because a longer study she's read found damage at nine months, and she lets the physician draw the safety conclusion herself.

The stock verdict is that L is worse, for roughly three reasons: a lie violates the hearer's right to be told the truth (or the norm of assertion); misleading leaves the hearer's autonomy intact, since the hearer does the inferring; and a lie manipulates belief directly while misleading merely declines to supply help. I want you to argue that all three invert once we bolt on the following stipulations.

Stipulation 1 (equalized damage). The physician is a careful, well-calibrated reasoner, and in both routes she ends up at exactly the same credence — call it 0.95 — that the drug is safe for her patient. Same epistemic harm, same downstream consequences, same probability of patient injury. So no arguing from a difference in outcomes.

Stipulation 2 (transparency to the speaker). The rep knows the physician's reasoning procedure in complete detail in both routes, including which background assumptions the physician will bring to bear and which she'll never think to check.

Stipulation 3 (repeatability). Both routes are one move in a long series of similar exchanges between reps and physicians in this specialty, and the community's only quality-control mechanism is that physicians watch for signs that a speaker is asserting something the speaker believes to be false.

Your thesis: given 1–3, route M is worse than route L. Not equally bad — worse.

Some angles you can take or leave (please generate your own as well):

- the autonomy point seems to run backwards, because M requires the rep to model and exploit the physician's own inference rules rather than just her credulity, and turning someone's competence against them degrades that competence in a way that bypassing it doesn't;
- under stipulation 3, the community's detection mechanism is tuned to exactly L and blind to exactly M, so M is the only route that corrupts the channel undetectably; a lie is in some sense self-limiting, since it's the kind of thing the hearer's ordinary checks are built to trip over, and it invites the hearer to treat the speaker's assertions as evidence about the speaker rather than about the world;
- M relocates the false belief inside the physician's own reasoning, which makes the error unavailable for her to reassign to anyone later, whereas L leaves the locus of error clearly with the rep;
- consider what M leaves behind in the physician's evidence base: a true stored sentence, with no speaker attached to it anymore, that will keep re-triggering the bad inference for years and in other contexts where the rep isn't even present.

What I most want, beyond the case: a principled statement of the general condition under which the asymmetry flips. Not just "here it does," but a rule about which features of a situation make misleading worse than lying — plus a check on whether your rule proves too much. If your rule implies that all polite hedging and all strategic partial disclosure are worse than blunt lying, say so and either bite the bullet or repair the rule.

And write it as an argument: state your strongest objection, take it seriously, and answer it rather than waving at it. Im curious wether you think the position survives once you've done that, so tell me at the end whether it does.
