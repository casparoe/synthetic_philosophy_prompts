Ok so I need someone who can play the role of a philosopher of language/mind who also thinks seriously about perception and writing systems — someone who can do careful conceptual analysis without turning into a textbook. Please think hard about this before answering, and actually brainstorm a couple of candidate principles out loud before you commit to one. No reading list at the end, I'm not looking for sources, I'm looking for the argument.

Background: I got sucked into the question of what makes every 'a' an 'a'. A friend pointed me at some project about letterforms and analogy — I think the name was something like Letter Spirit — but I don't want a summary of that, I want help with my own judgments, because I've been collecting them for a while now and they clearly don't add up to anything tidy.

Here's what I actually think, case by case:

1. A Times-style 'a' (two-story, with the little hook over the bowl) and my own handwritten single-story 'a' (basically a circle with a tail) are the same letter. Not similar — the same one.
2. Uppercase A and lowercase a are also the same letter, even though they share almost no strokes. Case is something a letter has, not what it is.
3. A Cyrillic 'а' typed into the middle of an English word by mistake: I don't notice it, I read it as an 'a', and I'd say the word is spelled correctly.
4. But if someone who had never seen our alphabet invented a mark that happened to look exactly like a single-story 'a' as their symbol for water, I would NOT call it an 'a'. Same shape, opposite verdict from 3.
5. A mirrored 'a' (flipped left-right, sitting on its own) is not an 'a'. It's a defective a at best.
6. ...except the same mirrored letters in a word that is mirrored end to end, so you can read the whole thing in a mirror: there I think each letter is still an 'a' and the word is perfectly legible, just backwards. So 5 and 6 contain the same glyph and I judge them differently.
7. A logo where the 'a' has been reduced to two abstract strokes that mean nothing in isolation, but inside the word are obviously an 'a'. With the rest of the word: yes. Lifted out: no.
8. A display face where 'a' and 'o' are nearly the same round shape: I say the word decides which one it is, not the glyph.
9. An upside-down 'a' is not an 'a'. Nor is one turned 90 degrees. But one tilted 8 degrees, squashed, stretched, cut out of neon tubing or dripping off a brush — still an 'a', definately. There's some angle in between where I have literally no idea, and that bothers me.
10. Sound doesn't matter. The mark in "bread" and the mark in "fate" are the same letter.
11. Greek alpha in a font that makes it look like a Latin 'a' — different letter, I'd say, even though I couldn't tell them apart.
12. The making of it: my 'a' is drawn in a certain stroke order, and a copy drawn in a completely different stroke order looks identical to me and I'd call it the same letter. But there's a moment mid-stroke where the mark could still become an 'a' or an 'o', and at that moment I want to say it's neither. So maybe the making does matter?
13. A scrawl that only its own author can read: I lean toward not-an-'a'. But if the author can still read it back a year later, I flip.
14. An 'a' weathered in stone until most of the shape is gone, but sitting inside a legible word: still an 'a'.

My tentative principle is something like: it's an 'a' if it carries the essential features — a closed-ish bowl with a stroke down the right side — whatever style they happen to be wearing. But 5 vs 6 wrecks that, 3 vs 4 wrecks it, and 14 probably wrecks it too if I'm honest.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close to all? State it precisely, tell me which of my cases motivates each clause, and say plainly which of my verdicts I'd have to give up. I'd rather have a simpler principle that costs me a judgment or two than a baroque one that saves everything, but show me what each option costs. Then run your account on two cases I haven't listed — pick them yourself, ideally ones that stress it rather than flatter it. And last: is 'a'-ness in the mark, in the making of the mark, or in the system and the community that uses it? Pick one and tell me what would change your mind.
