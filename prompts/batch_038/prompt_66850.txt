Our personal-identity reading group treats the sentence 'there is no self' as a settled conclusion rather than as a claim anyone there has argued for, and it is being made to do two incompatible jobs. One member invokes it to say that Buddhist thought anticipated the reductionist position: a person is nothing over and above causally connected physical and mental events, and strict identity over time is not what matters in survival. Another invokes the same sentence to say the position is self-undermining, since without a self there is no one to remember, to intend, or to bear the consequences of earlier actions. Lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct theses that this one sentence could express, from the near-trivial to the ontologically extravagant — including the difference between denying that selves exist and denying that they exist ultimately — and for each say what kind of claim it is: about what exists fundamentally, about what exists at all, about how experience is structured from the inside, or about which practical attitudes are appropriate. Say also, for each, whether it leaves room for persons existing conventionally and for the psychological-continuity picture, and which readings, if any, are exposed to the objection about memory and agency. Then identify the reading each of my two members is actually relying on, and tell me whether their disagreement survives the disambiguation or dissolves into a dispute over which thesis the slogan was ever meant to carry.
