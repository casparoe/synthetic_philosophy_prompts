---

Douglass's break with Garrison was announced publicly around 1850-51, developed in his editorials, and given its most famous expression in the July 4, 1852 address in Rochester; the fullest statement of the mature theory comes later, in the 1860 lecture he delivered in Scotland on whether the Constitution was pro- or anti-slavery. The break had a personal dimension — control of the paper, money, Douglass's resentment at being treated as a mouthpiece — but it also carried a real intellectual consequence, because it removed the reason Douglass had for accepting a reading of the Constitution that he had never found textually convincing. In his own later account he describes arriving at the new view by working through the accepted canons of legal interpretation, and the same conclusion was being pressed by the political abolitionists around Gerrit Smith and, in a much more aggressive form, by Lysander Spooner's 1845 constitutional argument.

The strategy itself has two moves. The first is the familiar rule that a written instrument is to be construed in light of the purposes it declares for itself: where operative language admits of more than one reading, the reading that serves the document's stated objects governs. Douglass applies this to the preamble, whose objects include establishing justice, promoting the general welfare, and securing the blessings of liberty, and argues that no clause can be read to authorize a system that defeats all of them. The second move is textual-historical. The Constitution nowhere uses the words "slave" or "slavery." It speaks instead of persons held to service or labor, of persons imported, of persons bound to service. Douglass treats that euphemism as evidence, first, of the framers' shame, and second, of an intention not to build the institution into the fundamental law at all — a document that will not name a thing cannot be said to guarantee it. The clause ending the African trade after 1808 points the same direction, since it presumes an institution expected to expire rather than to expand.

Applied to the three-fifths clause, Douglass's point is that the provision recognizes the enslaved as persons and therefore as entitled to a share in representation — three-fifths of a person's political weight is still an admission of personhood — while at the same time penalizing the slaveholding states, since a state that emancipated and counted its Black population in full would gain seats rather than lose them. Slavery, on this reading, costs the South power. Against the fugitive clause he argues that a "person held to service or labor" is language broad enough to cover an apprentice or an indentured servant, so the clause identifies no slave at all, and that in any case it is a bare stipulation between states accompanied by no grant of power to Congress to enforce it — which is why the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 has no constitutional warrant.

It's worth stressing that the interpretive rule was not Douglass's invention, and it was not really what he and Garrison disagreed about. Construing ambiguous clauses by reference to the preamble's declared objects was Garrison's own rule of construction, and the split between the two men was fundamentally personal and tactical rather than a disagreement about what the Constitution says. That is precisely why Douglass could keep the method and simply arrive at a friendlier verdict: he was applying Garrisonian interpretation more consistently than Garrison had.

The real test came in 1857. Taney's opinion read the Constitution as an explicitly white-supremacist compact, and Douglass's response conceded much of the force of the point, treating the decision as showing that the anti-slavery reading was at best a dissenting interpretation rather than the law of the land. By the end of the decade he had largely stopped pressing it; the 1860 Scottish lecture is best read as a nostalgic restatement of a position he no longer held, delivered to an audience that wanted to hear it.

On the harder half of the question, Douglass is more careful than his reputation suggests. The reading settles what Congress may do — abolish slavery in the territories and the District, refuse it any protection or extension, legislate nothing for the rendition of fugitives — but it does not settle what the states must do, since the Constitution leaves slavery where state law has put it. It therefore made political engagement available to abolitionists who had previously refused to participate in a corrupt system, which is arguably its largest practical achievement. It also, however, implies that the Reconstruction Amendments were largely declaratory: on Douglass's account the privileges of Black citizens were already secured by the original text, which is why he opposed the Fifteenth Amendment as redundant, "a waste of political capital," as he put it.
