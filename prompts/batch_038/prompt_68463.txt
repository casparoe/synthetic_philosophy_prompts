Im the TA for a second-year course and we just wrapped a three-week unit on classical Indian epistemology — students had the opening of the Nyāya-sūtras in translation plus some secondary material, and then sat a timed exam. One short answer was worth 20 marks and they had 40 minutes. Heres the question and one of the responses.

**Q3.** Nyāya admits four pramāṇas. Name them and say what each is supposed to deliver. Give the stock example for upamāna, and set out the Nyāya argument that upamāna can't be reduced to perception, to inference, or to testimony. Close by saying how many pramāṇas the Buddhists admit and what they do with the ones they reject.

**Student:**

Nyāya accepts four pramanas: pratyakṣa (perception), anumāna (inference), upamāna (comparison) and śabda (testimony). Perception is the cognition that arises when a sense faculty comes into contact with its object; inference is the cognition of something not presently in contact with the senses, on the basis of a pervasion (vyāpti) between the reason and what's to be proved; testimony is the cognition arising from the statements of a reliable speaker (āpta); upamāna is the cognition that arises from similarity.

The example: a townsman who has never been to the forest is told by a forester that a gavaya is an animal like a cow. Later he goes into the forest, sees an animal resembling a cow, and comes to know that this is the gavaya. That cognition is what upamāna delivers — the knowledge that the gavaya and the cow are similar.

Nyāya holds, with the Buddhists here, that perception proper is free of conceptual construction, so seeing-a-cow-as-a-cow is not strictly perception but a compound of perception and memory. That's why upamāna can't be reduced to perception: the cognition of the gavaya depends on remembering what the forester said, and a non-conceptual perception can't carry a memory. Nor is it inference, since inference needs a universal relation between the reason and the target — smoke and fire — and there's no such pervasion between a remembered cow and the animal in front of you; similarity isn't a property that pervades anything. And it isn't just testimony either, because the words alone give you a sentence you can't apply to the world until you go and look.

Finally, the Buddhists admit only perception and inference. The Naiyāyikas themselves concede that testimony gets its warrant from an inference about whether the speaker is reliable, so śabda is in that sense a species of anumāna kept on the list out of deference to scripture. Elsewhere the Sāṃkhya admit three pramāṇas, dropping upamāna, and the Vaiśeṣika admit only two.
