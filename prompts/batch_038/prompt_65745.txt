Third year in a quantitative psych program — mostly IRT and measurement invariance — and for the past year I've been the stats person on a project with two philosophers who work on the cross-cultural intuitions debate. You know the shape of it: if ordinary people in different countries give different answers to the same thought experiment (does the guy who gets it right by luck really *know*? is it okay to redirect the runaway thing at the cost of one life?), then philosophers can't lean so hard on their own answers as evidence, and maybe the answers aren't tracking anything. My actual job has been the questionnaire side: back-translating the vignettes, checking whether a "somewhat agree" in site B means the same thing as a "somewhat agree" in site C, whether the response scales are even functioning the same way. Reading thier literature over the last few months I keep getting the feeling that the whole argument is a measurement argument in a trenchcoat, and I want to find out if that feeling survives scrutiny.

So: do the translation properly. I know my own framework cold and basically nothing of theirs beyond what I've had to read for this project, so write it for me in the vocabulary I live in — latent traits, construct validity, indicators versus constructs, differential item functioning, metric versus scalar invariance, measurement error, stimulus equivalence, sampling frames. The four claims I most want mapped: (1) that documented variation in intuitions across groups undercuts the evidential weight of philosophers' own intuitions; (2) the expertise move, that trained intuitions are more reliable than untutored ones and so the variation studies miss the relevant population; (3) the deflationary reply that these studies measure verbal reports about a translated scenario rather than intuitions, so nothing follows about the underlying state; (4) the worry that "intuition" isn't a single construct at all, so the cross-site comparisons are comparing apples and oranges from the start. For each one, tell me whether there's a clean counterpart, whether it only translates if I bend my vocabulary, and if there's no counterpart at all just say so plainly instead of hedging.

The part I actually care about is the residue. What in the philosophical argument has no psychometric expression whatsoever? My guess is at least two things — some of these intuitions are supposed to be about what *has* to be the case rather than about what people tend to think, and buried in the middle of the debate is a question about which intuitions get to be authoritative, which isn't a question about instrument quality. Tell me whether the leftover stuff shows my field is missing a category, or shows their disagreement is thinner than it looks from the outside. Two house rules: don't smuggle in psychometric assumptions and label them translation — if a chunk of the argument only works once you assume there's one well-defined latent trait sitting behind the responses, flag that as something you had to add, not as a finding. Also don't bother being even-handed, I'd rather have an honest map than a diplomatic one.
