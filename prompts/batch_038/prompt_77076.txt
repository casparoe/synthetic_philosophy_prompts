So I'm deep in a novel about a city that keeps a digital twin of itself and runs it to stress-test policy decisions — flood plans, transit reroutes, budget shocks, that sort of thing. One chapter has the twin running in what my characters call rehearsal mode: instead of simulating forward in a single line, the cluster computes a big fan of candidate futures in parallel, scores them, commits to one, and wipes the rest. The thing that's been eating at me is that during the rehearsal those discarded branches are physically running. Bit for bit, volt for volt, they're as real a computation as the branch that survives. The only difference is that nothing downstream ever inherits them. No memory, no trace, no consequence.

Anyway, I need the scene. Two of my characters end up arguing about whether the discarded branches are the kind of thing that could be harmed by getting wiped, and I want the argument to be genuinely good on both sides — I'm so tired of AI-fiction where one character is basically a philosopher-shaped prop who exists to lose.

Character A holds something like a process view: what makes a computation morally interesting is that it's actually part of a causal stream, a state of affairs that makes a difference and leads somewhere. A computation whose output is annhilated the instant it's produced was never a real computation in the relevant sense, just a very expensive way of rearranging silicon. She'll bite the bullet that this makes the difference between the surviving branch and the discarded ones a real moral difference rather than a bookkeeping accident. And she knows about the old worry that if you're sloppy about what counts as implementing a computation, a rock in your garden implements basically every finite automaton — she takes that worry as a reason to demand causal-historical seriousness, not a reason to bail on functionalism altogether.

Character B holds close to the opposite: moral status should supervene on what a system is intrinsically like while it's running, not on what bystanders happen to do with its output afterwards. If the discarded branch runs the same processes in the same order with the same internal dynamics, then it is that system, and whether an admin later hits save or hits delete is a fact about the admin, not about the patient. B has a duplication argument up thier sleeve too — run the identical branch twice in the same instant and ask whether that makes the harm twice as bad or the same harm twice, and press A on what her answer commits her to.

I want actual engagement: each one takes a real swing at the other's strongest point and lands something, and at least once somebody concedes a genuine sub-point instead of just restating. No straw men, nobody converts, nobody wins. But the disagreement should move — by the end they've narrowed it to some smaller, sharper crux that neither of them expected going in, and I'd like that crux to be about something neither of them started out caring about.

Put them somewhere physical and a bit mundane — machine room or the corridor outside it, mid-cycle, with the heat and the fan noise and the fact that a wipe is scheduled in about four minutes doing some of the dramatic work. Roughly 900 to 1200 words. Keep it sounding like two smart people who have thought about this way too much rather than a conference panel, so please don't lean on jargon as a crutch; between the two of them I'd rather the words sentience and moral patient showed up once each, max.
