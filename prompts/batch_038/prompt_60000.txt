I'm deep into a novel set in the 1870s and one of my main characters is an antiquarian who makes his living giving lectures to provincial societies. I've drafted him a stretch of one of those lectures, in what I hope is period voice — it's my own pastiche, not lifted out of any real book, so please don't go hunting for a source or telling me it's misattributed. The trouble is that in trying to sound Victorian I may have written something that is either confused or secretly right, and I can't tell which. Here it is:

"There are who object that from a thing buried we may learn only that it was buried. I answer, that the grave is not a mere receptacle but a sentence, and that the manner of its arrangement is a predicate. Consider the blade laid beside the dead, but bent double, or broken across the knee before ever it was laid down: for what end should a man spoil a good weapon, unless that the thing which passes with the dead must first be unmade for the use of the living? It is here that we may lawfully call upon the testimony of ruder peoples now surviving in other quarters of the world; not as though these were our own forefathers standing still while we advanced, which is a folly, but because where an usage has been carried whole from one state of society into another, and there persists after the reason of it has perished, the later form is a fair index of the earlier meaning. The same holds of institutions. Where a hundred bronze weights, dug from a hundred unrelated barrows and market towns, answer to one another within the width of a thumb-nail, we have not a hundred accidents but one custom; and a custom so exact cannot hold itself in being without some hand to see it kept, which hand is what we mean by an office, and an office implies a body that gives it. So from the sameness of the things we ascend to the rule, and from the rule to the rulers."

A few things I need from you, and I'd rather you be blunt than kind:

1. Walk me through it sentence by sentence in plain modern English first. Several of these clauses I can only parse on the third read and I need to know whether I've made them unparseable by accident or whether they're merely dense.

2. The bent-blade business. What kind of inference is he actually making there? My sense is that he isn't reading meaning off the object at all — the meaning comes from the fact that somebody deliberately spoiled something valuable, and the spoilage is the only residue of the decision that survived. If that's right, what does he need to assume about the people who did it that he never states? Something about cost, or about what counts as deliberate rather than merely convenient?

3. He pointedly refuses the crude version of the ethnographic analogy — the move where ruder peoples are treated as our own ancestors standing still. Does his substitute actually buy him anything? He says the later form indexes the earlier meaning only where a usage has been "carried whole" into a new state of society and outlived the reason for it. I'm worried I've just buried the uniformity assumption inside that phrase rather than getting rid of it. Tell me whether that's a genuine problem or whether the disclaimer is doing real work, and what an analogy of this sort would have to look like to be sound instead of merely convenient.

4. Then the weights. He climbs from "a hundred bronze weights agree with each other" to "there was an office" to "there was a body that conferred it." How much does regularity in surviving artifacts actually license you to say about institutions? I want to know at which rung the possibilities start multiplying instead of narrowing, and how wide the gap is between "there was a shared convention" and "there was someone enforcing it." My instinct is that a convention can police itself and he's smuggling in a magistrate. Is that fair to him or am I being anachronistic?

5. Last one. Suppose I want a rival antiquarian of the same decade — not a modern skeptic with hindsight — to take this lecture apart in public. What's the sharpest objection available to him that my character hasn't answered? I want the rival to actually land a blow, not just sound contrary. No names or reading lists, I can't chase those down and don't want them; just the arguement. Though if there's a standard way of describing the general predicament my antiquarian is in — inferring unobservable beliefs and rules from objects that survived more or less by accident — I'd like to know what that predicament is usually called and what the main families of reply to it look like.
