I am the president of my high school's astronomy club, and this spring the club's Student Activities Committee has to rule on a proposal I would rather not have been the one to receive. Two years ago a donor paid for a small dish, an outbuilding, and a transmitter permit that allows us to broadcast a narrowband signal at modest power toward a chosen target. A senior member wants to use the transmitter this observing season to send a short, deliberately simple message — an encoding of counting numbers, our school's coordinates in time and space, and an invitation to reply — toward a star system with a dozen or so potentially habitable planets. The club voted 9–7 in favor. The committee, which includes our faculty advisor, a parent who is a licensed radio amateur, and two student representatives, is split, and because I chair it, I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before we meet. I want your help drafting that document, and I want it to be one the committee can actually act on rather than an essay about the ethics of the situation in general.

For context that the document itself must supply: our objection is not technical. It is that the transmission may be reckless on grounds drawn from what is sometimes called the dark forest hypothesis. Please explain that line of reasoning to readers who have never heard of it, in three or four sentences and without condescension: the idea that a civilization which reveals its location risks being destroyed by others who cannot afford to take chances with it, that silence is therefore the dominant strategy, and that any species patient and powerful enough to have survived long enough to find us is exactly the kind of species we should least want to find us. The memo has to take that hypothesis seriously as a premise rather than treat it as the plot of a novel, and it should say explicitly what would have to be true for it to hold.

Three arguments have been made in our meetings, and I want the memo to work through all of them rather than defer them to a footnote.

First, the member who proposed the transmission argues that the marginal risk is zero. Earth has been radiating television, radar, and military signals for the better part of a century, and any civilization close enough to be reached by our little dish has already had ample opportunity to notice us; one more message at four hundred watts adds nothing to a leak that is already happening. The objecting member answers that this cannot be right as a general principle, because if every group reasoned that its own contribution is negligible against a background it did not create, the background would be exactly the thing that eventually gets us noticed, and no single act would ever have been the one to blame. Please decide whether that objection defeats the first argument, weakens it, or misses it, and say why in terms a non-philosopher can follow.

Second, there is a question about the arithmetic of tiny probabilities and enormous harms. Some of us suspect that once the harm at stake is the extinction of everything that has ever mattered to anyone, any nonzero probability, however small, licenses refusal, and that this makes the whole dispute unarguable. Others suspect that this move proves far too much, since it would also forbid driving to the meeting, and that a principle which cannot distinguish our transmitter from a school bus is not doing any real work. The memo should say whether expected-value reasoning of this kind can settle the case at all, and if it cannot, what should take its place.

Third, and this is the point I most want handled carefully: several members have noticed that the decision itself may be a signal. If a watcher could learn anything from us, it might learn that humans broadcast their position even after the danger has been explained to them — or, if we refuse, that we did not. Our deliberations will be summarized in the school newspaper, and our reasoning is not private in the way the two sides assume. Please tell the committee whether this consideration should weigh on the decision, whether it cuts in one direction or genuinely in both, and whether it changes anything if we conclude that no watcher could plausibly be tracking the content of a local newspaper.

Here is what I need from you. A memo of roughly two pages, addressed to the committee, that opens with a single-sentence recommendation — approve, reject, or approve under stated conditions — followed by the reasoning that supports it and then by the conditions or the grounds for refusal. It must close with something actionable: what happens to the equipment and to the observing season under each option, a schedule tied to the window in which our target is actually visible, and a statement of what the club would do instead with the transmitter if we decline. The strongest case against your recommendation has to appear in the memo and be stated as its advocate would state it, not as a straw version of it; the senior member who proposed this has done careful work and deserves to be answered, not managed. Write in a formal, measured register suitable for circulation to administrators, and do not include citations, a bibliography, or a list of further reading — I will be the one signing this, and I cannot defend sources I have not read.
