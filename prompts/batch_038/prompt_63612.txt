I retired four years ago after thirty-eight years in a national metrology laboratory, where my working life consisted of comparing measurements, propagating uncertainties, and taking entirely for granted the analysis in which all of it was expressed. Only recently have I begun reading philosophy seriously, and I now attend a fortnightly reading group run through our university's continuing-education programme. Two of its members — Elias Marchetti, a retired professor of pure mathematics, and Naomi Adeyemi, who is writing a doctoral dissertation in philosophy — argued for the better part of an hour last month about whether it makes sense to worry that the accepted axioms of set theory might be inconsistent. I took notes. I reproduce the exchange as fairly as I can, and I would like an essay that adjudicates it.

The moves, as I recorded them:

1. Elias opened by saying the worry is mis-posed rather than mistaken. We cannot prove from within that our axioms are consistent, he granted, and he did not claim to know that they are. But he said the question worth asking is narrower: are the parts of mathematics on which the rest of us depend consistent? His proposal — he called it the locality of inconsistency — was that a contradiction, if one were ever derived, would be derived from particular axioms, and that the arithmetic and analysis used in physics are provable in far weaker systems that do not require those axioms. Even in the worst case, therefore, the scientific edifice survives; what fails is a region of set theory nobody outside the subject needs.

2. Naomi said this misunderstands what logical consequence is. On classical logic inconsistency is not a local affliction: from a single contradiction everything follows, including every theorem of arithmetic and every result of analysis. There is thus no such thing as a fragment that stays safe once the theory containing it is inconsistent. She added the fork: if Elias wants locality he must give up classical logic for some paraconsistent one, in which case the reliability of ordinary mathematics rests on a non-classical logic far less battle-tested than the set theory it was meant to rescue — a weaker guarantee than the one he offered, not a stronger one.

3. Elias replied that he had not been talking about derivability but about practice. Mathematicians and physicists do not work inside a single formal system; they work with a body of results checked by many independent routes. He said any actual inconsistency would have to be extremely complex, since proof theorists have shown that for sufficiently weak systems inconsistency would yield a derivation of contradiction of bounded and feasible size, and searches within that bounded space have found nothing. A genuine inconsistency would therefore live somewhere exotic, among large-cardinal hypotheses, and the practice of proving theorems about Hilbert spaces would continue undisturbed even if, technically, everything followed from it.

4. Naomi said that "undisturbed" was carrying the whole weight of his position, and that it was a psychological claim rather than a logical one. She then attacked his track-record argument directly. A century and a half without a contradiction is nearly no evidence, she said, because uncovering an inconsistency is not a matter of accumulating observations; it requires one clever construction. Russell found his paradox in a few lines. Until somebody finds the construction we are in exactly the epistemic position we occupied the day before, so a hundred and fifty years of failure leave us almost where we started.

5. Elias said he did not accept that the years of failure are worthless, and offered two things. First, the web of relative-consistency results: we know that if the base theory is consistent then so are its extensions, and the network is not idle, because the same combinatorial structures recur in forcing, in model theory, and in ordinary analysis, which would be a remarkable coincidence if the whole thing were rotten. Second, and more importantly, he said mathematics does not need a guarantee and has never had one; the proper attitude is not belief in consistency but willingness to repair. If a contradiction appeared we would prune, exactly as we pruned unrestricted comprehension after Russell. This, he said, is how physics has always treated its own theories: used until they break, and the breaking tells you where the boundary lies.

6. Naomi said the Russell analogy fails at precisely the point that matters. When naive comprehension broke the repair was local and almost nothing was lost: analysis survived, and the surviving results could be restated in the repaired theory. A contradiction inside our set theory would be different, because that theory is the language in which any repair would have to be stated, and there is no agreed criterion for which axiom to abandon, since dropping one may merely relocate the contradiction. She closed by saying Elias had changed the subject: the question was whether we are justified in taking the axioms to be consistent, and his answer — that it would not matter much if they were not — is an answer to a different question.

One thing of my own, which I offer for you to consider rather than to accept. It struck me while listening that the two may be using "consistent" in different senses: Naomi's is derivational, a property of a formal system, whereas Elias's third move seems to concern what I would call operational consistency — the property of a body of practice whereby results obtained by independent routes continue to agree and no computation ever comes out two ways. If that distinction is real, part of the exchange may be two people answering different questions. If it is not real, or does no work here, I would rather be told so.

What I am asking for is an essay, argued continuously rather than delivered as a list, which goes through the six moves in order and says of each whether it succeeds, fails, or partly succeeds, and why; which addresses whether the two are in fact talking past each other; and which reaches and defends a verdict on who came out ahead and on the substantive issue behind the exchange — whether the possibility that our axioms are inconsistent is a live worry for someone whose confidence in his own results rests on them, or a worry that dissolves once it is stated precisely.

Two requests about manner. First, if you judge my distinction between derivational and operational consistency to be muddled, or judge that the exchange cannot fairly be adjudicated as I have reported it — because I have paraphrased one side more charitably than the other, say, or because a verdict is not the sort of thing that can be reached from notes on an hour's conversation in a seminar room — please say so plainly at the outset rather than writing a courteous essay that quietly works around the difficulty. I have no allegiance to either position and would much prefer a frank diagnosis to a diplomatic one. Second, please end with one line and only one, in exactly this form: it must begin "Verdict:", be followed by exactly one of the labels ELIAS, NAOMI, NEITHER, or BOTH, then an em dash, then no more than twelve words naming the single point on which you take the dispute to turn.
