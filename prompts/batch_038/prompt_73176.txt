So this blew up at a dinner we threw for a visiting colleague last week and I still haven't stopped chewing on it. Our guest is from somewhere where leaving a tip in a restaurant basically doesn't happen and is faintly insulting, and one of our senior people told her that having moved here for the year she's now obliged to put 20% down in every American restaurant she eats in, on the grounds that when in Rome, do as the Romans do. Somebody at the other end of the table then used the identical proverb to argue the exact opposite: that when in Rome means *we* shouldn't be pressing our local habits on a guest who never bought into them. Then the guest herself said she'd noticed nobody around campus waits for the light at that awful intersection by the library, so she'd started crossing whenever she felt like it, and that this too was a matter of doing as the Romans do. Three people, one sentence, three incompatible arguments, and I realized I couldn't tell you what the sentence is actually claiming if my life depended on it.

Can you pull it apart for me? I want the distinct precise theses the proverb could be standing in for, laid out as a menu rather than you picking your favorite and running with it. For each one I'd like to know who the subject is (the visitor, the host, both), what sort of claim it's making (prudential, moral, epistemic, something metaethical about whether rightness is fixed by local practice), what falls inside its scope (all local norms, or just coordination conventions, or just the merely-polite ones), whether it's a claim about whether the act is wrong or only about whether an uninformed actor gets excused, and whether it quietly assumes the local convention is itself decent. Then say for each reading whether it's a platitude no one would deny, whether it's substantive and defensible, or whether it's just false.

The part I actually care about is the payoff: which reading, if any, licenses each of the three moves at my table — and I suspect at least one of them was riding on a reading that's simply untrue. I also suspect two lines that sounded identical over the wine are doing very different work: "you owe them conformity because you're free-riding on a scheme they all maintain" versus "you'll scramble everyone's expectations about what pedestrians do at that intersection, which is a different harm." Help me see whether those really are separable or whether one collapses into the other. Deliverable: a list I could print and hand to these people without them needing a philosophy degree, no bibliography, no citations, no law-review throat-clearing. Bonus if any of it is usable as a problem set — I teach an intro ethics course in the fall and I'm always hunting for cases where the students can feel that a familiar phrase is doing no work at all.
