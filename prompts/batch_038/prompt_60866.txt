You are to write as a philosopher of mind who has also designed a good many seminar exercises in which the philosophical work is carried by the mechanics rather than by the facilitator's mouth. Think carefully before answering, and treat every constraint below as binding.

**Setting.** I convene an MA-level philosophy of mind seminar: sixteen students, one 110-minute session, a room with movable tables, a whiteboard, index cards, markers, and a bag of identical tokens. No laptops, no money, nothing of value to award. The students have each sat through one lecture on the exclusion problem and one on nonreductive physicalism, but I will not assume they retain anything more determinate than the mood that the mental might somehow be left out of the causal story. I want a game that makes them produce that exclusion pressure themselves, apply it, and then discover what their own application presupposed. They should not be told what the activity is about until the debrief; the vocabulary of the literature must not appear on any card, board, handout, or instruction sheet, and if a student names the problem aloud mid-game I need a specified facilitator move that neither confirms nor derails the round.

**The device I want you to develop.** Two ledgers are kept of the same events on the same board. A mover acts; a *mechanical recorder* writes down every token displacement in a vocabulary restricted to positions, contacts, and a published rule table; a *content recorder* writes down what happened in a vocabulary of what the mover was after, took to be the case, and did on purpose. An audit panel then rules, round by round, on whether any entry in the content ledger did causal work that the mechanical ledger did not already account for. The panel is scored against a sealed key that I hold, so that verdicts can be wrong and being wrong is visible. Please take this skeleton and make it a real game: refine it, replace components that will not survive contact with sixteen people, and specify whatever I have left unspecified.

**Round architecture.** I want four rounds that differ in causal configuration rather than merely in difficulty, so that the criterion the auditors are implicitly using gets forced into the open:

1. A baseline round in which the mechanical ledger plainly suffices, and in which the audit rule is learned.
2. A round in which the mechanical recorder cannot write a complete ledger without reading a card the mover holds, because the published rule table makes the token's path depend on what is written on that card. Here the content ledger looks indispensable, and the panel should be tempted to award it causal work.
3. A round of the same outward shape in which the mechanical ledger can be completed without the card, because a marked board position fully determines the switch. The panel must notice that nothing changed except their access to the determination, and must rule again.
4. A round in which the content ledger lets the panel predict a token's destination in advance while the mechanical ledger can be written only after the displacement, so that the panel must decide whether the criterion they have been applying all along tracked causation or tracked predictability.

Design the information structure deliberately: who may see the cards, who may see the rule table, who may confer, and when. The felt asymmetry between the two ledgers should be a product of access, not of the panel's cleverness.

**Required deliverables.** Materials list; role assignments and how to allocate sixteen people across them; complete rules and the scoring scheme, including what the sealed key says in each round and why; a timeline in minutes that fits 110 including the debrief; the debrief itself, which must map particular moments in play onto particular premises and moves in the argument rather than summarise the argument; the predictable failure modes of a first run and how to handle each; and variants for eight students and for thirty. In the debrief I want the familiar replies sorted by which premise they attack — the closure-type premise, the premise ruling out routine double causation, the exclusion principle itself, or the underlying criterion of causal relevance — including at least the reply which says that the mechanical cause is too fine-grained to be the rival of the content-level cause at all, so that no competition arises. Describe that reply on its own terms; do not send me to anyone's paper. Then say whether the mechanics of round 3 can be adjusted to give that reply traction, and what the adjustment costs.

**Two things I want you to be unusually frank about.** First, which feature of the argument this activity structurally cannot reproduce, and what I must therefore supply by hand: I suspect the game trivially favours the mechanical ledger because that ledger is inspectable by the students while the real physical account of a real action is not, and I want to know how much of that asymmetry your design manages to neutralise and how much remains. Second, which step in the debrief is most likely to collapse into me simply telling them the answer.

**Constraints on the write-up.** No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, no attributions of positions to named philosophers beyond what a seminar of this level already assumes, and no requirement that students have read anything beforehand. Everything the participants need must be statable on cards and a whiteboard. Do not invent sources.

**Format.** Open with a single sentence that states the core mechanism of the game and the one thing it is engineered to make the students feel; then proceed directly into materials, roles, rules, the four rounds, the timeline, the debrief, the failure modes, the variants, and the frank notes. No preface, no restatement of my request, no framing remarks about the topic. If you want to show your work — candidate mechanisms you considered and rejected, with one line on why each failed — put that at the very end, after everything else.
