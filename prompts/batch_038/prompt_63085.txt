I am a third-year philosophy, politics and economics student taking a seminar on global justice, and I would be grateful for some help untangling a slogan that has proved surprisingly difficult to argue against. A few weeks ago our discussion turned to whether an affluent state's obligations to the global poor are as stringent as its obligations to its own worst-off citizens. I suggested that a needs-based account applied impartially does not obviously license the difference in stringency that most of the class seemed to assume. A classmate replied, with evident confidence, that charity begins at home: on her account, one cannot owe a stranger in another country what one owes one's fellow citizens, and a state that funds foreign poverty relief before it has attended to disadvantage at home is simply getting its priorities wrong. Another student then added what he took to be a supporting point — that we know what our own people need in a way that we cannot know about people on the other side of the world — and the exchange moved on with the slogan unchallenged. I am inclined to think that compatriots do not have special claims of the kind she was asserting, but I could not find anything to say at the time, and I suspect my silence reflected my inability to pin down what the slogan was actually asserting rather than any real strength it possesses.

I am now drafting the opening section of my seminar paper around exactly this difficulty, so what I want is a disambiguation rather than a refutation. Could you lay out, as a list I can work through, the different precise claims that "charity begins at home" might be taken to express in a debate of this kind? My own guess is that it might be functioning as a claim about the scope of beneficence, as a claim about how much weight to give compatriots' equally pressing needs, as a claim about the relational source of certain duties, as a claim about what is permitted rather than what is required, as an empirical claim about the effectiveness of locally administered aid, or as a claim about the proper division of moral labour among agents and institutions; I expect there are readings I have not thought of, and I expect several of mine will collapse into one another. For each reading you identify, please say what it commits one to, whether it is a platitude that a cosmopolitan can accept without embarrassment, a substantive and contestable thesis, or simply false — and, the part that matters most for my paper, whether it is strong enough to deliver the conclusion my classmate drew from it, namely that a state may (or must) discharge its domestic obligations before attending to greater need abroad.

Two further things would be especially useful. First, please flag any point at which the slogan's apparent force depends on sliding between readings that a careful defender would keep apart, for instance between permission and requirement, or between an impartial principle that can endorse local partiality as an instrument and a principle that is itself partial. Second, I would like your view on whether the proverb's own vocabulary is doing any work here: my classmate said "charity," but the seminar was arguing about obligations of justice, and I have a vague sense that this substitution matters, though I cannot articulate why. Please do not trouble yourself with references or suggestions for further reading; I want the analysis itself, stated in terms I can check against my own reasoning rather than attributed to particular authors.
