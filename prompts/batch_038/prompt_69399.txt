I'm a volunteer on the emergency management advisory committee for the county I live in — small place, about 40k people, three canyon towns and a lot of dry grass. My day job is teaching 8th grade earth science, so I'm the one who's supposedly good with the maps. This spring we signed on with a vendor whose wildfire model was built out of eleven years of our own incident logs, and to their credit they were upfront in the demo that those logs include the evacuation orders we issued, so the forecast accounts for our behavior too. It gives you one map for 36 hours out and one number for the thing we actually care about. It went three for three last season, including two fires where our own road closures and bus convoys changed how things unfolded and it still called the outcome, which the company turned into a case study about us.

Now we've got the Alder Creek fire, which started Monday and is about 12 miles from the bench road, and the model says 71% that it reaches the inhabited part of the benches by Thursday evening. Dale — retired insurance adjuster, easily the most numerate guy on the committee — made an argument in the parking lot after the last meeting that I have not been able to shake, and I want the writeup to actually grapple with it instead of waving it off. He says: the number already assumes we do whatever we're going to do, because its trained on us. So the 71% is the number for the one world where we make the choice we end up making. Which means it can't be a reason for evacuating or against it — if we evacuate, that's in the number, and if we don't, that's in the number too, and either way the benches burn or they don't and the forecast already knew which. His conclusion is that the model gives us no guidance whatsoever and we should decide like we always did. And I kept thinking of that old story where the guy sees Death in the marketplace and rides all night to get clear of the city and Death is sitting there waiting, and says that was the meeting place the whole time — Dale is pretty obviously making the point that running is pointless. Here's the part I can't get straight: I sort of accept his premise and completely reject his conclusion, and my sense is that he's using a true-ish observation as a way to not make a call, because the fire chief is telling us that evacuating the benches pulls the two crews we'd otherwise be putting on the line, and nobody wants to be the one who sent 400 people out of thier homes for nothing.

Could you draft the written opinion I'll circulate before we meet Thursday at 6 in the grange hall? A page, page and a half, plain language — the committee is three ranchers, a nurse, Dale, and me, and nobody has taken a philosophy class. I need a clear bottom line we can act on: what we do about Alder Creek between now and Thursday, and separately what we vote on about whether this model goes into our written trigger matrix for the season and on what terms. I need the reasons laid out so Dale and the chief can push back on them in the room, and I need the strongest thing going for Dale's position stated honestly, including whether he's right that a forecast of the single future that's actually going to happen is useless for choosing between options — and if there's a different kind of output from the same model that wouldn't be useless, because that might be something we demand from the vendor before we renew in the fall. If the answer is that we evacuate anyway, I want the memo to say why the number didn't settle it and what did.
