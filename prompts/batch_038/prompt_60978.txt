so I've been going in circles on the centipede game and backward induction, and I finally sat down and wrote out what I actually believe, because I have to present the package to a reading group that's half economists and half philosophers and I fully expect to get shredded. tell me where it breaks before they do.

(1) backward induction is just iterated elimination of dominated strategies in a trenchcoat. I don't think it needs any special epistemic story to be valid — you work back from the last node and that's that.

(2) but I also find the self-undermining argument genuinely compelling: if P1 passes at the first node, P2's premises are already blown by the time P2 has to think, so the induction never gets off the ground. to me that's the real content of "the centipede is a paradox," not the Pareto-badness of the predicted outcome.

(3) lab data don't test any of this. people playing a McKelvey-Palfrey style centipede for real money aren't playing the game I'm theorizing about, so "a bunch of subjects pass at node 3" is not evidence against (1).

(4) I'm a Bayesian all the way down. off-path beliefs should be whatever you get by conditonalizing on the action you observed, given a type space. Harsanyi's trick is the right way to represent every kind of uncertainty about your opponent and I dont want any other machinery in there.

(5) the chain-store / reputation resolution is right: a small prior on a tit-for-tat type makes early cooperation rational for a completely normal player in a finitely repeated game.

(6) trembling-hand perturbations I don't buy at all. a tiny probability that somebody's hand slips is a cheat and I refuse to let a solution concept lean on it.

(7) I'm also drawn to forward induction — if P2 is handed a node they were never supposed to reach, the right reaction is to assume P1 is rational and had a plan, not to assume P1 screwed up.

(8) my own pet idea, which nobody I've pitched it to likes: the reason the experimental centipede doesn't unravel isn't crazy types, it's horizon uncertainty. if there's a small chance the game just stops after any given node, the endgame is never known, and passing early stops being the sucker's move. no type heterogeneity needed.

go through these pairwise. which ones actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do until you write them out carefully? rank the real conflicts by how bad they are rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each say what the cheapest revision is and what I lose by making it — I want the price, not a shrug. and be straight with me about confidence: give me a rough sense of how sure you are of each verdict, and single out the one point in your whole audit that you're least sure of. I'd much rather you flag one as shaky than hedge everything to the same degree.
