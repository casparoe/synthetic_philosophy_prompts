I clerk on the constitutional court of a mid-sized democracy whose charter contains an enumerated bill of rights, a general limitation clause permitting such restrictions on rights as are reasonable and demonstrably justified in a free society, and strong-form judicial review: when we invalidate a statute it is invalid, and the legislature's only routes back are a constitutional amendment or a supermajority override that no government has yet attempted. I have been asked to prepare a bench memo for a case to be argued next term, and my judge wants the memo to begin from our own track record rather than from first principles. I am deliberately not naming the jurisdiction. I want the analysis to run on the holdings set out below, not on real case law you may have memorized, and I want no citations, no case names from other systems, and no reading list. If any principle you identify corresponds to a position you know from the literature, you may say so in a clause; the substance must be your own articulation.

Our decisions in the eleven years since the charter took effect, in the order decided:

1. A statute removing the franchise from members of a small, geographically dispersed linguistic minority on the ground that they held secondary residence permits, with no provision for restoration: invalidated.
2. A national consumption tax whose incidence fell heavily on the poorest decile: upheld, with the observation that the distribution of the tax burden is a question the assembly is competent to settle and that the equality guarantee does not reach it.
3. A prohibition on a widely used analgesic, enacted in nine days under emergency procedure with no committee stage, on the strength of a single study later retracted; the patient population was large, enfranchised, and organized in an association that campaigned for repeal: upheld. We said that the cure for a hasty statute is a later statute. The assembly repealed it twenty-two months on.
4. A criminal prohibition on a ritual practice central to a large, well-organized faith that had twice lobbied the assembly successfully on other matters, where the same statute granted exemptions to three other faiths for comparable practices and the record disclosed no reason for the distinction: invalidated.
5. A general licensing and bond requirement for periodicals, facially neutral, enacted on a party-line vote six days after a newspaper printed corruption allegations against the governing party; the committee minutes recorded the sponsor describing the measure as the answer to that newspaper: invalidated.
6. A floor amendment, with no committee report and no articulated rationale, raising the minimum age for standing for municipal office from 21 to 30, in a charter that guarantees the right to stand for election subject to reasonable qualifications: upheld, on the ground that a line must be drawn somewhere and that those below it retain the vote and may campaign. My judge dissented, on the ground that a bright line drawn without any reason given is precisely what the limitation clause requires the assembly to justify.
7. A statute postponing one regional election for a single term after severe civil disorder, passed by a cross-party supermajority with a self-executing sunset guaranteeing that the election would occur within eighteen months: upheld.

The new case is this. The assembly has criminalized knowingly false statements of fact about the conduct of a federal election — the counting, the certification, the eligibility of officials to serve — after two years of hearings, expert testimony, and a public consultation; it passed on a cross-party supermajority. Lobbying by the press secured carve-outs for opinion, parody, and honest error. The act carries a five-year sunset and a mandatory review by the responsible committee, and it binds no future assembly. It also carries a clause reciting that the assembly has itself determined the restriction to be a justified limit under the limitation clause, and directing that judicial review is confined to asking whether the assembly manifestly exceeded the bounds of a determination open to it. Those most exposed are fringe candidates, small publishers, and partisans without institutional backing: all enfranchised, numerous, badly organized, and largely unrepresented in the proceedings that produced the act.

What I need from the memo:

- Set out the candidate principles that jointly account for all seven earlier decisions. There is more than one. State each as a rule a judge could apply — which feature of a statute it treats as decisive — and show how each survives holdings 3 and 4 in particular, since those two appear to pull in opposite directions.
- Take each candidate to the new case, say what it yields, and say whether it yields it easily or by a stretch.
- Give your best guess at our actual ruling, including the possibility of severance: invalidating the deference directive while leaving the criminal prohibition standing, or leaving the directive and declining to review the prohibition at all. Identify the closest analog among the seven, and say what makes it closest rather than merely similar.
- For each branch of the decision, state what principle we would thereby be adopting, and which of our own holdings would become hardest to defend afterward.
- Construct the further hypothetical case that would best discriminate between the two leading candidates, and say which way we should expect to decide it.
- Finally: where our track record genuinely underdetermines the new case, should the fact that we are unelected break the tie toward deference, or is that tie-break itself a substantive principle whose consequences we would then own? I want an answer here, not a summary of both positions.
