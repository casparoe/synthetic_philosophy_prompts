I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working in jurisprudence — mostly rules of recognition and constituent power — and I've been reading EU law on the side because I'm TA-ing a seminar that touches on it. There's a pattern in how the EU is put together that I keep circling without being able to name, so I want you to name it. Here's the pattern as I've got it (correct me if I've garbled something):

- The Union only has the powers the Treaties confer on it. No general legislative competence, no residual power to legislate for the common good.
- Amending the Treaties needs unanimity among the member states and, on the main routes, ratification by each of them under its own constitutional rules. Even the shortcut procedures, and the clauses that let you swap unanimity for majority voting, need unanimity to trigger and give national parliaments a veto window. So the Union cannot amend the instrument that constitutes it; its members, acting together, can.
- Meanwhile there's an exit clause a single member state can pull on its own.
- And yet the operative content of the order has changed enormously without any of that machinery being used. The Court of Justice decided early on that Treaty provisions can create rights individuals enforce in their own national courts, and that EU law prevails over conflicting national law including statutes passed later; ordinary national courts are supposed to disaply the offending national provision themselves rather than wait for it to be repealed or annulled. The Court has also built doctrines nobody wrote down — member state liability for breaches of EU law, implied external powers, fundamental rights as general principles — and it describes all of it as declaring what the Treaties always meant. It also treats the preamble's aspiration to ever closer union among the peoples of Europe as licence to read powers purposively rather than narrowly.
- And when a ruling would wreck a lot of settled arrangements, the Court sometimes says its interpretation only bites going forward.
- On top of that, several national constitutional courts — Germany's most famously — reserve the right to refuse to apply an EU act they judge to go beyond the conferred powers or to violate the unamendable core of thier constitution. A power asserted for decades and used, at most, once.

So: a legal order that cannot formally amend itself, whose content keeps changing anyway through a court that insists it isn't changing anything, enforced bottom-up by thousands of national judges told to set aside their own legislature's statutes, with the members' constitutional courts claiming the last word and never spending it.

What I want from you:

Be explicit about what it assumes about who authored the order (the states? the peoples separately? a single European people?), whether there's a determinate fact about what the Treaties mean that is independent of the Court's say-so, and whether declaring the meaning of a law and changing a law can come apart at all.
2. Separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits comfortably with. I especially want the underdetermined spots — places where two or three rival theories of authority would generate exactly the same institutional behaviour, so you couldn't tell them apart from outside.
3. Is there a real contradiction between the Court's "we only declare" self-description and its power to limit a ruling's temporal effects? And between "only the members can amend" and the Court's ability to shift the boundaries of the competences in practice? Tell me whether these are reconcilable and what the reconciliation costs.
4. What would have to change on the ground — procedure, not just theory — if we swapped the presupposed view for one on which the EU is a self-amending constitutional order authored by a single European demos? Which pieces of the current practice would become unintelligible under that replacement?
