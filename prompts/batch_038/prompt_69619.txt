You're an expert in formal epistemology and epistemic utility theory — imagine you're the person a forecasting program calls when they want their probability training grounded in something real rather than in slogans. Before you write the deliverable, spend half a dozen lines getting the underlying theory straight for yourself: proper scoring, strict propriety, why an accuracy-seeker's own current credence always looks best to them from where they're sitting. Then write the thing.

Situation: I teach philosophy but I also advise a small state epi-modeling group. Six analysts, no formal training, they submit probability forecasts monthly and an outside outfit scores them on a Brier-type rule we cant change and have no input into. I want to hand them a reusable audit they can run in about twenty minutes before submitting — steps in order, what counts as passing each step, where the audit stops and they just file the number.

It has to actually do four things:

1. Give them a pencil-and-paper way to check a set of numbers hangs together, without any Dutch-book arithmetic and without needing to know what a convex set is.
2. Confront the immodesty problem head on. Telling them "report your honest credence, it's the one that maximizes your expected accuracy" is useless to a person holding a pen — I need the procedure to say what to do instead, or to admit there's nothing to do.
3. Have a real step for the evidence question. There's a clean old result to the effect that free evidence is never expected to make an accuracy-seeker worse off, and my analysts violate it constantly by saying "let's just wait for Thursday's report" — but I know it has conditions and I want them stated rather than papered over.
4. Mark every place where the procedure is judgment wearing a lab coat.

Then run it on two cases: an analyst sitting on 0.15 for hospital-overflow-by-March who wants to hold it until a report lands Thursday; and two analysts at 0.3 and 0.7 who propose to average and file 0.5. Make the second one come out in a way they will hate.

Last thing: my own rule of thumb, which I've been handing out for years, is that if you can name a specific piece of evidence that would move you, you're obliged to go look for it before you report. Tell me whether that survives contact with the theory, and if it does, which step of the procedure it belongs in.
