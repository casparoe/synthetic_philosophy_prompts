So I was up until like 1am last night arguing with a friend of mine — she's from Aarhus, works on the admin side of my department, eleven years in the US now — about whether Americans are just being paranoid about government or whether the Scandinavians are the naive ones. We went in circles. Her line: look at the outcomes, low corruption, people comply, the state actually delivers, so the trust is earned, and it's also part of what produces those outcomes in the first place. My line: that's what a well-run protection racket looks like from the inside, and the reason there's so much checks-and-balances machinery here is that the place was founded by people who'd been on the receiving end of an unaccountable state. She said my position proves her point becuase I was assuming the thing that's supposed to be earned. Nobody budged, we were both a little drunk, and she has a whiteboard in her kitchen which I'm not going to get into. But walking home it hit me that a big chunk of the problem is that we were using one word for several different things and arguing about which of them was reasonable.

That's the seed I want turned into a proposal. My hunch: both sides of this debate treat "trust in government" as a single attitude with a single object, but there are at least three objects that come apart — trusting the particular people currently holding office, trusting the office itself (that whoever ends up in it is boxed in by its rules and by other people's ability to see what they're doing), and trusting the system's capacity to notice and repair its own screwups. Orthogonal to that, there's trust as a calibrated prediction versus trust as a stance you adopt partly because adopting it helps make it true, and I suspect the "Scandinavian trust is self-fulfilling, so it's a different kind of thing than American suspicion" move is where the real philosophical action is, and also where both sides get sloppy. I also suspect the reasonableness conditions differ per object: the predictive one is answerable to evidence in a straightforward way, the others maybe aren't, which would mean "is suspicion or trust the more reasonable attitude" is not currently a question with a determinate answer at all.

I'm applying to our center's little interdisciplinary seed fund — modest money, one semester of course relief, call it five months of actual work — and the review panel is two political scientists, one philosopher, and someone from the dean's office who tells applicants plainly that she reads for "why should the university spend money on this." Write me the two-page proposal. It needs: the question stated so that it has a determinate answer once my distinctions are in place; an account of why the existing framings leave it open — the performance-earns-trust side, the suspicion-as-default-rationality side, and also the reliance-versus-trust stuff and the account where trust is about believing the other party's interests encapsulate yours, which I think get partway there but not all the way; what I would actually do, stage by stage across the five months, and why that rather than the two obvious alternatives (commissioning or fielding a new survey — I can't do that, no data skills and no time; or a from-the-ground-up conceptual analysis of trust — too big and it would just rehash); what a successful deliverable looks like in a form the political scientists on the panel would recognize as useful; and a genuine failure condition, something like the distinctions not surviving contact with cases where it matters, or splitting the objects not changing anybody's verdict about which attitude is reasonable, in which case I've just relabelled things. Be honest about what could make this too big for five months. Leave out citations entirely, I'll bolt those on myself — refer to positions and arguments descriptively. Also don't write it as though I already know which side wins.
