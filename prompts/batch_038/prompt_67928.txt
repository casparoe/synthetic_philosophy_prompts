I am drafting a novel whose central character is a live-in housekeeper under a patron of genuine generosity: he has never refused her a request, never punished her for one, and by all appearances intends to go on indulging her. A political theorist friend who read the manuscript told me flatly that she is unfree, and when I objected that nothing whatever stands in her way, he repeated a maxim he plainly considers settled — no one is free who depends upon another's goodwill. I would like to answer him properly rather than merely feel that my chapter is confused.

Could you take that maxim apart for me? It strikes me as capable of standing for several quite distinct theses: something about how frequently interference actually occurs, something about what the patron could do at will and without answer, something about her having to watch her tone and ask rather than take, something about whether her position would survive a change in his temper, and perhaps something further about the relationship being a status rather than a record of conduct. Set these readings out separately, ordered from weakest to strongest, and for each state it precisely and say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or simply false.

Then tell me which, if any, entitles my friend to call her unfree while conceding that the patron has never interfered once. How does the strongest reading depart from the older idea of freedom as the absence of obstacles in one's way, and does it commit him to uncomfortable verdicts elsewhere — a wife supported by a devoted husband, a citizen whose rights rest on a parliament's continuing good humour? Please write throughout in British English.
