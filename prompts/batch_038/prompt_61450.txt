I'm a second-year philosophy student in a seminar on distributive justice, and for the past three weeks we have been working through the "equality of what?" debate: Sen's critique of resourcism and welfarism, Dworkin's equality of resources and his treatment of expensive tastes, and the capability approach that developed out of Sen's work. Our section has eight people, and our TA has a habit of announcing that she finds the capability approach "obviously right," which I don't think is helping any of us get clear on where the actual disagreements lie. To make the readings concrete, she has been using a running case all month: a university accessibility office with a fixed annual budget that has to choose between two schemes. Scheme A gives every student with a registered disability the same flat cash stipend, to spend however the student sees fit. Scheme B spends the money directly on individually assessed accommodations — captioning, note-takers, assistive software, extended exam time, accessible transport vouchers — with whatever remains going to the students whose needs are costliest to meet. After class on Thursday, two people in the section, whom I'll call Nadia and Theo, got into a sustained argument about the case at the coffee shop. I mostly listened. Neither was grandstanding, and both made points I couldn't answer, which is why I would rather have your assessment than my own. I'm writing my seminar paper on this case and I don't want to simply transcribe their argument; I want to know where it actually breaks down.

Here is the exchange as best I can reconstruct it:

1. Theo opened by saying Scheme B is plainly correct, because the whole lesson of the capability critique is that what matters is not the bundle a person receives but what that person can do and be with it. Identical cash converts into radically different functionings for a blind student, a student with a chronic illness, and a student with a mild processing disorder. Handing all three the same amount and calling it equality, he said, is to mistake the metric: it equalizes the input while leaving the substantive inequality untouched.

2. Nadia said that argument proves far too much. If the office's obligation tracks conversion rates, then it owes something to the student who is simply bad at statistics, to the one who is painfully shy in seminar, and to the piano major who needs a serious instrument to get out of her tuition what others get for free. She said Theo had collapsed the distinction between disability and expensive taste, and that the expensive-taste objection is exactly what sinks this kind of view: once you compensate for whatever makes a person's projects costly to pursue, there is no principled stopping point.

3. Theo said the analogy confuses two different things. The pianist's case concerns how much satisfaction she wants out of a bundle; the wheelchair user's case concerns general-purpose means that anyone would want regardless of their tastes. He said the capability view is oriented toward a set of central functionings — being able to move about, to be educated, to take part in the life of one's community — not toward maximizing anyone's preference intensity, and that the pianist can achieve the same functioning through far cheaper means than a concert-grade piano.

4. Nadia granted that framing but said it only relocates the problem. Somebody must decide which functionings count as central and how much of a given student's disadvantage counts as an obstacle to them, and once the office does that it has administrators adjudicating the inner lives of nineteen-year-olds. She said the cash scheme's real virtue is that it hands that judgment back to the people whose lives they are, and that Scheme B is the humiliating option, since it requires documentation, diagnosis, and a caseworker's approval before a student gets what he needs.

5. Theo said the "who decides" objection isn't neutral in the way she was implying. Scheme A is also a decision: it settles in advance that every disability is worth the same dollar figure, and it settles that by fiat rather than by looking at anyone. On humiliation, he added that plenty of disabled students would say the flat stipend is the degrading option, since it makes them privately purchase their way around barriers the institution itself created.

6. Nadia replied that this was an empirical claim, and empirically false in the direction that matters: if you actually asked the students, a good number would take the cash, and that is evidence about how they judge their own needs. Theo said she couldn't have it both ways, because the capability literature's account of adaptive preference formation is precisely a reason not to treat what disadvantaged people say they want as the measure of what they are owed — a student who insists she manages fine without captioning may simply have stopped expecting to be included.

7. Nadia said adaptive preference formation is a double-edged instrument he was swinging carelessly. If the testimony of affected people is untrustworthy because their expectations were shaped by deprivation, then there is no reason to trust Theo's assessment of their needs either, since his was shaped by a literature that tells him in advance what deprivation looks like. At that point, she said, his position amounts to "trust the theorist over the person," which is not a principle anyone should be comfortable operating under.

8. Theo called that a cheap move — distrust of one source of evidence is not general distrust — but he conceded that he had no good account of where the office should stop compensating, and said that if pressed he would compensate for unloving parents and for unattractive looks as well, in principle, since those too convert resources into functionings badly. Nadia said the concession was the whole argument: a view that compensates for everything compensates for nothing, and in practice it turns an accessibility office into an agency for managing students' personalities.

A third person at the table, who hadn't spoken until then, said they were both missing the point because they were treating the choice of metric and the choice of institutional mechanism as if they were one question. I couldn't tell whether that was a genuine criticism or just a way of refusing to pick a side.

Please go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right at each step. I'd like a rating for each of the eight moves — clearly mistaken, partly right, clearly right, or whatever scale you think fits — with a sentence or two of justification, and then a verdict on who is more right overall about the underlying question, which is what the metric of equality should be in a case of this kind, not merely which scheme the office ought to adopt. I'd also like your view on what both of them missed, including whether the third student's remark about metric versus mechanism actually identifies a confusion or just dodges. Please be committed rather than evenhanded: take a definite position on each substantive dispute and give your reasons, instead of laying out considerations on both sides and leaving me to weigh them, and say plainly which moves were bad arguments even when they came from the person you end up agreeing with more. If any of your verdicts depend on a contested reading of Sen or Dworkin, flag that dependency and say what the competing reading would do to the verdict.
