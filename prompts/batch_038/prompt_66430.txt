I co-teach an upper-level seminar on feminist ethics and every single year we come out of the care ethics unit with the students convinced that care ethics basically amounts to "be warm, stay close, put the relationship first" — a nice-feelings supplement to the real theories. I want to break that with a designed case, and I'd rather you build it to spec than grab an example off the shelf. So: construct a scenario in which a care ethicist is committed to the harsher action — ending or drastically stepping back from a hands-on caring relationship — while a Kantian deontologist is committed to continuing it. Fully inverted from the stereotype, so the one doing the abandoning is the care theorist and the one saying stay, keep showing up, don't override her is the deontologist. The point of the exercise is to test whether care ethics has any non-derived normative content of its own, and that's what should fix what counts as cheating here: if the care verdict is just a warm-toned restatement of something the deontologist could reach by her own route, the case fails and I want to know that it failed.

Constraints, and they're all load bearing. (a) Realistic and ordinary — a family, a friendship, a parish visiting rota, not a trolley, not a lifeboat, no sci-fi. (b) The scenario itself should be one paragraph. (c) The cared-for has to be fully competent and articulate: no advanced dementia, no non-verbal patients, no small children, because the easy care-ethics move is "she can't tell you what she needs so you have to attend for her," and I want that move unavailable. Instead she accepts the care, is genuinely grateful, and would say no if you asked whether she wants the carer to step back — but she'd say no for a reason that is a symptom of the problem rather than a free expression of what's good for her, something like the fact that saying yes would mean telling the person who loves her that this love has been quietly harming her. (d) The care ethicist's verdict can't come from the carer being burnt out or from the carer's own interests — that's the cheap version everybody reaches for. It has to fall out of care ethics' own machinery: motivational displacement, attentiveness, Noddings' insistence that caring isn't caring until it has been taken up and responded to by the person cared for, Tronto's fourth phase, and the worry about the carer's own project silently replacing the ends of the cared-for. (e) No head-counting and no appeal to overall welfare. (f) The foreseeable cost of stepping back should be real and concrete — a bad few months, a fall that isn't catastrophic, something she valued going undone — but not disastrous, because if the care ethicist is obviously reckless the deontologist wins for boring reasons.

Then show your work. Walk through what each theory says about the case and why, in enough detail that I could hand it to third-years, and give the Kantian her best version rather than a strawman — I assume she'll lean on expressed consent, the duty of beneficence, and the charge of paternalism. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work in generating the split, tell me what happens to both verdicts if I delete that feature, and tell me how a defender of the deontological side would attack the case — I'm expecting something along the lines of "your care theorist is just paternalism with nicer vocabulary," or "you smuggled consequentialism back in through the reception condition," or "letting the carer's read of an unexpressed need override the person's actual choices is exactly what care ethics is supposed to forbid." Finish with the honest verdict on whether care ethics did any independent normative work, and if its the latter, say what would have to be added to the case to make it not the latter. For seeding: the rough shape I had in mind is a woman in her fifties who moved back to run her mother's life after a mild stroke — the appointments, the pills, the money, the phone calls, the driving — where the mother used to be the one who ran things and held a job and hosted everybody. But definately throw mine out if you can get the inversion from somewhere better.
