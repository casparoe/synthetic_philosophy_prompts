I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and in November I am running a forty-five-minute Saturday seminar for about twenty-five readers: intelligent, no mathematics beyond school algebra, and several of them have already met the popular accounts of ergodicity economics and the claim that maximising expected value is a confidence trick. The topic is the Kelly criterion and the difference between maximising expected value and maximising long-run growth rate.

Please do not explain the material to me; plan how I should explain it. I want the order in which the pieces have to arrive and what each one depends on — in particular, how much of compounding and multiplicative structure must be in place before the split between arithmetic and geometric means means anything at all, and whether ergodicity should be named at this level or left as jargon I expressly decline to unpack. I am minded to build the whole session around one repeated wager that either grows a stake by 50 per cent or shrinks it by 40: tell me whether that case can carry the weight or whether it will mislead, and if it cannot, what should replace it and what properties the replacement must have.

Then map the misconceptions. I expect at least that expected value is simply an error, that the Kelly formula reveals your edge rather than consuming an estimate of it, and that the logarithm is nothing but risk aversion under a new name. Say at which point in the sequence each must be headed off before it sets, and which I should let the audience voice for themselves.

Close with one question I can put to the room that would separate those who have grasped why averaging across players and averaging across time come apart from those who have merely learned to distrust the phrase expected value, and tell me what I should announce at the outset as beyond the scope of the session.
