You are to act as a philosopher of language serving as the neutral drafter for an adversarial collaboration. You are equally at home in formal semantics (compositionality, embedding, projection, multidimensional and use-conditional accounts of meaning) and in the sociolinguistics of taboo, sanction, and reclamation. Two camps have been arguing past each other for years, and you have been asked to produce the document they should have produced. Think carefully and treat both positions as sophisticated, resourceful, and worth their defenders' allegiance; neither may be caricatured, and you may not adjudicate in your own voice.

**The two parties.**

*The Semanticist.* Derogatory content is part of the conventional, literal meaning of a slur. A slur is not simply a neutral term plus a bad reputation; something derogatory is encoded by the word itself, and that is why substitution of a co-referential neutral counterpart is never innocuous. Within this camp there are sub-variants — some locate the derogatory content in truth-conditional descriptive content (e.g., a moralized predication of the sort defended by Hom's combinatorial externalism), others treat it as a separate, non-displaceable expressive dimension (the Potts-style line), others as indexical on the speaker's own social allegiance (Nunberg's social indexicality). Your document should note where these variants matter and where they do not.

*The Prohibitionist.* Slurs carry no derogatory content in their meanings at all. What makes a word a slur is that its use is standing-prohibited; the offense, harm, and sanction attach to the act of using a prohibited word, not to anything the word says or encodes. On this view (the Anderson–Lepore line) the word does no semantic work beyond that of its neutral counterpart, and attempts to read derogation into the lexicon mistake a social fact about use for a fact about meaning.

**Shared stipulations.** Both parties agree to work with an invented word, so that no real slur appears in the document. Let *skrell* be a slur for members of a group called the Velani, and let *Velani* itself be its neutral counterpart, co-referential with it. Both parties accept the following as background data and methodology: that competent speakers judge the substitution of *skrell* for *Velani* as never merely stylistic; that the offensiveness of an utterance containing *skrell* cannot be removed by adding a disclaimer of intent; that reclamation by in-group speakers is real, widespread, and partial; that the harms are serious and not merely about hurt feelings; and that compositionality, together with the standard tests for truth-conditionality (negation, embedding, indirect report, mention) is the shared framework for deciding whether some content is literally encoded. Both also agree that speaker intentions alone cannot settle the matter.

**The cases the collaboration was convened to settle.** Treat all five as stipulated: their facts are granted by both parties, and only the interpretation is contested.

- *N1, the decreed neologism.* Fifteen years ago activists deliberately coined *skrell* as a slur, publicized the stereotypes it was meant to carry, and got it adopted: it now appears in graffiti, is shouted in confrontations, and is listed among offensive words. It has no earlier history. Yet a large fraction of speakers, including many Velani, report being offended by its bare utterance while explicitly disbelieving every stereotype attached to it and being unable to say what it conveys beyond the fact that it is not to be used.
- *N2, the prohibited neutral.* *Brill* began as an ordinary, neutral demonym for the Velani. A sustained campaign has made its use prohibited: it now reliably draws the same sanctions, offense, and social cost as *skrell*, and every competent speaker knows this. Is *brill* a slur now? Does it have derogatory meaning? What do the two theories predict about whether it embeds and reports like *skrell*?
- *N3, the scrubbed speaker.* A speaker S has produced a large corpus in which *skrell* occurs only under negation, in conditionals and questions, and in bare mention — for example in sentences denying that any Velani is a *skrell*, warning a child to report anyone who calls them one, and asking whether someone was so called. S never uses the word assertorically. Both parties accept that hearers nonetheless find many of these tokens jarring, and that S is socially sanctioned for some of them.
- *N4, the unknowing user.* A lexicographer who grew up outside the community, holding no derogatory beliefs and unaware of the prohibition, uses *skrell* in good faith in a public lecture. The audience takes offense. Both parties accept that the offense occurred and that the speaker is not blameworthy in the ordinary way.
- *N5, the two communities.* In community A, *skrell* has been reclaimed and is used affectionately among Velani themselves; in community B it remains a prohibited slur with no reclamation. A speaker from A uses it in B, among Velani and non-Velani alike. Both parties accept that the utterance offends in B and does not in A, and that the same speaker, returning home, offends no one.

**Required deliverable.** Aim for roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words, in four parts, with headings.

*Part 0 — Diagnosis.* Before drafting anything, work out aloud, in about three hundred words, where these two camps have been talking past each other. Identify the senses of *meaning*, *content*, *convention*, and *offense* on which the dispute is merely terminological, and state the regimentation you will use in the rest of the document so that no agreed claim and no disagreement row is vitiated by equivocation.

*Part I — Agreed claims.* A numbered list of between twelve and twenty claims both parties can sign. These must be substantive, not platitudes: they should include claims about the five cases, about what the embedding and report data show and do not show, about reclamation, about the relationship between prohibition and meaning, and about which phenomena any adequate account owes an explanation of. Every claim must be worded so that a committed defender of each side would sign it without qualification.

*Part II — Disagreements table.* A table with the following columns: the issue; the Semanticist's formulation of the disagreement, phrased so that the Semanticist accepts it as a fair statement of their own view; the Prohibitionist's formulation, likewise; whether the disagreement is genuine or verbal, with a reason; if genuine, what exactly would have to be settled to resolve it; the case, argument, or piece of evidence both parties accept as bearing on it (draw on N1–N5, using at least four of them, and you may add further agreed-upon tests); and each party's prediction, recorded in advance, about how that test will come out.

*Part III — Cruxes and pre-committed concessions.* Rank the genuine disagreements by how much settling them would move the debate, and say why. For each of the top three, state what each party has agreed in advance to give up if the crux resolves against them — the specific claim they will abandon and the specific revision to their view they will accept. Be concrete: a concession that amounts to nothing more than a willingness to reconsider is not a concession.

*Part IV — Residual disagreement.* Two short paragraphs, one signed by each party in the first person plural, stating what remains disputed after all of the above and why each side thinks its residue is not merely verbal.

**Constraints.** Do not decide who is right, and do not split the difference by inventing a third view of your own unless both parties could sign onto it as a genuine compromise. Do not produce a bibliography, a reference list, or citations of any kind; where you allude to positions in the literature, describe them rather than cite them. If you judge that some part of the dispute is not resolvable at the level of semantic theory at all — because it turns on a normative or political question about what a theory of meaning is for — say so explicitly in Part 0 and locate it there rather than smuggling it into a row of the table.
