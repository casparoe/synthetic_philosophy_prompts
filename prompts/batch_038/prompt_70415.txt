I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working on imprecise credences, so updating on conditional information is adjacent to my area but not it. A chapter in a handbook-style volume meant to bring non-specialists up to speed on formal epistemology glosses the Judy Benjamin case as follows:

'Judy begins with credence 1/2 that her fiancé's company is stationed in the North of the country and 1/2 that it is in the South, and, conditional on its being in the North, equal credence in each of the North's two quarters. A source at headquarters whom she trusts reports that if the company is in the North, the chance that it is in the North's first quarter is three in four. Because that report concerns only how things stand within the North, it cannot bear at all on whether the company is in the North, and her credence in North must therefore remain exactly where it was. This is precisely what plain conditionalization delivers: read the report as the material conditional "not North or first quarter," update on that proposition, and nothing moves, since "not North" already entails it. The only reason people conclude that Bayesianism requires a special rule for conditionals is that they confuse the probability of a material conditional with a conditional probability. Adams' thesis — that for purposes of updating an indicative conditional may be treated as its material counterpart — has to be applied with care, but once it is, Judy Benjamin shows only that the standard framework was never incomplete.'
