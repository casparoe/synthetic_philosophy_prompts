Bit of a weird request. I'm a physio in a chronic pain clinic — I know the clinical side cold but the philosophy I've picked up piecemeal on my own time — and I've finally written down where I've actually landed on pain and its badness. Before I talk about it with a colleague who does philosophy properly, I want someone to tell me whether the set hangs together.

Here's what I hold:

1. What makes a pain bad for the person having it is how it feels. That's the whole of it. Damage, cause, duration, what it means to the person, how much it disrupts their life — none of that adds to or subtracts from the badness except insofar as it changes the feel.

2. The asymbolia cases are the clearest window onto this, and I take those reports at face value. These people feel it. They point to where it is, they'll tell you it's sharp or burning or 6 out of 10, they call it pain. And they don't mind. No guarding, no withdrawal, they'll keep walking on the thing, they don't avoid whatever caused it — by some accounts they'll go back and press the sore spot again out of curiosity.

3. So the feeling and the disliking are seperate components, and a pain can be there in full and be affectively neutral. I'm committed to that.

4. But when the disliking is there, I don't think it's some extra raw feel glued onto the sensation. I think it's the having of a demand that the sensation stop. The hurting just is the wanting-it-to-stop.

5. In clinic I run on reports, not scans. Clean imaging plus agony gets treated; shredded spine plus no complaint doesn't.

6. I don't think the kids born without pain sensation are deprived of anything. They've lost a warning system, so you protect them from themselves, but there's nothing bad going on inside them that the rest of us are spared.

7. The asymbolia people, though, are missing something important, and that's part of why I'd treat them rather than leave them to it. I wouldn't shrug and say nothing's wrong there.

8. And I think what a patient says about their own experience right now can't be overridden from outside. There's no third-person fact that beats "I feel it."

9. Except in practice I do sometimes treat a report as untrustworthy — somebody says 10/10 and I suspect the number is inflated or they're performing for a script — so I guess I don't fully believe 8 either.

I know there's a live argument about whether the asymbolic is having a neutral pain or having something that isn't a pain at all, and I've read both sides, but please work from the content rather than from who's on which side — I dont remember the names anyway.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real ones by severity instead of dumping them in one pile, and for each one tell me precisely what would have to go — not "rethink 4," but which half of it, and what I'd lose by giving it up. Then tell me the cheapest single revision, what it costs me downstream, and whether there's one change that would clean up most of the mess at once.
