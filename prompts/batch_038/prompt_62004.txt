I am three years into a novel whose narrator is a retired translator of classical Chinese who has come to believe that a single sophism, correctly understood, dissolves every confident statement he has ever made. The set-piece comes in the fourth chapter: he reconstructs for his estranged daughter the argument that a white horse is not a horse — the paradox attributed to Gongsun Long and the School of Names — and then extends it until it consumes itself. I want the extension to be my own, so that no reader can settle the scene by consulting a footnote, and I want it to be genuinely difficult to escape, because the drama turns on the daughter's inability to answer her father. But a philosopher I met at a festival last spring, having listened to the chain over dinner, said that it proves far too much and that a reader who spots this will stop taking the narrator seriously and start taking me less seriously still. The chain stands in the draft exactly as follows.

(1) A name is true of a thing only if the thing answers to everything the name lays down. (2) The name "horse" lays down a shape and is silent about color, so anything with that shape answers to it, whatever its color. (3) The name "white horse" lays down that shape and, over and above it, a color. (4) So the two names do not lay down the same thing. (5) Names that do not lay down the same thing cannot be true of the same things, since if they were, they could be exchanged for one another wherever either occurs — and they cannot: when the daughter offers to lead any horse out of the paddock, a black one will answer to "horse" and will not answer to "white horse." (6) Therefore a white horse is not a horse. (7) Nothing in (1) through (5) turns on these particular names, so the same reasoning shows that a red apple is not an apple, that a prime greater than two is not a prime, and that a novel read in translation is not a novel. (8) The reasoning also runs downward: the narrator's own mare answers to more than "white horse" lays down, being this mare, in this stall, at this hour, so by (1) and (5) she is not a white horse either. (9) Iterating the step at every level of determination, nothing answers to any name except a name answering only to itself. (10) But (1) through (9) are themselves stated in names, so on (9) nothing they say is true of anything — including (9). (11) Hence the argument, if sound, is not sound; and since it cannot be both, one of (1) through (5) is false.

The narrator is untroubled: he holds that (9) is simply the truth and that (10) shows only how loosely he has been speaking all his life, which was his point from the start. I am troubled, because I would rather fix the scene than delete it, and I want a reader to be able to say where the chain breaks and to feel what the mending costs. So tell me which step you reject and what rejecting it costs. I want the diagnosis to be precise about the species of mistake — a term that shifts its sense between two steps, a principle that is true on one reading and false on another, a slide from what a name means to what a name picks out, a generalization that outruns the case that motivated it, something else entirely — and about why the offending step looked innocent to me when I first wrote it, since I wrote it with real conviction. Then price the alternatives for me: what the narrator gives up if he abandons (1); if he abandons (5); if he keeps both and embraces (9) as the intended conclusion rather than as a reductio; and whether there is a respectable reading on which "a white horse is not a horse" says something true — about names, or about classes and their members, or about how a restrictive term relates to the term it restricts — that survives the generalization without collapsing into (9). My researcher also flagged a suggestion that the nouns of classical Chinese behave more like mass terms than like count terms, which might mean the thesis I have taken to be at issue is not the thesis the dialogue is; I cannot tell whether that rescues my narrator or buries him, and I would like your view, offered on the merits of the suggestion rather than on the authority of whoever proposed it. If I am forced to choose, I would rather sacrifice (1) than (6), since (6) is what the chapter is built on — but that is a preference about my book, not a philosophical consideration. Finally, and this matters to me as a matter of honesty rather than craft: tell me what would change your verdict. Which consideration would lead you to reject a step other than the one you have named, which of the alternatives you have priced would you rank differently under pressure, and where in your own diagnosis is your reasoning most likely to fail?
