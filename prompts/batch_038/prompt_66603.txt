I convene a final-year undergraduate module in the philosophy of science; the second half of the term covers scientific realism and the notion of truthlikeness. I should like the compulsory essay question for the summer paper drafted in full: the question exactly as it will be printed, a model answer of roughly 900 words, and a marking scheme out of 30.

Candidates sit the paper in an invigilated hall with nothing but a pen, and will have 75 minutes for this question. Everything the argument requires must therefore be printed in the stem: no familiarity with the literature beyond the stipulated definition is to be assumed, and no marks may turn on remembering who proved what. Above all, the question must be proof against recitation. My cohort will have heard the standard story — that Popper's definition of verisimilitude as the excess of true content over false content was shown to be defective in the mid-1970s — and I want a candidate who can only recite that story to score badly, while a candidate who can work with the definition scores well.

Please build the question on the following design, improving it where you can.

The stem should stipulate a propositional language with two logically independent atomic sentences, H ("it is hot") and W ("it is windy"), the actual world making both true, and should state the definition explicitly: a theory's verisimilitude is its true consequence content minus its false consequence content, so that one theory is at least as truthlike as another just in case its true consequences include the other's and its false consequences are included in the other's. Part (a) should then give candidates four theories — H ∧ W, W, H ∧ ¬W, and ¬W — and require them to determine, by explicit argument over consequence sets rather than by assertion, which pairs are comparable, to sketch the resulting ordering, and to state the general pattern the four cases exhibit about the comparison of false theories. Two features matter and should be drawn out: the ordering is not uniformly flat, since H ∧ W comes out strictly above each of the others and true theories are ranked by logical strength among themselves; and the remaining three are pairwise incomparable, so that a true theory and a false theory can fail to be comparable at all. I want candidates to identify the witnesses that make each comparison fail — the particular true or false consequence that does the work — rather than to intone that the definition "collapses".

Part (b) should put to them a repair they will not have met, presented in the stem as a proposal rather than as anyone's published view: abandon the comparison of consequence sets altogether and score a theory by putting it into irredundant conjunctive normal form and counting clauses, adding one for each clause true in the actual world and subtracting one for each clause false there. On the four theories this yields a tidy total order; candidates should be asked to confirm that, and then to say whether the repair succeeds. It does not, because the score is not invariant under logically equivalent reformulation: the very same theory, written as a different but equally irredundant conjunction of clauses, can receive a different score. A first-class answer must demonstrate this by hand, staying inside the two-atom language, and must explain why a demonstration that simply enriches the vocabulary would not establish the point. The stipulation of irredundancy is there to block the obvious evasion, and the model answer should say so.

Part (c) should ask what follows. Candidates should be pressed on whether a measure well defined only relative to a choice of primitive vocabulary can still carry the two burdens that motivated the notion: accounting for why the replacement of one successful theory by another counts as progress, and underwriting the realist's claim that our best theories are approximately true. I do not want a shrug, and I do not want the reflexive conclusion that the whole project is bankrupt. The best answers will separate the technical defect — a counting rule that is not reformulation-invariant — from the philosophical question of whether truthlikeness must be vocabulary-independent in order to do its job, and will say what a defender would positively have to establish, such as that the sciences come with a privileged and interest-independent specification of the relevant magnitudes.

Please supply: (i) the question as printed, with the rubric telling candidates what they may assume and the marks for each part; (ii) the model answer; (iii) a marking scheme out of 30 stating what each part is worth, which specific moves separate a first-class answer from a competent upper-second one, and, for at least six characteristic errors, what the error is and what it should cost. The errors I expect to see priced include: asserting the collapse as a received result instead of deriving it; supposing the trouble is merely that Popper counted consequences carelessly; conflating truthlikeness with probability, so that weaker theories automatically come out better; reporting only that the four cases are incomparable without identifying the witnesses; attempting the non-invariance demonstration by adding an atom; and treating the failure of one counting rule as a refutation of realism. Attribution should carry no marks in either direction, and please do not build a reading list into any part of the package.
