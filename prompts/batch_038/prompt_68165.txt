My friend and I argued until two in the morning about the moral equality of combatants, and he left me with a chain of reasoning I cannot dissolve, though I am confident it cannot be sound. He laid it out as follows.

(1) The traditional view holds that combatants on both sides may permissibly kill one another whatever the justice of their cause, because what makes the killing permissible is not the agent's cause but the target's having forfeited his right not to be killed.

(2) What produces that forfeiture is being dangerous to others, not being blameworthy: a man who slips from a rooftop and will crush you if he lands may permissibly be killed mid-air, though he has done nothing wrong and cannot stop himself.

(3) So liability to defensive killing requires only that a person threaten, not that he be responsible or culpable in any further sense.

(4) But threatening is something a person does at a time: an enemy soldier asleep in a barracks, or home on leave, threatens nothing at that moment.

(5) Hence by (3) and (4) he is not liable; and since by (1) the right to kill is exhausted by the target's liability, he may not be killed.

(6) That cannot stand. It would render nearly every killing in an actual war — wars being fought largely against people who are not firing at that instant — the killing of someone who retains his right not to be killed, and it would confer on a soldier who sets down his rifle to eat the same protected standing as a farmer who never took up arms.

(7) Therefore (3) is false: liability requires something beyond posing a threat, namely responsibility for it.

(8) And once (3) goes, the falling man in (2) shows only that a threat need not be culpable to be liable, not that responsibility is beside the point. The conscript who fights under an unjust flag without knowledge or fault is then not liable either, and the moral equality of combatants collapses into its opposite: only those responsible for an unjust threat may be targeted.

He says this is what the symmetry in Walzer always implied and that the revisionists have merely been honest about it. I want to keep (2), since the falling man really does seem killable, and I want to keep (6), since (5) would make war unfightable; but I cannot see how to keep both and refuse his conclusion. Which step should I reject, why did that step look safe, and what does rejecting it cost — for the Walzerian and for the revisionist separately, if the price differs between them?
