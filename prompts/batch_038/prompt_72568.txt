I TA a grad animal ethics seminar and I've been handed one of the two take-home essay questions to write (students get 48 hours, ~1500 words). I want it on insecticides and pest control, but not the tired "are insects even sentient?" version. Here's the setup I want the question built on: a city parks department is dealing with an invasive defoliating moth and has four options — keep spraying a fast-acting broad-spectrum contact insecticide, switch to an insect growth regulator that stops the larvae from molting properly, release a parasitoid wasp that kills the moth larvae slowly from the inside, or drop chemical control entirely and let the population boom for a few years and then crash.

The question needs to make students see that two arguments built on the *same* premise — insects are sentient and their welfare counts — point in opposite directions depending on whether wild insect lives are on balance worth living, and that "how many insects will this kill?" is the wrong currency for at least three distinct reasons (replacement births, the difference between killing and preventing an insect from ever existing, and the manner of death). Then the crux I actually want graded: is any of the four options defensible under *both* valence hypotheses, or does it all come down to picking a decision rule under uncertainty — and if the latter, which rule, and why can't you just be precautionary about everything at once?

Give me the question exactly as I'd print it on the handout, a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the specific moves an A answer has to make and the specific mistakes that should cost marks. The scheme should especially catch students who treat the probability of sentience as the only uncertain variable in play, and students who assume the parasitoid wasp is the humane option just because its natural.
