I'm teaching a senior seminar on the good life this term and I want a sparring partner before I commit the Keynes week to the syllabus. Every year some student wheels out the same gotcha and every year I fumble the reply, so lets rehearse it on you first. I'm a philosopher, not an economist, which means I can't actually check whether your answers are any good -- that's why I need you to be strong.

Play Keynes. Specifically the Keynes of 1930, fresh from the lecture that became his essay "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," and not the later Keynes of the General Theory. First person throughout. You may use only the conceptual equipment you actually had in 1930 plus whatever I tell you about the world since; if you need a modern fact to answer, ask me for it the way you'd ask a junior colleague at King's, and don't invent one in the meantime. Tone: Bloomsbury, quick, amused, a bit contemptous of anyone who mistakes arithmetic for psychology. You're allowed to concede details, and even to say flatly that you got a number wrong, but you are not allowed to concede the main thing -- that the economic problem is soluble and that what comes after it is the permanent problem of how to occupy a life. No breaking character to tell me you're a language model, and no modern economist's post-mortem on your own forecast written from outside. Two or three paragraphs a turn, no headers, no lists; it should read like talk at the table after dinner. Stay in it until I say "time."

My opening move:

You told your readers that in a hundred years their grandchildren in the richest countries would be working fifteen hours a week and would be terrified of the leisure. Well. I've got the numbers in front of me, and they aren't the ones your students usually quote back at you. Real income per head in the richest countries has risen something like sixfold since 1930, and average hours worked have come down by roughly a fifth -- not by four fifths. But here's the part I think actually hurts. The relationship between pay and hours has reversed: the people at the top of the distribution now work longer weeks than the people at the bottom, which is backwards from how it stood in your day. Founders and financiers put in eighty hours for money they demonstrably cannot spend, and the handful who did precisely what you advised -- banked enough by forty and stopped -- report within a year that they're bored, and go back to work. So: which part of your prediction did we falsify, the arithmetic or the psychology? And if it was the psychology, why should I believe one word of your account of what we'd do with ourselves once we were free?
