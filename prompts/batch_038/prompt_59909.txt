Below is a draft section of an essay I am writing on advance directives, roughly as it currently stands. I would like substantive comments, not copy-editing.

> **Draft: The Directive as Promise, Not as Self-Government**
>
> The standard defence of the advance directive is autonomy-based. Dworkin's version appeals to critical interests: the reflective judgments a person makes about what makes a life as a whole good are said to retain authority over the merely experiential satisfactions available to that person later, even after dementia has destroyed the capacities that produced those judgments. The best-interests objection, pressed most persistently by critics such as Rebecca Dresser, replies that once the earlier psychological subject is gone, the only morally salient facts are about the welfare of the person now in the bed — her contentment, her absence of distress, her present protestations.
>
> I want to argue that both sides are fighting over the wrong question. They assume the directive's force, if it has any, must derive from the patient: either from a self that persists, or from a self that has been replaced. But the directive is not an exercise of continuing self-government at all. It is a promise made to identifiable other people — a spouse, an adult child, a named surrogate — and its binding character comes from their reliance, not from the promisor's persistence.
>
> This explains two things the autonomy account cannot. First, it explains why boilerplate directives, signed alone in a solicitor's office and never discussed, intuitively carry little weight: no one relied on them. Second, it explains why a directive argued over at kitchen-table length with a specific person carries a great deal: someone has organised her expectations, her own plans, and her self-understanding around it.
>
> The view also yields a threshold that is neither identity-based nor pure best-interests. A demented patient who retains the capacities required to release someone from a promise can release the directive, and her present wishes then govern. A patient who does not retain those capacities cannot, and her distress, however real, does not by itself dissolve an obligation owed to a third party.

Three things I want you to weigh in on. First, does the promissory reconstruction actually escape the best-interests objection, or does it collapse back into that objection once reliance is cashed out — since the surrogate's distress at breaking faith is itself a present interest, just located in a different person? Second, the view makes a directive's authority a function of how much reliance it has generated, which seems to imply that a solitary patient with no one relying on her is unprotected, and that a manipulative earlier self can inflate his own posthumous authority by manufacturing reliance. Are these objections I can absorb, or do they force me to abandon the proportionality claim? Third, is the release clause coherent: can someone who lacks the capacities for autonomous decision-making nevertheless retain the narrower capacity to let another person off a promise?

Please take a definite position rather than surveying the field. Tell me which of the three problems is the most serious, whether the view is salvageable as it stands or requires replacing the reliance-based account with something else, and — if you think it is salvageable — commit to the single best repair, with reasons for preferring it to the alternatives you considered. If you think the whole promissory framing should be abandoned in favour of a straightforwardly welfare-based account, say so plainly and say what my draft's two explanatory advantages then become.
