I teach a graduate seminar whose standing question is what a community owes its own memory when the documentary record contradicts it. Before we read anything, I want the students to locate themselves in that question, so I would like a decision tree — a single-page diagnostic — that sorts them by what they would actually decide in concrete cases, not by which school they believe they belong to.

Build the tree out of the following seven cases. Students will answer each node by reference to a case; they should never be asked for an allegiance, a self-description, or an author.

1. A town's founding story holds that its first settler sheltered fugitives in the cellar of the meeting house for a winter. Parish registers, tax rolls, and a surviving diary place no such person in the town, and the earliest written trace of the story is a newspaper item sixty years after the supposed events, published during a fundraising campaign for the building's restoration.
2. A rural community remembers a massacre at a crossroads in the 1940s, and three generations name the same field. No mass grave has been found and the regional archive holds no report — but that archive was assembled by the provincial security apparatus that operated in the district, and is known to have destroyed files elsewhere.
3. A deposited treaty text fixes a boundary; a nation's oral account places the agreed boundary elsewhere and holds that the men who signed lacked authority to cede the land at all.
4. Two neighbouring towns commemorate the same riot of a century ago, each as the attacked party; each version is supported by its own local newspaper of the period, and the two papers had opposite political allegiances.
5. A war memorial lists forty-seven names. Archival work establishes that four of the men died in another country, in another year, of disease, and that one name corresponds to no identifiable person. The list was compiled in 1919 by a bereaved mother from a subscription book.
6. A survivor's account of her own deportation gives dates, ages, and a sequence of events that conflict with transport lists and camp registers; she has told the same account for fifty years and says the documents are what is wrong.
7. A community's calendar, street names, and annual holiday are organized around a battle that almost certainly never happened; the story demonstrably built the town's school and its mutual aid society over 150 years.

Six to eight nodes, eight to twelve leaves. Order the nodes by which questions logically presuppose which rather than by the order in which the literature happens to discuss them, and give one line on why each node sits where it does. The distinctions I want the tree to turn on — add others or argue that one of these is redundant:

- whether the memory is even making a claim about what occurred, or is doing something else: constituting the group, marking membership, orienting the present;
- whether the record is a neutral third party or is itself the work product of one of the disputants, so that its silences are data;
- whether the two accounts are answerable to the same standards of warrant at all;
- whether the remembering community speaks with one voice or is internally divided, which bears on who can accept a correction;
- whether a correction can be made without destroying something that a truer belief cannot replace, and whether that loss counts morally or only as a regret;
- whether it matters if the correction originates inside the community or is imposed from outside;
- whether the point of getting it right is epistemic or restorative.

For leaves, I want at least these positions represented — described here, not named, since you should supply the names: the record wins and the memory must be revised however painful; the inverted view that the archive is the interested witness and the memory the more reliable one; the view that there is no conflict because the memory is not in the business of assertion; the view that memory and history are different regimes with different functions, so the demand for reconciliation is itself a mistake; the view that oral tradition is testimony of a special kind, to be weighed under calibrated skepticism rather than set aside; the view that a story's institutional effects justify preserving it after it is known to be false; the view that the truth must be told but only in a form and at a pace the community consents to; and the view that a public space can carry both versions, labelled, without adjudicating between them. Where a position has an established name you are confident of, give it; where you are not confident, describe the position and say so plainly instead of coining a label.

Each leaf should carry: its verdict in the seven cases (or, where the verdicts are uniform, a single sentence to that effect); one sentence on the commitment it would least like to be held to; and a confidence mark on whether the position is genuinely defended in roughly the form you have given it.

Close with four things: any pair of leaves your nodes cannot really separate; every answer-combination your nodes permit that no leaf occupies, with a diagnosis of each — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored; which cases your tree routes to the same verdict by different paths, since I want to know whether the cases are doing independent work; and whether any one of the seven cases strains a tree built from the other six.

Finally, state how confident you are in the tree as a whole — in particular in the ordering of the nodes and in your attributions — and single out the one node, leaf, or attribution you are least sure of, saying what would settle it.
