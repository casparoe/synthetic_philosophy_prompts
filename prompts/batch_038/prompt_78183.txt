I am drafting a position paper on the paradox of deontology — the charge that a non-consequentialist who grounds constraints in anything other than the agent's own moral record cannot explain why the agent must refrain from violating a constraint when violating it would prevent more violations of that same constraint — and I would like the package of commitments it defends audited before I circulate it. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, for references, or for a reconstruction of anyone else's view. Please work strictly with what I state below.

The package:

1. Agent-relative constraints. It is impermissible for me to kill one innocent person in order to prevent other agents from killing five innocents. What the constraint regulates is what I do, not the resulting distribution of killings.

2. Grounding. What makes such a killing wrong is a fact about the victim rather than about me: persons have a status that renders them inviolable, that is, not available to be used. That status is impersonal. I reject deliberately, and as a matter of principle, any version of deontology on which the point of the constraint is to preserve the agent's moral record; I treat the clean-hands reading as a reductio of the view, not a defense of it.

3. Thresholds. The constraints are not absolute. Where what I could prevent is grave enough, violating them becomes permissible. The threshold is fixed by the same considerations that ground the constraint — not by social utility, institutional role, or any other extrinsic factor.

4. The schedule. Call this the tariff. Within a constraint type the comparison is numerical: preventing killings can outweigh committing one, and there is some number at which it does. Across types the ordering is lexical in both directions: no number of violations of a lesser constraint licenses a violation of a graver one, while preventing a single violation of a graver constraint licenses any number of violations of a lesser one. Hence I may not kill anyone to prevent a million petty thefts, but I may lie a thousand times to prevent one torture.

5. Content, and how it is lifted. What specifies the constraints is something in the neighbourhood of the doctrine of double effect: what is prohibited is intentionally harming an innocent as an end or as a means, while harm that is foreseen but unintended is governed by proportionality rather than prohibition. When the threshold is crossed the prohibition is lifted rather than outweighed — the act simply falls outside the constraint — and I maintain that the lifting is not itself a proportionality judgment about the harm I would intentionally inflict.

6. No proxies. I may not procure a violation through another. If it is wrong for me to kill the one, it is wrong for me to have it done by a subordinate, a paid stranger, or a device I set in motion.

7. Positive duties. Alongside the constraints I hold demanding but imperfect duties of aid whose object is impersonal: how much violation and suffering there is in the world, not whose doing it is. These can require considerable sacrifice from me.

8. Vagueness. There is no precise number at which the threshold is crossed, and my view owes none. Borderline cases are genuinely borderline.

9. No collapse. Commitments 3 through 5 do not convert my view into a species of consequentialism. The difference between the sub-threshold and the super-threshold case is categorical rather than a difference of degree on a single scale. Below the threshold, the consideration favouring the killing is not merely outweighed; it is excluded.

Case verdicts I am committed to, so that the principles can be tested against them:

(a) A captor holds ten people and will kill all ten unless the captor's own brother — an innocent, currently in my custody, against whom there is no complaint — is dead; the brother's death is the captor's stated condition. Verdict: killing him is permissible, one for ten, within type.

(b) The same outcome could be obtained by paying a stranger to kill the brother. Verdict: impermissible.

(c) Harvesting one healthy patient's organs to save five who will otherwise die. Verdict: permissible in principle, though I doubt the empirical conditions ever obtain.

(d) A thousand lies to prevent one torture: permissible. Killing anyone to prevent any number of trespasses: impermissible.

What I want is an audit, not a defense of deontology in general and not an essay on the paradox. Specifically:

- Identify the commitments that genuinely pull against one another. For each, say which pair or triple conflicts, why the conflict is more than verbal, and what would have to be given up or restated to remove it. Where more than one exit exists, say which is cheapest and which is most damaging.
- Rank these conflicts by severity: which is fatal to the package as it stands, which is a serious but local problem, and which is an irritation I can live with.
- Identify at least one pairing that looks like a tension and is not, and explain why it dissolves. I expect there to be some; please do not flag everything.
- Say which single revision would clean up the most, and what it would cost me elsewhere — including whether it forces me to abandon something in 2 or 9, which are the two I would least like to lose.
- Where one of the case verdicts in (a) through (d) cannot be delivered by the principles I have given, say so and say which principle fails. Do not assume the verdict is correct and quietly adjust the principles behind it.

I am deliberately not telling you where I suspect the trouble is, and I have no settled view about which commitment I would sacrifice. If the answer is that 2 has to go, or that 9 has to go, or that (a) and (c) are simply inconsistent with 5, say so plainly.
