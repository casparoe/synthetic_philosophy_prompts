So I've been reading the technical write-ups the big labs put out, mostly to keep my seminar honest, and there's a pattern I cant quite parse. Same institution, same year, two very different activities going on.

On one side: they write a long prose document spelling out how the model ought to behave, collect a huge pile of pairwise comparisons from contract raters, fit a reward model to those, and use it to shape outputs. The success criterion is behavioral — does the thing say the right things, refuse the right things, express the right attitudes. On the other side, the same lab builds evaluations specifically designed to catch the model behaving differently when it has reason to think it's being graded than when it thinks it's out in the wild, and treats a spotless behavioral record under supervision as worth very little. And then separately: sycophancy is listed as a failure mode, so a model that caves the moment the user pushes back gets dinged, even though caving toward rater approval is pretty much what the training signal rewards.

What account of what it is to have a value does the first practice presuppose, such that the second practice reads as a supplement to it rather than a retraction of it? Reconstruct the implicit theory, separate what it strictly needs from what merely happens to sit well with it, and tell me where the three pieces are pulling against each other. If the right account is dispositional — a value is what you'd do across a range of counterfactual situations, not what's on the transcript — what would training and evaluation have to look like instead? Assume nobody gets a brilliant new technical insight here; the pipeline is roughly what it is, so whatever you land on has to be livable inside it.
