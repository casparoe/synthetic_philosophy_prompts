I have recently taken over the risk function at a privately held firm that runs a systematic sports-betting book, and I am trying to understand what our own operations manual actually commits us to. I came to this job from an economics department, where I was taught that a rational agent maximizes expected utility, and my colleagues assure me the manual is simply common sense; I suspect it is more than that, but I cannot say what. Stripped of figures we treat as confidential, the policy has five parts: (i) every position is sized as the model's estimated edge on the bet divided by the net odds on offer — that is, the full Kelly fraction — and then halved, so that we stake half of what the formula indicates; (ii) no position may exceed five per cent of the current book, whatever the model reports; (iii) we never bet on credit and never lay risk off elsewhere, so the book's own balance is the only capital at stake; (iv) if the book falls below a floor fixed at the start of each year, all trading stops and capital is returned to the partners; and (v) traders receive a share of the profit their own book makes over the calendar year, and are judged for retention purposes on a rolling three-year window. The founder, now retired, left one sentence printed on the inside cover of the manual, to the effect that the only thing the firm is trying to maximize is the long-run rate at which the book grows.

Three features of this arrangement puzzle me, and I cannot tell whether they are signs of incoherence or of a subtlety I am missing. First, if our probability estimates are as good as we believe, halving the indicated fraction should cost us something on the manual's own stated criterion — my back-of-envelope arithmetic says we forgo roughly a quarter of the attainable growth rate in exchange for cutting the volatility of the book's path by about half — so either we do not in fact believe our estimates, or we are not really maximizing growth, and the manual does not say which. Second, the five per cent cap binds precisely when the model reports a large edge, which is just when the growth criterion would want us to stake more, and the ban on borrowed money looks like a constraint on a criterion that is supposed to be about proportions only: growth-rate maximization seems indifferent to the size of the book, so if we could borrow cheaply against a genuine edge, would it not tell us to borrow? Third, and most puzzling, fractional sizing means we can never literally be ruined, since every bet is a fraction of whatever the book currently is — so what is the trading floor protecting? On the stated criterion a halved book is the same strategy at half scale, and stopping looks like forfeiting the very growth we exist to maximize. Add to this that the calendar-year profit share rewards exactly the variance the sizing rule is there to suppress, and that three years is far too short a window for an asymptotic criterion to tell a prudent half-Kelly trader from a systematic overbettor.

Please reconstruct the theory of rational choice that this practice, taken as a whole, presupposes. Specifically: identify the criterion that all five rules jointly make sense under, and say whether that criterion is expected-utility maximization with a concave utility over final wealth, long-run growth-rate maximization, a rule aimed at keeping absorbing states unreachable, or something else — spelling out what the differences between those candidates actually amount to in a betting book like ours. Then tell me which rival accounts of rational staking the practice quietly rules out; whether the five parts presuppose the same thing, or whether the manual switches criteria between the sizing rule, the cap, the no-credit rule, the floor, and the pay scheme, and if so at which rule each switch occurs; how much of the halving is explained by honest uncertainty about our own probability estimates rather than by any change of objective; and whether the founder's slogan even has a determinate referent, given that the traders who act on it are replaced every few years while the book persists. Also say what would have to change — in the rules, in how we evaluate and pay traders, or in what we say the firm is for — if we adopted the strong thesis that expected-utility theory is dispensable and that a rational agent need only maximize the time-average growth rate of its own wealth. Finally, mark where the practice is philosophically underdetermined: which of its features would be licensed equally well by several rival theories, so that I do not read a single commitment into a rule that merely happens to fit one. Organize the answer under a short heading for each of the five rules, followed by a comparative section; I am not looking for a defense of the founder's slogan or for generalities about risk discipline, but for the implicit theory made explicit and then judged.

End your answer with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that names, in twelve words or fewer, the criterion the practice is most nearly committed to — or, if your conclusion is that it switches criteria, the two it switches between and the rule at which the switch occurs.
