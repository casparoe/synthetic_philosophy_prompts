**Was Malthus right but early? (Workshop draft, third pass)**

Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population appeared anonymously in 1798 and was substantially rewritten and enlarged in 1803, with the later edition adding long historical surveys of the checks on population across many societies and, importantly, giving a much larger role to what he called the preventive check — later marriage and moral restraint — alongside famine, disease, and war. The core comparative claim is familiar: population, when unchecked, increases geometrically, while the means of subsistence can at best increase arithmetically. He took the geometrical ratio from an empirical datum rather than from pure speculation, namely the observed doubling of the population of the American colonies roughly every twenty-five years. The conclusion he drew was that any improvement in productivity tends to be absorbed by a larger population, returning the mass of people to subsistence.

On the face of it, the world since 1800 has refuted him. Global population was somewhere around a billion when he wrote and is now about eight billion, while average calorie availability per person has risen rather than fallen. Yet the verdict "refuted" is delivered far too cheaply, and for three reasons.

First, the mechanism by which the predicted return to subsistence failed to materialize was not the mechanism Malthus had in mind. His preventive check ran through delayed marriage and self-denial, and he was hostile to contraception. The demographic transition instead ran through contraception, falling infant mortality, rising female education and labor-force participation, and a shift in the returns to children from quantity toward quality. When a tendency theory's predicted outcome fails to appear because an auxiliary assumption about the causal route was mistaken, the central claim has not thereby been tested to destruction; that is a routine consequence of the fact that theories face experience only bundled with their auxiliaries.

Second, the Malthusian mechanism has local confirmations that are hard to dismiss. I believe — though I may be conflating nominal with real wages, or England with Western Europe generally — that English real wages rose sharply after the mid-fourteenth-century plague, perhaps on the order of doubling within a few decades, as the land-to-labor ratio moved abruptly in labor's favor, and that they then drifted back down over the following century or so as population recovered. If that is broadly correct, the model predicted both the shock response and the eventual reversion.

Third, and this is the point I most want to press, the escape from the trap looks as though it was financed out of stocks rather than flows. Fixed nitrogen manufactured industrially by the Haber–Bosch process, mined phosphate and potash, fossil groundwater, fossil energy used as both fuel and feedstock, and a one-time reallocation of labor out of agriculture are all depletable or non-renewable in relevant time scales. A commonly cited estimate is that on the order of half the nitrogen in the average human body today originated in industrially fixed nitrogen rather than in biological fixation. If so, Malthus's constraint was on flows, and the two centuries of apparent refutation were paid for by drawing down a stock. That is perfectly consistent with his being right but early.

The trouble is that the deferral defense is unrestricted. Almost any failed long-run prediction can be redescribed as not yet due, and if that were acceptable, no long-run theory could ever fail. Popper's point that an ad hoc modification reduces a theory's empirical content, and Lakatos's contrast between progressive and degenerating research programmes, are supposed to police this, but neither tells us how expensive a given rescue is. So I want to distinguish three kinds:

- **Type-shift rescue.** The logical form of the prediction is silently changed — a dated prediction becomes a tendency, a level becomes a rate, an era becomes "eventually." Almost always illegitimate, because it changes what would count as disconfirmation without announcing that it is doing so.
- **Parameter rescue.** The form is preserved and a number is revised. Cheap and often legitimate, but only where the number was independently measured rather than tuned to fit the anomaly.
- **Auxiliary rescue.** The core is preserved and a mechanism assumption is replaced. Legitimate only if the replacement is independently motivated and was available to the theorist in advance.

Call that last condition **escape-hatch licensing**: a theory earns credit for surviving an anomaly by an auxiliary its own framework could have anticipated, and earns none for surviving by an auxiliary invented afterward. Licensing is what separates being early from being unfalsifiable.

Applying this to Malthus gives an awkward result. The stock/flow reading fails licensing at the level of the text: nothing in either edition of the Essay distinguishes stock-financed from flow-financed growth, and Malthus treated land as fixed without drawing the depletion inference. But it arguably passes at the level of the research programme, since the classical economists' rent theory already treats a fixed natural factor as the binding constraint on distribution, and it is a short step from there to a flow constraint. So the rescue is half-licensed: ad hoc relative to the book, defensible relative to the school. That is why I want to say Malthus is neither refuted nor vindicated but demoted — from a prediction to a boundary condition on any growth model that assumes its inputs are flows.

I want to end by noting that the same diagnostic bears on contemporary long-run claims about resource exhaustion, climate damages, and technological unemployment. The lesson is not patience with forecasters of collapse. It is that we should ask whether a theory told us in advance what would delay its predicted outcome, and for how long. A theory that specifies its own delay conditions can be wrong in an informative way; a theory that does not is not early, it is merely untestable.

My verdict: Malthus was not right but early. He was right about a mechanism and silent about a boundary, and "right but early" should be reserved for theorists who specified the conditions of their own delay.
