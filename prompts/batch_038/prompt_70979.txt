I am drafting a paper on money pumps, and its argumentative spine is an analogy I would like audited before I commit to it. The analogy: a money pump is simply the Dutch book argument stretched out in time — the same coherence argument, with the bets handed over one after another rather than all at once. My co-author and I use it to license a deliberately strong conclusion, namely that a cyclical preference ordering is itself a defect of rationality. Just as an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability calculus is guaranteed to lose money and is therefore incoherent, an agent whose preferences cycle is guaranteed to be relieved of cash and is therefore irrational, and no appeal to transaction costs, to hard choices, or to the context-dependence of comparison can rescue the ordering.

Please audit the analogy for me.

First, the correspondences. Say what plays the role of the bookmaker, of the odds, of the agent's acceptance of a bet, of the guaranteed loss, and of the state of the world in each of the two cases; indicate where the mapping is exact and where it requires a stretch.

Second, the disanalogies. Here I want them separated into those that bear on the use my co-author and I are making of the analogy and those that are merely incidental, because I suspect that much of the disagreement in the surrounding literature turns on running these two classes together. One candidate I would especially like you to weigh: my co-author argues that the analogy already breaks at the level of foresight, since a synchronically incoherent agent cannot decline his way out of a Dutch book however well he predicts the bookmaker's offers, whereas an agent with cyclical preferences who knows the offers will recur can simply refuse the first trade. Tell me whether that claim is right, and if it is, what it does to the inference from "exploitable" to "irrational."

Third, what the analogy quietly imports. I have in mind assumptions about the agent as a price-taker facing arbitrarily small trades, about the divisibility and costlessness of those trades, about whether the norm being invoked is synchronic coherence or something diachronic, and about whether the object under assessment is a preference ordering or a decision procedure.

Finally, the verdict. Does our conclusion survive the audit intact, in weakened form, or not at all? And if the Dutch book comparison turns out to be the wrong lens, propose a better analogy for the phenomenon money pumps are supposed to reveal, and say what the better analogy would license that this one does not.
