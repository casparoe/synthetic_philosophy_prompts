I run a philosophy of science seminar for our first-years, and last week we did the pessimistic meta-induction. It was the final session before reading week, the room was stifling, and two of the students got into a proper stand-up argument during the second hour. I mostly stayed out of it because I wanted to see how far they could push it on their own, then I had to close the session. So: I'm going to reconstruct the exchange as neutrally as I can manage — I won't tell you where I lean, partly because I'd rather have a referee who isn't anchored, partly because I honestly changed my mind twice during the hour. Ivan is writing on nineteenth-century thermodynamics; Sofia did four years in a wet lab before coming to philosophy. Neither is a strawman, I promise.

(1) Ivan opens with the graveyard: caloric, phlogiston, the luminiferous ether — each posited as a real thing, each doing genuine explanatory work, each abandoned. His point is that success has never once bought truth, so the no-miracles argument can't get off the ground. He sharpens it with Carnot: the results about the limiting efficiency of heat engines survived the death of caloric essentially intact, which he says proves you can have all the predictive success and none of the ontology. So the pessimist doesn't have to deny that our current theories are successful — she only has to note that success and truth have been coming apart for two centuries.

(2) Sofia says the sample is rigged. He's counting every theory that ever died and reporting a ratio. Restrict the base to theories with the kind of success ours have — quantitatively novel predictions confirmed, unification across previously separate domains, precision — and the retention rate looks completely different. She also says he's committed to a base-rate fallacy: if success comes in degrees, then generalizing from the whole population of dead theories to theories at the top of the success scale is just bad statistics, whatever the history shows.

(3) Ivan says fine, grant the restriction, and it gets worse rather than better. In exactly those well-confirmed domains the record shows that the serious rivals were never on the table: the scientists of each period enumerated what they took to be the live options, confirmed one of them, and were wrong about the space itself. So our confidence that we've identified the uniquely successful theory in its class is precisely the kind of confidence history shows to be unreliable. Restricting to well-confirmed theories doesn't save realism, it targets the pessimistic generalization.

(4) Sofia fires back with something she says she made up in the shower, and I thought it was the best move of the hour. She says (3) is self-undermining. The claim that scientists in period P systematically failed to conceive of well-confirmed alternatives is itself an empirical generalization about the past, supported by the documentary record — letters, prize competitions, textbooks, the minutes of academies. If well-confirmed empirical claims are no guide to truth, then the pessimist's own evidence base isn't either. So Ivan is in a dilemma: either he exempts historiography, in which case he owes an account of which well-confirmed claims are truth-conducive and why science isn't among them, or he doesn't exempt it and his induction undercuts itself along with everything else.

(5) Ivan says that's an equivocation and he's not buying it. The pessimistic induction was never about observable matters; it's about inferences from success to the truth of claims concerning unobservable posits. What the documents say happened at the Académie in 1820 is not an unobservable posit in the relevant sense — you can go read it. So the induction is domain-restricted by construction, and self-undermining nowhere. But then he presses the advantage: the same restriction kills the preservative reply. Nobody thinks the equations survived; what died was the medium the equations were equations of, and a medium is exactly an unobservable posit.

(6) Sofia answers on both fronts. On the first: if you can take the formalism over and leave the interpretation behind, you've conceded that the formalism carries content that outruns any particular ontology, and that concession is itself a realist concession about something — you don't get to call it a hollow victory. On the second: the graveyard is selective in the other direction. Count the retention column — Newtonian gravity as a limiting case of general relativity, the wave equations of optics, the ordering principle of the periodic table — and the success/truth correlation in the restricted domain isn't nearly as bad as he claims. He only gets his conclusion by tallying one column.

(7) Ivan's closer is that the retention column is retention under reinterpretation, which is not the thing realists advertised. Mass isn't mass in relativity; Mendeleev ordered by atomic weight and had to invert a couple of pairs, and the ordering that survived was by proton number; light is neither a wave in a medium nor a little corpuscle. What's left is a structural skeleton. And he adds a case he clearly enjoyed: Lorentz's ether theory was never refuted by any experiment at all — it stayed empirically near-equivalent and simply went out of fashion, killed by parsimony worries about the contraction hypothesis rather than by data. So the actual selection among well-confirmed rivals wasn't driven by empirical success either, which is the last link in the success-to-truth chain. A skeleton, he says, is entirely compatible with pessimism, and Sofia has retreated from "true" to "structurally true" without noticing she moved.

Could you go through this move by move and rate each of the seven on a five point scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating rather than per side? Then tell me who's ahead overall and whether it's close. I'd especially like your read on (5): does restricting the induction to unobservable posits actually rescue Ivan from the dilemma in (4), or does it defang the induction so much that it stops being interesting — and can he have both? And finally, name at least one thing both of them are missing, since I suspect there's a whole dimension of this debate that neither one touched all hour.
