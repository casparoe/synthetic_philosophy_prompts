I run a small reading group — mostly philosophy grad students, a couple of very keen undergrads, one physicist who comes for the snacks — and this week we're watching Arrival instead of reading anything, which I know sounds like a cop-out but hear me out. I want to use it as a way into semantic externalism, and I keep failing, because the moment the words "learning an alien language rewires your brain" come up, the conversation slides into Sapir-Worf and determinism and nobody gets anywhere. So I need a reading that deliberately pulls the film toward Twin Earth instead. Here's the relevant stuff, so you don't have to reconstruct the plot: linguist Louise Banks is recruited after twelve craft show up; she and the physicist Ian Donnelly work on the Montana ship, where two heptapods (nicknamed Abbott and Costello) write in a nonlinear logographic script, Heptapod B; as Louise gets fluent she starts having what look like memories of a daughter, Hannah, who we're led to believe died of an illness, and it slowly emerges they're premonitions. The crisis point is a translation dispute: asked why they've come, the heptapods produce something rendered as "offer weapon," China reads it as "use weapon," and everyone starts shutting down; Louise argues the same sign could be "means" or "tool," and she floats that the Chinese team got a belligerent reading because they'd been establishing vocabulary by playing mahjong with their heptapods, a competitive game. The heptapods have come to hand over their language because in three thousand years they'll need humanity's help, and Louise ends up choosing to have the daughter anyway, knowing exactly how it goes.

Here's what I actually want you to do with that. Take the mahjong line seriously as a piece of philosophy, not as flavor: it looks like a claim that two teams in the same internal state, doing the same task, can attach different content to the same utterance purely because of how they've been embedded in the world — which is close to the shape of Putnam's argument, and it's the opposite of the Sapir-Worf story the film otherwise seems to be telling (that what matters is the structure inside your skull). So: does Arrival end up internalist or externalist about meaning, and does it notice that it's in tension with itself? Then work through the "weapon/tool" crux as a genuine reference-fixing problem rather than a plot device — what settles whether the heptapod sign means weapon or tool? Louise's charitable intentions, the pattern of everyone's use, what the heptapods actually intend and will later do, or something about the world itself in the way Lewis's reference magnetism is supposed to work? Is there a fact of the matter, and if there is, does the film commit to one? Relatedly, Louise can't possibly be a full member of the heptapod linguistic community — she's a partial speaker who has to defer to Costello and to Ian about what their terms pick out — so what does Putnam's division of linguistic labor say about what her own English and her own Heptapod B utterances even express, and is it worrying that her best translation is achieved while she is least competent in the language she's translating from?

And I'd like the slow-switching angle chased down, because I think it's the most interesting thing in the film and nobody in my group has raised it: if Louise's immersion gradually changes the content of her ordinary words, when does the change happen, and does the Louise who remembers her pre-contact thoughts still have those same thoughts to remember? That's the standard externalist worry about memory and self-knowledge, and the film's whole narrative device — a woman discovering that her most intimate memories are actually of a future that hasn't happened — seems to me like an unusually good vehicle for it, or a bad one, and I want you to argue which. So write it up as criticism, in prose, no bibliography, no reading list, no verbatim quotes of anybody (paraphrase Putnam and Burge and the rest rather than quoting them, and don't feel obliged to date anything). Take the philosophy seriously enough to say what its arguments actually require, and then be honest in both directions: flag the places where the film just isn't doing externalism at all and the reading is me imposing it, flag any place where I've got a plot detail wrong, and tell me whether you think Arrival gives the externalism debate anything that argument alone couldn't — including the harder question of whether a language whose utterances aren't in the business of having extensions at all (Heptapod B is written as a whole, in advance, as a performance rather than a description) even gets externalism off the ground. I'll be turning your piece into a handout, so about 1,500 words, readable by a smart second-year who's done one unit on reference but has never heard of Boghossian.
