Retired four years ago from isotope geochemistry — spent my life measuring boron and strontium ratios in rocks and worrying about mass spectrometer drift — and I've gone and fallen into philosophy of science headfirst, mostly because retirement was unbearable. Trouble is, I also let myself get talked onto my society's Landmark Paper committee. We're eight of us, mostly working people, and we vote on which old papers get the plaque and the retrospective essay. Lately I've been reading about verisimilitude, the claim that a theory which is flat-out false can still be nearer the truth than another false one, and it has completely scrambled my sense of what our votes are even about.

Here's our record over four years, as near as I can reconstruct it:

- Honored a 1954 paper whose mathematics was essentially right but whose explanation invoked a mechanism nobody now believes ever existed. Rationale from the floor: the equations survived, the story didn't, fine.
- Declined a wildly influential 1930s framework that sorted an entire field correctly but rested on a substance that was never there. Rationale: it taught people to classify, not to explain.
- Honored a paper whose headline number turned out to be off by roughly 40 percent, because it swallowed three previously separate subfields into one and because its successor keeps it as a limiting case.
- Declined a gorgeous, still-taught model that's false in detail but whose predictions sit comfortably inside today's error bars. Rationale: nobody learned anything from it that they didn't already have.
- Honored a paper that made a bold quantitative prediction nobody could test for twenty years and that eventually came out right — even though the author said at the time he didn't believe it himself.
- Split 4–4 and tabled a pair of rival theories that were equally accurate everywhere anybody had looked and diverged only where no data existed. I voted to table. I couldn't tell you why.

Now next month's candidate. A 1971 paper proposing a mechanism for isotopic shuffling during low-temperature alteration of seafloor rock. Four features matter: (i) it fits the data that existed at the time better then the currently accepted treatment does — genuinely better, we had a postdoc rerun it against the 1971 dataset, and the old model wins on those points; (ii) it wins by positing a process we're now confident doesn't happen; (iii) it predicts nothing outside that narrow window, and it was ignored at the time, partly because the author died young and was by all accounts impossible to work with; and (iv) it implies a novel qualitative affect nobody has ever gone looking for, though a preprint this spring hints at something loose in that neighborhood. The working scientists on the committee say a fictional mechanism is disqualifying, full stop. I suspect they're wrong but I can't yet say so in a way that survives a room.

So — what should we vote, if we want to be consistent with ourselves rather than merely squeamish? Which of those six is the closest analog, and exactly where does that analogy snap? The part I actually care about: our record looks to me like it was made under at least three different implicit rules, something like "count up the true consequences," something like "average distance from the truth over the questions the theory actually asked," and something like "did it buy us a prediction we couldn't otherwise have had." Reconstruct those rules (or better ones if mine are wrong), say what each delivers for the 1971 paper, and tell me which one our record leans on hardest. Then tell me what principle we'd be signing onto with each possible vote — because I've started to wonder whether we're in the business of measuring closeness to the truth or measuring contribution to the growth of knowledge, and those don't look like the same job. If the record genuinely underdetermines this case, say so bluntly and point to the dimension none of our six precedents touches. And finally: what case, dropped onto a future slate, would force this committee to show its hand? I want something where two of those rules are forced to vote differently in public, so I can stop guessing at my colleagues.

One caveat, and it's a real one: I probably have mixed up which of those rules is Popper's original and which is somebody's later repair of it. I learned this from a paperback and a great many empty afternoons, so correct me where I've garbled it.
