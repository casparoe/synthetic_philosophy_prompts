Working through a chapter on the value of information, and my gut verdicts aren't cooperating with the textbook line — the one where free information can't be worse than no information, because you can always ignore it. Where I actually land:

- Scan that decides between two treatments: get it, obviously.
- Same scan, except the treatment comes out the same either way and it only settles a fact about me: I'd still get it, and I don't think that's irrational, even though "ignore it" delivers the identical outcome.
- Friend who wants to look up the base rates for her kind of startup. She insists she'd keep going whatever it says. My verdict: don't look.
- Me refreshing my inbox to see if my paper got rejected early, while also insisting I'd start the next project either way. My verdict: fine, look. I know these two are suppossed to be the same shape.
- A pill that would make me love the job I'm planning to quit. Don't take it, even though the me who takes it would be thrilled about it.
- News that the machine that produced my test result was miscalibrated, when a retest is expensive and I can't un-hear the original result: don't want to know, I'd be worse off in limbo than I am now.
- Finding out whether a study I'm relying on was pre-registered, before finding out what it says: worth it.

The principle I keep reaching for is: information is worth having iff, once you have it, you'll end up doing something your present self endorses — counting everything your future self will know and want. But that hands my friend and me the same verdict, and I'm not willing to give up the difference between those two cases.

Is there a principle, or a short set of them, that captures most of this? I want each clause tied to the case that forces it, your call on two cases I haven't listed — paying to have a result withheld from me when I know I'd handle it well, and learning the odds of a risk I can no longer do anything about — and an honest account of which of my verdicts has to go.
