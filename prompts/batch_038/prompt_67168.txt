In six weeks I am giving the second half of a job talk — the part where I present my own paper — at a philosophy of science colloquium. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the view, and not a survey of the literature on it.

**The audience.** Roughly twenty people: philosophers of science who are naturalists and would describe themselves as empiricists, but who take the indispensability argument to have settled the ontological question in favor of mathematical realism; two Quineans in the front row who hold that confirmation reaches mathematical ontology only as a package deal with the whole empirical theory, and who are suspicious of any view that tries to carve the package up; one visiting philosopher of mathematics who is an outright platonist; one person who works on formal verification and leans constructivist; and two or three graduate students who have absorbed enough experimental philosophy to be hostile to appeals to what mathematicians "intuitively" accept. Nobody in the room is a working research mathematician, but several teach or supervise in joint programs with the mathematics department and are used to mathematicians in the audience. What this room cares about is whether a view has content: what it rules out, what it predicts, and what it costs.

**The view I am defending.** Empirical science does not justify mathematical belief by showing it to be true; it justifies it by showing it to be reliable, and reliability is not truth-conduciveness in disguise. Four claims, in order of how much I depend on them:

1. The empirical success of a theory confirms the *reliability* of the mathematics used to derive its predictions — reliability in the sense that the mathematics, applied to well-characterized empirical inputs, systematically yields outputs that bear out — and does not confirm the *truth* of that mathematics. The gap is not merely verbal: reliability is a property of a practice, truth is a property of sentences, and the first can obtain where we have no license to assert the second.
2. This breaks the standard move from indispensability to realism. Holistic confirmation delivers warrant for mathematics, but the warrant it delivers is weaker than the realist needs, and no amount of additional predictive success closes the gap.
3. Warrant therefore comes in degrees indexed to how heavily a body of mathematics has been *exercised* in successful prediction and in engineering practice that has demonstrably worked. The analysis applied in a validated numerical scheme is strongly warranted. Mathematics developed without empirical application acquires a weaker, derivative warrant through its inferential entanglement with the exercised parts. I accept that this yields a large and unevenly distributed agnosticism, and I do not regard that as a bug.
4. I am not a fictionalist and not a nominalist. Mathematical sentences are truth-apt; I am simply agnostic about what makes them true and about whether anything does. The view is intended to be compatible with platonist ontology, which is part of what I am selling: it is supposed to be an account of justification that both sides can share.

**The argument sketch I will give in the talk.** Empirical confirmation works by showing that a theory's successes are not flukes. For physical claims the best such account is approximate truth. For mathematical claims there is a rival and strictly weaker account available: the successes are non-accidental because the mathematics is reliable. Nothing in the empirical evidence distinguishes the two accounts, since both predict exactly the same record of successes; and science has no use for mathematical truth beyond reliability, so there is no internal pressure to prefer the stronger hypothesis.

**What I want from you.** Write the prep sheet.

Please structure your answer with exactly these sections, in this order:

- **Opening concessions** (about 200 words). The two or three things I should grant in the first minute, before anyone asks, because they will be asked anyway and because conceding them costs me nothing. Say explicitly what I must *not* concede in the same breath.
- **The objections, ranked** (about 1,600 words total; between seven and nine objections). Order them by how much damage they would do to the view if I handled them badly, not by how likely they are. For each: a label identifying which segment of this particular room raises it; the objection phrased the way that segment would phrase it, in the first person, as a question I would actually hear from the floor — using their vocabulary, not mine; a reply deliverable in about two minutes of speaking; a severity rating (fatal / serious / survivable / soft) with one sentence of justification; and one sentence naming where the reply is weakest, including whether the weakness is one I can repair on the spot or one that shows up later in print.
- **The load-bearing reply** (about 250 words). The single answer that has to land for the talk to be judged a success, which objection it belongs to, and the one-sentence version I should be able to produce even if the rest goes badly.
- **The question I am hoping nobody asks** (about 250 words). State it, say why it is worse than everything on the ranked list, and give me either the least bad answer or an honest account of why there is none.

Two constraints on the whole sheet. Everything in it must be usable by me in the room: no invented quotations, no citations, no named attributions of objections to particular authors, and no reference to sources I could not be expected to have memorized. The objections should be generated from the four claims and the argument sketch above and from the composition of this audience — a different room would raise different ones, and I want these, not the generic ones.
