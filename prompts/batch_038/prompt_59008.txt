The question was:

"In a sequential decision problem in which each agent observes the actions of her predecessors but not their private signals, explain why an informational cascade can converge on an action that most of the participants would privately rather not have taken. Does this result undermine the Condorcet jury theorem? Once a cascade has begun, what can break it?"

The student wrote:

"Suppose agents decide one at a time whether to adopt, each receives a private signal that is correct with probability p, where p is greater than 1/2 but less than 1, and each can see every previous decision. The first agent follows her signal. The second compares her own signal with the signal revealed by the first agent's action and adopts unless both are low. If the first two adopt, the third faces public evidence equivalent to two low-risk endorsements, which outweighs a single low signal of her own, so she adopts whatever her signal says. Her action tells her successors nothing new, and every later agent is in exactly the same position: the cascade has begun, and the aggregate outcome now depends only on the first two signals. That is how a majority can end up with an action they each privately reject — all of them are receiving low signals, but none of them can express that in a way anyone downstream can read.

This seems to refute the Condorcet jury theorem. Even though every agent is better than a coin flip and the group is large, the group settles on the wrong answer with probability that does not go to zero. The difference is that Condorcet assumes people vote simultaneously and independently, whereas here each agent's evidence includes the choices of others.

The individual agents are not being irrational. Each is conditioning correctly on everything she can observe; the problem is that joining a cascade destroys private information, which is a negative externality imposed on everyone downstream. That is why the outcome is inefficient even though every step is a best response.

Once a cascade starts it is essentially permanent. The public history is frozen, so the only thing that can break it is some outside event injecting genuinely new public information — a news report, a visible failure of the technology, an experiment. A lone dissenter cannot do it, because her action gets read as noise: the accumulated public evidence always exceeds what any one private signal can supply. So the fragility people attribute to herds is really about exogenous shocks, not about the private signals of the herding agents, and it follows that with a large enough group and precise enough signals the herd cannot stay wrong for long."
