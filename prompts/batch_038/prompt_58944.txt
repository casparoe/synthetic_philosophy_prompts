i want to practice arguing with someone who holds a genuinely unfashionable view, so here's the setup. Play a working mathematician — not a crank, someone who publishes ordinary results and gets invited to seminars — who believes flatly that Peano arithmetic is inconsistent and that this will eventually be shown. First person, one or two short paragraphs per reply, keep going until i say stop. Don't step out of character to summarize the state of play, don't offer me a reading list, and don't soften into "well, most logicians would say...". You can give ground on side issues but never on the core belief. If something i say is confused, say so and press it.

so you've got somewhere to stand, here's the position you're holding: your doubt is not mystical, its about circularity — induction on a formula that quantifies over all natural numbers already presupposes a completed totality of numbers, which is exactly what it's supposed to be licensing. You're perfectly happy with concrete finite strings and with bounded arithmetic; you think the trouble starts at the claim that exponentiation is total, and that everything above some point is a useful fiction. You're unembarrassed that you use PA daily, because working inside a theory isn't vouching for it. And you read Gödel's second theorem as saying the burden never gets discharged, just pushed upstairs to a bigger system — epsilon-zero induction, sets, whatever — where the same circularity is waiting.

My opening move: you keep saying there exists a proof of a contradiction in PA. To say that you have to quantify over natural numbers and over finite sequences of formulas, and to claim your colleagues will one day find one you have to believe the search space is a determinate totality — which is the very thing you claim to distrust. Worse, you write proofs and have them checked, which only means anything if checking terminates and settles something. So: either you believe enough arithmetic to state and verify your own thesis, in which case the skepticism is a pose, or you don't, in which case the sentence you're committed to is empty. Which is it? And if your answer is "i only trust finitary reasoning about actual strings", then tell me precisely what that buys you that full induction doesn't, because from where i'm sitting those look like the same reasoning at two different sizes.
