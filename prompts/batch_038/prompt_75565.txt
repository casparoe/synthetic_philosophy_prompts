I sit on the program committee of a mid-tier ML conference — think second or third in prestige, not one of the giants — and this year's policy question has the board completely deadlocked, so we've asked for written positions ahead of the March meeting.

The issue: a growing share of our submissions are results that could only have been produced with compute no university group can touch. Not "we used a big cluster," but training runs where the ablations alone cost more than most of our authors' annual research budgets. Right now we review them exactly like everything else — no disclosure requirement, no separate standard, no questions asked.

The two camps talk past each other constantly. One side says we'd be insane to turn that work away: those papers are why anyone submits or attends here at all, and if we screen them out they go to a bigger venue and we spend five years sliding into irrelevance. That side also points out that basically no one in the room actually believes our own screening would change the field's trajectory by even a hair. The other side says that if you run the numbers on where this leads, you get a field where the only interesting empirical questions are the ones a handful of well-funded labs can afford to ask, where a clever idea that needs no GPUs can't compete, and where an entire generation of grad students learns that the discipline they picked has nothing for them. There's a compromise floating around — mandatory compute and energy disclosure plus some kind of separate evaluation category — but the first camp calls it stigmatizing the exact work that keeps us alive and the second camp calls it a fig leaf.

Draft the memo I'll circulate to the committee. It needs a clear bottom line — accept as-is, accept under specific conditions, or decline this class of submissions — plus the actual policy language I'd move for, a note on who enforces it and how (disclosure that nobody checks is worse than none), and a realistic account of what it'd do to our numbers over the next three to five years.

Two things I want it to really wrestle with rather than gesture at. First, the "you can't defect alone" objection: if one conference acting by itself provably can't shift an equilibrium, how much weight should that carry, and is there any version of the objection that actually holds? Second, the mirror-image point the field-health camp has to answer — if the situation really is as bad for the discipline as they claim, then a policy that speeds up the deterioration can't be good for us as a venue either, since our whole standing rests on the field staying healthy. So either that claim is overblown or the "we're too small to matter" argument cuts both ways. I want the memo to say which horn it takes.

Please be forthright about where your reasoning is shakiest — which step in the chain would you expect an economist on the board to attack first — and close with the single piece of evidence or argument that would flip your recommendation to one of the other options. Don't hedge into a both-sides shrug; I'd rather have a committed position I can push back on than a survey of the disagreement.
