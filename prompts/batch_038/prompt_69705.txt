I have just been seated on my state's K–12 science standards review committee, and the first substantive fight I have walked into is not the one I expected: what our standards will say about Pluto. Half the committee insists we are settling terminology and should print whatever is least confusing to a thirteen-year-old; the other half insists we are reporting what astronomers established about the solar system and have no business second-guessing it. I would like the underlying episode analyzed through the analytic–synthetic distinction, with Quine's Two Dogmas of Empiricism as the lens.

The facts, as our committee has established them. In August 2006, at its General Assembly in Prague, the International Astronomical Union adopted a formal definition of "planet" for the first time, requiring that a body orbit the Sun, be massive enough for its self-gravity to pull it into hydrostatic equilibrium, and have cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit. It created a new category, dwarf planet, into which Pluto was placed along with Ceres and Eris, and it voted down an alternative that would have kept dwarf planets as a subclass of planets. The trigger was empirical: Eris and a growing population of Kuiper Belt objects comparable to Pluto, with Eris turning out to be the more massive. Yet a substantial body of planetary scientists rejected the definition at the time and still reject it, arguing for a geophysical definition on which roundness is what matters; and the criteria do not sit perfectly with the intended extension, since the definition is framed only for bodies orbiting the Sun and at least one accepted planet is not obviously in hydrostatic equilibrium.

Now the analysis, running in both directions. First: did the 2006 resolution change the meaning of a word, or report a discovery? Lay out what each answer commits us to about the sentences we printed before 2006 — did they become false, or merely obsolete, or something else — and say who bears the cost on each account: the textbook publishers, the astronomers, the students. Second: given that the definition was adopted for empirical reasons and remains revisable for empirical reasons, does the episode confirm Quine's picture, on which no statement is immune to revision and none is confirmed or refuted in isolation, or is it an anomaly — a scientific community appearing to do by resolution precisely what Quine argues cannot be done cleanly? Third: is the continuing dispute with the geophysical camp substantive or merely about how to use a term, and what could settle that?

Finally, tell me where the lens runs out. Quine may be right that every sentence in our standards is revisable in principle, but we must print something this year, and I do not see holism telling us which revisable sentences are worth entrenching in a curriculum. Say plainly what the theory is silent about here, then give me your recommendation for the standard, with the reasoning exposed.
