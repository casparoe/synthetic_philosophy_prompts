my reading group has two people who have been going at each other about the units of selection for basically a whole semester. One is a hardcore replicator-first Dawkins type: organisms and groups are just vehicles, only replicators are units of selection, and "group selection" talk is at best a bookkeeping convenience that can always be rewritten without it. The other is a Sober-Wilson style multilevel person: selection is a process that can run at several levels at once, and a group with group-level heredity is a genuine unit, not a fiction. They talk past each other constantly and I'm tired of it. Write the document the two of them should have co-signed after an actual good-faith adversarial collaboration.

To keep them from just rehashing the textbook examples, run the whole thing through this case. I invented it, and I want you to tell me if it's internally incoherent before you use it, because if it is that changes everything:

A colonial sessile invertebrate, call it C. Colonies grow by budding and can also break into fragments; two fragments that share a self-recognition allele fuse into a single body with mixed cell lineages, so a lot of adult colonies are chimeras. Within a colony there are two heritable cell-lineage strategies: disperser-biased cells, which convert most of their resources into larvae, and bud-biased cells, which convert almost all of it into local growth and make very few larvae. Inside a colony, and especially inside a fused chimera, bud-biased lineages reliably take over the body — they just out-proliferate. Across colonies it goes the other way: bud-biased-dominated colonies grow fast but release few larvae, so they leave fewer new colonies. Complication one: the bud-biased lineage also carries a resistance factor against a boring parasite that otherwise wipes out disperser-biased colonies in the densest, most crowded parts of the reef, and crowding is where fusion is most common. Complication two, and this is the one I care about: there's a separate mobile element, plasmid-like, that moves horizontally through the water column between unrelated colonies and also gets passed down vertically. It carries a policing function that suppresses bud-biased proliferation. It spreads readily into colonies that contain no bud-biased cells at all — where it does nothing but impose a small fecundity cost — and it can hop into a different, related species sharing the same reef.

Given that, here's what I want out of the joint statement:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign. I'd bet money they can both sign that one and the same set of recurrence equations predicts the frequency changes in all three entities (cell lineages, colonies, the mobile element), so pin down exactly what's agreed there and what is *not* covered by that agreement — because I suspect each of them thinks the covariance partitioning settles something it doesn't.
2. A disagreements table: one row per live dispute, with columns for the issue, the formulation of that issue the replicator person would accept as a fair statement of their own view, the formulation the multilevel person would accept for theirs, a judgement of whether that row is substantive or verbal, and the crux underneath it.
3. For every crux, the derivation, experiment, or field observation both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance — not "it depends on the parameters," an actual signed prediction.
4. Explicit answers to: does the mobile element's spread count as group selection, colony-level selection, or a selfish element whose interests happen to align with the host's, and can the two camps agree on any of that? And do fused chimeras constitute a level at which there is heredity at all, given that fusion partners are frequently close kin but not always?
5. At least one row where you say neither side can currently answer, plus what specific data would be needed to answer it.
6. If some chunk of the dispute turns out to be about the word "unit," say which chunk, and give the two definitions side by side so I can see how much of the semester was verbal.

Keep it in their joint voice, like something both would put their names on, not a referee's report.
