I am revising a draft for a short paper on Kepler and astrology, and I would value critical comments rather than encouragement. The draft is below. My questions follow it, and they are specific: please answer them in order, and please be blunt about the parts that fail.

**Draft: The Daughter Who Keeps the Books — Kepler's Astrology as a Research Programme**

**1. The standard picture.** Kepler is usually presented as a transitional figure: a mathematician of the first rank who happened to live before astronomy and astrology had separated, and who cast horoscopes largely because that was how imperial and municipal mathematicians earned their salaries. On this reading, his annual prognostications at Graz, Prague and Linz belong to the economic history of science, not the intellectual history; his astronomical work is the real Kepler, and the astrology is scaffolding that later generations quietly removed.

**2. What I want to argue instead.** The scaffolding reading gets the direction of dependence backwards. For Kepler, astrological influence was not a residual superstition but a positive physical hypothesis with a determinate mechanism, and that mechanism is the same one that structures his mature cosmology. On my reading, his account runs roughly as follows. What matters is not a planet's position within a sign but the angular relation between two planets as seen from Earth. Some angles are privileged because their ratios correspond to figures constructible by straightedge and compass, and hence to the harmonic ratios of musical consonance. Those configurations do not push or pull on bodies; they excite a sub-lunar faculty — a quasi-perceptive capacity of the Earth as a whole, responsible for weather and for the behaviour of living things, and, in the individual case, a capacity imprinted on the soul at birth — much as a tune stirs a listener without exerting mechanical force on him. The influence is thus a triggering of a resonant system by a geometrical pattern.

This is why the continuity with the *Harmonice Mundi* is not an embarrassment to be explained away. The harmony of the world and the efficacy of celestial configurations are, for Kepler, the same harmony considered from two sides: as the architecture God used, and as the signal that terrestrial nature is tuned to receive.

**3. The reform, not the retreat.** The consequence is a systematic pruning of traditional practice, which Kepler carries out explicitly in the treatise on the more certain foundations of astrology written in his first Prague years, and defends a decade later in the *Tertius Interveniens* — a text whose title announces a third-party intervention between those who swallow astrology whole and those who dismiss it entirely. What survives is what has harmonic warrant: the major aspects. What goes is what has none: the significance of the twelve signs as such, much of the traditional house doctrine, and the apparatus of particular prediction. Correspondingly, astrological claims are restricted in kind — to weather, to temperament and general disposition — rather than merely hedged in degree. Kepler's position is thus not a compromise between credulity and scepticism; it is a reconstruction on a single principle.

**4. The failure, precisely located.** Here is the part I am least sure of. Kepler's astronomy succeeded because a hypothesis was scored against one unforgiving quantity: the observed positions of Mars, which eventually forced the ellipse. His astrology had the first half of that discipline — a principled criterion for which configurations count — but not the second. No configuration was ever abandoned because a prognostication failed, and Kepler himself acknowledged that astrological pronouncements admit of being read loosely. So the indictment is not that he believed astrology, but that he exempted it from the very discipline he was in the business of inventing. He kept the criterion and dropped the cost.

**5. A distinction I want to defend.** I do not think this is just Popper's falsifiability applied to an easy case, and the paper turns on whether I can show it. Unfalsifiability is a property of a proposition's logical form; what goes wrong in the almanacs looks different. The astrological hypothesis is not vague in its statement but diffuse in its *scoring*: it is matched after the fact against a large, unweighted, open-ended set of outcomes — a cold spring, a prince's illness, a bad harvest, a war — any of which can be nominated as the confirming instance, with no rule fixed in advance for which outcomes count or how much weight each carries. I provisionally call this evaluative diffuseness, and claim it is separable from unfalsifiability: a hypothesis can be perfectly precise in form and still be scored diffusely, and conversely a hypothesis can be scored by a single designated quantity even when its content is broad.

**6. A test case I have not yet worked out.** Kepler spent years defending his mother against a prosecution for witchcraft, and won her release. I want to ask whether the evidential standards he deployed there — the insistence that the accusers' testimony be internally consistent and that specific causal claims be pinned down — are the standards missing from the almanacs, and whether the comparison is illuminating or a category mistake.

**My questions**

1. **Accuracy.** Please go through sections 2, 3 and 6 and flag every claim that is wrong, overstated, or a misleading compression. I am especially unsure about: whether Kepler really did dismiss the signs and most of the house doctrine as firmly as I claim; whether the "excitation of a faculty by an angular configuration" mechanism is a fair reconstruction rather than my invention; whether the *Tertius Interveniens* is correctly characterised as intervening against wholesale rejection as well as wholesale acceptance; and whether anything I say about his mother's prosecution is inaccurate.

2. **Novelty.** Is the thesis in section 2 anything more than the standard transitional-figure account with new vocabulary? Concretely: if a specialist in early-modern astrology read this, would sections 2 and 3 contain a single claim they had not seen before, or would they recognise it as the received account of Kepler's reformed astrology?

3. **The load-bearing distinction.** Does evaluative diffuseness in section 5 earn its keep, or is it falsifiability renamed? Please try to break it: give me the strongest case that it collapses back into Popper, and the strongest case that it collapses into something else entirely — a Bayesian point about the absence of a fixed likelihood, or a point about ad hoc rescue, or a sociological point about who does the scoring.

4. **Question-begging.** In section 3 I infer that the natural/judicial split follows from the harmonic principle. But have I shown that, or have I assumed the split was principled and then supplied a principle that would explain it? What would count as evidence that Kepler himself drew the line for the reason I attribute to him, and what evidence would show he drew it for convenience?

5. **Anachronism.** Section 4 accuses Kepler of exempting astrology from a discipline he was inventing. Is that unfair, in a way that would sink the argument rather than merely complicate it — given that he had no conception of a unified method governing both enterprises? If it is unfair, is there a version of the charge that survives?

6. **The strongest objection.** State the single best objection to the paper as a whole, the one I would most want to have answered before submitting it, and tell me where a concession should be inserted rather than a rebuttal attempted.

7. **Structure.** Should the paper open with the mechanism (section 2) or with the economic-scaffolding reading it aims to displace (section 1)? And is section 6 doing work, or is it a suggestive aside that a referee will ask me to cut?
