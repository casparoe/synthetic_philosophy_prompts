I got through most of it fine but there's this stretch where he's arguing with other peoples definitions of what counts as a machine and I honestly can't follow the logic. Here's the bit:

"Another explanation of the difference between tool and machine is that in the case of a tool, man is the motive power, while the motive power of a machine is something different from man, as, for instance, an animal, water, wind, and so on. According to this, a plough drawn by oxen, which is a contrivance common to the most different epochs, would be a machine, while Claussen's circular loom, which, worked by a single labourer, weaves 96,000 picks per minute, would be a mere tool. Nay, this very loom, though a tool when worked by hand, would, if worked by steam, be a machine. And since the application of animal power is one of man's earliest inventions, production by machinery would have preceded production by handicrafts. When in 1735, John Wyatt brought out his spinning machine, and began the industrial revolution of the 18th century, not a word did he say about an ass driving it instead of a man, and yet this part fell to the ass. He described it as a machine 'to spin without fingers.'"

and just before it he dismisses the other definition — tool = simple machine, machine = complex tool — saying that from the economic standpoint it's "worth nothing, because the historical element is wanting." Then a couple paragraphs later he lays out the three parts of fully developed machinery (motor mechanism, transmitting mechanism, tool or working machine), stresses that the tool/working machine is the part the industrial revolution actually started from, and gives what I think is his own definition:

"The machine proper is therefore a mechanism that, after being set in motion, performs with its tools the same operations that were formerly done by the workman with similar tools."

What I'm stuck on:

Is he saying a definition that holds across all epochs can't do the work he wants a definition to do here, and if so what work is that?

Is there one criterion I'm not seeing, or is he switching between a taxonomic question (what makes this a machine) and a historical one (what makes this specifically modern industry)?
