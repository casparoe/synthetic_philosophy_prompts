I'm a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course whose current unit pairs Block's distinction between access consciousness and phenomenal consciousness with global workspace theory. I've volunteered to run the revision session for my classmates — about twenty of us, all of whom have done the assigned reading, none of whom has read the neuroscientific literature — and rather than summarise the debate I want to hand them a self-diagnostic decision tree that sorts each of us by where we actually stand on the dissociation question, not by which label we would choose from a list.

Please build the tree to the following specification: five to seven nodes and eight to ten leaves, each leaf a named position (or, if no one defends it, marked as unnamed with a one-sentence diagnosis). Every node must be a concrete case or a yes/no commitment about a finding, and no node may use the vocabulary of the debate as a term of art — nothing like "phenomenal," "access," "broadcast," or "report" — so that a classmate can answer from the case alone. The cases I want the nodes built out of are: a partial-report display in which subjects insist the array vanished before they could read it yet can name several items when cued by location; a counting task in which an unexpected figure crosses the frame and goes entirely unnoticed; a masking study in which sensory cortex responds to a stimulus the subject denies having seen at all; and a guessing task with a cortically blind subject who denies seeing anything yet localises above chance.

Two requirements matter most to me. First, the tree must keep two axes apart that our seminar constantly runs together: a constitutive axis (can experience obtain when none of its content is available for reasoning and verbal guidance of action?) and an evidential axis (could it ever be *shown* to obtain, given that every measurement of experience goes through report or task performance?). Mark, at each leaf, which axis its central commitment really sits on, and tell me which pairs of classmates will discover that they have been answering different questions. Second, find places for these four views if the logic of the tree allows it: the view that global ignition is what experience consists in, so that no dissociation is possible even in principle; the view that locally recurrent processing within sensory cortex suffices for experience even when nothing becomes available for report; a graded or partial-awareness view that denies the dissociation question has a yes/no answer; and a view on which consciousness attaches to attended intermediate-level perceptual representations rather than to globally available ones.

Then flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate on this evidence, and say what further finding would separate them or why no finding could. Note any combination of answers the nodes permit that no actual position occupies, and say briefly whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because no one has explored it. Exclude integrated information theory, panpsychism, and the hard problem except where a leaf's commitment genuinely depends on them, and say where you have cut and why.

End your response with a single line in exactly this form:

Unoccupied cell: <the combination of answers, or 'none'>
