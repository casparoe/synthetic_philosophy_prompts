My philosophy of mind seminar has been arguing about whether a colleague's constant consultation of a large language model makes that model part of her cognitive system, and one participant tried to end the discussion by reading out Clark and Chalmers's parity principle: "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process." One student objected that this is a stipulation about the adjective "cognitive" and so proves nothing; another replied that if it were only a stipulation it would be idle, hence it must be a substantive claim about where minds are. Lay out, as a numbered menu I can take back to the seminar, the distinct propositions that sentence could be asserting — readings concerning concept application, concept revision, the metaphysics of coupled systems, and the evidential force of the counterfactual, including readings on which that counterfactual cannot be evaluated at all because nothing resembling the model's output could happen in a head. For each, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false; then say which readings, if any, would deliver the conclusion about the language model, and whether "functions as a process" is satisfied by outputs she merely consults on demand or requires something further, such as endorsement and integration.
