I keep tripping over how ancient-history people sort these three stories, and I cant quite tell what rule its actually following.

Troy: the place is real, Hisarlik, and one of the settlement layers burned around 1190-ish BCE; there's also a place-name in Hittite letters that looks like the same city. But the poems come down to us out of oral tradition, maybe four centuries after that burning. Verdict: no consensus, but most people allow a historical core while rejecting the gods and the horse.

Arthur: the first text that names him turns up around 828, roughly three centuries after the battles it describes, and the writer who covered those wars much closer in time never mentions him at all. No site, no archaeology. Verdict among specialists today: essentially a legendary figure, no fire under all that smoke.

Exodus: the Sinai is close to empty of second-millennium occupation, including the oasis where the story parks the group for decades, and the headcount in the text works out to a couple million people and their herds, which is the sort of thing a state bureaucracy and its archaeology would register. Verdict: not as described, and yet most scholars still allow a historical core, some small group that came out of Egypt.

So two of these have a three-to-four-century gap between events and first surviving text, and one gap buys you a kernel while the other buys you a myth. What view of evidence and testimony does that practice presuppose? Does it assume oral poetry can carry a real place-name and real obsolete equipment across four centuries but not a real war-leader's name across three, and if so, what's the theory of transmission that licenses the difference? Is the operative principle something like: absence of traces only counts against a claim in proportion to how visible the claimed event would have been? Does the practice apply that explicitly, or just happen to reach defensible verdicts by other routes? And why does the historical-core hedge survive in all three cases despite such different evidence — does it presuppose that foundational stories are distorted memories rather than things anyone ever sat down and invented, and what rival account of how legends get made does that quietly exclude? Tell me what would have to change in these verdicts if the field swapped its implicit rule for an explicit one.
