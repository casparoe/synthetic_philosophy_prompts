Last night I spent the better part of three hours arguing with a friend about Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun*, and I have come away genuinely unsure which of us was wrong. He is a graduate student in philosophy of computation; I write criticism for a living and am currently assembling a longer piece on how recent literary science fiction handles minds. His claim is that the novel is, whether or not it intends to be, an argument against the Church-Turing thesis: Capaldi's project of building a body for Josie and having Klara continue her is meant to fail, and the reason it must fail is that Josie's mind is not effectively calculable — so the book tacitly endorses the denial of a strong physical version of the thesis. My objection was that he is reading a metaphysical claim into a novel that never makes one, and, more sharply, that his argument only gets off the ground if one already assumes the thesis is the sort of thing a work of fiction could confirm or refute, which is precisely the disputed question about its epistemic status. He said I was dodging; I suspect I was, a little, and I would like the reading you produce to be the thing I should have said instead.

So: please take both the philosophy and the novel seriously, and do not let either collapse into an illustration of the other. On the philosophical side I want the piece to be literate about what the thesis actually says and about the live disagreement over its standing — the thesis as a claim about effective calculability, about what an idealized human clerk working through rules on paper can achieve, and how that differs from Gandy's distinct thesis about the limits of physical mechanism; the three candidate statuses (a stipulation or piece of conceptual analysis that fixes the extension of *computable* and is therefore true by fiat and immune to counterexample; an empirical generalization, and in its physical form a claim about the causal structure of the world, falsifiable at least in principle; or a methodological norm governing what will count as an acceptable finitary procedure); Cleland's complaint that Turing ran together effective procedures with ordinary mundane ones such as following a recipe, so that the thesis rests on a category confusion rather than being false; and the hypercomputation literature — accelerated machines, Zeno-style supertasks, Malament-Hogarth spacetimes — together with the standard rejoinder that such devices lean on idealizations of infinite precision, unbounded energy, or exact timing that dissolve the very notion of procedure they were supposed to preserve. I need you to say which of these statuses, if any, the novel's own commitments require, and whether my friend's argument survives once you notice that Klara's sun-worship is a wildly false causal ontology executed by an impeccably finite routine, which is a very different thing from non-computability.

On the literary side, work through particular material rather than themes in the abstract: the solar theology and the bargains Klara strikes — the trek to the barn she takes to be the Sun's resting place, the destruction of the Cootings Machine at the cost of part of the P-E-G Nine solution she carries in her head, the second plea and Josie's subsequent recovery; the society of lifted children with its on-screen tutors and its quiet sorting of who counts as educable; Chrissie's instruction that Klara practise imitating her daughter, and the many rooms Klara imagines inside Josie, any of which she believes she could learn given enough attention; and the closing scene in the yard, where Klara arrives at the thought that what was special about Josie was never inside Josie but inside those who loved her. That last move is the question I actually care about, and it is the one my friend thought I was evading: the ending relocates the target. What is worth replicating turns out not to be a property of the system being simulated but a property of the attitudes surrounding it, and if that is right, the interesting failure of Capaldi's enterprise has nothing whatever to do with what is or is not Turing-computable. Does the novel thereby dramatize a distinction the thesis debate has no vocabulary for — between what can in principle be computed and what can be specified or picked out from inside a practice of love and grief — or is that only me doing to the book what I accused my friend of doing to it? Answer that, and be honest in both directions: where the computational lens genuinely illuminates a scene or a character's choice, where the novel is simply indifferent to it, and where pressing the analogy distorts the fiction, the philosophy, or both. Finish by telling me what, if anything, the novel contributes to the debate over the thesis's status that argument alone could not have supplied. No plot summary, no survey of interpretations, and please do not hand me a bibliography — I want criticism, reasoned out on the page.
