I'm putting together the take-home for a second-year grad seminar on risk and regulation — all philosophy PhD students, so they've read the standard stuff. I want one essay question, sized for about two hours of work, worth 25 marks, on the precautionary principle, and I want the difficulty to come from a place they can't reach by reciting the familiar lines (that PP is vague, that there's a strong/weak distinction, that the Sunstein-style objection makes it self-defeating or irrational). They all know that cold and it'd be worth about four marks of real thinking.

The angle I want is that PP needs a comparison class of options before it can even get started, because its trigger is usually phrased in terms of "an activity" that "raises threats" — and what counts as the activity, as opposed to the background, is exactly what's up for grabs. So here's the case I want the question built on, and I'd like you to use these details pretty much as given rather than inventing a cleaner one:

A water utility serving about 200,000 people has started detecting a fungicide metabolite, F-met, at 6 µg/L in its intake. There's an established association with a developmental defect at concentrations of 25 µg/L and above; below that, the dose–response is simply unknown, and nobody has a view on whether there's a threshold. Three options are on the table. (A) Status quo — no additional treatment, keep doing quarterly monitoring. (B) Retrofit an ozonation stage ahead of the existing filters: cuts F-met by roughly 85%, but the intake water is bromide-rich, so this reliably generates bromate, which does have a well-characterised dose–response and a small quantifiable excess risk. (C) Pilot a new catalytic medium that cuts F-met by more than 99%, but the medium sheds a breakdown product that has never been toxicologically characterised at all; shedding depends on water chemistry that varies seasonally, and there is no probability estimate of any kind for the harm, not even an order of magnitude.

The board has adopted this rule, and I want it quoted verbatim in the student-facing question so everyone's working from the same wording: "Where an activity raises plausible threats of serious or irreversible harm, and the causal pathways to that harm are not established with confidence, the activity shall not proceed and the burden of demonstrating safety falls on those proposing it."

What I want students to have to do is show that the rule delivers contradictory verdicts on this case under different defensible ways of fixing the comparison class — depending on whether the baseline is the pre-detection status quo, the plant as currently operated, or the option actually being proposed — and then say what it would take to fix the baseline without collapsing the rule into either a licence for inaction or a blanket veto on everything, including continuing to run the plant at all. I'd also like the question to make them confront whether a maximin or "reasonable worst case" reading rescues determinacy here, given that option C is the one where there is no worst-case estimate to maximise against, which is precisely the situation PP is supposed to be built for.

Please give me: the question text exactly as students would see it (with the constraints and the mark allocation on the face of it); a model answer of around 700 words; and a marking scheme totalling 25 that names the specific moves a full-credit answer has to make, puts a number against each, and says which mistakes should cost marks — including the one where a student just says "it depends how you define the baseline" and stops, which I expect at least a third of them to do.

One thing about the model answer: don't let it hedge. I want it to land on a definite verdict about whether the rule as stipulated picks out a unique recommendation in this case, commit to one repair of the comparison-class problem rather than surveying three and shrugging, and say why the repairs it rejects fail. If you think a particular answer strategy is the right one and should get full marks, say so flatly. Same for the marking scheme — I'd rather it be arguable and decisive than neutral and unusable, and I can soften it myself if I need to.
