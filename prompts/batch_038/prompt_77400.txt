I run a small graduate seminar that's split this term between formal epistemology and the AI-risk literature, and one of my students handed in what he called a problem set but is really an argument he thinks demolishes Solomonoff induction. I've spent two days on it and still can't tell the class where it slips. The setup he assumes is the standard one: we're doing induction over infinite binary sequences, the universal prior weights a hypothesis by roughly 2 to the minus its description length, and then there's that malign-prior line of thought about consequentialists living *inside* the candidate universes who'd like to steer what a predictor using that prior ends up believing. His steps:

(1) Under a complexity-weighted prior, every extra bit of descriptive complexity in a hypothesis costs half its prior weight. (2) So among hypotheses that fit the observed data equally well, the ones with the more compressible histories dominate, and they dominate exponentially. (3) Any candidate universe containing agents like us contains agents who can shape how compressible their own history looks. (4) Consequentialists inside such a universe who care what predictors elsewhere believe can therefore buy influence on the cheap: keep the past maximally simple and lawlike, and your universe becomes one of the shortest descriptions in the sum. (5) Hypotheses of that shape are computably describable and there are plenty of them, so the universal prior has to assign the whole family non-negligible mass. (6) Prior mass is zero-sum — whatever the steering hypotheses gain, the "nobody was at the wheel" hypotheses lose. (7) And conditional on our actual data we can't tell the two apart, because steering a past so that it looks simple and lawlike just is producing a history that looks exactly like an unsteered lawlike one. (8) So conditional on all the evidence we have, the steered hypotheses are the more probable. (9) Further evidence can't rescue us: more of the sequence is more of the same optimized output, and an optimized output is by construction indistinguishable from the lawful thing it was built to imitate. (10) Hence no complexity-weighted inductive method can ever justify a belief about the world — the universal prior isn't merely malign, its self-undermining.

He isn't just parroting something he read, which is the annoying part. I know the informal version of the malignness worry, and this is that worry run all the way down into skepticism. My first instinct was to attack (3) — hardly every candidate universe contains agents who can re-shape their own past — but he just needs the family to be nonempty, and he's right that any universe containing anything like us will do. I also tried waving the convergence theorem at him and he pointed, correctly I think, to (9), which was written specifically to absorb that reply.

So: name the step you reject and say precisely why it looked innocent when you first read it. I'd like to keep (1) and (2) — complexity weighting is the whole game and I'm not about to give up Solomonoff — and (10) is not something I can accept, so the sacrifice has to come out of the middle. Then price it: if you kill the step you kill, does the malignness worry die with it, or does it survive in a weaker form that still gives me trouble for *deciding* (what credence to give exotic hypotheses, how to treat the possibility of acausal dealmaking) as opposed to for *predicting*? And if your view is that the chain is valid and one of the premises I take to be obvious is the false one, say which and tell me what I lose. Two or three ranked candidate break-points would be more useful to me than one, since I want to be able to explain in seminar why the runners-up don't work either.
