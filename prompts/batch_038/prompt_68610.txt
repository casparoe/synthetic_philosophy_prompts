I chair the panel that hears complaints about misleading advertising at a national self-regulatory body. Our rulebook says an advertisement may mislead by what it implies even when every sentence in it is true, and a large share of what reaches us turns on exactly that: staff have already established that the literal content is accurate, and the only live question is whether a false implied claim was conveyed. Panelists are not philosophers, each case gets about forty-five minutes of preparation, published decisions must be in plain language and must identify the implied claim we found together with a reason the ordinary consumer would take it that way, and because decisions are appealable the reasoning has to be reproducible rather than a matter of taste.

Build us a decision procedure out of Grice's account of conversational implicature. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, and the points where the procedure stops reasoning and hands the issue over to evidence of what consumers actually took away. It must tell us three things in particular: how to fix the content of the candidate implied claim when several are available, including cases where the disputed point is the strength of the implication rather than its topic; how to test whether an implication is genuinely worked out from the advertisement plus ordinary expectations rather than imported by the complainant; and what to make of the observation that implicatures can be cancelled, given that our settled practice is that a small-print qualification usually does not cure a false overall impression — is cancellability a diagnostic, a remedy, or neither? Also address the awkward fact that advertising is not cooperative conversation in Grice's sense, since the consumer knows the advertiser is trying to sell them something, and say what survives of the maxims under that description.

Then run it on three invented advertisements: a fruit drink labelled "made with real fruit juice" where juice is three percent of the volume; a store window reading "up to 40% off" where the forty percent applies to a single slow-moving line; and a mortgage advertisement saying "our customers pay an average rate of 3.1%", which is true, but with a median of 4.8% because the average is pulled down by a small closed portfolio of legacy loans.

At least one of these should come out against my expectations, and I would rather be told so bluntly than have the procedure tuned to match the panel's instincts. Close with what the procedure cannot settle — where it is judgment wearing a procedural costume — and with which distinctions you dropped to make it usable, and why dropping them is safe.
