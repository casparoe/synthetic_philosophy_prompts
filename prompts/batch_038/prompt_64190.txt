You are going to help me write an opening address. Write as a professional philosopher whose specialty is population ethics and the ethics of intervening in the lives of wild animals, and who has spent enough time in front of practitioners to know how a room of people with jobs to do hears an academic. But the address will be delivered by me, in my voice: I am a journalist who covers science and ideas for a national magazine, and I am not a professor, so the register has to be that of a good reporter who has thought harder about this than his editors have — not that of a keynote in a hotel ballroom.

Everything you need is in this message. I am not looking for references, and I do not want any.

The occasion. A funder has convened a two-day, closed-door workshop at a coastal research station, and the convener has asked me to give the opening remarks, roughly seven minutes, at 9:00 on the first morning. The stated purpose of the workshop is to produce a short list of research questions that everyone in the room would agree are worth funding. Nobody expects this to work. The reason I was invited is that I am neither camp: I have written admiringly about habitat restoration and I have also written, unforgivably as far as some people are concerned, about what restoration costs the animals who are already there.

The room. About thirty-five people. Roughly half are field ecologists and conservation practitioners — an island invasives program lead, a state wetland restoration ecologist, two people from a land trust, a wildlife veterinarian, a fishery biologist, a couple of agency staff whose names will appear in any press release. About six work explicitly on wild animal welfare: two academics, three from advocacy organizations, one program officer. There are also two people from a tribal natural resources department. Several of the conservationists hold something like the first-do-no-harm principle from medical ethics as a professional commitment and will hear any welfare talk as an attack on the practice that has kept them employed; several of the welfare people believe, sincerely and on evidence, that most wild animals' lives are worse than nonexistence and that the conservationists are therefore curating suffering and calling it a landscape.

The case I want at the center of the speech. I reported this; use the details exactly as I give them, and do not embellish them. A coastal land trust in the Pacific Northwest breached a dike and returned about three hundred acres of diked farmland to tidal marsh nine years ago. Before the breach, the project's soil ecologist estimated that the fields held on the order of two hundred million arthropods and several thousand voles; she now thinks that figure was conservative. Essentially all of them drowned or were displaced in the first winter. Nine years on, the trust's headline success metric is nesting pairs of a target marsh bird: four before the breach, sixty-one last spring. The trust's own ornithologist told me that in a good year roughly three-quarters of those nests fail, mostly to predation and flooding, and that most of the chicks that do fledge die before their first winter. The trust is now deciding whether to breach a second, larger dike on the adjoining parcel. The decision memo quantifies benefits in acres and nesting pairs. It says nothing whatever about the animals in the first parcel's soil, and nothing about the animals the second parcel's soil currently holds.

Notice what that metric is. It counts animals produced per season at their most visible moment. It is a throughput measure. That is exactly the quantity a welfare-minded person is suspicious of, and I want the speech to notice it without turning the notice into a gotcha.

What the speech has to do. Think this through carefully before you write; the diagnostic is where I want the thinking to be visible. It must (1) tell that case plainly and let it be uncomfortable for everyone, (2) separate three disagreements the room is otherwise going to run together into one shouting match — a disagreement about the facts (how many animals there are and how their lives go), a disagreement about what counts (whether the value of a marsh is exhausted by the welfare of the animals in it, or whether species, processes, and relations carry weight that no sum of individual lives captures), and a disagreement about what to do when the first two are unresolved and the second dike decision is due in a quarter — and (3) make one real concession against my own lean, and then hand the room its task. It must not settle anything. The person who wants the second dike breached and the person who thinks breaching it is a mass harm must both be able to sit through it without feeling insulted, and neither may leave thinking the question went their way.

It also has to face the objection I expect in the first ten minutes, which is that raising this question at all hands an alibi to people who want to fill in wetlands: if the animals are better off dead, the developer's bulldozer becomes a mercy. State that objection in its strongest form and answer it in a way that is not a suggestion that we stop talking.

Constraints. Nine hundred fifty to eleven hundred words. No philosopher's name, no book title, no citation, no allusion that would send anyone to a search bar. At most one term of art, and if you use one, define it in a single sentence a field technician would follow on first hearing. Do not invoke the land ethic, do not close on a line of poetry, do not open with a variant of in these uncertain times, do not stack rhetorical questions in threes. Banned outright: both sides have a point, it's not that simple, we need to have a conversation, at the end of the day. Say something usable to the two people in the room who will have to sign a decision memo this quarter. Do not have me apologize for being a journalist and do not have me claim a neutrality I do not have — I do have a view, which is that this question is real and that most of the people I work with have never seriously entertained it; let that show once, briefly, and then get out of the way. Finally, two people from a tribal natural resources department will be present: do not write a speech that invokes Indigenous knowledge as a way out of the dilemma, and do not write one that behaves as though the land has no prior custodians.

Deliverables, in this order. First, a diagnostic of no more than three hundred words: which of the three disagreements you think the speech should put its weight on and why; the two or three sentences you expect to land hardest against me, i.e., where my exposure is if someone quotes the address out of context; and what you deliberately left out and why. Second, the speech itself, written to be read aloud, with paragraph breaks that indicate where I would pause. Third, separately, a hundred-word replacement opening paragraph for the version of the morning in which the room has already had a fight at breakfast and I can tell within a minute that they know I am about to try to calm them down.
