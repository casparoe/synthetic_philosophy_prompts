I sit on the safety review board of a small regional airline and we have to sign off on the internal report for last month's incident — turboprop, hard landing, right gear collapsed, four passengers injured, no fatalities. The chain our investigators laid out: a retaining bolt in the right gear had been passed its replacement interval for eleven days and nobody swapped it; the maintenance program calls for a second mechanic to verify that swap and the sign-off for it is blank; the torque-verification rig that would have flagged the bolt was pulled offline six weeks earlier for a software update and never put back in service; and nine years ago a now-retired structures engineer wrote a memo saying the bolt's design should be revisited, it was declined, and he never raised it again.

The problem we can't agree on is the form. It gives us one line for the cause of the occurrence and then an open list of contributing factors, and the regulator treats those very differently — the cause line is what gets read into enforcement against individuals, contributing factors only feed safety recommendations. Two of us want the missed replacement in the cause line and everything else as contributing. Our staff engineer says none of them belong there at all: the cause is the cracked bolt and the collapse, these are all things that didn't happen, and calling an absence a cause is just a polite way of saying somebody's to blame — plus once you list failures to act you never stop, since why isn't it also a cause that the manufacturer never redesigned the bolt, or that we never hired enough mechanics to make the schedule stick? Another member points out that the second-mechanic verification and the torque rig were each by itself enough to catch the bolt, so of neither can you say the injuries wouldn't have happened without it, which by the test we normally use means neither is a cause — a result he thinks is obviously absurd.

Report goes to the regulator in nine days, we meet Thursday. Draft the findings section I'll circulate: what actually goes in the cause line, what goes in contributing factors, and why the placement matters given how the regulator uses each. Then give us a standing rule the board can adopt so we're not relitigating this every time an omission comes up. Engage the staff engineer properly instead of just outvoting him — if he's right that we're smuggling blame into a causal field, say what the honest version of the form would look like instead. And tell me what to do about the fueler from the handling company who told an investigator he'd noticed chafing on that gear leg two days before the flight and mentioned it to nobody: we have no jurisdiction over him and I dont know whether he belongs in this report at all.
