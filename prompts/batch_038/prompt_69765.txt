ok so I'm putting together something for a philosophy-of-mind reading group and I want a piece that's funnier than anything I can find. Here's the ask:

Write me a complete advice column — the newspaper kind, with reader letters and the columnist's replies — where the columnist is a fully committed extended-mind theorist, the sort who thinks Otto's notebook beliefs are as real as Inga's. Give her a name and a one-line bio, call the column whatever you like, and let it run about 750 words across three letters.

The letters should be:
1. someone whose ex-partner is threatening to delete the shared notes app and family calendar they built over nine years;
2. a student asking whether handing his phone in at the exam door counts as an accommodation he's owed or as cheating he should be punished for;
3. a commuter who's worried that the same billboard she drives past every morning is slowly becoming part of her.

Set it in a world where a court has already upheld a claim for wrongful de-scaffolding after a company wiped a shared drive, and where universities make students declare their "cognitive peripherals" on the exam cover sheet. Treat both as boring settled background, mentioned in passing, never explained.

The jokes have to come from her getting the debate right and following it all the way down. So she can never retreat behind the skull — no "but the real memory is still in there," no "of course you'd still be you without it." When she needs a line between a notes app that's part of a mind and a billboard that isn't, she has to draw it out loud, using the actual criteria (constant availability, automatic endorsement, trust built up over time), and then get cornered by her own line by the third letter. I want coupling-versus-constitution and the cognitive bloat worry to arrive as pressure from the letters and from her own advice, not as her lecturing the reader. Keep it deadpan: practical, warm, slightly clipped advice-column register, and dont let her wink once.

Target the thesis and the confidence of its deployment, not any named living person — the columnist and all three letter-writers are invented, and you can allude to the notebook case without making anyone a caricature.

Then at the end, drop out of the fiction for maybe five sentences and tell me where the piece actually lands. No both-sides mush: commit to whether the column is satirizing externalism or satirizing its critics or something third, say which objection you think genuinely does for this columnist and which ones she could answer if she were cleverer, and flag anywhere you bent the literature to make a joke land.
