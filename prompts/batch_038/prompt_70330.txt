I'm preparing a short talk for a workshop on machine agency, and the thesis I had settled on is that sphexishness is the wrong test for whether an artifact has anything worth calling autonomy. The wasp that Hofstadter and Dennett made famous is not a counterexample to freedom but a limit case on a spectrum of behavioral sensitivity, and a model that keeps re-issuing a tool call that has already failed three times is sphexish in the same sense, only to a lesser degree, that I am when I keep pushing a door marked "pull." I know the entomology is contested — Fred Keijzer has argued that the anecdote does not survive the experimental record and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest may be adaptive anyway — but that is beside my point, so please grant that the wasp is exactly as rigid as the story says. What matters is that a colleague helped me sharpen my claim into a numbered argument, and the argument now appears to prove considerably more than I wanted. It runs as follows.

(1) A system is flexible, in the sense the wasp lacks, only if what it does varies with features of its situation that bear on whether what it is doing is worth doing: the displacement of the prey bears on the worth of the drag-and-reinspect routine, and the wasp cannot use that fact.
(2) Every actual system — wasp, person, trained model — is at every moment exhaustively described by a response function from total states of system and environment to behavior.
(3) So which features bear on the worth of what the system is doing is itself fixed by that function; the function supplies its own criterion of relevance, and nothing else could supply one.
(4) But a system that receives its criterion of relevance from something other than itself and cannot revise it is running a routine whose trigger conditions were assigned to it — the wasp's predicament exactly.
(5) Hence every system is sphexish relative to the level at which its function is fixed, and what looks like flexibility in others is only our ignorance of their function, while what looks like rigidity is only our knowledge of it.
(6) Since a system that cannot do otherwise than its function dictates is not free, the wasp draws no line between insect and person: nothing is flexible, and nothing is free.
(7) Therefore sphexishness is not merely the wrong test for machine autonomy but no test at all, being either universally satisfied or universally unsatisfied.

I want to keep (1), since without it "flexibility" has no content at all, and I cannot live with (6) or (7). I have already tried objecting that (2) is false of learning systems, whose response functions change with experience; my colleague answered that the change is itself governed by a fixed learning rule, and I cannot see how to reply without an infinite regress, which I would rather not court in front of an audience. I suspect some expression is doing double duty between (3) and (5), but I have been unable to isolate it, and it is also possible that the argument is simply valid and I have been wrong about what flexibility amounts to. So: which step fails, and is the defect an equivocation, a grain or scope shift, or a false premise? Explain why that step looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down. Then, for each alternative exit you considered and rejected — including simply denying (6) on compatibilist grounds — say what accepting it would have cost my argument instead. Open with a single sentence naming the failing step and the nature of the defect, and keep the whole diagnosis under 600 words; I want no citations or reading list, only the analysis.
