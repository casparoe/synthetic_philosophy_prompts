Last night a friend and I argued about Hume's Law until nearly three, neither of us budged an inch, and this morning I suspect we were not really disagreeing so much as describing two different things with the same words. I would like a careful explanation of the distinction I think we were circling without ever naming it.

Here is the shape of the exchange. He is a naturalist in metaethics. He cheerfully conceded that no valid deductive argument can have a conclusion containing moral vocabulary unless some premise does — but, he said, the concession costs him nothing, because that is not a discovery about morality. It is an instance of the general conservativeness of logic: introduce a fresh predicate into a language and you cannot thereby derive anything new in the old vocabulary. By the same route you could show that nothing containing 'is a hobbit' follows from hobbit-free premises. So the is-ought gap, on his telling, is pure bookkeeping, and anyone who thinks it threatens naturalism has mistaken a theorem about vocabularies for a theorem about the world. I replied that this evades the real issue rather than settling it, since what matters is not formal derivability but whether moral claims can be supported by non-moral evidence — and that once he is permitted to contribute a bridge premise, for instance the identification of wrongness with causing net suffering, the inference goes through and the conservativeness point has simply been stepped around. He answered that this relocates the problem instead of solving it: the bridge premise is now doing all the work, and whether it counts as moral or non-moral is a matter of stipulation, which if anything proves his bookkeeping charge.

Could you lay out the conceptual geography here? I want four things. First, the conservativeness-style argument stated properly, together with a precise account of what it does and does not establish — in particular, whether it is compatible with there being some substantive asymmetry between moral and ordinary descriptive vocabulary that fails for 'is a hobbit', and if so, what that asymmetry would have to be. Second, the distinction between a thesis about logical consequence and a thesis about evidential or explanatory support, and why moving between them is not just relabeling. Third, the classification problem: what makes a premise non-moral, whether any principled sorting survives the naturalist's bridge premise, and how things change if the bridge is a necessary but a posteriori identity, so that the argument is materially rather than formally valid. Fourth, an honest verdict on which of us was closer to right, and if a genuine issue is left standing, a formulation of it that has a determinate answer rather than dissolving on inspection.

No citations, please; I know where to look those up. What I need is the distinctions drawn sharply enough that I can see exactly where our two descriptions of the disagreement stopped matching.
