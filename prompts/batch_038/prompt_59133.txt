Question: Williams argues that all reasons for action are internal. Explain his argument. Then explain how McDowell's reply resists it, and assess whether McDowell succeeds.

Student's answer:

"Williams's internalism says that for an agent A to have a reason to φ, φ-ing must connect to A's subjective motivational set, S — the collection of desires, projects, loyalties, and dispositions to evaluate that A actually has. An internal reason statement, 'A has a reason to φ,' is true when some element in S would be served or furthered by A's φ-ing, where the connection may run through what Williams calls a sound deliberative route: correcting A's false beliefs, imagining possibilities A had not considered, weighing alternatives, and taking advice from other people. An external reason statement, by contrast, says that A has a reason to φ even when nothing in S could be brought to bear on φ-ing by any amount of sound deliberation. Williams argues that such statements are false, and that the external reasons theorist can never say what would make one true: with internal reasons the explanation is available, since the reason explains how A could come to be moved, while with external reasons there is nothing left to explain them by.

This is where Williams's internalism is most often confused, so it is worth being precise. Williams is not claiming that A must feel moved right now. His thesis is about moral judgment: if A sincerely judges that she ought to φ, then that judgment itself carries motivation with it, and someone who denies this is describing an incoherent psychology. The external reasons theorist, on this picture, is really someone who thinks you can make a sincere moral judgment and remain utterly unmoved by it.

McDowell accepts Williams's framework but attacks the limits Williams places on deliberation. On McDowell's view, a sound deliberative route may itself generate motivations the agent did not begin with, because coming to see clearly what a situation requires can move a rational agent; so a reason can genuinely be A's even though nothing in A's actual S pointed toward it. But since Williams's own machinery, generously extended, yields McDowell's conclusion, the distinction between internal and external reasons collapses: given enough deliberation, any reason anyone attributes to A becomes an internal one. McDowell therefore succeeds, though only by showing that Williams's internalism was never really about motivation at all."
