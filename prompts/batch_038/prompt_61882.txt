I spent thirty-five years running a mass spec lab — isotopes, mostly groundwater and some paleoclimate work — and since retiring I volunteer two days a week at a local dig and in the finds shed, plus I've finally got round to reading philosophy, which I never had time for. Something's been nagging me about the archaeology side and I want to know whether there's a principle under it or whether it's just my gut.

My starting rule, which I'd like you to test rather than accept: an object licenses a claim about what people *thought* only when there is no purely practical story that would have produced that same object in that same place. I sort the inferences I meet in site reports into three heaps — things explained by the job the object does, things explained by the tradition the maker was trained in (everybody round here threw rims that way for two centuries), and things where neither covers it and you seem to need a thought in somebody's head. Only heap three counts, on my rule. I'm not convinced the rule survives contact with my own verdicts, which is why I'm asking.

Here they are.

1. A fired clay figurine, seated woman, crushed into the floor debris of a house right beside cooking pots and a quern. I won't let anyone call it a mother goddess or a fertility cult or say the household worshipped anything. Most I'll allow: somebody in that house owned it and bothered to keep it.

2. Same figurine type, but forty-odd of them deliberately stacked in a pit with no domestic refuse in it, beside a burned heap of juvenile sheep bones. Here I do say there was a shared repeated practice of depositing these things, and I'll even say the depositors probably thought the depositing accomplished something. I know that's a bigger jump than 1 and I'm comfortable with it anyway.

3. Cubical stone weights from six sites in one valley, same century, all in clean integer ratios to each other. I say that's a convention, and a convention implies somebody could settle it and make it stick — an institution, maybe not a state.

4. Pottery from the same six sites, same century, rim diameters clustered within a couple of millimetres, near identical fabric. I will not call that a standard or evidence of authority — I say it's a few potters copying each other. People tell me 3 and 4 are inconsistent, uniformity is uniformity, and I can't say why they're wrong.

5. A four-year-old buried with more and finer goods than any adult in the same cemetery. I say ascribed status, born into a rank. Solid. But an adult in that same cemetery with a handful of shells from 300 km away I won't call a trader or a chief or an envoy — could be a gift, could be inherited, could be the one person who actually made the trip. Both cases are goods you didn't make yourself and they feel completely different to me.

6. No walls, no weapons, no perimortem trauma over 500 years at a well dug site with good bone preservation: I refuse to call it a peaceful society. No tablets, no inscriptions, nothing: I also refuse to say they kept no records, because you can write on bark and wax and wood and it rots. Consistent so far. But a 200-year assemblage in sealed contexts, well preserved, with not one piece of non-local material — no obsidian, no marine shell, no metal — I'm happy to say that community wasn't plugged into exchange. That's also an argument from absence and I think it goes through. I can't articulate the difference between that one and the peace one except that I feel it.

7. A rayed figure painted on 200-odd vessels across three generations. I'll say the motif meant something to the people who commissioned and used it — nobody reproduces random squiggles for a century. I won't say there was a sun god or a sky deity or a myth. Same pile of pots, so I want my principle to tell me why the weak claim is safe and the strong one isn't.

The one I flip flop on: bricks in the same length:width:height ratio across a whole region for centuries. Some days that's coordinated labour, hence somebody giving orders; other days that ratio is just what falls out of holding a brick in one hand and needs no ruler at all.

So — is there a principle, mine repaired or something better, that gets all or most of these? I want it stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forced it in, not just a mood. Then tell me what it says about the case I keep going back and forth on, and about something I haven't listed at all — say a prone burial with the limbs bound and a stone slab on the chest, which everybody round here wants to read as fear of the dead. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it cannot keep: I'd much rather lose one verdict than end up with a rule that has nine seperate knobs on it. If my three heaps is just a bad way of carving this up, say so and give me a better carving.
