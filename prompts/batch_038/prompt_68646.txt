I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Szasz and we finished exactly where we began, which makes me suspect the disagreement is not the one either of us thinks it is. Her view is roughly Wakefield's: a mental disorder is real when some internally caused dysfunction — the failure of a mechanism that evolution shaped to do something — combines with a judgment that the resulting condition harms the person, and the naturalist work is done by the dysfunction clause, not the harm clause; Boorse, she adds, shows that disease can be defined without values, so Szasz only gets his result by assuming a disease must have a demonstrable bodily lesion, which nobody has believed for a century. Mine is closer to Szasz's: the diagnostic categories do not carve etiological kinds, they were built to manage conduct, and the medical framing quietly settles questions of excuse and authority that no factual claim about mechanism can settle. Notably, we agreed on nearly everything first-order — that people suffer, that some conditions respond to medication, that involuntary commitment is a grave imposition — and the arguments still never touched. I kept producing reasons about what diagnosis does; she kept producing reasons about what causes conditions.

Diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate it. Is this verbal, a dispute over which of two concepts of disease the word carries, and does anything substantive remain once the senses are separated? Is a hidden empirical question doing the work — whether psychiatric categories track etiologies, or whether dysfunction is identifiable without a prior about harm? Are we answering a metaphysical and a political question under one heading? Or does the standoff follow from a premise neither of us should grant, such as that a single verdict is owed to a single concept? Then say what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal or empirical result, a change of question — or a reasoned case that neither of us should expect to move the other, and why.
