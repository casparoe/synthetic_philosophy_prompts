Keynes's 1930 essay on economic possibilities for our grandchildren assumes that once scarcity is solved the danger will be leisure itself: he separates absolute needs, which are satiable, from relative needs, which rise with the general standard of living and never stop, and his fifteen-hour week leaves us either competing with our neighbours or facing a collapse of ordinary people who have no traditional purpose to fall back on. Forster's The Machine Stops, written two decades earlier, grants the abundance and produces inhabitants who do neither — Vashti and her world are past wanting altogether, exchanging second-hand ideas through screens, worshipping the apparatus that supplies them, with bodies too atrophied to walk. Read the story against the essay. Does Forster name a failure of the good life that Keynes's two-category taxonomy of needs has no slot for, since his people have not redirected desire but extinguished it, or is their condition Keynes's relative-needs problem wearing an ascetic costume? And does the story's argument depend on the Machine actually stopping, or do the opening sections already make the case before anything collapses? I want the economics treated seriously rather than as a foil, and I want you to say plainly where Forster's real target — Wellsian utopianism, what he calls the sin against the body — pulls the story away from the Keynesian frame rather than into it.
