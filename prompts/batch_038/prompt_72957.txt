Our department's upper-level course in social choice theory and its philosophical foundations examines in three weeks, and I am responsible for the section on Arrow's theorem. The students have seen the formal setting, the four conditions, and the decisive-coalition proof in full; they can recite the statement of the theorem flawlessly, which is precisely the problem I want the question to defeat.

Please write one essay-and-calculation question, answerable in 75 minutes and worth 25 marks, whose crux is that independence of irrelevant alternatives occupies a double role: it is the condition that does the load-bearing work in the proof, and it is also the condition whose rejection looks most philosophically defensible. I want students forced to hold both halves of that at once.

Deliver three things.

**1. The question.** It must supply a profile of your own design — four alternatives, nine voters in three blocs — on which candidates compute the Borda ordering, then delete one alternative and recompute, showing that the social ordering over the survivors has changed. Keep the arithmetic light and arrange the numbers so the shift is decisive rather than a tie-break artifact. The question must also embed a short passage, attributed to a candidate from a previous year, advancing three claims: that IIA is implausible because it prevents a social ordering from reflecting how strongly or how marginally people prefer one alternative to another; that since Borda is a perfectly reasonable rule and violates IIA, the theorem merely exhibits the inconsistency of a tendentiously chosen axiom set; and that the practical question of which rule to adopt is therefore left untouched. Candidates are to be asked what is right and what is wrong in each claim, and what survives the objection.

**2. A model answer** of roughly 900 words, including the completed tables. It must explain that ordinal profiles carry no information about intensity in the first place, so what Borda exploits is not intensity but the global configuration of the rankings — and that this configuration is exactly what renders the rule sensitive to the entry of similar alternatives, an institutional cost rather than a technicality. It must explain why IIA is what licenses the profile constructions that drive the decisive-set argument, so that the theorem is not the discovery of an inconsistent grab-bag. And it must distinguish escaping the theorem from escaping the underlying problem, taking account of both the enriched-information route (cardinal and interpersonally comparable utilities) and the restricted-domain route (single-peaked preferences), without claiming that either makes the normative difficulty go away.

**3. A marking scheme out of 25.** Allocate marks to named must-hit points and state, for each, the error that typically costs them. Then state explicitly where the boundary between a first-class and a good-but-not-excellent answer falls — I expect it to lie in whether the candidate treats the objection to IIA as settling the matter or as relocating it. Include deductions for the mistakes I actually see: conflating Arrow's theorem with Gibbard–Satterthwaite; reporting that the theorem shows ranked-choice voting is impossible or unfair; treating clone-dependence and strategic misrepresentation as the same phenomenon; asserting that the theorem presupposes interpersonal utility comparisons; and invoking single-peakedness as a solution without noting that it restricts the domain the theorem was about.

No bibliography, reading list, or citations, and marks must not depend on naming anyone: assess the reasoning.
