Two people in my epistemology reading group got into a fight last week and I still don't know who won. The setup they were arguing over: your employer offers you a $50k bonus if, by the end of the quarter, you genuinely believe the new strategy is going to succeed. Dana said that's a perfectly good reason to believe the strategy will succeed — it counts in favor of believing, which is just what a reason is. Marcus said no: the bonus is a reason to *want* to believe, or a reason to get yourself into a position where you'll end up believing, but it can't be a reason for the belief itself, because belief is the kind of state that can only be moved by considerations bearing on whether the thing is true. Dana's reply was that this is stipulation — you build "truth only" into the definition of belief and then announce that the bonus got excluded, which assumes exactly what was up for argument. Marcus said it isn't stipulation, it's constitutive, and the giveaway is that you literally cannot believe something on command for money: try it, and you find you can only fake it or go collect evidence, which is precisely the difference between the two kinds of reason he started with. Dana said thats an empirical claim and it's false — people pull it off all the time through self deception and evidence shopping and repeating it to yourself until it sticks; and if you can do it, the can't argument collapses and the bonus is bearing directly on the belief. Marcus's last move was that even granting that, all she's shown is that you can cause yourself to have the belief, which is the state-given business he was happy to concede from the start, not that you can believe *because of* the bonus.

Where does each move land — which ones are good and which are smuggling something in? And is there something both of them are treating as obvious that they shouldn't be?
