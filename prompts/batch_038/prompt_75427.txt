Been going in circles on Sen's liberal paradox for a couple weeks now (the Lewd and Prude thing) and I've ended up with a list of the ways people say you can get out of it. What I want to know is which of them actually work. The list, as I've got it:

1. Say Sen's liberalism condition just isn't a rights condition. Rights attach to how choices get made, not to which social states end up ranked on top, so there's nothing there to trade off against Pareto in the first place. Nozick's line, roughly.
2. Same complaint, different formalization: describe rights as game forms, i.e. as the strategy sets a person controls, rather than as protected pairs inside a social ordering, and the cycle never shows up.
3. Let people waive. If the right-holder can contract their protected pair away, the Pareto-better state becomes reachable without anyone's right being violated. Gibbard.
4. Restrict the domain. The paradox needs "nosy" preferences, so rule out profiles where somebody has preferences over features of the state that sit inside someone else's protected sphere. Blau.
5. Stop requiring the social ranking to be transitive. Use a choice function, or ask only for acyclicity, and a cycle isn't a contradiction anymore.
6. Drop Pareto. Treat unanimous preference as giving no reason whatsoever for the social ranking.

Plus a seventh that isn't from the literature. A friend of mine who does mechanism design says all six are missing the obvious move: both conditions are being treated as hard constraints when they clearly admit of degree. So build a rule that's *allowed* to violate them and have it minimize a weighted sum of rights-violations and Pareto-violations, weights set by how much you care about liberty versus efficiency. Then there's no impossibility, just a tradeoff curve, and the political question becomes where on it you want to sit.

Sort all seven into three tiers by how well they hold up as an actual resolution — and by "hold up" I mean: does it diagnose what's really going on, does it cost less than it saves, and would a social choice person accept it as an answer rather then as a restatement of the question. One or two sentences each on why it lands where it does. If two of them turn out to be the same move in different clothes, say so and collapse them; I'd rather have an honest list of five than a padded list of seven.

Then two specific things. Where exactly does #7 go wrong? My friend and I keep arguing about whether it's just #6 with extra steps or whether it fails for a genuinely different reason, and I want the version of that argument that would settle it. And separately, give me a single number between 0 and 1 for the fraction of the seven that you think are real resolutions rather than relabelings — a number, not a range, and tell me what would move it.
