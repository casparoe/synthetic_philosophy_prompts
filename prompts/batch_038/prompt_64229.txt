I'm on the editorial review board for a big intro-biology textbook, and Thursday we vote on a bundle of flaged passages that turn out to be the same problem wearing different clothes: the manuscript uses "function" in two different senses and nobody caught it until an outside reviewer blew up.

Three of the flagged items:

- The digestive chapter calls the appendix vestigial, then a sidebar two pages later says its function is to shelter good bacteria so the colon can be repopulated after a bad bout of diarrhea. The reviewer says we can't have it both ways inside one chapter.
- Chapter 12 leans on the panda's "thumb" — an enlarged wrist bone, a jury-rigged bit of anatomy — and the sentence reads "the function of the panda's thumb is to strip leaves off bamboo," which is a job the bone does now but not obviously what it was originally shaped for.
- The genetics chapter says that in cystic fibrosis the CFTR protein's function is "disrupted." Read literally that's odd, because the mutant protein simply doesn't do the thing, so it isn't failing at anything it's currently doing.

Our one board member who actually reads philosophy of biology says there's a whole literature here, and that "function" can mean either what selection shaped a trait to do or whatever it currently causally contributes to, and that the two come apart precisely in these cases. That hasn't settled anything. Half the board wants to standardize on the historical sense everywhere and rewrite to match; one member wants to ban "the function of X is to Y" outright as design talk in disguise and substitute "X contributes to Y"; and the physiology authors say strip function language out of thier chapters and the text stops being readable for a nineteen-year-old.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the vote. It has to land somewhere a copyeditor could apply it without asking me follow-up questions: a rule for telling which sense a given sentence is using and what to do about it, a short glossary entry we could print as-is, and your verdict on each of the three flagged items — what should the sentence say instead? Second-pass edits go to the printer in about six weeks, so nothing that amounts to a rewrite of the book. Be straight about which of the two senses your rule privileges, and what a student loses if we privilege it. And give the ban-it member a real hearing rather than a strawman: the strongest version of the worry is that function talk, even the historical kind, trains students to read purposes into nature, so say why the policy you're recommending survives that, or mark where it doesn't.
