I'm a second-year graduate student in a political philosophy reading group that has spent a term going back and forth between the anti-commodification camp and the market-liberal camp, and our discussions keep stalling for the same reason: everyone reaches for the canonical cases — kidneys, votes, surrogacy, sex work, paid blood donation, queue positions, prison upgrades — and in every one of those cases the various objections pile on at once. There is exploitation to point at, there are background inequalities to invoke, there are third parties whose reliance gets damaged, and there is something expressive that seems degraded, so no one can tell which feature is actually carrying the weight of the verdict. I want to break the deadlock with a case built to order rather than inherited, and I'd like you to build it.

Here are the specifications. I want a realistic, present-day market in a good or service that is not one of the canonical examples above (and please do not smuggle in a near variant of one: no paid organs under another name, no bought civic duties, no rented wombs). The case must satisfy all four of the following. First, the item must be one that can simply be given away, and the gift version of the same transfer must be uncontroversially permissible — even admirable — so that money is the only variable that changes between the two versions. Second, none of the features Debra Satz lists as making a market noxious may be present: as I have them in my notes, that means no party is extremely vulnerable, the parties' bargaining positions are roughly equal, participating does not degrade the participants' capacities to form and pursue their own ends, and the market neither produces nor entrenches inequality of a kind that damages democratic citizenship; add to that no harm to non-participants and no deception of anyone relying on the transaction. Third, the liberal permissibility-transfer argument — the Brennan and Jaworski line that if you may do something or give something away, then adding a price cannot introduce a new wrong absent some independent wrong — must have no easy escape hatch: there is no fraud, no breach of a duty owed to a third party, no epistemic signal being corrupted for an audience, and what is sold must genuinely be the thing that is given away in the gift version, not merely the seller's labor time. Fourth, and this is the point of the exercise, an Anderson- or Sandel-style expressive theorist must nonetheless be able to condemn the market purely on expressive grounds: buying and selling this good manifests an attitude toward it that is incompatible with the attitude the good properly calls for, and that condemnation must not secretly depend on harm, inequality, degraded consent, or bad effects on the participants. My own hunch is that the promising territory is goods whose point is to express an attitude rather than to bring about an outcome, but if you can meet all four specifications with something structurally different, so much the better.

For the deliverable: open with one sentence naming the good and the single feature of the case that is doing the explanatory work. Then give the scenario itself in one paragraph of at most 150 words — concrete, with an actual price and an actual institutional setting, the kind of thing that could appear in a newspaper's business section tomorrow. Then a four-point check, one or two sentences per point, mapping specifications one through four onto named features of your case and showing where each verdict comes from rather than asserting it. Then a short collapse test: delete the payment and leave the gift identical in every other respect — does the objection survive, and if it does, say so plainly, because then you have failed to isolate monetization. Close with one paragraph on how a market liberal should attack the case — including the objection that your expressive theorist is reporting a preference rather than a reason — and what would distinguish a genuine expressive wrong from a residue of distaste. Please keep the entire response under 600 words. If you judge the four specifications to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly, identify which one has to go, and then give me the closest achievable case; do not quietly weaken a specification without flagging it.
