I'm applying for a one-year dissertation fellowship (humanities, no lab, just me and a library card) and I need about 1500 words of proposal. My half-formed idea is about Simone Weil's essay arguing for the abolition of political parties. Basically everyone treats it as a cranky maximalist tract — parties manufacture collective passion, so get rid of them, end of story — but I think she's actually giving two different arguments and nobody keeps them apart, and that the difference matters.

Argument one is epistemic: a party's function is to produce and sustain a collective passion, and passion of that kind is structurally hostile to the sort of patient, disinterested attention she thinks you need to get anywhere near truth or justice. Argument two is volitional: the party makes itself the end, it aims at its own unlimited growth, so the member outsources her judgement and lying stops costing anything because the person speaking isn't really her, it's the party through her. My hunch is that the second is downstream of the first — that growth-seeking is only a problem because growth is achieved by passion-manufacturing — and that if that's right you get a strange result: her essay isn't primarily an argument for abolition, it's an argument about design. A party could in principle be built with features that suppress the generation of collective passion (no whip, no party line on particular questions, members speaking and voting as individuals, the option of the party dissolving itself kept alive), and then the critique would have nothing to bite on. Unless the growth criterion does independent work, in which case no design fix survives, because a party that doesn't seek to grow is outcompeted and dies.
