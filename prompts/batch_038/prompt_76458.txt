Process engineer here, I read philosophy of science on the commute, and for the last year or so I've been keeping a private ledger of thought experiments with a verdict next to each one: did it actually tell me something about the world, or did it just reshuffle words I already had? I want to know what principle is behind my verdicts, because my current rule of thumb clearly isn't it.

Here's the ledger.

- Galileo tying the fast stone to the slow stone: if the heavy one falls faster, the pair should fall slower than the heavy one alone because the light one drags on it, but also faster because the pair is heavier. Contradiction, so they fall together. I genuinely came away believing something I hadn't before, and I'd have bet on Aristotle beforehand. YES.
- Einstein at sixteen chasing a light beam and finding that a frozen light wave is nonsense. YES.
- The man in the falling elevator who can't distinguish falling from floating. YES, strongest entry on the list.
- The train and the two lightning strikes. YES — I don't think I could have gotten to relative simultaneity any other way.
- Newton's bucket, with the water climbing the walls once it's spinning relative to nothing in the room. YES, but uneasy; I can't say what it established exactly, only that rotation doesn't behave like translation.
- Stevin's loop of spheres draped over the back-to-back slopes: it can't turn forever, so the pull on each side has to be proportional to the slope. YES, and note this one is pure "perpetual motion is impossible."
- Carnot's ideal engine, where you suppose a better engine exists, run the pair backwards, and get work for nothing. YES, and again the whole weight is on the impossibility of perpetual motion.
- Feynman's little ratchet and pawl, the toy that's supposed to rectify thermal jiggling into useful turning. YES, but interestingly it works by telling me the picture fails rather than that it succeeds.
- Pushing a perfectly rigid rod a light-year long to beat light to the punch. NO. I used to think this worked. It gives a false conclusion, and I know why: no such rod exists, the shove travels at the speed of sound in the material.
- Schrödinger's cat. NO. I come away unsettled and no better informed; I don't hold a view about measurement that I didn't hold before reading it.
- Frictionless plane, spherical cow, infinitely long wire: these do work for me in my actual job every day, but I wouldn't call them knowledge-yielding, they just make the arithmetic tractable. MARGINAL.

The pair that breaks my rule is Carnot and Stevin against the rigid rod. My rule of thumb has been "trust it if the thing described could in principle be built," and a long rod in a lab is far easier to build than a reversible engine cycling between two reservoirs with no losses at all, yet the rod lies to me and Carnot doesn't. Both Carnot and Stevin also pull the same move — imagine a machine, then notice you'd bet against it running forever — so I've been half-tempted to say that's the real principle, that the good ones are all disguised betting arbitrages. But that obviously can't cover the falling elevator or the lightning strikes, where there's no machine and nothing to bet against.

So: what principle, or small set of candidate principles, actually fits this ledger? State each one precisely, say which of my cases motivated each clause, and tell me what it predicts about thought experiments I haven't listed — including at least one you'd expect me to have listed and didn't. And be honest about which verdicts it can't save. If the cost of keeping the Carnot/Stevin/rod trio is dropping my YES on Newton's bucket or on Galileo's stones, say so and argue for it rather than quietly accommodating everything. Finally, if the betting-arbitrage idea is worth anything, I want to know what it's really tracking, because I cant shake the feeling that its a correct observation attached to the wrong explanation.
