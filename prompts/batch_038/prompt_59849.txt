write a dialogue between two friends arguing about aesthetic testimony. One of them hasn't read the novel in question but is perfectly happy to assert flat-out that it's great, because a critic whose verdicts she's tracked for ten years raves about it. The other thinks that's a borrowed performance: testimony can get you the belief that the book is admired, maybe even that it's probably good, but it can't get you to actually find it good, so saying "it's great" in your own voice is going through the motions of a judgment you don't have.

Keep both sides genuinely sharp, no strawmen. The deferring friend needs a real answer to two things: why aesthetic trust should differ from trusting your doctor or a historian about stuff you'll never check yourself, and the bootstrapping worry that you could only spot a reliable critic by exercising aesthetic judgment you'd have to get first-hand anyway. The acquaintance-side friend shouldn't be allowed to hide behind "you just have to feel it" — make her say what exactly the testimony fails to deliver, and why it fails to deliver it.

Work in one concrete test case they both chew on: someone deaf from birth who has read every score, analysis, and review of a certain symphony. What, if anything, is he missing that a hearing listener has? Around 800-1000 words, actual back-and-forth where each responds to what the other just said rather than taking turns making speeches, and nobody gets converted at the end.
