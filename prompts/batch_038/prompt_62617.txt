I follow a good deal of commentary on markets that people find objectionable — payment for kidneys, commercial surrogacy, surge pricing on water and generators after a hurricane — and I have noticed the same reply being made to the objection that such trades are repugnant, but I do not know whether that reply has a name.

The reply runs roughly as follows. Someone says the trade degrades what is traded, or that certain things should not be for sale at any price. The defender answers that this is a visceral reaction of a familiar kind, and notes that reactions of this sort once attached to practices now entirely unremarkable: life insurance as speculation on human life, paid blood donation, usury, the purchase of military commissions. Then the defender adds an empirical point. The feeling fades: once the market has operated for a decade or two, people stop finding it repugnant, and younger cohorts report no discomfort at all. The fading is then offered as confirmation that the original reaction was a prejudice rather than a perception of something wrong.

What troubles me is that the fading is not independent of the market's success. The market's spread is itself what habituates people, resets the expectations the trade originally violated, and changes what the good means to those exchanging it. So the disappearance of the reaction may be produced by the very arrangement the reaction was a protest against. The style of reasoning that would license saying a population's settled approval of an arrangement tells us nothing about the arrangement's merits, because the arrangement shaped the approval, is simply not turned on the settled absence of disapproval.

Is there an established name or literature for this structure? I am aware of the general idea that preferences are formed by the circumstances they are then cited to justify, and of the charge of dismissing a belief merely because of its origin, but neither seems to fit a case where a causal explanation of a reaction is used to vindicate a practice on the grounds that the reaction has died out. If there is no term, coin one and define it, and say how it differs from those two neighbors. Then tell me where its edges are: which uses of the habituation point are legitimate, and how the structure compares to the mirror-image conservative claim that a durable repugnance is itself evidence of a moral truth.
