I have no academic training in philosophy. I left school at sixteen and have spent thirty-one years driving a diesel van for a small haulage firm in the Midlands. For the last few years, though, I have been reading and thinking about collective harm on my own account, largely because of a standing argument with my daughter: she does not fly, does not eat meat, and recently turned down a better-paid job because it would have meant a longer car commute. I have never given up the van. I built the argument below to explain why I did not think I was doing anything worse than she was doing, and to my alarm it now appears to prove considerably more than I intended. I should be grateful if you would tell me where it goes wrong, if anywhere, and what has to be paid to put it right at that point.

1. An act of mine can be wrong on account of what it brings about only if it makes some identifiable person's situation worse: there must be someone who ends up worse off, in the world as it will actually be, than they would have been had I not done it.

2. My van's exhaust cannot make anyone's situation worse in that sense. The climate answers to cumulative emissions accumulated over decades, and the share attributable to one van over one year is smaller than any unit in which a change to a person's life could be registered. No particular child gets asthma because of me; no particular harvest fails because of me; no particular house floods because of me. Had I parked the van for good, the same people would have had the same lives.

3. So my driving is not wrong on account of what it brings about.

4. Nor can it be wrong on any other account. The only remaining candidates concern my attitudes — that I am indifferent, that I take a free ride, that I profit from a system I disapprove of. But suppose my neighbour shares my opinions about the climate exactly and simply has no van: we should not say he does anything wrong. An attitude is not a wrong unless it issues in an act that makes a difference, and mine makes none.

5. Nothing in (1) to (4) mentions vans, or the climate, or anything else peculiar to my situation. The reasoning holds wherever a harm is produced by a great many contributions and no single contribution is separately detectable. So: if a hundred marksmen fire at one man and each bullet would have killed him by itself, then no marksman leaves him worse off than he would otherwise have been, and no marksman does wrong; and if a thousand households each draw one additional bucket a day from a shared reservoir until the reservoir runs dry, no household has left anyone worse off either.

6. The same reasoning runs backwards, and this is the part that troubles me most. If my driving makes no difference, then my not driving makes no difference either. My daughter's refusal to fly has left nobody better off. She has done nothing wrong, but she has also, morally speaking, done nothing: the costs she has borne have purchased nothing. She is not better than I am. She has merely been more expensive.

7. Taking (3), (4), (5) and (6) together: no act is ever wrong on account of what it brings about, no act is ever wrong on any other account, and no abstention is ever to anyone's credit. Wrongness has simply drained out of the whole region of conduct by which crowds of people affect one another.

I cannot accept (7). I am confident that the hundredth marksman does wrong, and I am confident that my daughter's refusal to fly is not on a level with my van, though I cannot state why in a form that survives the argument. I would far rather keep (1) than (7), and rather keep my judgement about the marksman than my judgement about my daughter; I am not attached to (4) at all, and would willingly lose it if that dissolved the difficulty.

So: which step ought I to give up? Please commit to one, even if you think several are defective, and tell me what abandoning it costs. If your view is instead that the argument is sound in its logic and the only question is which premise is cheapest to surrender, say so plainly and rank the premises by cost. In either case I want to know what the standard lines of response are here and what each of them is committed to — but please give me no reading list, no bibliography and no names to chase. I have no library card and no journal access, and in any case I want the reasoning rather than the labels.

Three things I should especially like addressed. First, whether (2) is even true: my daughter insists that what matters is not whether any particular person ends up worse off but whether I have shifted the odds for a great many people, and I cannot tell whether that reply repairs (1) or demolishes it. Second, whether the two illustrations in (5) really belong to the same species as the case in (2), or whether I have slid from one sense of "makes no difference" into another without noticing; I suspect I may be using the phrase loosely, but I cannot pin down where. Third, what is left of (1) if the marksman's shot is to be wrong — some account on which contributing to a set of acts that is jointly sufficient to harm can itself be a harming, or a rejection of the whole counterfactual way of thinking about harm, or something else I have not imagined.

Please write your answer in British English.
