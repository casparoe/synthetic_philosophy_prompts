**Essay for comment (opening section of a 4,000-word workshop paper)**

Three Joints, Not One Wound: Re-Reading the Athenian Triptych

*Draft v3 — I am a political theory graduate student working from translations, with no Greek and no ancient-history training. This is the argumentative core of the paper; the rest is apparatus.*

Athenian democracy is conventionally indicted on three counts: Mytilene in 427, the Sicilian Expedition of 415–413, and the trial of Socrates in 399. The three are grouped together because each appears to show the same thing — a mass of citizens deciding something grave, badly, under the pressure of feeling, with nothing between the feeling and the decision. On that reading the lesson is architectural: direct democracy lacks the buffers (a second chamber, a court of constitutional review, a cooling-off period) that keep appetite from becoming policy. The three episodes function as three samples from one distribution.

My claim is that this grouping is the source of the indictment's rhetorical force and also the source of its emptiness, because the three episodes fail at three different joints in the same machine.

**Mytilene: no device for suspension.** After the revolt of Lesbos, the Assembly in a fury of the moment voted to put to death the entire adult male population of Mytilene and enslave the women and children, and dispatched a ship with the decree. The next day it repented, and the ambassadors and their Athenian supporters persuaded the authorities to put the question again. In the second debate Cleon defended the decree and Diodotus attacked it; Diodotus won, and he won by explicitly refusing to argue from pity or from the injustice of punishing a whole city, resting his case instead on Athenian interest — the argument that spared Mytilene was an argument about what paid. A second trireme was sent to countermand the first and reached Lesbos before the executions began. Some thousand ringleaders were killed anyway, the walls were razed, and the land was handed over to Athenian settlers.

What matters here is not that Athens decided atrociously but that its undoing of the decision was accidental in kind. Thucydides explicitly draws the moral that the reversal demonstrated the Assembly's capacity to correct itself; I take that as his own judgment, not merely his report. There was no standing procedure by which a decree could be suspended between vote and execution, no requirement that a capital measure be re-debated at a remove of days, no officer whose job it was to slow it down. What saved Mytilene was the physical speed of a trireme and the willingness of its crew to row hard. Athens had the capacity for second thoughts and no machinery for them: a correction that was contingent, not designed.

**Sicily: advice contaminated by candidacy.** The expedition was voted in the spring of 415. Nicias, who opposed it, tried in his second speech to deter the Assembly by inflating the force required, and the Assembly responded by granting the generals full powers to take what they liked; the sabotage was read as ambition. Alcibiades, the expedition's most vigorous advocate, was recalled to Athens to answer charges of impiety before the fleet had left Piraeus, escaped, and went over to Sparta. What remained was commanded by men whose competence was uneven and whose judgment was compromised by the sheer scale of what they had been given. The fleet was destroyed at Syracuse; Nicias and Demosthenes were executed; the survivors were held in the stone quarries.

The joint that failed here is not timing and not, in the first instance, passion. It is the structure of advice. The citizens best placed to know what conquering Sicily would cost were the same men competing to command the conquest. Nicias's knowledge was deployed as a bargaining move; Alcibiades's confidence as campaign rhetoric; and no participant had an incentive to state plainly that Sicily could not pay for its own subjugation. Expertise was endogenous to ambition, and the Assembly had no channel of information that did not run through a candidate. That is a defect of epistemic design, not of emotional control.

**Socrates: an unbounded charge.** In 399 Socrates was prosecuted by Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon for failing to acknowledge the gods the city acknowledged, introducing new divinities, and corrupting the young. He was convicted by a modest majority of a jury of five hundred, proposed as his counter-penalty free maintenance at public expense, and was executed within days of the verdict.

This is the case usually offered as the paradigm of democratic hysteria, and it is the weakest instance of the three. A properly constituted popular court applied an established procedure and reached a verdict within its competence. Nothing in the machinery broke. What broke was the specification of the offense: impiety was nowhere defined, so any citizen could prosecute almost any intellectual or religious innovation under that heading, and the jury had no standard against which to test whether the charge described a crime at all. The escalation in the penalty phase is a second-order effect of the same defect — a defendant facing an undefined charge has no way to calibrate a proportionate counter-proposal. Athens here failed at legality, not at democracy, and the two are worth keeping apart.

**Payoff.** The standard indictment predicts all three episodes, which is why it is never tested. A mechanism-level account predicts differentially: Athens should correct decisions whose consequences become visible quickly enough (Mytilene), should err where the best-informed are also the candidates (Sicily), and should over-criminalize wherever an offense is left undefined (Socrates). The fourth-century settlement looks like an Athenian response to the same three joints rather than to a single wound: the distinction between laws and decrees and the transfer of lawmaking to boards of legislators, the action against illegal proposals — unattested in our sources before about 415 — taking over the disciplinary work that ostracism had done, and the reorganization of the courts after the restoration. If the Athenians had diagnosed one general pathology, they would have built one general buffer. They built three.
