I am four years into a novel whose central institution is the standing committee that maintains a national manual of psychiatric disorders — a body with the authority of a classification system and the temperament of a standards bureau — and the book requires a document I have twice failed to write myself. I should like an excerpt from the committee's internal handbook for incoming members, between 700 and 900 words, laid out as numbered sections beneath a bland heading, and containing at least one sidebar or worked example plus one blank form reproduced in full. Its subject is the scheduled retirement of terms. In the novel the excerpt falls to a junior member who has been asked to draft the retirement notice for a label she has lately come to suspect describes her, which is why the document must be sincere rather than sinister: nothing in it is intended to be cruel, and nobody on the committee is a fool. They are diligent, empirically minded, and in their way humane, and every rule they have written follows impeccably from a rule they wrote earlier.

Here is the machinery, which I want handled as plumbing rather than as paradox. Once a term is published, the people who fall under it begin to hear it, to describe themselves by it, and to arrange their conduct around it; the term accordingly alters its own extension, prevalence moves for reasons having nothing to do with whatever the criteria were meant to track, and the criteria require retuning. The handbook treats this as a routine operational hazard, of the same order as a misprint, and should be explicit that it applies only to certain categories — those whose objects can hear their own names — and not to others, giving at least one clear example of each kind (a lesion, a fungus, or something of that sort on the unresponsive side). It assigns every published term an estimated useful life, at the end of which the term is retired not for having become false but for having become familiar. It includes a worked example with prevalence figures from before and after publication, drawn from a wholly invented disorder, in which the criteria proved so learnable that subjects came to present precisely as specified. It describes the protective measures the committee has tried — numeric codes substituted for words, embargoed nomenclature, staff instructed not to use the terms socially, patient-facing literature withheld pending review — and records flatly that each measure was in due course published, and therefore ceased to work. Somewhere the committee's own members appear as a category subject to the same rules, and the closing section should note, without alarm, that a recently coined term for the sort of person who resists classification has itself been observed to spread.

On tone and constraint, three requests, and I hope the last one will not seem perverse. First: deadpan throughout, no winking, no arch asides. The target is the managerial fantasy that an institution can name people and remain untouched by the naming, and I would rather the piece leave the harder question open — whether any classification of persons could avoid changing what it classifies, or whether the committee's predicament is simply what happens when the objects of study can read — than settle it with a punchline. Second: invent every disorder name, because I will not have a line in my book that could read as a joke at someone's expense for living under a real diagnosis; the coinages should sound exactly like plausible committee work, with the right bland Latinate vagueness and the right committee-approved hedging. Third, and this is the perverse one: I have a friend in the philosophy of the human sciences who tells me the pattern my committee is managing has a name and a literature, and I have deliberately not looked it up, because I want the handbook written from inside the practice rather than from the theory. So please do not use the phrase looping effect, or making up people, or interactive kinds, or name any philosopher, or cite or allude to any literature. If you recognize the idea anyway, let the recognition show only as precision — every procedure should be exactly what a well-run committee committed to that idea would actually do, and every beat that lands for a specialist should also read, to everyone else, as entirely ordinary officialese.
