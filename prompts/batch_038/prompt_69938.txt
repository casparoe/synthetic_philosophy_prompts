I co-teach a graduate seminar on algorithmic accountability, and our opening session is always the one on the impossibility results for fairness criteria in risk scoring. The students tend to arrive treating those results as a piece of mathematical trivia — an unfortunate quirk of the definitions that a sufficiently thoughtful vendor or a sufficiently well-drafted regulation could surely engineer around — and the second half of the term is largely spent un-teaching that. This year I want to open the session by handing out a piece of satire instead of lecturing: something that shows, rather than asserts, how an entire compliance industry can be built out of the very escape hatches the theorems leave open.

The piece I want is a product launch page for a fictional fairness-compliance platform — call the vendor whatever you like, and invent the regulatory backdrop that created its market: a fictitious supranational instrument (an "Article 4(2)"-style concordat, a certification mark, a designated audit regime) that formally requires a deployed risk score to be simultaneously well-calibrated across protected groups, equal in false-positive rates, and equal in false-negative rates, with exemptions available only in the exceptional cases the theory identifies. That is the joke's engine, and it should drive the whole product line: build the vendor's tiers, its add-ons, and its "professional services" catalogue out of exactly the configurations in which joint satisfaction is possible, and out of the purely measurement-level moves that let a client report compliance without changing any decision anyone actually receives — threshold migration, re-segmenting the population until cells happen to match on base rate, reclassifying the reference group, retiring the score to an internal "advisory" status, and so on. Include the standard launch-page furniture: a hero headline and value proposition, a feature grid across pricing tiers (with the enterprise tier promising all three criteria at once, described in a way that is technically truthful and commercially hollow), a short FAQ in which a prospective client's entirely reasonable questions get non-answers, one glowing customer testimonial from an institution that got lucky, and a fine-print section. Somewhere in that fine print there should be a single sentence, buried in legalese, that concedes the theorem outright and completely — the only fully honest sentence in the document.

Constraints: roughly 800 words, deadpan throughout, and no winking — the vendor should never acknowledge that it is selling around a mathematical result, and the reader should be able to reconstruct the result entirely from the shape of the product. Get the substance right; the piece fails if a specialist in the field has to wince at a misstated condition or a wrong characterization of the exceptional cases, and succeeds if that specialist recognizes that the satire has merely taken the standard regulatory promise literally. No citations, no bibliography, no named real researchers, no real companies or real statutes, and nothing that would require looking anything up. Do not explain the theorem in an authorial voice anywhere, including in a closing note — if it needs explaining, the piece needs rewriting.
