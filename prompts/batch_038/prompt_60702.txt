I serve as the volunteer note-taker for the programs committee of a mid-sized community health nonprofit, and last Thursday's meeting ran ninety minutes over schedule because two members got into a sustained argument that nobody could resolve. I have since been asked — as the person who wrote it down, who is a part-time data analyst rather than a clinician or a philosopher, and who has no stake and no settled view of my own — to obtain an outside reading on the exchange before we take a vote. The practical question in front of us is mundane. We send SMS reminders to reduce missed appointments at our clinics; our no-show rate is about 28 percent; and our outreach lead proposed a set of new message variants to A/B test next quarter. Two of the four proposals are what the argument was actually about. The first is a targeted descriptive-norm message: "Most patients in [neighborhood] kept their appointments last month. We've held a slot for you — please confirm by tomorrow." The second is a personal-obligation message carrying a photograph and first name of the clinician the patient is booked with: "Maria has been looking forward to seeing you Thursday. If you can't make it, please tell her so she can offer the time to someone else." Both are true — the norm figure is real, and we do ask staff to write those lines — and the outreach lead reported that in a pilot elsewhere the second variant produced the larger gain.

Here is the exchange as I recorded it, in roughly the order it happened:

1. Dev, a family physician on the committee, said the second message is manipulative and should not be sent. "The first one tells people something true and lets them decide. The second recruits guilt and the fear of letting Maria down. Neither of those is a reason to go to the clinic — and supplying reasons is the whole of what persuasion is. So the second message isn't persuading anyone, it's pressing buttons, and the mark of button-pressing is that it works on people who would refuse it if they saw it coming."

2. Priya, a behavioral scientist who consults for us, said that test is too narrow to be usable. "Almost nothing in ordinary communication is a reason in that pure sense. Framing is unavoidable: I can tell a patient that nine in ten people who attend on time do better, or that one in ten do worse, and the second phrasing works because it frightens, and the fear does the work. Either you conclude that all communication is manipulation, or you accept that a message can run through emotion and still be legitimate. The question isn't mechanism — it's whether the person would accept the influence if she understood it. Publish the message, explain how it works and why we chose it, and if the patient says 'fine, send it,' it isn't manipulation."

3. Dev said that definition dissolves the problem rather than solving it. "'People like you in your neighborhood do this' is precisely the sort of message that survives being published — nobody objects to being told what her neighbors do — and yet it is the one doing the least honest work here. The figure is true only where we chose to look. We will send it in the ZIP codes where it is high and stay quiet in the ones where it is low. A statement whose truth depends on where you point the searchlight isn't informing anyone; it's using them. And on your test it passes, which is what's wrong with the test."

4. Priya granted the point but said it cut against Dev. "Notice what you just did: you conceded the mechanism and objected to the selection. That's a complaint about cherry-picking, not about manipulation — and it's a complaint you can fix by not cherry-picking. Meanwhile you still haven't given me a reason to drop the Maria message, which nobody objects to being told about and which delivers the bigger health gain to exactly the people who need it. Our no-shows skew heavily toward patients with the worst transport and the least schedule flexibility. Eleven points there is eleven points of care actually delivered. If guilt is what gets someone to a blood-pressure check that keeps her out of the emergency room, I would rather be effective and slightly embarrassed than spotless and useless."

5. Dev said he did not dispute the outcomes, only her entitlement to weigh them that way. "Our patients aren't customers. They come to us in a position of dependence, and we accumulate knowledge of them — names, clinicians, neighborhoods, and in a couple of years a non-adherence history — that no advertiser is allowed. The Maria message spends that knowledge. It works because she believes we are on her side. Each message is small, but the small ones are what teach people what we are."

6. Priya said this finally located the disagreement in the relationship rather than the technique, which she took for progress, but that it didn't get Dev where he wanted. "If the relationship is what makes the influence permissible, then disclose it inside the relationship: a line in the intake packet saying that our reminders are designed using behavioral science and include messages that appeal to how you feel about your clinician. Consent obtained, relationship intact, message still sent. And your 'spends trust' claim has a deeper problem — you have no mechanism. You're positing a finite account that a text message withdraws from, and you can't say what makes it finite or when it hits zero."

7. Dev said that the burden of arguing for disclosure sat with her, not him. "Telling someone you're about to use her guilt doesn't stop the guilt from working. That is supposed to be your own field's result — warning people about a bias doesn't immunize them against it. If disclosure is your safeguard and disclosure is inert, you don't have a safeguard, you have a formality. So I'm back where I started: certain techniques you simply don't use on people who trust you, whether or not you've told them, and recruiting a named person's disappointment is one of them."

8. Priya called that a draw — she agreed disclosure is no cure-all but denied it followed that the technique is impermissible — and Dev said he would accept "not impermissible, but requiring more justification than you have given" if she would say it out loud. She wouldn't. We moved to the next agenda item without a vote.

Please take this on in the role of a philosopher who works on the ethics of influence and behavioral public policy and who is willing to say which of two decent arguments is the better one. Begin by thinking carefully and laying out, before you score anything, the candidate criteria that were actually in play across those eight moves — bypassing or supplying reasons, transparency and hypothetical acceptance, the relationship and the knowledge it confers, aggregate outcomes — and say plainly which of them can carry the weight each of the two wanted it to carry. Then adjudicate move by move: for each numbered turn, tell us whether the point succeeds against the point it is answering, whether it introduces a distinction the other side should simply have conceded, and where it overreaches. After that, answer the two questions we actually need settled: is there a real difference between the two message variants, or was Dev's objection to both of them all along really about something else; and does the disclosure-plus-relationship move at 6 rescue the Maria message, or is Dev right at 7 that disclosure is inert here? Close by telling us what both of them missed — some consideration neither raised that should change how we vote. I do not need references, a bibliography, or a reading list; the argument should stand on its own reasoning. Because we are going to act on this, be explicit about how fragile your verdict is: identify the single consideration or counterargument most likely to flip your rating of the most consequential move, say which of your judgments you hold least confidently and why, and if you think one of them is right overall, say so plainly instead of splitting the difference between two people who were each half-right.
