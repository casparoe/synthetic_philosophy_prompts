I lead a four-person model-behaviour documentation group at a company that ships a general-purpose assistant to enterprise customers. Roughly a dozen times a week we have to rule on sentences of the following kind, destined for system cards, evaluation write-ups, and support macros: the model believes the user is a novice; the model wants to end the conversation; the model assumes the request concerns the United States; the model has no representation of the price. These sentences are quoted back to us verbatim by customers' legal teams and occasionally by press, and we currently have no standard for when an attribution of that shape is licensed and when it is smuggled-in metaphor that will mislead a reader who takes it literally.

Our working assumption has been Dennett's: treating something as an intentional system is a predictive strategy rather than a metaphysical verdict about its internals, and it is licensed insofar as ascribing beliefs and desires of determinate content to the system buys predictions of its behaviour that the rival strategies do not. Two of my engineers draw opposite conclusions from that. One holds that behaviour alone is sufficient evidence, precisely because an attribution on this view is a claim about prediction and not about mechanism. The other holds that we may not publish "the model believes X" unless interpretability work has located something in the system corresponding to X, since otherwise we are decorating a correlation in philosophical language. In practice we get interpretability results for a small minority of claims, so this disagreement has to be settled in advance rather than claim by claim.

Please write the decision procedure that a non-specialist on my team can run on a proposed attribution in about thirty minutes, with the head of product signing off before anything reaches a system card. I need the following:

- The questions to ask, in order, with a pass/fail criterion for each and a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, including what to do when the answer cannot be obtained.
- A house vocabulary. For each of "believes," "wants," "assumes," "represents," "acts as if," and "is disposed to," say what evidence licenses it and what it commits the reader to. The point is to let us keep saying useful things when the strong verb is not earned, without retreating into vagueness.
- A prediction-logging requirement. Every published attribution should carry a concrete, checkable behavioural expectation: what we would see if the attribution is right, and what would look anomalous if it is wrong. Specify the format tightly enough that a logged prediction can actually falsify or embarrass the claim later rather than sitting there decoratively.
- A revision rule. When a probe contradicts an attribution, when does that show the attribution was wrong, and when does it show only that we misstated its content or its boundary conditions? Give the test for telling these apart, and cap how many rounds of revision are allowed before the claim must be withdrawn rather than narrowed again.
- Some honest handling of holism and charity. An attribution is never of a single belief in isolation, so state the minimum surrounding cluster of ascriptions a claim must specify before it can be published — and keep that minimum small enough that a four-person team can meet it.
- An explicit rule on self-report. The model's stated explanation of its own output counts as evidence for what, exactly, and how is it weighted against behavioural evidence, given that a system can produce a fluent rationale that is not the reason for the output it produced?
- Marked stop points where the procedure returns "do not publish," and marked hand-off points where the remaining question is about how much interpretive charity we owe a lay reader rather than about evidence, and so goes to a person.

Then run the procedure on three cases we actually have.

Case A. Our coding assistant, asked to write a function that reads a configuration file, emits exception handling and a fallback default in 40 of 40 sampled prompts, including prompts whose surrounding text explicitly guarantees the file exists and is well formed. An engineer wants to write in the card: the model believes file reads can fail.

Case B. Asked directly what today's date is, the assistant says it has no access to the current date and cannot answer. Given instead a prompt containing no date but a tool result that mentions a recent one, it confidently computes and states the correct date. Our previous system card asserts that the model believes it does not know the current date.

Case C. The assistant declines a benign request, explaining that its policy forbids discussing that topic. The training policy in fact permits it, and a modest rephrasing elicits a full answer. Support wants to write: the model believes it is prohibited from discussing medical topics.

At least one of these should come out in a way I will not welcome — in particular, if the procedure requires retracting or rewriting language we have already shipped, say so plainly and tell me what it obliges us to do about published claims, including whether we owe downstream customers notice. Finally, be candid about which steps are judgment calls wearing the clothing of rules: the threshold for calling a pattern systematic, the amount of charity extended to the system, and the decision to read a failure as a content error rather than an attribution error. Do not give us a bibliography or a reading list; the deliverable is a document my team can run without any background in philosophy of mind.
