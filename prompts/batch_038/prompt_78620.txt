I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've got a piece coming up where I want to be right rather than just even-handed.

The setup: a startup is selling school districts a "readiness score" for four-year-olds, built out of a polygenic score for educational attainment. The pitch deck lines I've got in front of me are roughly:

(a) "our score accounts for about 11% of the variation in years of education in adults of European ancestry"
(b) "educational attainment is around 60% heritable, so we're capturing about a sixth of what matters"
(c) "this is prediction, not destiny — an R² of 0.11 is a modest but real effect, exactly what every other screening tool delivers"
(d) districts that turn it down are "rejecting a measurement because it's inconvenient"

The districts in question serve a population that's something like 40% non-European ancestry, and the plan is to use the score to decide which kids get extra pre-K tutoring.

So: what do the most important implications of what heritability actually is — variance ratio rather than individual attribution, population- and environment-specific, the twin-versus-GWAS gap, genes and environments being tangled up in each other — imply for this story specifically? I want the three or four that should carry the piece, in the order you'd run them, with the reasoning for the ordering: if a reader only keeps one thing, which one changes what they'd conclude about the district's plan? Then take (a) through (d) one at a time and tell me which survive contact and which are doing slight-of-hand. I especially want to know whether (b) is even the right kind of arithmetic — is a ratio of variance-explained to heritability meaningful here, and does "a sixth of what matters" describe what the score is picking up? And whether (c) is a fair defense or a category mistake about what screening tools are for.

Then flag the implication nobody in this fight seems to be raising: what a prediction score can and can't tell you about who actually benefits from an intervention, since that's the district's real question and I suspect it's where the strongest criticism lives.

Prose first, then a table I can keep on my desk while reporting. Columns: claim (short label); verdict (one of: holds up / true but misleading / doesn't follow / needs an empirical check I should go do); why, in a sentence or two; and the sentence I could write instead that would be accurate.

No reading lists or citations please — I'll chase sources myself once I know what I'm looking for. Just the reasoning.
