Just got off a four hour argument with my friend R, who does like three silent retreats a year so she came armed, and I still cant tell what I actually think. Her line: the only thing that ever makes anything bad is craving or suffering — wanting what you don't have, or hurting — so getting what you want matters only insofar as it makes the wanting stop. She calls it tranquilism. I kept agreeing with her in the moment and then disagreeing later, so here are the cases and my verdicts, and I want to know what principle they add up to, if there's one.

1. The settled guy. No ambitions left, nothing he's chasing, mildly pleasant days, no pain. Better off than:
2. The achiever. Hits a big goal every couple of years, and the wanting-the-next-one starts before the celebrating is over. Never exactly frustrated. I said her life is going worse than the settled guy's and I still think that, but I can't point at anything going wrong inside it, which bothers me.
3. Chronic pain patient who's stopped fighting it — years of practice, the pain is still there but she no longer craves relief. Better than the same pain plus the struggle against it. But still worse off than someone with no pain who also doesn't crave anything. So the pain counts against her even though she isn't resisting it, and I think that wrecks the view.
4. My cousin wanted to be a musician, ground at it for a decade, gave it up, is genuinely happy now doing something smaller. Doesn't pine. I say: going well, full stop.
5. Same texture, different history. A woman raised in a community where girls were never allowed to expect much of a life, so she never wanted more, is content, suffers nothing. I say her life is going worse than my cousin's. R said I'm describing the identical state and inventing a difference. I don't think I am, but I can't say what the difference is tracking.
6. Rich guy who has everything he wants, knows it, and is scared of losing it. Worse off than the settled guy with almost nothing, even though his want-satisfaction is way higher.
7. Getting exactly what you intensely wanted, and the getting turning out to be smaller than the wanting was. Doesn't seem to add much to the life. I agreed with this one instantly and don't know why.
8. Easy one: pain plus desperate unsatisfied craving for the pain to stop. Worst case, no argument there.

So — is there a principle that gets all eight? I mainly want to know whether it can hold 3 next to 4 and 5, the pain that isn't being resisted beside the contentment that was never allowed to get bigger, because those two are where R and I spent an hour talking past each other. Then tell me which of my verdicts a decent principle forces me to give up, and don't spare number 2 if that's the one. And finally the case I keep flip flopping on: someone takes something — drug, retreat, whatever — that switches the craving off entirely. No suffering, no wanting, and also no longer bothered that her sister is miserable. On R's view that's the best life on offer. My gut says something has been lost, but every time I try to name what, I catch myself smuggling in something other than craving and suffering, which is supposed to be the only thing I'm measuring with. Say what the principle does with that one, and hand me a ninth case I haven't thought of that would actually stress-test it.
