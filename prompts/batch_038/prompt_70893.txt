I run the simulation side of a last-mile grocery delivery company — a few hundred vans, six micro-fulfillment sites. Somewhere along the line I ended up chairing what we call model review: a 45-minute meeting where somebody presents a sim and the room decides whether leadership is allowed to use its output for a real decision (open a site, change staffing ratios, switch routing vendors). We do maybe eight of these a year, the decisions behind them run from about $150k to a couple million, I sign off jointly with our director of ops, and the room is usually two engineers, one ops manager who has never looked at code, and somebody from finance.

The fight is the same every single time. An engineer says an assumption is unrealistic — pickers in the sim never take an unscheduled break, travel times are a fixed average, customers always accept the first delivery slot we offer. And the person who built the model answers that assumptions don't have to be realistic, only the predictions do, and waves at Milton Friedman's old methodology essay, the one where he argues that the more significant a theory is the more unrealistic its assumptions tend to be because it's abstracting away from a bunch of detail. I've heard people call that move the F-twist. Meanwhile the ops manager objects that "that's just not how a picker behaves," which I'm pretty sure is a different and weaker complaint, but I can't say why in a way that lands in a meeting.

I have no philosophy or econ training, so I need you to build me the actual procedure. Give me an ordered set of questions I can run on any unrealistic assumption during those 45 minutes: what a sufficient answer to each one sounds like, which answers should make me stop and say the model can't be used for this decision, and which steps are really me making a judgment call that's dressed up as procedure. It should separate the different jobs an unrealistic assumption can be doing — I have a fuzzy sense that "we dropped X because X barely moves the output" and "we only claim this holds where X is true" aren't the same move, and that Friedman's argument doesn't cover both. And it has to tell me how to turn "it predicts well" into something I can actually check in the room without rerunning anything: we can require sensitivity runs beforhand, but not new experiments during the review. Don't send me to papers or a reading list, I won't read them, put whatever's needed inline. If I've got Friedman backwards anywhere above, just say so.

Then run it on three real ones from our queue:

1. Staffing model for a site we're opening. Assumes pickers are on-task for the whole shift — no unscheduled breaks, no call-outs. Validated against three weeks of pick-rate data from our busiest existing site; matches total picks-per-shift within 4%. Decision on the table: how many pickers to hire for opening month.

2. Routing comparison that assumes drivers take the shortest-time path and always find parking. It reproduces our actual delivery-time distribution across 40 days in two different metros. Decision: pick between two routing vendors.

3. The one I expect to lose. A whole-network sim I built myself for the site-selection decision. Deterministic average travel times, historical weekly average demand, and the parameters were tuned on the same 18 months of data we then used to confirm that it predicts well. It says site #4 is worth $1.9M over three years and it's already in a deck I sent up.

For case 3 don't soften it — if the procedure says the number can't carry the decision, I'd rather find out from you now.

Last two things: tell me where this procedure will misfire, i.e. the kinds of model or assumption it gives a confident wrong answer on and what I should do instead in those cases, and give me a one-line verdict format I can paste into the review doc, something like usable for X / not usable for X / needs Y first.
