In three weeks I am giving a thirty-minute paper, with fifteen minutes for questions, at a workshop on the foundations of decision theory and mechanism design. The thesis is that Hofstadter's superrationality is not a third way between rational choice and morality but ordinary expected-utility maximization operating under one unusual evidential premise: that every player is running the same reasoning procedure, so that my own output is evidence about theirs. Three consequences carry the paper.

First, in symmetric games this premise licenses maximizing over the diagonal only — over the profiles in which everyone plays what I play — which is why superrational players cooperate in the prisoner's dilemma, and why, once the payoff for unilateral defection is made large enough, the superrational recommendation turns into a mixed strategy rather than a pure one.

Second, in asymmetric games the diagonal contains no good outcomes. Take the Platonia dilemma: twenty people are told that a vast sum goes to whichever of them sends a telegram by noon, but only if exactly one does; if none send, or several send, nobody is paid. No pure strategy adopted alike by all twenty wins, since both "send" and "stay silent" lose when universalized. The only superrational recommendation available is that each randomize independently with probability one in twenty, which maximizes the chance that exactly one sender emerges.

Third — the part the paper presses — that recommendation is fragile in a precise way. It requires agents alike enough to arrive at the same mixed strategy, yet unlike enough that their random draws come out independent. Identical reasoning and uncorrelated noise have to be held apart, and the superrational premise does nothing to secure the second half: if the twenty are similar all the way down to the seeds they consult, the prescription defeats itself. My repair is to read the premise as a claim about the deterministic component of an agent's algorithm only, with the stochastic component stipulated exogenous.

The room will contain two or three game theorists who work on correlated equilibrium and common priors and will want to know in what sense any of this is a solution concept; one committed causal decision theorist who regards evidential decision theory as refuted and will therefore read my reduction to an evidential premise as a reductio rather than a clarification; a couple of philosophers who suspect the whole business is a magazine puzzle column dressed in game-theoretic notation; and one or two people working on coordination among separately designed machine agents, who will ask whether the independence stipulation is ever satisfiable in deployment. Nobody has read the 1983 essay closely, so I cannot lean on exegesis, and I will have no slides up during the questions.

Please prepare my question-period sheet. I want the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the thesis, each phrased in the idiom its raiser would use rather than in mine, with a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds standing at a podium and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Separate the objections that merely irritate from the two or three that could sink the paper if handled badly. Tell me what to concede in the opening ten minutes so the concessions are mine rather than extracted from me, which single reply has to land for the third consequence to survive the session, and which question I should most hope nobody thinks to ask. I have my own candidate — that no participant in a real lottery knows the size of the pool, or who else in it is reasoning this way, so the one-in-twenty prescription cannot be common to all twenty, and Hofstadter's own reader-facing version had to tell people to imagine a die with as many sides as the expected number of respondents. But tell me whether that is genuinely the most dangerous question in the room or whether something else is worse.
