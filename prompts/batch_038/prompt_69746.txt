I have been running a monthly meetup for the local rationalist crowd for about six years now: twelve to sixteen people, mostly autodidacts, two with philosophy degrees, all of them comfortable arguing and none of them shy. Our standard drill is steelmanning. Someone states a position, someone else reconstructs it as strongly as they can, the room judges. It works, but I have grown dissatisfied with it, because it trains people to reproduce an opponent's case while leaving their own assertions completely unindexed. It never forces anyone to say from where they are speaking, about what, at what time, or in what respect — and in practice a good chunk of our disputes turn out to be two people making true claims from standpoints that were never made explicit.

Reading around this, I landed on the Jain material: the doctrine that reality is many-sided and nothing can be captured by a single unqualified assertion, together with the associated technique of conditioning every claim, and the companion theory of partial standpoints. My recollection of the machinery is imperfect, so please treat it as a sketch to be corrected rather than as a spec. I believe the conditioning technique yields a sevenfold schema — in some ways it is; in some ways it is not; in some ways it is and is not; in some ways it is inexpressible; and then three further modes pairing inexpressibility with each of the first three — and that a claim gets indexed along respects such as substance, place, time, and condition or mode. I also gather that the point is not epistemic humility about our ignorance but a claim about the objects themselves: things genuinely have many aspects at once. If I have garbled or oversimplified any of this, correct me before you design around it; I would rather the activity rest on the real thing.

What I want is a runnable activity, not an explainer. Approach the task as a philosopher with working knowledge of classical Jain epistemology who also designs and has field-tested experiential teaching sessions for smart non-specialists. Think carefully before answering, and begin with a short brainstorming pass: sketch three or four candidate mechanics, two or three lines each, stating what philosophical pressure each one would actually generate and what it structurally cannot generate. Then commit to one and develop it fully.

The constraints. Twelve to sixteen adults, one two-hour session in a rented community room, whiteboard, index cards, a timer, and whatever I can print at home. No budget, so any payoff scheme must run on points or standing rather than money. No lecture up front and no vocabulary handed out at the door: participants should encounter the demands of conditioned assertion as rules of a game and only learn what the tradition is called, and what its parts are named, in the debrief. They should run into the problems themselves, in the play.

Specifically, I want the session to produce four experiences, in roughly this order:

1. Qualification as a discipline rather than a hedge. Participants should find that being forced to index an assertion sharpens some of their claims, dissolves others, and makes visible which of the claims they were prepared to fight about were doing no work at all.
2. The collapse problem. At some point the room should be tempted to conclude that every claim is true from some standpoint and that the game therefore cannot be lost. The mechanics should make that move visibly break something, so that they discover on their own that conditioning requires a constraint on which standpoints count — and that supplying that constraint is where all the difficulty lives.
3. Self-application. The requirement that every assertion be conditioned is itself an assertion. Let them run into this from inside the game, ideally by making the game's own rules available for play.
4. The cost. Under full conditioning, ordinary communication and joint decision-making should degrade in a way they can feel — which means the activity needs a component where the group must actually commit to something under time pressure while every contribution has to be qualified.

For the deliverable: complete rules and roles; the exact materials, including the content of any claim cards I need to print; a scoring rule; a minute-by-minute facilitator timeline; and a debrief that maps specific moments from the play onto specific parts of the doctrine, including where the four experiences above are supposed to land and what to say if one of them fails to land. Then tell me honestly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this — I expect the sharpest person in the room to turn it into a lawyering contest, and I expect people to game the scoring with trivially qualified claims — and how to handle each failure mode with an in-game fix rather than a speech. Note also which single component you would cut if I only have seventy-five minutes or only eight people. Finally, be frank about which feature of the view this activity cannot reproduce and that I will simply have to supply by talking; I suspect it is the metaphysical backing and the soteriological stakes, but tell me what you think.

Two housekeeping requests. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I cannot look anything up and would only be guessing at what you name. If you want to gesture at a source or a classical example, describe it in prose. And do not soften the diagnosis: if part of what I have asked for is not achievable in a two-hour game with non-specialists, say so and tell me what to build instead.
