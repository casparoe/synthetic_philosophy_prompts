I'm reading a trade book on artificial intelligence, and its chapter on the philosophy of mind opens with this summary of the computationalist debate:

"Putnam's claim in the 1960s was that the brain is literally a digital computer, executing a program whose inputs are sensory stimulations and whose outputs are behavior. That thesis has been refuted twice over. First, Searle showed that syntax is not semantics: a system that manipulates symbols according to rules cannot thereby understand anything, so even a behaviorally perfect simulation of a mind is not a mind. Second, Putnam himself showed that every physical system implements every computation, because you can always define a mapping from a system's states onto the symbols of whatever program you like — which leaves computationalism either true of thermostats and rocks or empty. What survives is the observation that brains process information, which amounts to no more than saying that they respond to their environment."

I find this compelling, and I take the author to be more careful than most popularizers, but I've run into suggestions that the debate is still live. Please go through the summary claim by claim: what would a philosopher of mind working on computation accept, what is a fair simplification, and what is actually wrong? In particular, do Searle's thought experiment and Putnam's triviality result establish what the author says they establish, or do they leave a substantive version of the thesis standing? And does the deflationary last sentence follow from those two results — or is it in tension with one of them?
