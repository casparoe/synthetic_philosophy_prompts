I'm the partnerships lead at a small coalition of about a dozen advocacy organizations — animal welfare, global health, criminal justice reform, and two faith-affiliated family-services groups. Members bring me two or three proposals a month in which each offers to soften, suspend, or fund something the other cares about, on terms neither would accept for free: our animal-welfare member asks a global-health member to stop serving meat at its fundraisers, and the global-health member asks in return that the animal-welfare member redirect part of its lobbying budget to bed nets. We have no framework, so each proposal is settled by whoever argues hardest.

Please write a decision procedure for evaluating these trades that my two-person team, neither of whom has studied philosophy, can run in about an hour per proposal. Ground it in what the moral trade literature actually establishes rather than in general negotiation advice: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it must hand the decision back to the member board because the question is one of values rather than facts (the board signs off by two-thirds on anything the procedure flags). It should address whether each side can be assessed in its own evaluative terms or whether some exchange rate between values is unavoidable; trades in which a party is asked to give up something it considers not merely suboptimal but flatly impermissible; harms to parties who are not at the table, including future people and nonhuman animals; publicity, and whether a trade that has to be kept quiet is thereby disqualified; and repeat play, where refusing trades preserves social pressure against conduct we think wrong while accepting them trains counterparts to expect payment.

Then run it on three cases: the meat-for-budget exchange above; an outside group that campaigns against abortion access offering to fund and lobby for our criminal-justice member's sentencing-reform bill if the coalition stays neutral on an upcoming abortion ballot measure; and two members agreeing to withdraw opposition to a freight corridor in exchange for donations to their own programs, where the corridor's costs fall on residents and habitat downstream.

Flag which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say plainly what the procedure cannot decide. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration would flip your verdict on the second case, and where your reasoning about the procedure is most likely to break down.
