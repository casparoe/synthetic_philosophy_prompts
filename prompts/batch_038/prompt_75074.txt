Ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole about gene patents and the thing that's bugging me is less the law than the philosophy underneath it.

Here's the practice. In 2013 the US Supreme Court decided that a company which finds and isolates a gene — the BRCA1 and BRCA2 breast-cancer genes were the ones at issue — can't patent it, because a naturally occurring stretch of DNA is a product of nature and snipping it out of the chromosome doesn't change anything about the information it encodes. But in the same breath the Court said the company *can* patent cDNA: the molecule you build in the lab by copying a gene's coding regions with the non-coding introns left out. The reasoning runs, in the Court's words, that "the order of the exons may be dictated by nature, but the lab technician unquestionably creates something new when introns are removed from a DNA sequence to make cDNA."

And the bit I can't get past: the cell already makes an intron-less version of that same sequence, as messenger RNA, every single time the gene gets read. The information in the patented cDNA is information nature wrote. The only difference is that it's sitting in DNA chemistry in a tube instead of RNA chemistry in a cell.

So what picture of genetic information does this line of reasoning presuppose? Is the Court treating DNA as a molecule or as a text, and does it switch between the two depending on which one gives the answer it wants? The isolation half leans on "you didn't change the information," which sounds like DNA is a message; the cDNA half leans on "the technician made a new molecule," which sounds like DNA is chemistry. Are those actually incompatible readings of what a gene is, or is there some single account of what counts as a human contribution rather than a natural one that generates both results and just gets described inconsistently?

Please separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely happens to sit comfortably with — my hunch is it's compatible with several quite different theories of genetic information (DNA as literally semantic, content-bearing code; DNA as a purely causal template that we only *describe* informationally; "the gene" as a functional posit rather than any particular stretch of stuff), and I want to know which of those the cDNA/genomic DNA split actually forces. Then tell me what would have to change in patent practice, or in the way we talk and write about genes, if you swapped the presupposed view for one of the rivals. If you think the practice is flatly incoherent, say so and point at exactly where it breaks; if you think it's coherent but for reasons nobody articulates, give me the best reconstruction you can.

Last thing — tell me how confident you are in the reconstruction overall, and single out the one point in it you're least sure about.
