So at work we fit a bunch of candidate models, report AIC and BIC for each, pick whichever scores lower, and when the two disagree we just note it and go with the simpler model to be safe. Nobody really thinks about where those numbers come from. But the derivations look like they point in opposite directions: AIC is built to minimize expected predictive error and is famously not consistent — give it enough data and it keeps adding parameters forever — while BIC is a Laplace approximation to a Bayesian posterior over models and is consistent, homing in on the true model provided the truth is actually one of the candidates. So one criterion assumes no model in your set is true and the other assumes the truth is in your set, and the practice uses both and treats their agreement as reassuring.

What does that practice presuppose, philosophically? Two things I want pinned down. (1) Both penalize complexity, but AIC's penalty is a flat 2 per parameter and BIC's grows like log n. If the penalty were really a prior over hypotheses — the way a Solomonoff or Kolmogorov-style prior is — it shouldn't shift with how much data you happen to have. So is BIC's razor an a priori simplicity prior at all, or is it something else entirely, and if it's something else does that mean the Occam's razor statisticians use isn't the thing philosophers argue about? (2) Is reporting both scores and defaulting to the simpler model coherent, or an unacknowledged slide between "simple means probably true" and "simple means predicts better out of sample"? And what would have to change in the practice — the criteria, what gets reported, the tie-breaking rule — if we replaced them with a straight universal prior over computable models?
