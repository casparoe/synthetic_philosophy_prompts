I want help thinking through a conceptual issue in the hole argument, and I'd like an explanation rather than a survey. Assume I already have the mechanics: a diffeomorphism d that is the identity outside some bounded open region H, the drag-along construction, the resulting pair of models that agree on every field outside H but differ inside it, and the conclusion that if manifold points have identities independent of the fields defined on them, then the state of the world outside H fails to determine the state inside it.

The issue concerns what is actually doing the work. Two glosses are common, and I have been treating them as interchangeable, which I now think is a mistake.

Gloss 1 (dynamical metric): the argument bites because in general relativity the metric is a dynamical field rather than fixed background structure. In a special-relativistic field theory, dragging the matter fields through d while holding the background metric fixed yields a configuration that is not a solution of the theory at all, so no rival model is generated and no indeterminism arises.

Gloss 2 (gauge freedom): the argument bites because the diffeomorphism group acts as a symmetry of the whole system, so no field is privileged as supplying a standard of identity for the points; in a background theory the symmetry group is far too small to contain any nontrivial transformation localized inside a hole.

I built a toy theory to pull these apart. Call it T. On a manifold M diffeomorphic to R^4 the fields are a Lorentzian metric g and a scalar field φ, and the equations are Riemann(g) = 0 together with the wave equation for φ with respect to g, subject to geodesic completeness of g. Note what T does and does not have. Every model of T has a metric isometric to the standard Minkowski metric, so on the extensional way of drawing the background-independence line — an object counts as absolute if it has the same value, up to diffeomorphism, in every model of the theory — T's metric is absolute and T is a background-dependent theory. Its gauge-invariant content is just that of special relativity: a matter configuration on flat spacetime, up to Poincaré transformation. On the other hand, both of T's equations are local and generally covariant, so dragging g and φ together by an arbitrary diffeomorphism takes models to models, and the diffeomorphism group of M contains transformations supported entirely inside H. The drag-along construction therefore goes through in T exactly as it does in general relativity, and produces a second model agreeing pointwise with the first on both fields outside H and differing inside it.

With that in hand, I'd like you to explain:

1. What precisely distinguishes gloss 1 from gloss 2, and which one T favors. Is T a counterexample to the claim that the hole argument requires the metric to vary from solution to solution, or is the flatness constraint itself a way of varying nothing that I have misdescribed?

2. Someone will say T cheats: because its geometry is rigid, it supplies a standard of identity for points across models that general relativity lacks, so one can use the isometry between g and its drag-along to re-identify the points of the two models and dissolve the interior disagreement. I do not see how that works, since the isometry is not unique — composing it with any Poincaré transformation works equally well, and different choices re-identify φ differently. Tell me whether I am wrong here, and if I am not, explain why a rigid geometry fails to restore point identity.

3. Special relativity is deterministic: Cauchy data on a spacelike hypersurface fixes φ everywhere, and T, once quotiented by diffeomorphisms, has the same determinate content. So in what sense is there any indeterminism in T? I want a clear account of how "the theory is deterministic after quotienting" and "the drag-along yields distinct models agreeing outside H" can both be true, and what the haecceitist is and is not adding to T.

4. Whether T settles anything about the Kretschmann-style complaint that general covariance can be imposed on any theory whatsoever and therefore has no physical content.

I am asking for an explanation, but not a neutral one. Take a definite stand: tell me which gloss you think correctly identifies what the hole argument requires, whether T genuinely separates them or whether something in my description of it is broken, and where my reasoning above goes wrong if it does. If you think the toy theory fails — for instance, that the flatness constraint smuggles in absolute structure in a way that blocks the construction — say so plainly and explain why, rather than hedging with a menu of readings and leaving the choice to me. No references or further reading needed; I want the reasoning, not the literature. Pitch it for someone comfortable with the differential-geometric formulation of general relativity but not a specialist in the philosophy of spacetime.
