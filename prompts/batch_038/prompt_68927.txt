**Question** (one of two; 100 points; students were told to write approximately 1,200 words):

> Nagel's "Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness" (1971) treats the commissurotomy data as raising a problem for the thesis that consciousness is unified. First, explain the experimental arrangement that generates those data and what the patient's behaviour and verbal reports show. Second, evaluate the inference from those data to the claim that a single patient can be the locus of two subjects of experience: does the split-brain evidence show that the unity of consciousness fails, or only that it can be made to fail under artificially engineered conditions? Finally, say what would have to be true for the answer you give to be correct.

**Answer as submitted** (approximately 1,150 words):

> The operation was developed as a treatment for epilepsy that had not responded to anything else: severing the corpus callosum and the anterior commissure stops seizures from generalising from the hemisphere in which they begin to the other, at the cost of cutting the main channel by which the two hemispheres exchange information. The experiments exploit the crossing of the visual pathways. Because stimuli from the right half of the visual field of each eye project to the left hemisphere and stimuli from the left half project to the right, a patient held at fixation and shown a picture for a few tens of milliseconds in one hemifield receives that picture in one hemisphere only; the exposure is short enough that an eye movement cannot bring it across, and in a disconnected brain there is no callosal relay to carry it over.
>
> The findings are striking. With the stimulus in the left hemifield, the patient, asked what he saw, says nothing appeared, or that he is only guessing, and yet his left hand — controlled by the hemisphere that did receive the stimulus — reaches behind a curtain and selects the depicted object from an array, or draws it. He can also match it to a word card by touch with that hand. With the stimulus in the right hemifield, naming is immediate and untroubled. A related class of finding concerns action rather than perception: when information available only to the right hemisphere causes the patient to do something, the speech-dominant left hemisphere, which did not receive it, will produce a fluent and confident explanation of why he did it, and the explanation is invented. The unity at issue in these cases is not merely the availability of information for reasoning and report, since the unseen object plainly guides the hand that retrieves it; what is missing is joint availability to a single point of view.
>
> That absence is enough to settle the metaphysics. If two experiences are not co-conscious — not such that they can be jointly reported, jointly remembered, or drawn on together in a single course of reasoning — then they cannot belong to one subject, since a subject at a time just is a maximally co-conscious set of experiences. The patient who denies seeing the object while his hand retrieves it is therefore in a state of belief and a state of disbelief about the same content at the same moment, and the only way to describe that without contradiction is to say that there are two of them: two subjects sharing a body and, outside the laboratory, sharing a stream of behaviour so closely coordinated that they are rarely caught disagreeing.
>
> It would be a mistake to treat this as an artefact of the apparatus. The tachistoscope and the fixation point do not create the division; they remove the callosal traffic that ordinarily conceals one that is already there. An intact brain is two subjects in such continuous high-bandwidth communication that their separateness is almost never detectable, so the unity of consciousness in normal human beings is not a fact about consciousness at all but a fact about transmission capacity. What would have to be true for this to be right is simply that the disconnection effects are quantitative rather than qualitative — that the intact corpus callosum integrates the same two streams, more thoroughly, rather than constituting one.
>
> Nagel accepts the two-subjects reading and then draws the deflationary conclusion: whether we say the patient is one subject or two is an empty question, a choice among descriptions of the same physical facts, with nothing further at stake. Once this is granted the puzzle dissolves and there is nothing in the commissurotomy data that we have failed to understand.
>
> One feature of the case nevertheless deserves emphasis, because it is not disposed of by any of the above. The patient reports no sense of duality whatever; he is not aware of being two, and nothing in his experience strikes him as partitioned. That report is itself part of the data, not a nuisance to be explained away, and any account on which no one is in a position to give it has not yet described the situation it set out to describe.
