My roommate and I got into it last night about cognitive penetration of perception and we went way too long — he's a vision science person, I'm more the philosophy side — and I came away feeling like I'd talked myself into something I can't actually defend. So: I want the case against my own view, as hard as you can make it.

The view I'm now committed to is basically this: the penetrabilists haven't lost on the evidence, they've lost on a definition. Every time someone produces a putative case of belief or desire changing what a subject actually sees — the old coin-size one with poor kids, the memory-color one where a banana under gray light looks yellower than a luminance-matched patch, the ones where priming shifts how racially ambiguous faces get categorized — the reply from the other side is always the same move. Whatever survived the controls, they say, wasn't cognition: it was attention, or an expectation operating as a low-level prior, or a post-perceptual judgment that got misreported as an experience. And notice that attention gets treated as a neutral pipe. It's classified inside the perceptual system by stipulation, so anything routed through it is automatically exempt from counting as penetration. But where you look, what you hold in the center of gaze, and what you're set to detect are chosen by exactly the states everybody already agrees are cognitive — what you want, what you expect, what you're looking for. So the impenetrabilist side can absorb any result whatsoever without ever having been wrong. Pylyshyn's penetrability criterion and Fodor's encapsulation are doing all the load-bearing work, and the criterion has been quietly upgraded from a discovery into an immunity clause. That's the position.

Now tell me where I'm exposed. I can generate the list of problems for the other side myself, that's not the exercise. I want:

- What results would actually show attention isn't the escape hatch? I mean the specific shape of them — effects that persist when attention and fixation are pinned down and matched across conditions, versus attentional capture that's demonstrably stimulus-driven and expectation-free. Does the exogenous cuing literature give my opponent a clean example of modulation that can't be traced back to a belief or a goal, and if so how much of the debate does that settle?
- Is there a version of the impenetrabilist reply that is not definitional fiat and does real empirical work? Something a fair referee would count as a genuine constraint on what qualifies as a penetration case rather than a way of disqualifying candidates in advance. If there is, my whole argument loses its edge and I'd rather know now.
- Where I think I have to give ground: the infant and animal data (same illusion in subjects who plausibly lack the relevant beliefs), the results where people are told the true state of affairs and the experience doesn't budge, the illumination-prior stuff where the penetrating "belief" looks suspiciously like a hardwired assumption about light coming from above. For each, say whether it's fatal to me, merely awkward, or actually compatible with what I'm claiming.
- Whether my position is unfalsifiable in exactly the way I'm accusing the other side of being. "Anything you call cognition I'll call attentional selection, and anything you call attentional selection I'll call cognition" is symmetric, and I suspect I'm hoist with my own petar here. If the symmetry is real, say so plainly and then tell me what would break it.
- And if it is symmetric: is that even a problem for a claim of this kind? Mine is a methodological thesis about how a debate is being run, not a thesis about the visual system. Can a view whose content is "this question isn't empirically resolvable as currently posed" itself be empirically resolvable, and what would have to happen in the literature for it to be shown wrong?

Rank all of it at the end — which of these, if I found out it were true, should make me drop the position outright, and which only mean I need to reword it into something weaker but still worth saying. Be honest if the answer is that I've just described the other side's stance from a different angle and there's no disagreement left. He'd love that and I'd rather hear it from you first.
