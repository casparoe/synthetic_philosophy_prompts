Ok so I chair a five-person volunteer grants panel (small fund, maybe a dozen decisions a year) and last cycle we walked face-first into the doctrinal paradox / discursive dilemma, except with a twist I havent seen anyone discuss. Our bylaws say we fund a project iff all three of these hold:

E — there's decent evidence the intervention does what the applicant claims
C — we actually have the staff/volunteer capacity to oversee it this year
N — it doesn't duplicate something already in our portfolio

Here's how the votes came out (y = that member judges the premise true):

| member | E | C | N | their own conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ada | n | y | y | don't fund |
| Ben | n | y | y | don't fund |
| Chris | y | n | y | don't fund |
| Devi | y | n | y | don't fund |
| Ezra | y | y | n | don't fund |

Every single member voted don't-fund. Unanimous. But aggregate premise-by-premise and you get E (3–2), C (3–2), N (4–1), which jointly entail fund. So conclusion-based says don't, premise-based says do, and the part that's messing with my head is that the premise-based answer isn't *anybody's* answer — its a group judgment that no individual member of the group actually holds. If we'd used it, we'd have sent a grant letter that all five of us would have refused to sign individually.

We need to fix a rule ex ante for next cycle, before anyone knows which side of the table they'll be sitting on, so I want to hear the real argument rather than a shrug.

A few things about what I want:

**Role.** Answer as a social choice theorist / formal epistemologist who has also spent a lot of years actually sitting in collegial decision-making rooms — someone who's equally comfortable with the impossibility results and with how a five-person panel behaves when the coffee is bad and the deadline is Friday.

**Don't** re-explain the basics to me. I know the three-juror version and the "are the roads important / is the weather right / do we have funds" toy by heart. Assume I've read the standard material and spend your words on the parts that are actually load-bearing in the profile above. No bibliography, no reading list, no "see also" section — I'm not going to chase papers, and please don't invent citations or page numbers either. If you're unsure who said what, just say the idea without attaching a name to it.

**Step 1 — brainstorm first.** Before any dialogue, think carefully and give me a short bullet list (150 words-ish) of the strongest points available to each side *given this specific vote profile*. I especially want the points that only bite in the weird case where the panel is unanimous against and the group is unanimous for — arguments that would not show up in the textbook three-juror setup.

**Step 2 — the dialogue.** Then write a dialogue (~900 words) between two people arguing about which rule our panel should adopt:

- **Iris**, premise-first. Thinks a committee's job is to be responsive to reasons, that the group can be right about the conjunction even when no member sees it, and that collapsing everything into one yes/no on the outcome throws away most of the information in the room.
- **Marcus**, conclusion-first. Thinks legitimacy attaches to decisions, not to reason-slices; that a body acting against the unanimous will of every one of its members is not a group agent but a runaway process; and that the whole premise/conclusion split smuggles in an arbitrary labeling of the agenda.

Both of them are excellent — same IQ, same familiarity with the literature, same amount of committee scar tissue. Neither gets to be a strawman or to lose by being slow. Their replies have to actually engage the move the other one just made, not reset to their opening position; I want to see them trade real blows and occasionally get pushed backwards.

Things they must genuinely grapple with, at some point in the exchange:
1. Accountability. If the funded project fails, who owns that decision when no member endorsed it? Does premise-based aggregation dissolve or relocate responsibility?
2. Manipulability. Someone has to decide in advance which propositions count as "premises." Is that designation itself gameable — e.g. by adding or splitting a premise, or by writing E as two separate criteria? Does Marcus's objection here actually land, or is it just "all rules depend on framing"?
3. Strategy. Does premise-based voting invite members to lie about a premise they believe in order to control the outcome? Does conclusion-based voting invite the opposite — lying about the conclusion to avoid being the swing vote? Which distortion is worse for a five-person recurring panel?
4. The information argument runs both ways. Iris says conclusion votes discard information; Marcus should have something to say about what premise votes discard, or about whose information gets to count.
5. The impossibility framing. Have them be explicit that this is a choice of which condition to sacrifice, not a search for a rule with no downsides — and each should name, out loud, what their own preferred procedure is giving up.

At least one real concession from each side, and if you're tempted to have them settle on some boring hybrid rule (weighted supermajority on premises, sequential voting, whatever), the hybrid only earns its place if one of them derives it inside the dialogue and the other attacks it and it survives. Otherwise end in honest deadlock.

**Last line of your whole answer**, on its own line, exactly in this form (this is the bit I'm parsing, so no extra prose after it):

Verdict: [premise-based | conclusion-based | neither] | sacrifice: [six words or fewer, naming what the winning procedure gives up]

So something shaped like `Verdict: premise-based | sacrifice: <you fill this in>` — and that verdict should be *your* call about my panel specifically, not just a summary of who argued better.
