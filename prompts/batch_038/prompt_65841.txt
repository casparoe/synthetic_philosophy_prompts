Okay, I'm in a little reading group on infinity and time — mostly grad students plus one friend who does math and thinks philosophers are hopeless — and last week we got stuck on Hilbert's hotel. One of the guys, call him Marcus, turned up with a version of the hotel that nobody could dispose of, and now he's insisting it rehabiltates the finitist's complaint against an actually infinite past. The session ended with three people just repeating "but n ↦ 2n is a bijection, we're done" at him, which isn't really an answer, so I want the position argued properly rather than waved away.

Marcus's setup: the hotel is full, rooms 1, 2, 3, ..., and the infinite bus pulls up. Now add one extra rule, which he calls the Bell Rule — guests only change rooms when a bell rings, the bell rings once per second, and at each ring any given guest may step at most one room down the corridor. Stipulate the boring stuff away: everybody steps simultaneously, so no one ever arrives at a door that's still occupied, corridors don't jam, nobody starves en route. To get the guest from room n to room 2n takes n rings, so the guy from room 4000 settles after a bit over an hour, and the one from room 10^9 settles after roughly 31 years. Marcus concedes the point everyone usually makes against supertask skeptics: each individual relocation is finite and gets completed, so there's no task in the sequence that's impossible on its own terms. What he says never happens is that the reshuffle is *finished*. At every finite time t, guest n is sitting in room n + t, so every room is still occupied and not one of the bus passengers can be checked in. The state the hotel is supposed to reach — all the even rooms filled, all the odd ones empty and available — is a state that exists only at the limit, and there is no finite moment at which it obtains. His conclusion is that the hotel example, once you make the process explicit like this, doesn't refute the finitist at all: it just assumes there is such a thing as a completed ω-length reshuffle, i.e. exactly the actual infinite that's supposed to be in question. The bijection is doing the work of a promissory note, not a payment.

So: argue for Marcus's conclusion — that once the Bell Rule is in place, the Hilbert's-hotel rejoinder to the argument against an actual infinite is question-begging. I want a real argument, not a survey of the debate, and please don't hand me a bibliography or a list of who said what, I'm after the reasoning itself. A few things it needs to cover: (1) name the premise that's actually load-bearing — something like "a state that is not realized at any finite time is not realized" — and say whether you think it needs a temporal gloss or whether it's really a claim about what it takes for a collection to be complete; (2) take on the two rebuttals I expect, namely that the Bell Rule is an artificial physical restriction on what is really just a bookkeeping structure (the bijection holds whether or not anybody walks anywhere), and that the limit moment is no more mysterious than 0.999... equalling 1; (3) tell me how much the argument gets you — does it support the strong claim that a beginningless past is impossible, or only something weaker about physically performable supertasks — and which of those you'd actually bet on. And if you think there's a place where Marcus is smuggling something in, say where, but argue the case first and hedge after, because our last session was 90% hedging.
