Then give your full analysis, organized under the four headings (a) through (d) below. Write in American English.

Here is the practice I want examined.

In most wealthy countries, prenatal screening for certain chromosomal conditions is offered to every pregnant patient, and the professional norm governing the conversation around that offer is nondirectiveness. Counselors and clinicians are trained not to steer. They describe the condition, they describe the options — continuing the pregnancy, ending it, pursuing further diagnostic testing, planning for neonatal care or palliative care — and they decline to indicate which option is better. If a patient asks point-blank what the counselor would do in her place, the trained response is to return the question to the patient. The norm is ordinarily justified by appeal to the thought that it is the patient's values, not the clinician's, that ought to determine the outcome.

At the same time, the program of which those conversations are a part is evaluated in frankly directional terms. The headline performance statistics for a screening program are its detection rate and its false-positive rate; a program that identifies the large majority of affected pregnancies is treated as a better program than one that identifies half of them, and it is in those terms that funding, audit, and quality improvement are discussed. Programs do not, so far as I can tell, report as headline figures the proportion of patients who declined screening, or the proportion who afterward said the offer had felt like an expectation. Meanwhile the commercial side of the same enterprise runs in a direction the counseling side disavows: gamete banks publish donor profiles organized by trait — height, eye color, educational attainment, sometimes athletic or musical history — and demand is known to concentrate heavily on some profiles rather than others, even as the professional consensus holds that reproductive selection is legitimate when aimed at health and treats a request to select *for* a disability as a paradigm of misuse.

My questions are these.

(a) What theory of value must nondirectiveness presuppose in order to be anything more than professional etiquette? Distinguish, in particular, between the strong claim that there is no fact of the matter about which of these lives would go better for the child, the weaker claim that there may be such a fact but clinicians are unreliable judges of it, and the purely procedural claim that the decision belongs to the patient whoever is right about it. Which of these does the training actually commit the counselor to, and does the practice work equally well under all three?

(b) Is the cluster internally consistent? The counseling encounter disclaims a direction while the program's success metric supplies one. Is there a coherent view on which both can be affirmed — a division of labor between institutional aims and individual encounters, say, or a distinction between what a system may pursue and what an individual clinician may say — or does the practice amount to a covert endorsement of something like a principle of procreative beneficence, according to which prospective parents have strong moral reason to select, among the children they could have, the one expected to have the best life, while refusing to say so out loud? Note that the child who would have been born is not obviously harmed by not being born, so it is unclear whose interests the direction is serving; tell me what the metric is tracking, and for whom.

(c) Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with. Identify where the cluster is philosophically underdetermined — which of its features would look identical under several rival accounts, among them a disability-rights critique of the whole arrangement, a welfarist or consequentialist one, a Kantian account of respect for persons, and a purely defensive account on which nondirectiveness exists chiefly to distribute legal and moral risk. Then tell me what further features, if I went looking for them, would discriminate among those rivals: what would I have to observe in scripts, in statistics, in the design of the offer, or in how clinicians talk among themselves, to know which theory is actually load-bearing?

(d) Describe what would have to change if the implicit theory were replaced — first, by an explicitly comparative and welfarist one, stated openly rather than smuggled into a metric; and second, by one on which the aim is not the best child but the least pressured decision. Be concrete about the counselor's script, the reported statistics, and the structure of the offer, and say what each replacement would have to give up.
