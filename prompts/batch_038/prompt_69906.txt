Write a satirical open letter of between 750 and 900 words from a wine critic who has just been struck off the register of an accreditation body called the Institute of Certified Palates. The letter is addressed to the Institute's governing committee and is to be published in a trade magazine. The premise, which the piece should never announce but simply enact, is that the Institute's rules of accreditation have been lifted from David Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste" and are applied by its officials with perfect consistency and no sense of humour.

The charges against the letter-writer should map onto the qualifications Hume demands of a true judge: delicacy of palate, practice, comparison with other instances of the same kind, good sense, and freedom from prejudice. Invent a specific and faintly absurd finding of fact for each of these — for example, that his palate was ruled out of practice because he spent fourteen months on medical advice drinking nothing but water; that he fails the comparison requirement because he has never tasted outside two French regions and therefore has nothing to calibrate against; that he is prejudiced because his spouse makes one of the wines he scored highly; that he lacks good sense because, when the committee asked him for his reasons, he could only repeat that the wine was delicious. Let him contest every charge on its own terms, and let him lose every one.

Then give him the argument he thinks is his best. He has the delicacy the Institute claims to prize: years ago he identified a fault in a cask that the cellar staff had declared sound, and he was vindicated when an old key on a leather thong was fished out of the bottom of the vessel — an incident that should visibly echo the story Hume retells from Cervantes, though you must allude to it rather than reproduce it. The Institute's answer, which the writer quotes from its letter to him in his own words rather than from Hume, is that his detection is now inadmissible precisely because he is no longer accredited, and that accreditation can only be evidenced by the joint verdict of accredited judges. He should notice that this makes the Institute's standard unappealable from outside the Institute and believe he has caught them in a circle; the committee's reply, which he reports and finds contemptible but which the reader should recognize as the theory itself, is that the standard was never a rule to be applied but a list of people, and that the list is kept by the people already on it.

Add one further move near the end. His dissent should be reclassified under the allowances Hume makes for differences of humour, temperament, and the manners of the age: his preference for high-acid wines is diagnosed as a personal bias to be generously allowed for rather than as a counterexample to the standard. He should find this flattering at first, and only afterward realize that the same diagnosis is available for any dissenter, so that no disagreement is structurally capable of counting against the consensus, and that the founding cohort of the register was itself selected by a trade association's majority vote — a fact he finds scandalous until he sees there was nothing else on offer.

The letter-writer must remain a sincere Humean throughout. He does not believe that deliciousness is a quality sitting in the wine independently of anyone's tasting it, and he must never argue as though it were. If he slips into that kind of realism, the slip has to be visible to the reader and be part of the joke: he catches himself, or the committee catches him, or he leans on it for a paragraph before having to retract it. Do not let him win. The letter should close with him entirely convinced that he is right and having said nothing the committee has not already filed.

The target is the institutionalization of a consensus-based standard of taste, not Hume the man, and not any real critic, publication, region, or certification body: every institution, wine, and person in the letter must be invented. The comedy has to come from getting the philosophy right, so that a reader who knows the essay laughs rather than winces, while a reader who has never heard of it can still enjoy watching a man destroyed by paperwork. Keep the voice deadpan, aggrieved, and self-serious from the first sentence to the last: no winking at the reader, no authorial asides, and no passage that explains what is being satirized.

Paraphrase Hume's criteria and his arguments in your own words and do not put any quotation from him or from anyone else anywhere in the piece; likewise include no footnotes, no bibliography, and no list of works referred to. Present the letter as continuous prose in the manner of real correspondence — a salutation, then unbroken paragraphs, then a signature — with no headings, no subheadings, no bullet points, no numbered clauses, no bold or italicized emphasis, and no tables.
