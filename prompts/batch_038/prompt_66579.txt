In December 1845 Søren Kierkegaard invited a satirical weekly to attack him, and within six days it did. I would like that episode — the so-called Corsair affair — treated as a test case for the account of the press, the public, and leveling that he published a few months later, with the test run honestly in both directions: what the theory says the participants faced and did, and what the participants' actual conduct says about the theory.

The facts, so that you need not reconstruct them. A young critic, Peder Ludvig Møller, reviewed Stages on Life's Way in a literary annual he edited, praising parts of the book while treating Kierkegaard's broken engagement as the key to the pseudonymous authorship. Møller was associated with Corsaren, the successful Copenhagen weekly edited by Meïr Aron Goldschmidt, then in his twenties. Kierkegaard replied in the respectable daily Fædrelandet under the pseudonym Frater Taciturnus, made Møller's connection to the weekly public, and in effect solicited its attention, observing that every writer of consequence had been abused there and that he alone had been denied the distinction. The weekly obliged, and from January 1846 ran caricatures built out of a handful of repeatable physical details — the stoop, the cane, the thin legs, the trouser legs of unequal length — alongside invented backstage conversations, mock advertisements issued in the names of his own pseudonyms, further pseudonyms of the paper's invention, and a fabricated identification of Frater Taciturnus with a real and unfortunate Copenhagen figure. The consequence was not confined to the page: Kierkegaard, who walked the city for hours daily and stopped to talk to strangers as part of his working method, became an object of street mockery and of following, and he later described the whole business as an examination he had been put through and as the source of what he came to know about the crowd. In the same weeks he published a declaration attaching his own name to the pseudonymous books; in his second and final public reply he argued that a paper shielded by contempt, by editorial anonymity, and by a hired legal responsibility could turn silence into fear and always keep the last word, and he proposed answering it by inverting its values — refusing its praise and commissioning its abuse, so that its power would be exposed as a service one could order. On 30 March 1846 he published the long review of Thomasine Gyllembourg's novel that appears in English as A Literary Review, or Two Ages, or On the Present Age. Its diagnosis is that the present age is reflective and passionless, that its destructive movement is leveling, that the daily press and anonymity are the instruments of that movement, and that the public is an abstraction, a phantom: not a people, not a generation, not a community, not a society, and not the particular men who are said to belong to it, since they belong to it only insofar as they are an abstraction. He distinguished that public from the crowd and from the people, and he kept developing the analysis for the rest of his life, most compactly in the claim that the crowd is untruth. In 1855 he issued a run of numbered pamphlets under his own name, sold in the street, attacking the established church and the daily press.

Here is the analytical machinery I want you to use. It is mine, not Kierkegaard's, and you should discard it if it does not earn its keep. Under the heading of the public, I think three separable features are usually run together: agent anonymity, meaning that no participant is individually identifiable or answerable for their part; authorial vacancy, meaning that nothing the collective produces is traceable to a decision anyone made, so that the collective cannot be addressed, argued with, or held to account as an agent; and target interchangeability, meaning that the mechanism does not depend on who is singled out, since anyone would have served.

First, then: which of these three does Kierkegaard's account actually require, which are merely its usual accompaniments, and which does the Corsair affair exhibit? My working suspicion is that the affair exhibits none of them cleanly. Goldschmidt was a named editor who decided what to print; the caricatures worked precisely because they fastened onto features nobody else possessed, and the hoax depended on intimate knowledge of Kierkegaard's own pseudonymous apparatus; and the jeering he met in the street was a crowd in the literal sense — present, mutually visible, individually addressable — rather than an abstraction. If that is right, is the affair a counterexample to the theory, a case it describes at a level other than the one usually assumed, or evidence that its real object is narrower and sharper: the transfer of responsibility, whereby one man can be sued but not shamed while a readership consumes a persecution without putting its name to it?

Second, the evidential standing of the case. Kierkegaard solicited the attack, and the diagnosis of leveling appeared within three months of the first caricature, while the campaign was still running. What sort of confirmation can a symptom theory offer when the theorist has arranged the symptom, and when the theory's central term is fitted to the theorist's own most vivid experience? Weigh the readings available in the very same materials that require no appeal to leveling at all: a weekly that needed recognizable copy and acquired a walking advertisement; a rivalry over a university chair; a quarrel about pseudonymity and the disclosure of authorship. Does the theory have resources to distinguish leveling from an ordinary abuse campaign that happens to possess a press instrument, or does leveling end up naming whatever an age does to a man it dislikes?

Third, the silences. Identify the features of the episode on which the theory says nothing — the target's own agency, the economics of a publication that must sell, satire as a genre (an engine of leveling, or its opposite, since it lives on particularity?), the effect of his public declaration of authorship on the paper's leverage — and for each say whether the silence is a gap the theory could fill without strain or one that would force it to change shape. Do not press it to speak where it does not.

Fourth, the pamphlets of 1855. Does the man who diagnosed the press as the instrument of leveling end up refuting that diagnosis, conceding to it, or demonstrating the exception he thought remained — a single named individual standing behind every sentence and handing it across a counter himself? If that practice supplies the theory's missing normative half, say so, and say what follows: that the 1846 diagnosis was never fundamentally about numbers, but about who can be required to answer for a sentence.

Close with a verdict on whether the Corsair affair confirms, strains, or simply fails to engage the theory of the press and the public, and with one further paragraph on whether the mechanism survives translation into a case in which the instrument has no editor at all and no one has solicited the attack. Write it as continuous analysis under the four headings above plus the verdict; assume familiarity with the biography and with the secondary literature and omit both, and give no citations or reading list. Wherever you are reconstructing rather than reporting, mark it as such.
