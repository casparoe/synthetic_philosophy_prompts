Our department has spent two years arguing about the frame problem, and neither camp has yet produced a formulation the other recognizes as responsive. I want you to draft the document they should have written: a joint statement that a computationalist and an embodied-cognition theorist could both sign in good faith after a genuine adversarial collaboration.

To keep the parties from being strawmen, fix them as follows.

Party A holds that the frame problem is a representational engineering problem with a recognizable solution shape. In its narrow sense it was a bookkeeping difficulty about how to represent the many facts an action leaves unchanged without writing a separate axiom for each, and A maintains this was substantially solved in practice by nonmonotonic and event-calculus-style formalisms. In its broad sense it is the problem of determining, from a large store of candidate representations, which are relevant to the present situation, and A maintains that relevance-realization mechanisms — learned similarity structure over embedding spaces, attention that gates information by task context, anytime and resource-bounded inference that trades completeness for responsiveness, active perception that changes what gets sampled rather than what gets inferred — have jointly demonstrated that relevance can be computed without exhaustive search.

Party B holds that these are relocations, not solutions. B's claim is that relevance is not something a system infers at all: it is something an organism inherits from standing in a situation already articulated by its needs, its body, and its history of coping. Any mechanism whose relevance-fixing work is done by a corpus, a task specification, a reward function, or a designer has been handed its frame from outside. On B's view A's engineering successes are real but parasitic on human frames, and the frame problem is not one item on a research program but a standing condition on whether genuine agency exists.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. **Agreed claims**, numbered. These should include what both sides accept about the narrow technical problem; what both accept about the behavioral context-sensitivity of contemporary learned systems; what each side concedes about the other's central insight; and what both agree needs explaining that neither currently explains. Every numbered claim must be something a real partisan of that side would sign — not a triviality both sign because it asserts nothing.

2. **A disagreements table.** One row per residual disagreement, with columns for: the disagreement stated in Party A's own preferred vocabulary; the same disagreement stated in Party B's own preferred vocabulary; and a classification of the relationship between those two statements — equivalent, non-equivalent but jointly satisfiable, genuinely incompatible, or verbal. For each verbal row, identify precisely which part is verbal and what remains once the vocabulary is neutralized.

3. **Cruxes**: the sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side. I suspect three, and I want you to assess them, discard any that turn out not to be cruxes, and add any I have missed:
   - whether a training corpus plus reward specification constitutes an externally supplied frame in the sense that matters, or is merely one way of fixing a prior that an internal mechanism then updates on — that is, whether "outsourced relevance" is a genuinely distinct category from "acquired relevance";
   - whether a system can be wrong about relevance in a way not tracked by any failure of its own objectives, which B's talk of an already-articulated situation seems to require and A denies is coherent;
   - whether the narrow problem's technical solutions transfer to the broad problem at all, or whether the two have been conflated to everyone's detriment.
   
   For each crux you keep: the case, experiment, or argument both sides agree would bear on it; each side's prediction, recorded in advance; and what each side commits to abandoning if that prediction fails. If a crux is one on which no shared evidence is even in principle available, say so explicitly and draw the consequence for the disagreement's status.

4. **A short assessment** of how much of the dispute is verbal, which parts are not, and whether the non-verbal residue is one disagreement or several that have been bundled together.

Constraints: write the document itself rather than a discussion of how to write it; no citations, no named philosophers, no reading list — I want formulations, not provenance; do not adjudicate in either side's favor beyond what the verbal-versus-substantive assessment requires; and where you must choose wording, prefer phrasing that a hostile partisan would find unfair to their own side rather than to the other's.

End with exactly one line in this form:

Crux: <short phrase>

naming the single crux whose settlement would do the most to resolve the disagreement.
