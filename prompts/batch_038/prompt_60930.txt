I have been invited to give the closing address — eight to ten minutes, so roughly 1,200 words — at next month's swearing-in of this year's cohort of deputy sheriffs in a county of some 400,000 people. The room will be mixed: the forty-two deputies themselves (several with prior military service, and at least one who has already confided to the training commander that he does not much care for professors), their spouses and parents, the sheriff and his command staff, a county commissioner, and the academy chaplain, who has asked only that I not be hostile to the profession. The oath is administered immediately before I speak, so the whole cohort will have just said aloud, in unison, that they will support the constitution and faithfully discharge their duties.

I want the address built on Pascal's fragment on justice and force — in one translation, "not being able to make what is just, strong, one made what is strong, just" — and on a distinction one of my graduate students pressed on me last term, which I want the speech to make load-bearing rather than merely allude to. She distinguishes force that is *constitutive* of a right (absent someone able to act, the right is not yet a right but only a wish) from force that is merely *instrumental* to a right whose content is already determinate. Pascal's diagnosis, on her reading, is that the second sort is perpetually in danger of passing itself off as the first: because justice is disputable and force is not, we slide into treating the settled fact of force as settling the question of justice. Use that distinction to say something true and non-obvious to people who are about to hold force, without collapsing into either congratulation or accusation, and without asking them to assent to anything I would regard as false. Pascal should not be named before the third paragraph and should be introduced through a concrete scene rather than a citation; no Scripture, the room being religiously mixed; no reference to any particular local case or shooting.
