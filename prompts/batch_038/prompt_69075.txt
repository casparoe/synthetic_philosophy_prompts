I'm in the second year of a history-of-philosophy PhD, and I've just come from a fintech seminar in which the speaker recruited Berkeley's *Querist* for Bitcoin. The argument ran roughly as follows: Berkeley had already shown in the 1730s that money is not itself wealth but a token whose only job is to keep industry moving; paper notes issued by a national bank could do that job better than gold or silver, since their value comes from use and acceptance rather than from the metal; therefore there is no difference in kind between those notes and a cryptocurrency, and the objection that Bitcoin has nothing behind it was answered three centuries ago.

I'd like an audit of that analogy. Begin with the correspondence table: what plays the token, the issuer, the security, the demand for it, and the circulation that is supposed to stir up industry. Then separate the disanalogies that actually threaten the conclusion from the merely decorative ones — my suspicion is that Berkeley's treatment of money as a public instrument aimed at employing idle labor, and his impatience with hoarding, are carrying weight the speaker silently dropped. Say also whether reading the *Querist* through the sign theory of *Alciphron* rescues or sinks the comparison, and whether a chartalist reading makes Berkeley an ally of the crypto claim or its opponent. Finally: does the conclusion survive? If not, tell me what Berkeley's account would predict about a token that is chiefly held rather than spent, and propose a better analogy if one exists.
