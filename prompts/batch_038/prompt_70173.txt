I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, so forgive me if this turns out to be a dumb question.

My team runs an internal coding agent. It triages bug reports, writes tests, opens PRs against our monorepo. Here's the setup we landed on, and I honestly can't tell anymore which parts are principled and which ones just accreted:

- every run gets a brand-new container. No persistent memory whatsoever — anything it knows it re-reads from the ticket and the repo each time.
- it can read everything, write nothing. Output is a draft PR. A human has to approve it before it merges.
- no network egress except to our own internal APIs.
- hard kill at 30 minutes, mid-thought if need be.
- it never decides to do anything. Every run starts from a job a human queued up.

But at the same time: the bot has a name and an avatar. It has an account with a two-year contribution history and a reputation. People say things like "Atlas has been sloppy lately" and "don't let Atlas anywhere near the auth code." We review its PRs exactly the way we'd review a junior hire's, we leave it comments, and we track its accept rate over time like it's a performance record that belongs to somebody.

And here's the part that actually bugs me. The human approval gate sits at the merge. But merging to main isn't the irreversible step — we deploy to prod automatically off main, hundreds of times a day, no approval, way bigger blast radius. So we put the human checkpoint on the most reversible action in the chain and let the least reversible one run unattended. A human eyeballing the agent's diff tells you who wrote the change. It doesn't really tell you what the change will do.

So: what theory of agency does this whole setup presuppose? We describe the thing in agentic language the whole time ("the agent decided to…", "the agent got confused") while engineering out basically every feature I'd associate with being an agent — no memory, no self-originated goals, no persistence, no power to make anything irreversable happen. Is that just paranoid risk management, or does it commit us to something — like, that agency is a grab bag of independent switches (persistence, goal-origination, irreversibility, external effect) that you can toggle off one at a time and still legitimately call the result an agent? Which rival accounts of what an agent is does the design quietly rule out?

Same question for the identity stuff. We've given it a social identity — a name, a reputation, a track record — while denying it every psychological one. Does that mean we're implicitly committed to something like: who an agent is, is a bookkeeping and attribution fact, not a fact about continuity of memory or character? Is that coherent, or are the two halves of our practice pulling against each other?

And the merge-versus-deploy thing: what view of responsibility puts the checkpoint at authorship instead of at consequence? Is that view actually defensible, or is it just an artifact of the fact that PR review tooling existed before anybody on the team thought about the question?

Two things I want to be careful about. First, please separate what the practice strictly forces on us from what it merely fits comfortably with — my suspicion is that a lot of this is underdetermined and several totally different theories would license exactly the same container config. Second, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would push you to a different verdict, and where in here your reasoning is most likely to fail. I don't want a confident-sounding answer that's secretly a coin flip.

Also, at the end, say what would have to change in the setup if we swapped the presupposition out — e.g. if we took seriously that agency requires persistence across time, or that responsibility attaches at the point of irreversible effect rather than at authorship. What breaks, what do we have to build, what do we have to give up?
