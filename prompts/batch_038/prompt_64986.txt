Write an original short story of 1,800 to 2,400 words on the socialist calculation debate, and in particular on the market-socialist reply to it: the proposal that a central planning board could simulate a competitive market for capital goods by acting as a Walrasian auctioneer — publishing trial prices for producer goods, collecting returns from enterprises reporting surplus or shortage at those prices, revising prices upward where excess demand was reported and downward where excess supply was reported, and iterating until the plan balanced.

Observe the following constraints, which exist to push the story away from the shapes this subject usually takes.

Setting. Invent an unnamed country and an unnamed planning directorate. Do not use the Soviet Union, Gosplan, Cuba, Chile, or any real agency; do not date the story; do not have any real economist, statesman, or planner appear in it.

Forbidden scenes. No scene may consist of two intellectuals debating whether central planning can work. No outside observer — a visiting journalist, a defecting professor, a curious grandchild, a Western trade delegate — may be used as a device for explaining the system to the reader. Nobody may deliver a speech about knowledge, prices, or freedom. The argument has to be carried by procedure, paperwork, and consequence.

Required point of view. Focalize the story through the person whose job is to run the iteration: someone who sets the trial prices, reads the returns, signs the revision sheet, and gradually comes to suspect that her algorithm is converging on the solution to a problem the economy stopped posing some time ago.

Required irony. The iteration must work. Round by round the reported imbalances shrink, the price vector settles, the balance closes, and her superiors are pleased. What fails is the world that the fixed point describes. Do not write a crash, a famine, a purge, or a queue that turns into a riot; make the successful convergence itself the catastrophe.

Three mechanisms, each to be dramatized rather than explained:

1. Testimony instead of scarcity. Because an enterprise that reveals spare capacity this quarter is rewarded with a harder target next quarter, managers understate what they can produce and overstate what they need. The auctioneer is therefore adjusting prices in response to what enterprises say, not to what is scarce. Show the returns arriving, and show her reading them.

2. Prices with no teeth. Because the state bank covers losses and reissues credit, no enterprise that ignores a price signal can be punished for ignoring it. Show what a price is worth when nobody who disregards it can fail.

3. The uncoded particular. Include one specific, transient piece of local knowledge that no statistical return can carry — a seam of ore that satisfies the grade specification on the assay form but behaves badly in the furnace this month, a washed-out spur line, a winding drum that a foreman can hear going and no inspector's checklist records — and let the reader see both what a genuine market price would have compressed into a single number and what the planning form leaves blank instead.

Additional requirements. Track one commodity through at least three successive stages — extraction, intermediate processing, and a final use — so that the mis-coordination becomes visible differently at each stage. Include at least one piece of bureaucratic prose rendered inside the story: a return slip, a price-revision table, the wording of a line item on a form.

Deliver your response in exactly two sections, in this order:

1. The Story — your chosen title, followed by 1,800 to 2,400 words of fiction.

2. Craft Notes — approximately 300 words. Identify the passage in which each of the three mechanisms appears and name the device you used to convey it without exposition. Then state plainly which side of the calculation debate your story ends up endorsing, and give your own assessment of whether that verdict is earned by the fiction or merely asserted by it — including one place where you suspect you loaded the dice against the planners.
