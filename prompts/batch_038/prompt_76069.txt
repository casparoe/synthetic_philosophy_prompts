Something that bugs me about the epistocracy debate is that it's conducted almost entirely about imaginary institutions — somebody invents a council of the well-informed, and then we argue about whether we could ever identify who belongs on it. But Britain actually ran something like one, for real, for three centuries, and then dismantled it. I want you to work through that case.

The facts as I understand them (tell me if you think I've got any of it wrong):

- Until the Representation of the People Act 1948, which took effect at the 1950 general election, lots of British voters voted twice. Two main sources: university constituencies — graduates of Oxford, Cambridge, London, Wales, Queen's Belfast and the combined English and Scottish university groupings could vote for a total of 12 university MPs on top of their home seat — and the business-premises vote, which let you vote where you occupied qualifying commercial property as well as where you lived. Graduates were something like 7% of the electorate.
- The 1918 Act capped it at two votes per person. Before that it was genuinely open-ended: there are 1890s parliamentary accounts of one MP casually holding five votes, of a man boasting of thirteen, and of an Oxford tutor who voted eighteen times at the 1874 election.
- Before the late-19th-century reforms, property franchises were manufactured wholesale. Landowners subdivided a property on paper and handed the titles to dependents so they could register and vote as instructed. The qualification was purchasable, and sometimes conveyed back again the day after the poll.
- The university seats were mostly Conservative, but increasingly returned Independents in their last decades. Labour tried to abolish them in 1930 and lost the vote in the Commons; the 1948 abolition was a Labour government measure.
- And as far as I know the business vote never fully died — the City of London still lets companies appoint voters in local ward elections.

Now run it through the epistocracy-vs-democracy literature, in both directions.

First, what actually is this institution? My suspicion is that it isnt what modern epistocrats imagine. The university seats began under James I on something like "the universities are effected by Parliament's decisions, so they ought to be represented in it" — a stakeholder or corporate-interest theory, not a competence-weighting one — and only later picked up Mill-flavoured justifications about educated judgement. Is that right, and does the difference matter? Should this even be scored as an epistocratic experiment?

Second, the theory. Take Brennan's competence principle (that it's presumptively wrong to subject people to the decisions of incompetent or unjust decision-making processes) and Estlund's rejoinder, that legitimation has to be acceptable to all qualified points of view, which is supposed to sink epistocracy because any test of who counts as competent is itself reasonably rejectable. Add the demographic objection: that in any real system the "competent" turn out to be disproportionately propertied, educated, male, and the competence rationale ends up laundering a sectional advantage. For each, say what it predicts about the British plural franchise, and then check the prediction against what happened.

Third, the reckoning. Which features of the case does the epistocratic story actually explain, and where does the democratic story do better? Is the 1948 abolition evidence that such institutions fail because they're bad at producing good decisions, or because they can't survive their own legitimacy deficit even when they aren't bad, or because they were never about competence to begin with? And what does the City of London remnant do to the argument — a live plural vote that nobody on either side bothers to treat as epistocracy?

Don't make the theories speak where they're silent; I'd much rather read "the jury theorem has nothing to say about this" than a stretch. And no reading list at the end.
