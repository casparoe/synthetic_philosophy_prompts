You are a historian of analytic philosophy with an equally firm grip on Frege's technical work and on the methodological literature about how an author's non-philosophical writings bear on the interpretation of their philosophy. Before writing, think through the structure of the disagreement and begin your response with four or five bullet points naming the strongest consideration available to each side. Then write a dialogue of roughly 1,500 words between two philosophers.

Background you may rely on, and should not go beyond: Frege kept a private political diary in the spring of 1924, the year before his death. It was unpublished in his lifetime, first appeared in German in 1994, and in English in 1996. As widely reported, its contents include virulent anti-Semitism, contempt for parliamentary democracy and for the Weimar Republic, nostalgia for monarchy, and a longing for a strong ruler, set alongside recurrent complaints about the decline of intellectual life. None of this appears in his published philosophy, and the diary's discovery surprised scholars who had spent careers on his work.

The two characters:

- An **internalist**, who holds that the diary is philosophically inert: Frege's arguments about sense and reference, about the objectivity of thoughts in a third realm, and about logicism stand or fall on their merits, and treating a man's private political convictions as evidence about his doctrines commits a genetic fallacy while licensing a habit of suspicion that would gut the history of philosophy.
- A **contextualist**, who holds that the diary is load-bearing: not because it refutes anything, but because Frege's Platonism shares a *structure* with his authoritarianism — objective thoughts that command assent, and a hierarchy between those capable of genuine insight and those merely capable of holding something to be true. A philosophy that insists truth is apprehended only by the properly disciplined mind cannot shrug about who gets excluded from that discipline, or about what happens when that discipline is imagined in ethnic and political terms.

Requirements:

1. Neither character may be a strawman, and both must answer the other's actual moves rather than restate their own position at greater length.
2. Keep three claims rigorously apart, and have the characters force each other to say which one is in play at any given moment: (a) the diary changes how we should *read* Frege's doctrines; (b) the diary counts as evidence bearing on whether any of those doctrines are *true*, or on how much confidence they deserve; (c) the diary changes how we should *teach and cite* Frege while leaving (a) and (b) untouched. Each character should concede some of these and explain why conceding them costs little.
3. At least one substantive exchange must exploit Frege's own distinctions — above all the separation of grasping a thought from holding it to be true from acknowledging it as known — and the contextualist must not simply fold in the face of it.
4. Include a real disagreement about whether Heidegger is the right comparison: does a publicly argued political commitment that is woven into the philosophy itself make the Frege case easier or harder?
5. Put no words in Frege's mouth and quote nothing — not the diary, not the published works, not any secondary source. Paraphrase only, and explicitly flag any place where you are reconstructing rather than reporting.
6. Close with each character stating the strongest thing the other has said, and the specific argument or piece of evidence that would change their mind.
