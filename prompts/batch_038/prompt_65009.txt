I write novels for a living, and I have come to metaphysics late and from the wrong end — by way of a sculptor's studio rather than a library. I would like an essay on a controversy in the metaphysics of material constitution, but I want it framed around a sequence of cases I have not been able to find in the literature, chiefly because the literature's favourite statue is made of clay, and clay is never destroyed in the making of the thing it becomes.

Let me describe the studio.

My friend Ilse works in bronze by the indirect lost-wax method. She models a hare, thirty centimetres tall, in plaster. A piece mould is taken from the plaster; wax is pressed into the mould to make a wax replica; the replica is invested in refractory ceramic; the wax is melted out and lost; bronze is poured into the cavity. The wax hare and the bronze hare therefore never exist at the same time. The wax is destroyed in precisely the interval at which the bronze begins. And yet when I stood beside her as the shell was cracked open, I did not think: a wax hare has been destroyed, and a bronze hare has been made out of its remains. I thought: the hare has been through something.

From the same plaster mould she took a second wax replica and cast a second bronze hare — dimensionally identical to the first, perceptually indistinguishable from it, differing only where the chasing and the patina were worked by hand. She numbered them. Both went to the same gallery under the same title, were entered in the same records, insured under the same policy, photographed beneath the same wall label.

Then there was a fire in the gallery's storage room. One of the two hares — the one on the left-hand plinth, by the window — was melted into a slumped blob. The other survived unharmed. Nobody who was absent can now say which is which: the plinths burned, and the gallery's records never distinguished them beyond those plinth numbers. There is a fact of the matter, but it is a fact that no description of the surviving object can recover.

Six months later Ilse did something I have not stopped thinking about. She melted down the surviving hare. From the same plaster mould, still on the studio shelf, she took a fresh wax replica and cast it. The result is, by every measure she and I can apply, the hare she made thirty years ago: same mould, same hand, same alloy supplier, same chasing tools, same patina recipe, same stamp in the same place on the underside. She calls it a reprise — her word, not a term of art — and she insists it is the hare rather than a copy of the hare. Her confidence does not look to me like confusion. Separately, she melted the fire-blob and cast it, from a different mould, as a paperweight.

Here is the asymmetry I cannot resolve, and I want your essay to resolve it or to explain why it cannot be resolved:

- The wax replica was destroyed when the bronze hare came to be, although its matter persisted and although the shape was held throughout in the mould.
- The surviving hare, when melted, was equally destroyed, although its matter persisted and although the shape was held throughout in the same mould.
- Yet the reprise, made from that matter by that hand from that mould, is either a mere copy or the very hare of thirty years ago, and nothing in the physical description of the three episodes tells me which.

Please treat this as a live dispute rather than a curiosity. Weigh at least the following families of answer against one another: (a) that constitution is not identity, so the lump and the artefact are distinct coinciding things and all that ever survives my episodes is the lump; (b) that there is only one object here, and persistence conditions are relative to the sortal under which we track it, so my questions are underdetermined until I say what I mean by "the hare"; (c) that an artefact is matter organised by a form, so the reprise is a genuine third thing — neither identical to the original nor a mere replica of it. Bring in whatever else bears on the case: dominant kinds, four-dimensionalist or stage-theoretic treatments, the proposal that artefact identity is fixed by the maker's intentions or by the causal history running back through the mould, or the proposal that what does the tracking work in my story is not the world at all but the gallery's catalogue.

I am not asking for a survey, and I am not asking for sources or attributions — I have no way of checking them and I would rather you did not guess. I want a verdict. Tell me whether the reprise is identical with the original hare, and tell me what, on your account, makes the wax's fate different from the bronze's when the physical descriptions match.

Your account should also settle a variant I have withheld. Suppose Ilse had cast the reprise not from the surviving hare's bronze but from the fire-blob's bronze: same mould, same hand, different matter. Say what she would then have, and say which ingredient your view makes load-bearing — the matter, the mould, the maker's intention, or the institutional record.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which step in my sequence could I add, remove, or reorder to push you to a different verdict? And where do you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail: which single premise would you least like to have to defend in front of someone who disagrees with you?
