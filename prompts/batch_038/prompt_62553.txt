I am convening a graduate seminar on judgement under uncertainty, and before we turn to the forecasting literature I want a specification rather than a survey: what must an account of when the inside view has to yield to the outside view deliver in order to count as adequate? Fix on a practical purpose — a competent non-specialist deciding whether a three-year research programme will finish on schedule — and treat actionability as a candidate desideratum to be tested rather than assumed.

Set out the requirements, giving for each the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than the preference of one camp, and mark which are non-negotiable. At minimum the list should cover: genuinely one-off decisions, where no relevant frequency exists; agents whose case-specific information is real evidence rather than optimism; situations in which informed insiders systematically do worse than a base rate; the fact that any event sits in indefinitely many reference classes with divergent base rates, so that taking the outside view is incomplete without a rule for choosing among them; and predictions whose adoption would alter the very statistics on which they rest. Separate the requirements bearing on accuracy from those bearing on use — calibration from resolution, a reference class being available from its being the right one, deferring to base rates as a heuristic from deferring to them as a norm.

Then the part I most want: identify any subset of these requirements you judge jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect that fixing the reference class in advance, to block motivated class-shopping, cannot be reconciled with genuine responsiveness to inside information; argue the point rather than grant it. Finish by naming the requirement that eliminates the most contenders, and say in two sentences how a strict base-rate view and a view that always privileges case-specific knowledge fare against it. Please write in British English.
