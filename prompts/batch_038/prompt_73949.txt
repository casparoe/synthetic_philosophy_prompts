I've spent way too long this year arguing about the Fermi paradox on a forum, and somewhere around month three I noticed a shape in the arguments that I keep coming back to and I can't find a word for it. It goes like this. Someone says the empty sky is strong evidence that intelligent life is vanishingly rare, because if it were common we'd have noticed something by now after six-odd decades of listening. Fine, that actually seems right to me. But then a second person says we've only covered a tiny patch of sky and we've only really looked for radio, and anyone advanced would use tight-beam lasers or something we can't pick up. And the first person says: exactly, and that's *more* reason to think they're few, because we've now also ruled out the loud ones. Then the infrared surveys come back with no waste-heat signatures, and nobody has ever found a probe drifting through the solar system, and each of those empty results goes onto the same pile as fresh confirmation of the rarity claim. Five separate failed searches later the claim has absorbed all of them and come out stronger every single time, and it hasn't lost anything anywhere along the way. Meanwhile the estimate of *how* rare keeps getting more extreme rather than more precise, and there's always one more reason we wouldn't have seen them. My partner thinks I'm being pedantic, but what bugs me is that I can't tell whether this is legitimate or a trick, because I half agree with both sides — absence of evidence really is evidence of absence in some sense, isn't it?

Here's the comparison thats confusing me. When the LHC doesn't find some particle in a mass range, everybody counts that as real progress: a chunk of parameter space gets crossed off, and if you keep crossing off chunks the hypothesis eventually dies. That's also a null result and it also updates. But it updates by shrinking. The alien-rarity one never shrinks, it just gets bolder, and the failed search seems to feed it instead of costing it. Same surface structure, opposite behaviour underneath, and I can't articulate the difference. I don't think it's just ad hocness or "moving the goalposts" either, because nobody is inventing a rescue after the fact for a claim that's already been knocked down — each step on its own is a fair point about how hard detection actually is. Its only when you line six of them up that you notice the hypothesis has become impossible to dent and people are scoring that as a win.

So, is there an established concept for this thing, in philosophy of science or wherever? I've got fragments in my head — ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses, self-sealing arguments, unfalsifiability, the ravens paradox business where non-black non-ravens somehow count as confirmation, observation selection effects — and I can see why each is in the neighbourhood, but none of them seem to name what I'm actually describing, which is a hypothesis that gets fed *only* by null results and gets more extreme rather than narrower each time it's fed. Tell me which of the neighbours genuinely fits and why each of the others doesn't, and then tell me where you'd draw the line: does the LHC case count, does "we haven't found the wreck so it must have gone down in very deep water" count, does a doctor saying "no new symptoms, which is exactly what we'd expect from something this slow" count? And if there really is no name for it, coin one and define it properly — I want the boundary conditions and the cases that sit right on the edge, not just a label I can throw at people in the thread. You don't need to point me at any papers, I have no access and honestly wouldn't read them, I just want the idea carved out.
