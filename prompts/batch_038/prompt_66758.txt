Something's been bugging me about how cryptography actually gets done in practice, and I want to work out what it's committed to philosophically before I trust any of it.

Here's the setup as I understand it. We have a few primitives that are provably unbreakable, full stop — the one-time pad, Shamir's secret sharing, that sort of thing. Nobody uses them for anything except very special cases. Instead basically all real security — TLS, signing, banking, messaging apps — rests on problems we cannot prove are hard: factoring, discrete logs, and for the symmetric side, the hope that nobody finds a shortcut against AES. The "security proofs" people write are reductions: break the scheme and you can factor. But nobody can prove factoring is hard, and the general expectation is that we probably can't, at least not in any proof system strong enough to be interesting.

Meanwhile the same community has norms that sound like the opposite epistemology entirely: don't roll your own crypto, submit a design to years of open cryptanalysis before deploying it, formally verify the implementations, and treat a single working attack as fatal no matter how obscure. And key sizes get chosen by standards bodies — 128 bits here, 3072 bits there — with the justification being estimates of the running time of the best currently known algorithms plus a fat safety margin, which is an engineering judgement rather then a theorem.

So what theory of knowledge does this practice presuppose? A few things I want dealt with specifically:

1. The assumption is universal over an unbounded class — no efficient algorithm exists, for any adversary, including ones nobody will ever write down — but its a universal claim about an unbounded class — but the only evidence offered is that very clever people have failed. Does the practice presuppose an inference from absence of counterexamples to something law-like? And why does that inference feel legitimate here when it would be scandalous in, say, number theory?

2. There's a weird asymmetry: one algorithm refutes the assumption conclusively, and no amount of failed attacks confirms it. Some people talk about complexity assumptions as though they were empirical, more like a thermodynamic law than a theorem — is that what the practice is actually doing, or something else? Note that Shor's algorithm showed the hardness is contingent on what physics the attacker has available, which seems to make the empirical reading both better and worse at the same time. Say which, and why.

3. Why isn't provable security wanted? The one-time pad is provable and useless at scale, because the key has to be as long as the message, used once, and delivered over a channel that is already secure. That suggests the practice presupposes logistics is the binding constraint and that mathematical hardness is a cheap stand-in for a physically secure channel. Is that a claim about knowledge or just about cost? And can more than one theory license it — i.e. where exactly is the practice philosophically underdetermined, such that a realist about complexity classes and someone who thinks hardness is just a sociological fact about our current algorithmic toolkit would both be perfectly happy with the identical deployed systems?

4. Is the whole thing internally consistent at all — the "demand proof" norms sitting on top of an admittedly unprovable foundation — or does it only look inconsistent because "proof" is doing two different jobs at the two levels?

Then the counterfactual: what would have to change, in practice and in how the field describes itself, if we adopted a strictly information-theoretic stance and refused to trust anything that isn't unconditionally secure? Do we end up with quantum key distribution everywhere and enormous key-management overhead, or do we discover that the thing we were really leaning on was never hardness but something else — trust in a community, say, or in the slowness of publication?

Please answer in British English. Be concrete about the practice itself, and keep separating what it strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with.
