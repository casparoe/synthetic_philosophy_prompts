I am designing the opening exercise for a one-day workshop whose attendees are mostly cognitive scientists and HCI researchers, with a few philosophers in the room; nobody should need to know the literature in order to be sorted correctly by it. The deliverable is a decision tree that carries a participant from their intuitions about particular artifacts to a named position in the debate over the extended mind. Please build it in three layers and present them in this order.

**Layer one: the logical space.** Before naming any position, lay out the discriminating questions as axes and indicate which combinations of answers the axes jointly permit. The axes I want represented are: whether a process's being cognitive depends in part on where it happens rather than only on what it does; whether the extension claim is a metaphysical claim about constitution or a methodological claim about how best to carve up systems for explanatory purposes; whether tight causal coupling, however reliable and however much the agent leans on it, could ever amount to constitution; whether what extends is cognition proper or only states we would ordinarily call emotional or mnemonic; whether the relevant condition is a synchronic snapshot of coupling or a history of trust, endorsement, and skillful incorporation; whether the unit that extends is an individual or a group; and whether heavy environmental scaffolding is better described as a mind reaching outward or as a mind built from the outside in. Every axis must be answerable by someone who has never encountered the debate: phrase each as a concrete case or as a claim about a case, with two or three mutually exclusive options, and never as a question about labels or allegiances.

**Layer two: the tree and its leaves.** Nine to eleven questions, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. Mark which questions are load-bearing, where a single answer does most of the determining, and which are tie-breakers, and say what the instrument should report for a participant whose pattern matches no leaf or matches several at once. The tree must deliver at least the following leaves:

1. Parity-based active externalism: the artifact is literally part of the cognitive system in virtue of the work it does, on pain of an arbitrary double standard.
2. Second-wave extension: constitution requires continuous, bidirectional interaction and skillful incorporation, not a one-off retrieval from an external store.
3. Integration-and-endorsement extension: the artifact belongs to the cognitive system only insofar as the agent has standing policies of revising it, holding it answerable to their other commitments, and treating it as theirs.
4. Extended affect, contained cognition: moods, emotions, and memory-related states extend into artifacts and other people while cognition proper does not.
5. Constitution granted, autonomy lost: the artifact really is part of the cognitive system, and that is precisely why the agent's credit, responsibility, or epistemic standing is diminished rather than enhanced.
6. Vehicle extension without content extension: the processing is partly outside the skull while what it carries need not be wide.
7. The embedded view: the environment is indispensable to explaining cognition, but the boundaries of the cognitive system do not move.
8. Scaffolded construction: scaffolding builds the mind over learning, development, and evolution, so "extension" mis-describes the direction of dependence.
9. Mark-of-the-cognitive internalism: cognition has internal markers or a signature profile, so no external process could be cognitive however it functions.
10. Coupling-constitution internalism: the inference from causal coupling to constitutive membership is a fallacy, whatever one says about the functional profile of the coupled process.
11. Distributed cognition: the individual does not extend; the team does, and the right unit of analysis is the group-level system.
12. Methodological extensionism: the metaphysical dispute is idle, and the claim worth having is only that certain explanations run better across the boundary.

**Layer three: diagnostics.** Identify at least three combinations of answers that the axes permit but that no one occupies, or that a single author occupies only in passing — for instance, accepting the parity principle as a rule of inference while holding that no actual artifact satisfies the conditions of availability, trust, and endorsement that it presupposes; or granting constitution across the boundary while denying that any of what crosses it is cognitive. For each empty cell, say in one sentence whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is theoretically available but unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored. Then flag at least two pairs of leaves your questions cannot reliably separate — I expect the two internalisms, and the embedded versus scaffolded pair, to be candidates — and say what additional question would be needed to tell them apart.

**Anchor cases.** Use these, and only these, as the material for the questions: a researcher whose entire working memory lives in a notebook she consults constantly and could not reconstruct without; a generative assistant that drafts and revises a user's arguments, remembers their projects, and whose output the user endorses without checking; a navigation app the driver follows without cross-checking against anything; a design team's shared wall of sketches and notes, where no single member holds the whole design; a jazz musician's instrument during improvisation; and a cochlear implant, included as a foil so that participants must say why it differs from the notebook, if it does. Administer the tree twice, once anchored on the notebook and once on the assistant, and close with a short section interpreting divergence: what we should make of a participant who lands on literal extension in one pass and on the embedded view in the other, and whether that spread is evidence about the two cases or about the axes themselves.

**Constraints.** No bibliography, no reading list, no page references, and no quoted passages. Where a position has a standard name, use it and describe it; where it has none, say so and coin one. Each leaf carries exactly one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defender least wants to be asked about.
