I teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar: eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, a room with a projector and whiteboard, and a budget limited to paper and pens. Design an activity that makes students feel the force of heterophenomenology as a method rather than as a thesis — the practice of taking a subject's verbal reports and other behavior as data for constructing a theorist's model of the world as that subject takes it to be, while refusing the subject any special authority about that world, and holding that once everything in the constructed world and its functional role has been explained, no further fact about experience remains to be explained. I want them to run into the pressure of that method from the inside: to experience what it is like to have one's own avowals treated as evidence about something other than how things are with one, and to notice what the treatment brings into view and what it appears to discard.

Give me roles and rules, including a rotation under which no student ever interprets their own reports; the materials and stimulus setup; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps particular moments of the exercise onto particular methodological commitments. Build in one deliberate feature: the corpus of reports the class interprets should secretly include items produced by something we would not ordinarily count as a subject at all — a scripted actor or a text-generating program — which the class should be unable to identify. Explain what that reveal is meant to establish and what it cannot establish. In particular, I suspect that the protests of students whose authority has just been denied are themselves only more data of the same kind, so the exercise cannot settle whether the method is neutral between realism and eliminativism about experience and that I will have to supply that argument myself; tell me whether that suspicion is correct. Close with what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure.
