Last night I got into a two-hour argument with a friend of mine — she models flood risk for a regional water authority, and I teach a graduate seminar on the semantics of conditionals — and we ended up somewhere neither of us could climb back out of. We both accept the familiar datum: the divergence Adams drew attention to in 1970, where an agent cheerfully asserts the indicative about who shot the president while rejecting the matching subjunctive about who would have shot him had the actual shooter not done so. We also both accept, at least for the indicative, something in the neighbourhood of Adams's thesis — that a rational agent's credence in "if A, B" tracks her conditional credence in B given A, so that accepting an indicative is a matter of running the Ramsey test on one's own probabilities. What we could not settle is whether the divergence can run the other way. Every example either of us could produce, including the ones we dredged up from teaching notes, runs in the same direction: high credence in the indicative, low credence in the counterfactual. My friend's suspicion, which she could not defend but which she also could not shake, is that this asymmetry is not an accident of which examples happen to be memorable, but reflects something structural about how the two moods interact with evidence.

So here is what I would like you to do: construct a fully worked example of a single, ideally rational, non-confused agent who assigns high credence to a counterfactual of the form "if A were the case, B would be the case" while assigning low credence to the indicative "if A is the case, B will be the case," where A and B have exactly the same content in both sentences and differ only in mood. The example should be concrete and quantitative — give me the agent's situation, her evidence, and rough probability assignments — and it should satisfy three further constraints that we found surprisingly hard to satisfy simultaneously. First, the antecedent must be one she regards as unlikely but genuinely live, with a non-negligible probability attached to it, so that the case cannot be dismissed on the grounds that indicatives are simply infelicitous when the speaker believes the antecedent false. Second, the divergence should arise from the evidential structure of her situation rather than from any confusion on her part: roughly, A's obtaining should be strongly diagnostic of some background condition that, if it obtained, would defeat B, so that conditionalizing on A drags that background condition along with it, while the non-backtracking counterfactual holds the actual background fixed and lets B follow. Third, please do not reach for the stock furniture — no assassinations, no matches being struck, no dry matches, no lottery tickets, no coins.

Then tell me what you think the example costs. Which principle has to give way: the thesis that indicative credence is conditional credence; the idea that subjunctive credence is better modelled by imaging on the antecedent than by conditionalizing on it; the assumption that one and the same similarity ordering can serve both readings of a counterfactual; or the assumption that the two moods ever express the same content under different epistemic circumstances? I would also like your view on two objections my friend raised: that any such case is really just an agent equivocating between a backtracking and a non-backtracking reading of a single conditional, and that the whole thing is verbal, since no ordinary speaker would utter both sentences in the same breath. Finally, spell out the practical upshot in your example — if the agent has to decide tonight whether to pre-position sandbags downstream, which of her two conditional attitudes should govern the decision, and why — because that is where my friend and I actually fell out. I am not asking for a survey or for references; work from the conceptual resources themselves and make the case stand on its own.
