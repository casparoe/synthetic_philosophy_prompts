I've spent eleven years writing backend and infrastructure code — deploys, schema migrations, config management, consensus systems, on-call runbooks — and zero time in a philosophy classroom. I came across the debate about whether an advance directive signed by a competent earlier self retains authority over a later, severely demented self, and I want it translated into the vocabulary I actually think in, plus an honest report of what the translation fails to carry.

Here is the debate as I have reconstructed it from secondary sources. Please correct me if I have garbled the attributions; I care about the structure of the disagreement more than about who said exactly what.

Position A: an advance directive expresses what Dworkin called the person's critical interests — settled values about how their life as a whole should go, its shape and integrity — as distinct from their experiential interests, which are about how it feels to be them right now. On this view, the directive can remain authoritative even when the patient no longer holds the values that generated it and even reports being content. The case usually discussed involves a woman with advanced dementia who is visibly cheerful and paints and says she is happy; the directive says do not treat the pneumonia. Position A says honor the directive, because what is at stake is the integrity of a life as a whole, not the hedonic balance of the current moment.

Position B: the objection that draws on Parfit's reductionism about personal identity. If what makes a person at t2 the same person as the one at t1 is a matter of degree of psychological continuity — memory, character, projects, the capacity to endorse values — and that continuity has largely dissolved, then the being in the bed is not the person who signed. The directive therefore has no subject left to bind; honoring it amounts to letting the dead dictate to a different living person.

Position C: the objection, associated with Dresser, that Position A's clean split between critical and experiential interests does not do the work asked of it, and that the demented patient is a welfare subject with current interests that are directly at stake. On this view the earlier self's directive deserves weight as evidence about what this human being cared about, but not the near-absolute authority Position A gives it.

Now the translation task. Render each position, one at a time, in my working vocabulary. I mean specifically: version pinning and config drift; immutable deploys versus in-place mutation; process identity and PID reuse versus identity established by a causal chain of state; forks, rewrites, and whether a rewritten service is the same service; leases and their expiry; deprecation and end-of-life policy; who holds write authority over a system and under what conditions that authority can be revoked or inherited; rollback; forward compatibility and the fact that an old spec cannot anticipate the runtime it will be executed in. Do not substitute some other framework — no economics, no game theory, no neurology — even where it would be easier. I want to know whether my own conceptual toolkit is sufficient, and it is only informative if you use it and no other.

Be scrupulous about not smuggling. Where one of my terms quietly carries an assumption that the original position does not have, say so explicitly rather than letting the mapping do unmarked work. For instance, if identity in my vocabulary is always stipulated by an operator or a supervisor process, whereas Position B treats identity as something that can be genuinely lost, that difference is exactly what I want surfaced, not papered over.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want:

1. For each of the three positions: which claims map cleanly onto my vocabulary, which map only with distortion — and for those, name the distortion precisely — and which have no expression in it at all.
2. Take a clear stand on at least two specific residues. First: welfare. Nothing in my vocabulary has anything like it being good or bad for a running process. Is that because my framework concerns artifacts with no interests, and the gap therefore tells us nothing about persons, or because it exposes something incoherent or at least unearned in the way all three positions trade in the notion of interests? Second: authority. In my world, write authority is granted and revoked by something outside the system, and a config file has no standing except what some enforcement mechanism gives it. Is the whole debate about precedent autonomy just a dispute over who the enforcing supervisor is, or is there a genuinely normative notion of standing that my vocabulary cannot represent?
3. Tell me whether the translation makes the disagreement look less substantive than it is. In particular, is Position A best understood as insisting that identity is stipulated, like a PID that persists across a complete rewrite, while Position B insists it must be earned, like a chain of hashes where any break means a different chain? If that is the right diagnosis, say whether it dissolves the dispute into a merely verbal one or merely relocates it.
4. Flag the tempting mappings that are traps — the ones that look apt and are rhetorically satisfying but change the subject. I suspect at least two of these: treating the directive as a lease that expires, and treating the demented patient as a fork of the earlier person. Say whether my suspicion is right, and find others.

Finally, an epistemic request. Don't be diplomatic and don't hedge by summarizing all three positions as reasonable. Commit to a verdict on each of the residue questions above. Then tell me what would change your mind: which argument, case, or consideration would make you revise the verdict about a given residue, and where in this answer your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong — whether that is an unfaithful translation, a term of art I supplied that you adopted uncritically, or a place where the analogy between artifacts and persons did real argumentative work that it cannot support.
