I have painted signs by hand for twenty-two years — shop fascias, pub menus, the gold leaf on a funeral director's window — and I have no academic training whatsoever. But I have a puzzle I cannot put down, and I would like you to turn it into fiction rather than explain it to me.

Here is the puzzle. I can paint an "a" with a closed bowl or a nearly open one, with a straight tail or a hooked one, fat or lean, upright or leaning, and every passerby reads it without a moment's hesitation. Yet if I move one stroke a millimetre out of place, the same customer will tell me it "isn't an a" and will not be able to say why. Meanwhile I can letter a whole fascia in someone else's hand — I do this for a restorer who needs old signs continued — and my customers say the new letters look exactly like the old ones, and yet every one of them is still perfectly readable as the letter it is meant to be. So there seem to be two things going at once: something fixed that makes a letter the letter it is, and something loose that makes a set of letters belong to one hand. I have never been able to say which one is doing the work when I judge a sign, and I suspect I do not actually have a rule in my head at all, only a practice. Years ago I read a paragraph somewhere about people trying to get a computer to grow an entire alphabet from a single sample letter, so that all the new letters would feel as though they came from the same hand; that idea has stuck with me.

Your task. Act as a literary short-story writer who also happens to know the craft of lettering and the philosophy of concepts intimately — someone who could write a story that a typesetter and a philosopher would both recognize as being about their own obsessions. Think carefully before you write; do not begin drafting until you have planned the five cases below, and show me the plan.

The story to write:

- Setting: an invented city, Sessine, which has passed an ordinance requiring all public signage to be lettered in one approved alphabet, published as a single specimen sheet. Compliance is adjudicated by an Inspector of Letterforms, whose rulings on disputed signs become precedent for later ones. Invent everything: the city's history, the ordinance, the inspector's name. Do not use real typeface names, real cities, real legislation, or real historical figures. The only real-world vocabulary I want used is the ordinary language of letter anatomy, and it must be used correctly.
- Protagonist: the inspector, who was a sign painter before taking the post, so that they spend thirty years ruling against people who do what they used to do.
- Structure: five cases spread across roughly thirty years, each visibly shorter than the last — about 600 words for the first, tapering to about 150 for the fifth. Total length 1,400 to 1,900 words. Head each case with the year and a short description of the disputed sign.
- Required hinge: at least one ruling must turn on a single named anatomical feature of a letterform, and the reader must be able to see why that feature is genuinely borderline rather than simply decisive. If you are not certain a term means what you think it means, pick a different one.
- Required conflict: the first ruling and the last must pull in opposite directions, and the inspector must be able to defend both while stating the same principle on both occasions. Do not resolve this by revealing that the principle was secretly a different one all along. The point I want made is that the principle does not settle the cases; the cases settle it, afterwards.
- One case must concern a sign the inspector painted in youth, now weathered, brought before them by a shopkeeper who is proud of it.
- The specimen sheet must exist as a physical object in the story, and by the end it must no longer be the thing that was published.
- At least one scene must be about the work itself — mixing paint, cutting a stencil, the drag of a brush — so the reader is in a workshop and not a seminar.

Constraints on execution: no character may state a thesis. Ban the words "essence," "definition," "category," and "family resemblance" from all dialogue and narration; ban the puns about the letter and the spirit of the law entirely; ban the name of any philosopher; and end on an image, not a moral. The story must work for a reader who has never heard of any of these debates.

Output, in this order:

1. A planning section of no more than 300 words: the inspector's stated principle in one sentence; the five cases and what makes each hard; which anatomical feature the hinge case turns on; and precisely how the first and last rulings conflict while the principle stays fixed.
2. The story.
3. An afterword of no more than 200 words in which you describe, in your own words and without attaching labels or names to anybody, what general account of "what makes this letter that letter" each of the five rulings leans on — a list of required features, resemblance to the paradigm cases on the sheet, the settled practice of competent readers, the intention of the hand that made it, the history of how the form has been handed down — and say which of those accounts the story leaves looking weakest, and why.

Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations; I want your reasoning, not your sources.
