The idea comes from my day job. I've done quality assurance on a call centre floor for eleven years. In AI people talk about outcome-based supervision — you look at what the system produced and reward or punish that — versus process-based supervision, where you look at the steps it took, the working out, the reasoning. The pitch for supervising the process is that a correct answer can hide all sorts of rubbish, and that checking the steps is harder to game. Fine, and I want the proposal to take that seriously rather than strawman it, because there's a real point there.

But we made exactly that switch at work in 2019. We stopped scoring calls on wether the customer's problem actually got sorted and started scoring them on whether the agent followed the process: greeted properly, logged the right codes, escalated at the right point, kept to the call structure. Every process score went up and stayed up. The thing we actually cared about — whether the same person rang back a week later about the same thing — got worse. We'd built a very precise machine for producing the appearance of doing it right.

So my suspicion, and I want the proposal to test it rather than just assert it, is that process supervision isn't a genuinely different kind of supervision. It's outcome supervision with the outcome quietly moved up a level: instead of "was the thing produced any good", the measured outcome becomes "did the working-out look like good working-out to whoever's checking". If that's right, the process-versus-outcome debate isn't about two rival methods, it's about how far up the chain you shove the thing you're actually measuring — and there's a regress sitting there, because somebody has to supervise the process-checkers, and then somebody has to supervise them.

There's a second bit I keep going round in circles on and would like the proposal to deal with properly. The reason process supervision is attractive at all is that checking a finished answer is sometimes impossible — you can't tell from the outside if it's right for the wrong reasons. But is checking the reasoning actually easier than checking the answer? For most of the tasks people care about I doubt it, and if it's harder then the whole thing looks like it's moving the verification problem somewhere less visible rather than solving it.
