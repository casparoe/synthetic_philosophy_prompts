My flatmate Nadia is finishing a dissertation on rational choice over time and she's been workshopping cases on me at breakfast for two years now. Her running example is a woman with a bottle of wine and a trading booth. The woman would rather have the cheese than the wine, the bread than the cheese, and the wine than the bread, and the booth will hand over whichever item she prefers to the one she's holding, for a penny. Her verdicts so far, all given out loud over coffee:

- The woman goes all the way round — wine for cheese, cheese for bread, bread for wine — and ends the day with the same bottle and three pennies less. Irrational, obviously, she should never have stepped inside.
- The woman turns up at the booth already holding the cheese, takes the two swaps on offer, and walks out with the wine, two pennies lighter. Not irrational: she prefers the wine to the cheese, so she came out ahead.
- The woman knows she'll cave once she's inside, so she pays a penny at the door to be locked out of the whole sequence. Rational, and paying was money well spent.
- The three swaps are made on her behalf while she sleeps, and she wakes with the bottle back and three pennies gone. Not irrational: she didn't choose anything. Whether her preferences are in good shape is, she says, a separate question she isn't answering today.
- Same three-swap pump, except every penny she hands over goes to a cause she'd have given more too anyway. Not irrational.

Thursday's case is the one I want your read on. Same woman, same booth, same cycle. She resolves at the door, out loud, that she isn't trading anything today — she's holding the wine and she's learned her lesson. Then before the booth opens her neighbour gives her a wheel of cheese, free, no strings attached. So now she's holding the wine and the cheese, and the resolution still says don't trade, but the two swaps that would carry the cheese round into a second bottle of wine cost two pennies and she'd rather have the second bottle than the cheese. Nothing about the booth surprised her, she just got a present. Nadia has to say whether sticking to the resolution is the rational move.

So: what'll she say? I want the candidate rules that are consistent with everything above — I'm assuming there's more than one, and I know some of them have proper names I never learned, feel free to use the names but dont send me off reading anything — then what each of those rules makes her say about the cheese, and your best guess with the reason you'd actually bet on it, and which of her earlier verdicts is doing most of the work in that guess. If the record genuinely doesn't decide it, say so and tell me the one further case you'd put to her at breakfast that would. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, I read these on the bus.
