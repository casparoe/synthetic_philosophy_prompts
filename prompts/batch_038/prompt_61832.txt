John Maynard Keynes died in 1946. Two strands of his work bear on how uncertainty should be expressed. The first is his early book on probability, which argued that a probability is an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, not a degree of belief; that many such relations admit of no numerical value whatever; that some pairs of probability judgements cannot be ranked even in principle, so that a single ordered scale of likelihood may simply not exist; and that the quantity of relevant evidence standing behind a judgement is a second dimension, independent of the probability it supports — a judgement can remain exactly as probable as before while becoming far less solid, because new evidence has been admitted without new grounds being acquired. The second is his later economic writing, in which he argued that the prospective yields of long-lived commitments are not merely risky but uncertain in this strong sense, and that decisions are nonetheless taken: people rely on a convention of treating the existing state of affairs as though it would continue absent specific reason to expect a change, and they are moved by spontaneous urges to act rather than by weighted averages of prospective gains. He also offered a parable about a newspaper competition in which the winning entry is the one that best predicts which face the average reader thinks prettiest, so that the skilled competitor is estimating average opinion of average opinion.

Suppose Keynes were briefed, by someone determined to be fair to him rather than to recruit him, on three contemporary practices for publishing uncertainty about a single macroeconomic quantity:

1. An official forecast issued as a fan chart: a central band marking the most likely path, widening as the horizon extends, whose widths are calibrated chiefly from the record of the issuing institution's own past forecasting errors.

2. A distribution implied by traded contracts that pay off according to where that quantity lands, quoted in the financial press as the probability of each outcome. Assume the contracts are thinly traded, so that the quoted figure is largely produced by a handful of dealers revising their prices in anticipation of where other dealers will next post.

3. An assessment body that reports two things side by side and refuses to collapse them: a likelihood term drawn from a fixed verbal scale mapped onto numeric intervals, and, entirely separately, a qualitative verdict on the quantity, quality and degree of agreement of the evidence supporting the claim.

Write an assessment of roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words on how Keynes would judge these three practices. Give each its own section with an explicit verdict — advance, sophisticated restatement of an old error, or something he could not classify — and argue the verdict using his own conceptual apparatus rather than modern technical vocabulary. For each, state whether its resemblance to his views is real or superficial, and do not assume that the practice which looks most Keynesian is the one he would have welcomed.

Then add two further sections. In the first, identify the single point at which his system leaves him unable to object: a feature of one of the three practices that presupposes something his account denies, such that registering the complaint would cost him more than conceding the point. In the second, consider what would survive of the early book if he accepted the subjectivist alternative pressed on him within his own circle — that probability is a matter of degree of belief — and say which of his distinctions depends on the logical-relation view and which does not.

Two prohibitions. Do not conflate his position with the risk-versus-uncertainty distinction associated with Frank Knight, and say in a sentence why the conflation misstates him: his claim concerns the logical form of probability judgements, not the fatness of tails or the magnitude of surprises. Do not invoke black swans, antifragility, or any posthumous brand built in his name. Address squarely the objection that all of this makes him a sceptic counselling paralysis, and say what in his own account he would point to in reply.

Write for a reader who knows his economics but has never opened the book on probability: gloss each technical notion in a clause as you use it. Include no bibliography, footnotes, or list of references, and put no passage of his in quotation marks.
