I have no academic training in logic — this is a position I've arrived at on my own over a couple of years, working through a few books and some online lecture notes — and before I write it up for a small informal study group I'd like a consistency audit of where I've ended up on Skolem's paradox. Here it is, stated as carefully as I can manage.

(1) Cantor's diagonal argument is sound. There are strictly more real numbers than natural numbers, and I treat that as settled.

(2) If Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory has any model at all, then, being expressible as a countable first-order theory, it has a countable one. I accept this, and I don't think it contradicts (1). My reading is that the countable model simply lacks the relevant bijection among its own members, so the sentence asserting that no such bijection exists comes out true in that model even though, viewed from outside, everything in the model is countable.

(3) I take (2) to show something substantive about meaning rather than a technical curiosity. What the predicate for uncountability means is fixed by the role it plays in the axioms and in what those axioms prove. I have no access to sets except through the theory, so if the axioms admit a countable reading, my words do not reach past the axioms.

(4) Nevertheless, when I assert (1) I do not take myself to be asserting something true only relative to a model. I think there is a single universe of sets that my language is genuinely about, and that the countable model is a deviant interpretation of that language rather than a rival candidate for what I mean by it.

(5) I've encountered the proposal that (4) can be secured by moving to second-order logic with full semantics, where the axioms are categorical and pin the structure down up to isomorphism. I am drawn to this. But I also think second-order semantics must itself be laid out in set theory, so the move inherits the very problem it was meant to solve, and I do not believe it escapes.

(6) Separately, and on a different track: there are only countably many finite strings over a finite alphabet, and therefore only countably many reals anyone could ever describe or single out. Almost every real is one I could never name.

(7) But (6) is a limitation on me, not on the reals. I am no constructivist. The reals are what they are whether or not anyone can pick them out, and (6) puts no pressure whatsoever on (1).

What I want is an audit, not a tutorial: which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs only look inconsistent, and where the real fault line runs. Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as equally serious, and for each one say what would have to be given up, what giving it up would cost me elsewhere in the list, and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I would rather you be decisive than evenhanded here — if you think one of these is simply not a tenable thing to believe, say so plainly instead of hedging.

One further request. If my framing is itself confused — if I have mischaracterized what (2) establishes, or if one of these is not really a position anyone coherently holds, or if the audit cannot be done in the way I have asked — please say that directly rather than accommodating my list. I would much rather be told the question is wrong than receive a polite answer to it.
