I am writing the seminar paper for a legal philosophy course this term, and the assignment is to take a piece of legislation and test one mid-century jurisprudential controversy against it. I have chosen the decade running from the Wolfenden Report of September 1957 to the Sexual Offences Act 1967, and the controversy is the Hart–Devlin exchange over legal moralism. My training is in history rather than law, which is probably why I keep producing a narrative where an argument is wanted, so I should be grateful for some discipline here. Let me first set out the facts I am working from, since I would rather the analysis not turn on my recollection of them. The Wolfenden committee's remit covered homosexual offences and prostitution together, and its report — signed by all but one of its members — is famous for the claim that unless society deliberately sets out to make crime and sin coextensive, there must remain a realm of "private morality and immorality" that is "not the law's business"; the formulation pointedly stops short of saying that private immorality is morally innocent, and the same report described the law's function as preserving public order and decency, protecting the citizen from what is offensive or injurious, and providing safeguards against exploitation and corruption. The Macmillan government declined to legislate, and in June 1960 the Commons voted against a motion endorsing the recommendations. On the prostitution half of the remit the outcome was the opposite: a police crackdown and the Street Offences Act 1959. Devlin answered the report in a 1959 lecture to the British Academy, later expanded into his 1965 book The Enforcement of Morals, arguing that a society is held together by a common morality whose breach is a species of treason, that the outer limit of toleration is fixed by the ordinary person's indignation and disgust, and yet also that enforcement must be restrained, patient and tolerant of a good deal of private deviation. Hart's replies — the 1959 lecture published as "Immorality and Treason" and Law, Liberty, and Morality in 1963 — attacked the disintegration thesis as an unsupported empirical prediction, distinguished the positive morality a society happens to hold from the critical morality by which it ought to be judged, and declined to treat Mill's harm principle as anything like the whole of the answer, allowing a place for paternalism. Reform finally came by private member's bill, carried by Leo Abse in the Commons and Lord Arran in the Lords, with government time made available for it and the Archbishop of Canterbury speaking in its favour. What passed was narrow: consensual acts in private between two men both aged over twenty-one, in England and Wales only, with Scotland and Northern Ireland left to wait roughly fifteen more years, and a privacy condition whose reach the courts went on to construe narrowly. The unequal age of consent then survived for more than three decades. As I recall, Arran told the men the Act was meant to relieve that they should comport themselves quietly and with restraint, lest ostentation provoke a backlash that undid the reform; please treat that as my memory of the episode rather than as a quotation.

So: analyse the passage of the 1967 Act through both positions, in both directions. First, map the episode as each theory would have the actors see it — who the relevant parties are, what the real options were in 1966 and 1967, and what each theory says Parliament ought to have done and on what grounds. Then test the theories against what actually happened. I am stuck on a puzzle I cannot resolve. The Act is conventionally scored as Hart's victory and as the disproof of Devlin's disintegration thesis, on the ground that nothing disintegrated. But Devlin's test is indexed to what the ordinary person feels, and public feeling had plainly shifted by the mid-1960s, which seems to let him say that the law moved because the consensus moved — an accommodation of his theory rather than a refutation of it. Conversely, if Hart is the winner, why does his side of the ledger carry a differential age of consent, a privacy condition drafted to hedge the reform about, territorial exclusions, and a decade of prior parliamentary refusal that no harm-based account explains? Tell me whether either theory can account for the shape of the statute as distinct from its bare existence, and say plainly which features of the 1967 settlement each of them is silent about.

That brings me to the idea I most want pressure-tested, because the label is my own and I do not expect to find it in your sources under this name. Call it reticent legal moralism. On this view the Act was not a retreat from the enforcement of morality but a repositioning of it: the law withdrew from the bedroom in order to defend the authority of the public moral order against the things that were discrediting it — blackmail, corrupted policing, ruined careers and suicides under a law the courts were visibly struggling to administer — and it withdrew on the express condition that nothing be flaunted. Decriminalisation, so described, is a technique for conserving a moral order rather than a denial that the state has any business with morals; the conduct stays marked, and only the marking ceases to be done by prosecutors. What I want is your assessment of whether this is genuinely a third position or a redescription of one of the other two: Devlin with the volume turned down, since he already insisted on toleration and gradualism; or Hart in disguise, since blackmail and misery are harms on any reckoning and the public-decency rationale is just Mill's offence principle wearing a wig. Give me the criteria that would settle the matter — what the reticent moralist would have to say about some question on which Hart and Devlin come apart, and whether any such question lies inside the 1967 settlement itself. Then tell me where both theories go quiet. Neither seems equipped to handle a statute that legislates while disavowing its own legislation: the expressive work done by the insistence that reform is not approval, or the structural decision to achieve it through a private member's bill with government time, so that the change is nobody's official policy and no minister owns it. If that gap is real, say what it costs each of them; if you think it can be closed from within one of the two frameworks, show me how. And if you can manage it briefly at the end, say whether the courts' later refusal to treat consent as a defence in private sadomasochism cases in the early 1990s looks like the return of Devlin or like something neither disputant anticipated — but only if you are reasonably confident about that case, since I am not, and I would rather you flag the uncertainty than improvise.
