I have retired from experimental physics after thirty years on beamline and instrumentation committees, and have only lately begun reading the philosophy of science with any care; I now sit on a national panel that allocates roughly fourteen million euros annually to precision tests of quantum foundations. One proposal in this round divides us, and I must circulate a written opinion before we vote.

The applicants propose a Bell test in which the measurement settings at two widely separated stations are fixed in real time by single photons arriving from astronomical sources, and they interleave trials among sources of very different lookback time — stars within our own galaxy alongside quasars at several redshifts — so that any common cause linking settings to hidden variables would have to lie earlier than the emission event in each case. Their distinctive claim is not merely that this relocates the horizon but that it makes measurement dependence detectable: they will bin the data by the inferred age of the setting photon and test whether the magnitude of the violation declines as settings are drawn from the deeper past, on the reasoning that a residual common-cause mechanism should progressively lose its grip.

A senior colleague objects that this is theatre. The setting-independence loophole cannot in principle be closed, only pushed backwards, since a sufficiently early common cause remains available at every stage; and the predicted decline is entailed by no model the applicants have actually written down — a deterministic account locating the common cause inside the overlap of the past light cones would predict no trend at all. The instrument would absorb half our budget for six years.

Draft the panel's decision memo: recommend funding, rejection, or approval with conditions; state any conditions precisely enough that the applicants could meet or contest them; and give my colleague's objection its full force rather than waving it away. Please deliver it as exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, the first stating the recommendation, with no preamble and no closing remarks.
