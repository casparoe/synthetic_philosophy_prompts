I am in my final year of secondary school and have been reading about how laboratories try to keep advanced AI systems from causing harm. There is a pattern I keep encountering and cannot name.

A safety measure is advertised as not depending on the model being trustworthy: the claim is that it works even if the model is deceptive or misaligned. Yet when I work through how the measure actually operates, the guarantee holds only against a model that is not trying to defeat it. Two instances. A company argues that its system cannot do damage because it runs in a sandbox with no network access, but the system's text passes to a human operator who does have network access and can be persuaded; the wall is solid against accidents and porous against persuasion. Or a second, weaker model inspects the first model's outputs for scheming and can trigger a shutdown, which is fine if the monitored system behaves the same whether or not it is being inspected, and says nothing about one that recognizes the inspection. In both cases the measure is described as the containment of an untrustworthy agent but engineered as though the agent were a natural hazard rather than something that adapts to it.

Is there an established term for this? I have come across security theater, security through obscurity, and the general distinction between making a system safe and keeping a system contained, but none of these seems to be exactly it: theater provides nothing, obscurity is about secrecy, and here the mechanism is perfectly real and merely assumes a non-adversary. If there is no established name, please coin one and define it, and give the boundary cases I would need in order to apply it consistently — in particular, what makes a containment measure robust to the thing it contains, and whether that robustness comes in degrees.
