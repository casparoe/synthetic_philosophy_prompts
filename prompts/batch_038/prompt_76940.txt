I'm putting in for a year's research leave and I need a draft proposal — call it 1,000–1,200 words — that I can sharpen up before it goes to committee. Here's the half-formed idea, and honestly I'd like you to push back on it as much as build it up.

Start with Dennett's line that the self is a centre of narrative gravity: an abstractum, posited the way you posit a centre of gravity, doing organising work rather than naming a thing. Everyone quotes that and then stops. But there are three seperate components inside the analogy and nobody keeps them apart: the distribution (the actual pattern of behaviour, utterance, memory and commitment that the centre is supposed to summarise), the centre (the posit itself), and the calculation that assigns one to the other. In the physics case the calculation runs one way and the answer isn't up to anybody. In the self case the calculation feeds back — telling the story alters the behaviour the story was summarising — so its a fixed-point structure rather than a computation, and that's where I want to live.

So: the question the proposal needs to state crisply is whether we can get a genuinely normative notion of a *mis-centred* self — a narrative that isn't the right centre for its own distribution — without smuggling back the inner auditor Dennett is trying to get rid of. Then show why the existing answers leave that open. The narrative-identity crowd impose constraints (fit the facts, be articulable), but those bite on content, and a runaway narrative can satisfy them beautifully while still drifting from what it summarises; the deflationary side has no gap between seeming and being, so it can't even describe the confabulator or the memoir that nobody who knew the author recognises; the eliminativist response just walks away from the problem. Then the method — what I'd actually do, term by term — including why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives (go empirical, or do straight conceptual analysis of 'self'), whether any formal apparatus earns its keep or is decorative, and which case types I'd test a taxonomy of feedback pathologies on. Then what a result would look like and what would count as failure: I've got three candidate failure conditions in mind (mis-centring collapsing back into plain inaccuracy; every narrative turning out to be a fixed point of its own loop by construction, so the notion is empty; the diagnosis requiring a homunculus who stands outside the loop and checks it) and I want those argued for, not just listed. Finally an honest note on scope — the thing keeps threatening to become a book about personal identity rather than a year's work, and I'd rather admit that up front than have an assessor find it for me.

No bibliography, I'll bolt the citations on myself. I just want it to read like someone who's thought hard about the problem rather than read widely around it.
