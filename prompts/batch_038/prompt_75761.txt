A seminar I'm leading mixes first-year economics majors with first-year philosophy majors, and the topic is revealed preference and what utility is supposed to be. The mix is the difficulty: the economics students arrive convinced utility is empty bookkeeping, the philosophy students arrive convinced it names a quantity of felt satisfaction, and each group will think the other is missing something obvious. I want a plan for the seventy-five minutes, not the explanation itself.

Tell me the order the components have to go in and what depends on what — in particular, how much choice data must already be on the table before talk of mental states becomes legitimate. Then give me one case to carry both halves of the session: I was going to use a woman who says she prefers tea but buys coffee every morning, and I now suspect that case smuggles in the very picture I'm trying to unsettle. Say what a working example has to have and what it must lack. Then map the misconceptions — I expect "preferences cause choices," "choosing consistently shows the person is better off," and "if it can't be false it's worthless" — and pin each to the point in the sequence where it should be headed off, noting which subgroup will resist which claim and why the two resistances pull in opposite directions. Say whether a money-pump style argument earns a place and where. Say what I should openly refuse to cover at this level. Give me one closing question that would show the distinction has landed rather than a slogan. And one last thing: a colleague insists the honest upshot is that revealed preference theory is either trivially true or obviously false — should I build the session toward that disjunction, resist it, or leave it aside?
