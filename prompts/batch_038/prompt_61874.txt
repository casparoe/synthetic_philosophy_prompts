I teach a master's seminar in philosophy of history and I'm setting the final paper. Write one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes and worth 30 marks, on the concept of societal 'collapse'. It must not be answerable by rehearsing Tainter against Diamond or by surveying the familiar cases; instead it should hand students three cases and require them to formulate a definition that sorts them correctly:

(a) the abandonment of the southern Maya lowland cities between roughly 750 and 950 CE, with dynastic kingship and monumental construction ending there while the population persists and northern centres rise;

(b) the breakdown of the Late Bronze Age palatial systems around 1200 BCE, after which literacy, palace redistribution, and long-distance elite exchange vanish from much of the Aegean for centuries, though the population is not annihilated and eventually produces the polis;

(c) the end of the Roman Republic, where a regime dies but administrative capacity, cities, trade, and literature expand under the Principate.

The definition must return 'collapse' for (a) and (b) and 'not collapse' for (c), without being tailored to those three cases or so loose that any institutional change qualifies. The question should also require students to meet two further demands: to say what work 'collapse' is doing that 'change' is not, given that in both (a) and (b) one might identify the society with its population and cultural tradition rather than with its political structure — and if nothing that persists has been lost, what exactly collapsed; and to say whether a structural definition renders 'collapse' value-laden, since complexity carries costs and its loss can raise ordinary welfare.

Deliver the question as it would appear on the paper, a model answer of about 1100 words, and a marking scheme totalling 30 that names the moves an A answer must contain, what a solid second-class answer typically does instead — for instance defining collapse by harm to the population, so that (b) ceases to qualify, or by the loss of any political form, so that (c) qualifies — and how the marks are distributed across the moves.
