Ok so my friend R has been antinatalist-adjacent for years and I've quietly been collecting her verdicts on cases, partly because I'm trying to work out what she actually believes and partly because I suspect she doesn't know either. Most of this came out over several coffees and one very long drive to a wedding. Her track record, as near to verbatim as I can manage:

- Couple where one partner carries a gene for a devastating childhood-onset condition, 50/50 odds — flatly wrong, no hesitation, no "but it depends on the parents' reasons."
- Totally ordinary couple, no special risks, expecting what she called "a pretty good life with the usual heartbreak and back pain and slow decline" — also wrong, though she added "not monstrous, just wrong."
- When I asked whether a child who was *guaranteed* to be happy would change the answer, she refused the question: nobody can guarantee, "you're betting with somebody else's chips."
- She thinks it would have been better if humans had never existed, but she gets genuinely angry at any hint of hastening that, and she distinguishes sharply between a happy 30-year-old dying (a real tragedy) and that person never having been born (not a tragedy for anyone).
- Adopting a badly neglected existing kid is, in her words, "the best thing going" — but she insists it doesn't offset or license having your own.
- She has a well-managed chronic autoimmune condition. She says her own life is worth living and she wouldn't trade it, and also that it would have been better not to start it. She sees no tension there at all.
- People who are sincerely glad they were born are, she says, bad judges of the question; gladness isn't evidence.
- Dogs: a well-loved rescue, fine. Deliberately breeding them, she wouldn't commit, called it "a gray area I'm not touching."

So first: what rule is she running? I can think of at least four that look compatible with all of that — the Benatar-style asymmetry (the absence of pain is a good even when there's nobody around to enjoy it, while the absence of pleasure isn't a loss unless someone is deprived of it), a straight suffering-focused view where only harms generate reasons, a risk/no-consent view where the wrongness tracks variance and the impossibility of authorization rather than expected value, and some threshold view on which any life containing more than a certain amount of badness is not a life worth starting. Tell me which of these her verdicts actually pin down, which ones they leave wide open, and whether some fifth rule fits better than any of them — I have a hunch there's a person-affecting constraint doing work that none of my four capture.

Then the new case, which is real and which she has to weigh in on next week. Her sister is 39, secure, married, and has done PGT-M plus a pile of additional screening; the clinic's honest best estimate is that any child from these embryos gets about as good a start as medicine and money can arrange — healthy, wanted, two stable parents, no foreseeable catastrophe. The sister is also upfront that she doesn't just want a pleasant baby, she wants to give someone a life with actual meaning in it, and she keeps using the phrase "the gift of existence." But she's simultaneously in conversation with an adoption agency about a specific six-year-old with significant needs and a bad early history, and for reasons of age, money, and the agency's own rules she can realistically do one or the other, not both. Two questions bundled together: would R say conceiving *this* child is permissible, and would she rank it above or below the adoption?

Predict both verdicts, but show the working: go rule by rule and say what each one yields, and flag any place where two rules agree on permissibility but split on the comparison — I think that's where the interesting divergence is hiding, since the screening has supposedly eliminated the gamble that she leans on so hard elsewhere. Give me your single best guess about what she'll actually say and why, then name the one follow-up case or question I could put to her that would most cleanly separate the surviving rules. And if you think some of her recorded verdicts are flat-out inconsistent rather than merely underdetermining, say which one she'd most likely give up under pressure and why. This isn't for a paper or anything, it's for a reading group I'm in, so no need to point me at literature — I just want the reasoning laid out.
