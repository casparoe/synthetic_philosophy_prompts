I am a junior at a fairly competitive high school, and our history department has proposed making the second year of the required history sequence optional for any student who places out of it by examination. The department chair circulated a one-page memo to students and parents defending the proposal, and to my surprise she devotes the back half of it to Nietzsche's essay on history, the second of the Untimely Meditations, claiming him for her side. I belong to a seminar in which we argue school-policy questions from both directions, and I have chosen to argue against the proposal partly by disputing her reading of Nietzsche. My difficulty is that I have read only a handful of pages of the essay, from an anthology that stops before the second half, so I cannot yet tell how much of her account is safe to contest and how much I would be embarrassing myself over.

Here is the relevant portion of the memo, more or less in her words. "Nietzsche's essay on history is a polemic against the nineteenth-century cult of the past. He opens by declaring that he hates everything which merely instructs him without increasing or directly quickening his activity, and the remainder of the essay is his case for that declaration. His argument is the animal in the field: the beast is happy because it forgets at once and lives wholly inside the present, whereas the human being drags a past behind him that lengthens however fast he runs. The remedy is therefore to learn from the beast and to reduce the past to the smallest horizon within which one can still act. Nietzsche sorts historical study into three kinds — monumental, antiquarian, and critical — and ranks them: only monumental history survives his criticism, because it hands the person who means to achieve something models of greatness to imitate; antiquarian history is nostalgia in the service of mere preservation, and critical history is self-refuting, since one cannot condemn the past that produced the very person doing the condemning. His conclusion is that a healthy culture must be capable of forgetting, and that the Germans of his day, ruined by their historical education, had lost that capacity altogether and become imitators without a culture of their own."

Would you audit this for me? Please take it claim by claim and sort it into what a serious reader of Nietzsche would accept as fair, what is a defensible simplification of something subtler, and what is simply wrong. In particular I want to know whether the three kinds really are ranked the way she says; if not, what he takes the legitimate use of antiquarian and critical history to be, and what he thinks goes wrong with each once it detaches itself from life — because I have a vague memory from the anthology of his describing someone who feels at home among the old walls of his town, which does not sound like the nostalgia she is describing. I also want to know whether the beast and forgetting passage really yields the instruction to shrink the past as far as possible, or whether Nietzsche holds something she has collapsed into that; whether he can consistently say the Germans had lost the capacity for forgetting altogether if the essay is addressed to the young as something they can still act on; and whether the line she presents as his own opening declaration is actually his. Finally, please tell me which concepts the memo drops out entirely — the anthology mentioned a kind of plastic power and a standpoint above history, and I suspect both matter here.

One further question, and it is the one I am least sure I want answered. Does the corrected Nietzsche still help my side? I assumed that fixing her reading would hand me an ally, since he plainly cares about what history does to a person rather than about how much content is covered. But if his complaint is really about instruction that accumulates knowledge without transforming anyone, that sounds uncomfortably like an argument for her proposal rather than against mine. Tell me which claims in the essay I can honestly deploy, which I would have to surrender, and whether quoting her paragraph back at her would prove more dangerous to her than to me if anyone in the room has actually read the essay. You do not need to give me page numbers, translations, or further reading; I only want to know what to trust.
