I sit in on a good deal of graduate work-in-progress, and last Thursday I listened to two of my advanced students — I'll call them Owen and Nadia, both ABD, both competent, and both writing in epistemology — talk themselves into an impasse that I could not referee and have not been able to resolve since. The seed case was ordinary enough. Dr. R, a physician-researcher, concludes from a body of trial data that a drug is ineffective. Her colleague, whom we stipulate to be her equal in intelligence, training, access to the data, and freedom from bias, has examined the very same data and concluded that it is effective. Owen began by saying that Dr. R is entitled to hold her ground: she has reasoned as carefully as she can, and the bare fact that an equally competent person read the evidence differently supplies her with no new information about the drug itself. Nadia replied that it supplies her with new information about *hers*: given the stipulated symmetry, the colleague's contrary verdict is evidence that her own processing may have failed, and it would be flatly arbitrary for her to privilege her own read when, by hypothesis, the two of them are equally trustworthy readers. Owen then turned the case on her. Whether peerhood of that kind demands conciliation is itself a disputed question, he said, and the two of them are in exactly Dr. R's position with respect to it; so if Nadia's principle is correct, she must lower her confidence in the principle, and having lowered it she has correspondingly less reason to tell Dr. R to lower hers. Nadia said that is what the view predicts rather than a problem for it: the adjustment is graded, so she should shift her credence in conciliationism toward Owen's and then adjust her verdict about Dr. R in proportion to whatever confidence in the principle survives. Owen answered that this licenses a regress — she is equally Owen's peer on whether to conciliate about conciliation, and so on without end, so the view cannot issue a single recommendation about Dr. R until an infinite stack of adjustments has been completed, and a rule that can never be applied even once is not a rule of reasoning. Nadia said a regress is not vicious merely for being infinite, and that this one converges: if each stage requires moving halfway toward one's peer, the sequence of adjustments has a determinate limit, and Dr. R may simply adopt the limit, which is ugly in practice but perfectly coherent in principle. Owen's last move was that the convergence is a conjuring trick. If the limit is available at all, he said, it is available at the first step, so Dr. R can move straight to it and none of the intervening stages has done any work; the content of the view was therefore smuggled in by assuming both that the limit exists and that it sits at the average, and the average is precisely what is in dispute. He closed by observing that his own position has a fixed point too — the one at Dr. R's prior — so fixed-point considerations cannot break the tie between them.

What I would like from you is an adjudication of that exchange, move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the seven turns I have set out — Owen's opening, Nadia's reply about higher-order evidence, Owen's turn of the case against conciliationism itself, Nadia's graded-adjustment response, Owen's regress charge, Nadia's convergence defense, and Owen's fixed-point rejoinder — tell me who has the better of it and why, and be willing to award a draw or to say that one of them has simply changed the subject. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are neglecting; I have my own suspicions (that they slide between a principle governing what one ought to believe and a procedure for getting there; that they treat the symmetry stipulation as though it were evidence rather than a hypothetical; that the object case about Dr. R may be doing no work at all beyond supplying rhetorical momentum), but I want your diagnosis, not a ratification of mine. In particular, please say explicitly whether the seed case matters: would the meta-dispute come out any differently if Dr. R and her colleague disagreed about something with no practical stakes, or if the disagreement were between two people with unequal but merely comparable reliability?

Two requests about manner. First, I want the assessment to be committed rather than diplomatic: if a move is simply invalid, say it is invalid, and if you think the whole exchange rests on a confusion that neither party noticed, say that plainly and locate it. Second, and more importantly, I invite pushback on my framing. If you think I have reconstructed the argument unfaithfully — that one of these turns is not a real move but a distortion I have introduced in the retelling, that the exchange conflates distinct objections that need to be separated before any verdict is possible, or that grading seven turns individually would produce false precision — tell me so directly instead of working politely through the structure I have handed you. I would far rather be corrected about the setup than be given a tidy scoring of a badly posed problem.
