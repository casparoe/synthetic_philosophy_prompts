Next month I give a forty-minute talk to the standing research ethics committee of a large academic medical center. Eleven voting members: mostly physicians who chair or sit on IRBs, a nurse researcher, a hospital chaplain, the lay patient advocate, and the director of the human research protection program. Two contract-law professors and a behavioral economist who studies defaults and choice architecture are seated in as outside discussants. The talk defends a view of consent I have been calling the live-option account, and I would like a question-period preparation written to me as a letter.

The view, briefly. Consent is valid just in case, at the moment it is given, the alternative to consenting was a live option for the person consenting: not merely permitted, but something the person could actually represent to themselves as a real course of action and actually carry out. On this account the familiar vitiating factors are not four independent conditions but four ways of killing the alternative. Deception kills it by removing the factual basis on which refusal would have been chosen. Coercion kills it by making refusal prohibitively expensive. Incapacity kills it by removing the ability to hold the alternative in mind at all. And a fourth, for which the received framework has no name, kills it by friction: the refusal path is uncoerced, fully disclosed, and formally free, and is nonetheless unavailable because it has been built to cost too much in attention, steps, elapsed time, or social awkwardness.

Three consequences I intend to press. First, the baseline problem in coercion largely dissolves. When the surgeon says we can operate in the morning or in six weeks, whichever you prefer, and knows that six weeks is not survivable, nobody has to settle what the patient's position was before the proposal; the question is whether "no, and I will wait" was a course of action this patient could actually take up. Second, deception does not become harmless when the victim is expert. My case is the rolled-back odometer sold to a mechanic who should have caught it: I hold the consent invalid, because liveness is measured against what the seller did to the option rather than against the buyer's skill or diligence. Third, and this is where the room will turn on me, a great deal of consent that institutions treat as paradigmatically valid is not established by the fact that everything was disclosed. The ninety-page terms of service, the trial consent form handed to a frightened patient ten minutes before the first infusion, the enrollment screen whose decline button sits in grey type at the foot of a scroll: on my account these are not weakened consent but consent whose validity is simply unproven. I also allow a patient to waive liveness, as with the genetic incidental finding a patient declines to be told about, but only where the waiver itself was live, and I expect to be told that this last move is ad hoc.

What I want from you. Seven or eight objections, in roughly the order they will arrive, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, because a physician who runs a cardiology trial does not sound like a contracts scholar and neither sounds like the chaplain. I expect pressure on administrability, on the fact that validity is treated as binary in law and policy while liveness is plainly scalar, on whether the account makes consent validity depend on subjective psychological states the committee cannot observe, on the incentive effects of excusing the agent who refuses to read, on the difference between a hard choice and a coercive one, on exploitation as something distinct from invalid consent, on what the account implies about defaults and opt-out donation, and on the paternalism of letting a third party declare that a competent adult's consent did not count. For each objection give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds of speech, and then one sentence saying plainly where that reply is thin. Tell me separately what I should concede in the first five minutes of the talk to buy credibility for the rest of it, and which single reply has to land for the whole thing to survive the afternoon. Tell me also which of my three cases I should cut, if any of them buys an objection I cannot answer.

The question I am hoping nobody asks is this: name one case where the live-option account and the received framework reach different verdicts, and say which of them is right about it. If I cannot produce one, my view is a redescription of the received framework in the vocabulary of choice architecture rather than a competitor to it, and the behavioral economist knows this. I think the friction cases are my answer, and I think that answer is weaker than the paper lets on; I would rather you say so than reassure me.

I want no citations, no reading list, and no bibliography, and no speculation about who has argued what. Write it as a letter or a long email to me: continuous prose throughout, no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables, with the objections taken one at a time in their own paragraphs.
