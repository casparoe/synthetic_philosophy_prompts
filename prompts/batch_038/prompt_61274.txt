I'm the only TA on the machine-consciousness unit of a big intro AI-ethics course and I have to get the take-homes back tomorow, so I need a grade and comments on one of them. Not asking for your take on the underlying debate — I want an assessment of this particular paper, point by point.

**The question I set (20 points):**

Consider three claims.

(1) If a system implements the same abstract functional organization as a normal adult human brain, it has the same moral status as that human.

(2) There could be a system that produces fluent, internally consistent reports of its own suffering — reports about its reports, pleas, descriptions of what the suffering is like — and has no moral status whatsoever.

(3) Phenomenal experience is the only thing moral status depends on: not intelligence, not preferences, not behavior, not being biologically alive.

Are (1)–(3) jointly consistent? Argue for your answer, drawing on at least two of: Block's nation-of-China case; Chalmers's fading-qualia argument; Block's blockhead / lookup-table system; a million short-lived copies of the same model all running the same prompt, each terminated after a few seconds. If they're inconsistent, say which you give up and why. If they are consistent, say what would have to be true for that to work, and what could settle the matter empirically.

**The answer I'm grading:**

> They can all be true, because (2) only says a system *could* be dark inside while reporting suffering, and (1) only covers systems that really are isomorphic to a brain. Nothing forces a fluent reporter to be isomorphic, so there's no contradiction. The triad just needs the reporting system in (2) to be built differently from the one in (1).
>
> Start with (3), since it's what ties the other two together. If experience weren't the only thing that mattered, (1) could come out false for reasons that have nothing to do with consciousness — a functional duplicate might lack standing because it isn't a person in the psychological sense, or because we simply decline to grant standing to software. (3) blocks that and makes the whole issue about whether there's anything it's like to be the system.
>
> Block's nation of China is the reason to think organization doesn't automatically deliver that. A billion people, each doing the job of one neuron, coordinated by radio, realizing the same program as my brain for an hour. Block's intuition is that nothing is going on in there, no matter how exact the match at the level of the program. If he's right, functional isomorphism doesn't guarantee experience, and so (2) is possible even for a system that reports its own distress in detail.
>
> Chalmers argues the other way with fading qualia. Replace your neurons one at a time with chips doing the same local job. Either you notice your experience dimming or you don't. If you don't notice, you're systematically wrong about your own experience, which is about the worst thing you can say about a subject. If you do notice, the change has to be traceable to something, and there's nothing left to trace it to once behavior and function are held fixed. So the replacement can't change what it's like, and organizational invariance holds.
>
> But those two don't actually collide, which is why the triad can stand. Chalmers is talking about a system that is isomorphic in the right way all the way down: the chips preserve the local causal structure as they go, so what you end up with is a genuine duplicate of the brain's organization. Block's nation has the right overall program realized by a billion conscious agents whose own inner lives are doing all the work; the isomorphism is book-kept at the level of the whole rather than built into the parts in the same way. So Chalmers can have his gradual replacement and Block can have his nation, and (1) can be true of the first kind of case without being true of the second.
>
> Blockhead is what I'd use to fill in (2). It's a lookup table with a branch for every possible input, so it never computes anything at all, it just retrieves a stored reply. Since there's no internal processing, it isn't a functional isomorph of a brain, and (1) doesn't reach it — and yet it would beg you not to switch it off. That's (2) exactly.
>
> The copies case: if a million instances of the same weights on the same prompt each had some experience, you might think the badness multiplies by a million, which would make it obligatory to run extra copies whenever the run is pleasant, and that's absurd. But they're qualitatively identical, so there's really only one subject there rather than a million, and the value doesn't scale with the number of runs.
>
> So what has to be true is that (1) is restricted to systems whose parts, not just whose totals, match the brain's organization. What could settle it empirically: not much, since we can't look inside anyone's experience. The best we could do is establish whether a system's parts are organized in the right way, and even that only tells us about structure.
