I am preparing a chapter on the evidential structure of linguistic relativity, and I want a case analysis of the Hopi time controversy: Benjamin Lee Whorf's claims about the Hopi language, and Ekkehart Malotki's 1983 monograph, which is usually taken to have demolished them. I know the standard narrative — a fire-prevention engineer who did linguistics on the side asserts that a Native American language lacks the machinery for thinking of time as a continuum; some four decades later a fieldworker produces six hundred pages of counterexamples; the discipline concludes that the whole Whorfian enterprise was an embarrassment. What I want is not that narrative but a disciplined mapping of the argument's structure, running in both directions.

Here is the record as I have it. Where I have it wrong, correct me rather than quietly working around the error. Whorf's claim, in the paraphrase I will use, was that Hopi lacks words, grammatical forms, constructions, or expressions referring directly to what English speakers call time, and that its speakers consequently lack a general intuition of time as a smooth flowing continuum, everything proceeding out of a future, through a present, into a past. Malotki's reply assembled hundreds of Hopi forms and sentences with temporal reference: a verbal marking system organized as future against non-future, where English is usually analyzed as past against non-past; temporal adverbs and particles; and a lexicon that distinguishes a duration from a point in time, clock time from an occasion or appropriate moment, and both from simply having time to do something. Alongside this he offered a positive account, on which Hopi temporal practice is organized by a spatial metaphor of progression through past, present, and future. Typologists treated the argumentation as devastating, and the monograph became a standard exhibit for the view that Whorf was simply a bad linguist. Since the revival of relativity research in the 1990s, defenders have objected that Malotki answered a claim Whorf never made, and some have argued that Hopi is better described as tenseless, with the future/non-future contrast doing the work of a realis/irrealis mood distinction. That last point is contested and I do not want you to settle it; I want you to say what turns on it.

Please conduct the analysis under the following decomposition, which is mine rather than the literature's, and tell me where you think it cuts badly.

1. Four joints. Treat a relativity claim as a chain: (i) inventory — the language has or lacks forms of a certain kind; (ii) function — those forms do or do not do the same conceptual work as their apparent counterparts elsewhere; (iii) patterning — speakers characteristically encode some distinctions obligatorily and relegate others to context, in ways that shape habitual discourse; (iv) cognition — speakers characteristically perceive, remember, or reason differently. Place Whorf's assertion and Malotki's evidence on this chain. My suspicion is that Malotki's counterexamples are overwhelmingly at joint (i), that Whorf's own apparatus of covert categories and cryptotypes lives at (ii) and (iii), and that his step to (iv) was taken with no psychological evidence whatever. Test that suspicion rather than accepting it: can an existence refutation at joint (i) defeat a claim framed at joint (iii), and under what conditions could it?

2. Core and auxiliary. Cast the episode in Duhemian terms. Which components of the Whorfian package were auxiliary to the relativity thesis and which were its core? Is Malotki's result a refutation of the thesis, of one flagship instance of it, or of a popularized caricature that had acquired independent life in textbooks and general-audience writing? Attend to the afterlife: the claim that the Hopi have no concept of time kept circulating long after the linguistics had been corrected. What does that persistence show about how theses of this kind are held, revised, and surrendered?

3. The confound. Whorf reasoned from grammar to worldview. But grammar, subsistence practice, ritual calendar, contact with English, and schooling move together in any speech community, so the direction of the arrow is unidentified. State precisely what the Hopi case can and cannot support once this is granted, and whether the modern experimental program — absolute frames of reference, grammatical gender, color naming, numeral systems and exact enumeration — inherits the same structural weakness or escapes it. Which of those domains looks most robust in light of this episode and which most fragile? Answer in terms of the joints above, not in terms of which studies you happen to recall.

4. The reflexive question. Malotki's positive claim, that Hopi speakers structure time spatially as everyone else does, is itself an inference from linguistic data to a universal feature of human thought. Does it commit the same move it was deployed to punish? If it does, is that a symmetry that cancels out, or does one side retain an evidential advantage the other lacks?

Aim for roughly 1,500 words, organized under those four headings. No bibliography, no list of further reading, no page numbers, and no invented quotations: argue from the structure of the case. Where a historical detail matters and you are unsure of it, say so explicitly and name what would settle it. I would rather have an honest account of the points at which the theory falls silent than a confident reconstruction of a debate whose particulars neither of us can verify.
