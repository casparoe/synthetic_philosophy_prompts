I work in a small forecasting unit inside a regulator, and there's a pattern I've never been able to put a name to. Some questions we get asked behave: you collect more data, the number tightens, and two analysts who started a mile apart end up roughly together — component failure rates, say, or next year's passenger volumes. Others don't behave like that at all. Ask whether some structure will still be holding in fifteen years and more evidence doesn't tighten anything, the analysts just drift further apart. Yet both kinds go into the same column of the same spreadsheet, so to whoever reads the output they look identical.

The wierd part, and the one I can't find a word for, is that you can't tell from the inside which sort you're holding. Nothing about how the question is phrased marks out whether more evidence would converge or simply wouldn't help, so we constantly treat the second sort as if it was the first, because the template wants a figure. Related: one colleague will happily give numbers for the first sort and flatly refuses for the second, saying the question isn't the kind of thing that has an answer — and that gets read as him being evasive rather than as a claim about the question itself.

I know there's talk of risk versus uncertainty and of subjective probability, but neither quite reaches the not-knowing-which-sort-you're-in bit, which is where the damage actually seems to happen. Does either part have an established name? And if the second part doesn't, what would you call it, and how would you keep it apart from ordinary model uncertainty and from plain ignorance? Please answer in British English.
