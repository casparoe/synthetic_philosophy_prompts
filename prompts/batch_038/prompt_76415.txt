Two people from my building's tree committee got into a long arguement last week and I still can't tell who won. Context: the oaks on our street get stripped by caterpillars every few years, and the committee has to decide this spring whether to pay for a spray treatment. Both of these people are the type who relocates spiders with a cup, and neither one disputes that insects might be capable of suffering — that premise is shared, so it wasn't what they were fighting about.

Maya is against spraying. Her opener was that a spray deliberately kills an enormous number of insects all at once, and when you're unsure whether they can suffer, the burden of proof sits on the person proposing the killing, not on the person proposing to leave things alone.

Seth said she has the sign backwards. His point: those caterpillars overwhelmingly die anyway, and die badly — starvation once the leaves are gone, or parasitoid wasps that lay inside them and consume them over a couple of weeks. A sprayed caterpillar is dead within hours. So the real choice isn't death versus life, it's a fast death versus a slow one, and Maya is casting the fast one as the atrocity.

Maya said that only works if the deaths are fixed and the spray merely moves them earlier, which they aren't. Every caterpillar that dies to wasps this year is an adult that never lays next year, so letting the outbreak burn itself out shrinks the population and shrinks total insect suffering down the line. Spraying, she said, keeps the boom-and-bust machine running, and the machine is what manufactures the suffering.

Seth said her argument quietly assumes the population is sitting at some ceiling, where a death today really is a birth prevented tomorrow. In an outbreak year that's false — the caterpillars are exploding precisely because the oaks are flush and the winter was mild, so they're nowhere near any limit. In that regime the deaths the spray causes are extra, not traded off against future ones. He said her whole case flips on whether the population is regulated and she hasnt shown that it is.

Maya said fine, grant him the ecology, it still doesn't get him to spraying, because nobody knows whether insects are sentient to begin with. Put a 10% credence on it and every number on both sides shrinks by the same factor, so the comparison lands exactly where it would if we were certain — which makes all his population ecology beside the point, and the tie goes to not killing things on purpose.

Seth said the 10% does not cancel. It scales everything, sure, but the two options aren't the same magnitude once you multiply out how intense and how long the suffering is, and under that much uncertainty you should be guarding against the large-magnitude mistake rather than the small one. He added that "default to not killing" isn't a neutral stance at all: declining to spray is a decision too, it just outsources the deaths to wasps and starvation and spares Maya having to watch any of it.

They circled for another twenty minutes and I lost the thread. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each one, not just who wins overall? I'd like a rating for each of the six moves — clearly wrong / weak / fine / clearly right — with a sentence of justification each, and then a note wherever one of them is actually begging the question rather than arguing. Also: what are they both leaving out that belongs in the case, and is there a single empirical fact that would settle more of this than anything either of them said?
