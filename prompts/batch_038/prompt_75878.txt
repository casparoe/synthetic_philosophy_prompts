I convene a small evening seminar on dynamic choice theory, and we have just spent three sessions on money-pump arguments. The disagreement in the room is stubborn: half of us think that an agent whose choices can be cycled back to its starting point at a net cost has thereby been shown to be incoherent, while the other half think the argument smuggles in assumptions about foresight, about whether commitment is available and how much it costs, and about whether the agent is even allowed to have preferences that shift as she learns things. Discussion has plateaued into people restating their priors. For our closing session I want to try something different: a piece of short fiction that stages the structure concretely enough that we can argue about the characters rather than about the theorems.

Here is the situation I would like you to invent around. A municipal seed bank in a river-valley town holds three heritage lots of a staple grain, and each lot is best on a different dimension — one keeps longest in storage, one mills the most flour per measure, one tolerates a dry season. A traveling seed merchant calls at the bank repeatedly through the season, and on each call offers a swap between two lots plus a fixed handling fee, paid in a measured scoop of grain and collected regardless of which lots trade. The keeper of the bank finds every individual offer attractive when she weighs it on the day, and accepts. By the end of the season the bank holds exactly the three lots it began with, in the same proportions, and the ledger shows three scoops gone. I want three figures in the story besides the keeper: a young clerk who works the problem backwards from the end of the season and so refuses the very first swap, a retired superior who deals with the temptation by nailing a written rule to the wall and following it come what may, and the merchant, who should be neither a villain nor a fool — he is simply running a business that happens to fit the shape of the keeper's judgments. Set it somewhere invented and undated; I have no interest in botanical or historical accuracy, and please do not anchor it to a real region or year.

Two craft requirements matter more than the rest. First, keep the mechanism legible but not expository: no character may deliver a lecture, the phrase "money pump" may appear at most once, and the arithmetic of the scoops should be reconstructible from concrete detail — a marked bin, a chalk tally, a weighed measure — rather than from anyone explaining the setup. Second, build in a wrinkle that undercuts the tidy diagnosis: at least one of the three accepted swaps should turn out to have been motivated not by a standing preference but by an inference the keeper drew from the merchant's willingness to part with that particular lot, so that by the final tally it is genuinely unclear whether the cycle was ever in her preferences at all, or was instead manufactured by what each offer told her. Aim for 1,400 to 1,800 words in close third person, and end without adjudicating whether the young clerk's refusal is insight or paralysis. Please avoid a Faustian bargain framing, avoid any moralizing last paragraph, name no philosophers, and do not append an afterword, notes, or a glossary — I want only the story, and we will supply the argument ourselves.
