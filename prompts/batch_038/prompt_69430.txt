I am about to circulate a position paper to a reading group on automation and the left, and before I do I would like an audit of the commitments it rests on rather than a defence or a summary of them. Here is the package, stated as plainly as I can.

1. I take seriously Marx's distinction between machinery as such and its capitalist employment. The machine is an instrument of production with no politics of its own, which is why the same technical innovation can shorten the working day under one set of arrangements and lengthen it under another. It follows that socialists have no business opposing automation as such.

2. I also accept what he says about factory despotism: capital designs and deploys the machine system to break workers' autonomy, to deskill them, and to turn each of them into an appendage of the machine. I do not think this is an accident of how the machinery happens to get used. The technical form that capital gives to the labour process carries the control relation inside it.

3. My explanation of why automation is capital's own limit is the tendential fall in the rate of profit. Only living labour creates surplus value, so mechanisation raises the organic composition of capital and squeezes the rate of profit; the system is thereby self-undermining in precisely the activity it cannot stop pursuing.

4. I do not read that law as a collapse prediction. Marx lists counteracting factors — the cheapening of the elements of constant capital, the intensification of exploitation, foreign trade, and so forth — and I take the tendency to explain the recurrent form of capitalist crisis rather than its automatic terminus.

5. For me, though, the real Marxian automation thesis sits not in Capital but in the passage on fixed capital in the Grundrisse that later readers call the Fragment on Machines: once scientific knowledge and what Marx calls the general intellect become the chief productive force, direct labour time ceases to be an adequate measure of wealth, and production founded on exchange value breaks down from within. I treat this as the more radical claim of the two, and as the warrant for the thought that capitalism generates the material basis of its own successor.

6. On that basis I support a generous universal basic income as a transitional demand — not because it abolishes the value form, which I freely grant it does not, but because it decouples survival from wage labour and thereby normalises the expectation that socially necessary toil should shrink towards nothing.

7. A case verdict: a wholly automated production process would, on my reading, create no new value at all, and its output would be close to valueless. I think parts of logistics and manufacturing are already approaching that condition. I do not regard this as a reductio of the labour theory of value, for two reasons: prices are free to diverge from values, and in any case I hold that the value theory is not a price theory at all but a theory of a specific social form of domination.

I genuinely do not know where this package is weakest. Different colleagues have pointed at different places, and I have no settled view about which commitment I would rather give up, so please do not treat my order of presentation as a ranking of my attachment to them.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. I know the texts well enough; do not summarise Marx's theory of machinery for me or reconstruct the history of the general intellect debate. Trace instead what each commitment commits me to. Go through the seven and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against one another, which merely look as though they do and dissolve on inspection, and — for the genuine ones — what the cheapest revision is and what that revision costs me theoretically and politically. If any of my seven statements is simply not a position Marx held, or a reading nobody actually defends, flag that in the relevant row rather than quietly repairing it, and do not add commitments I have not stated.

Please give the audit as a single Markdown table, with the rows ordered by severity, genuine tensions first and apparent ones last, using exactly these columns:

- **Pair** — the commitment numbers in tension, e.g. 3 vs 5.
- **Status** — genuine or apparent.
- **Severity** — high, medium or low; for apparent tensions write n/a.
- **Diagnosis** — two or three sentences naming the specific premise that does the damage: which claim in one commitment cannot survive alongside which claim in the other.
- **Cheapest revision** — the smallest change to the package that removes the tension, stated as an edit to one or more of my numbered commitments.
- **Cost** — what I lose, theoretically or politically, by making that revision.

Below the table, in no more than four sentences, tell me which single revision clears the most, and whether what remains is one coherent position or two incompatible Marxisms that I have been holding side by side.
