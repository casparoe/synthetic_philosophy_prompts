I read environmental regulation, not philosophy of science, and I have been trying to work out what theory of value the planetary protection rules are built on. I cannot get a coherent answer, so let me lay out the practice as I understand it and ask you to do the reconstruction. Please tell me if I have got any of the facts wrong, and whether the correction would change your reading.

Under the 1967 treaty governing outer space, states are obliged to conduct exploration so as to avoid harmful contamination of celestial bodies, and also to avoid adverse changes in Earth's environment resulting from returned extraterrestrial matter. In implementation: landers bound for Mars are assembled in cleanrooms and their components baked for long stretches to drive the count of viable bacterial spores on board below a permitted limit; flight paths are biased so that a failed spacecraft is unlikely to strike a region where liquid water might let a hitchhiking Earth microbe multiply; an international committee sorts missions into protection categories of increasing stringency, and the agencies employ planetary protection officers to enforce them. When the Galileo orbiter ran out of propellant in September 2003, NASA deliberately destroyed it by crashing it into Jupiter rather than leave it in an orbit from which it might eventually fall on Europa, a moon with a subsurface ocean. Planned Mars sample return is handled as the strictest category of all, requiring containment of the returned material.

Two things strike me as sitting badly with that. First, the quarantine imposed on returning Apollo crews — three weeks sealed in a facility with their spacecraft and their samples — was dropped after the first few lunar landings, once the Moon was shown to be lifeless; we now cheerfully leave hardware and debris there. Second, every serious plan for a crewed Mars mission concedes that human beings cannot be heat-sterilized, that each astronaut carries tens of trillions of microorganisms, and that a habitat will leak. The working solution appears to be to declare certain regions off-limits and accept contamination elsewhere.

So: what does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of value as precisely as you can, and consider at least these candidates — that indigenous microbes have moral standing in their own right; that a possible second origin of life is a unique and non-replicable object of knowledge, valuable the way an undeciphered manuscript or an unexcavated site is; that this is simply institutionalized precaution under radical uncertainty about both probabilities and values; or that nothing is being protected except the integrity of future science. Then tell me which of these the practice strictly requires, and which it merely fits comfortably with, pointing to the features that discriminate between them. I would expect the tolerated spore limits and the off-limits regions to look more like pollution control — acceptable risk, best available technology — than like a prohibition on touching, and I note that we sterilize hospital instruments by the trillion without a second thought, so the protected thing cannot be microbial life as such.

Please also address these specific puzzles. What does it presuppose to treat a merely possible organism, one nobody has ever observed, as the bearer of a claim against us? What does it presuppose about biology, since the harm model assumes Earth microbes could metabolize Martian material and outcompete native life rather than share an ancestry with it? What explains the asymmetry between the abandoned back-contamination rules and the standing forward-contamination rules — does it reveal that knowledge rather than organisms is the real object, or is it an asymmetry about who bears the risk, which would itself be a normative commitment worth naming? And which rival views does the practice quietly rule out?

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were made explicit and applied consistently. If Martian microbes genuinely had standing, is a crewed mission permissible at all? Or does the practice's own logic amount to a right of first look that expires the moment we settle whether life is there — in which case the Moon is not an inconsistency but the pattern's predicted endpoint? Describe what a policy built on a different one of your candidate theories would actually look like on the ground.

Be explicit about how confident you are in each part: which readings the practice strongly supports and which are speculative extrapolations from details I may have garbled. Single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of, and say what a specialist would need to check to settle it.
