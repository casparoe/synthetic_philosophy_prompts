I am in the middle of a novel about gradual replacement: over eleven months a woman's neurons are substituted one at a time by synthetic counterparts that do the same work, and she goes on living, arguing, and finishing a book. A philosopher who reads the draft assures me the metaphysics is untroubling, that this is simply porting software to new hardware — the person is the program, the brain is merely the machine it first happened to run on, and a program moved to another machine is the same program, so she survives as a matter of definition. He adds that my closing scene, in which the preserved original brain is switched back on and there are briefly two of her, is no threat either: two instances of one program are not rivals, in the way that two runs of the same script are not two scripts.

I want the analogy audited before I allow it to settle anything. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what in my story plays the role of the source code, the compiler, the specification, the test suite that certifies a successful port, the machine, the running process, the saved state, the version number — and mark which of those roles my surgeons actually have and which are assumed for them. They never compile from a specification; they work on the thing itself, cell by cell, with no independent description to check their work against, and I suspect that absence costs the analogy more than my reader admits.

Then separate the disanalogies that do damage from the ones that are merely picturesque. I am most concerned about three properties of software that appear to be doing quiet work: that a program can be paused and resumed without anyone wondering whether it survived the pause; that it can be certified as the same program by equivalence of behavior under tests; and that its instances are interchangeable and non-rivalrous. Tell me what each of these presupposes about minds, verification, and identity that the analogy has not earned, and whether his conclusion — she survives, and the second woman is not a competitor — still stands once those presuppositions are exposed.

Finally, if the analogy cannot carry the weight, propose a better one, preferably drawn from the world of made things I already work in: translations, revised editions, performances of a score, restored buildings. And be candid about what your replacement smuggles in turn, since I doubt any of them arrives neutral.
