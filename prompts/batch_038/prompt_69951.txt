**The question as set**

> Question 4 (25 marks). Devlin and Hart agree that a society has a shared morality and that the criminal law can affect it. They disagree about whether that fact can justify criminalization. Hart frames the issue as whether the fact that conduct is immoral is "sufficient to justify making that conduct punishable by law?" Set out Devlin's argument for the legal enforcement of morals as precisely as you can, identifying its premises and saying what work each one does. Then explain the main lines on which Hart attacks it. Finally: does Hart's rejection of Devlin's argument establish that legal moralism is false? Justify your answer.

**The candidate's script**

> Devlin's argument begins from the Wolfenden Report of 1957, which recommended that homosexual acts between two consenting adults in private should cease to be criminal offences. Devlin rejects the distinction between private and public morality that the Report relies on. There is, on his view, no such thing as a purely private vice, because a society is not simply a collection of people who happen to live under the same government: it is a community of ideas, and what makes it one society rather than a mere aggregate is a shared moral code. From this he draws an analogy with treason. A political community may punish treason even where the treason causes no immediate harm to any identifiable individual, because treason threatens the existence of the community itself. Likewise a society may punish immorality, because immorality threatens the moral code without which the society would not hold together. This is usually called the disintegration thesis: remove the shared morality and the society disintegrates.
>
> Devlin's account of how that shared morality is identified is deliberately unsophisticated. It is not the view of philosophers or theologians, but the reaction of the ordinary reasonable man — in practice, of a jury — and the relevant reaction is one of intolerance, indignation and disgust. Where that reaction is strong, the immorality is one the law may suppress.
>
> Devlin's lecture was delivered in 1959 and Hart's book appeared in 1963, so Hart was replying to the lecture rather than to the 1965 collection of Devlin's essays. Hart's first objection is evidential. The disintegration thesis is a factual prediction, and Devlin offers no evidence for it; the historical record suggests the opposite, since moral codes have changed substantially — in sexual conduct, in religious observance, in attitudes to usury — without the societies in question ceasing to exist. Hart's second objection is that Devlin's argument reduces to the claim that whatever the majority feels strongly about may be enforced, which licenses the persecution of unpopular minorities. Since Hart holds that the state may interfere with liberty only to prevent harm to others, and since Devlin's central empirical premise is unsupported, the case for legal moralism fails.
>
> Hart's own position is Mill's harm principle, which he endorses without qualification. He is entitled to do so because of the argument of *The Concept of Law*: law and morality are conceptually distinct, so nothing about the existence of a shared morality can by itself generate a legal obligation. Once the disintegration thesis is shown to be empirically false, legal moralism is therefore refuted, since that thesis is the only support the position has. What Devlin gets right is that the enforcement question cannot be settled by insisting that morality is a private matter; he pays for that insight by making the feelings of the majority a source of law, which is exactly what a liberal criminal law cannot permit.
