I'm delivering the opening address at a two-day workshop that my university's archaeology department is co-hosting with the staff of our state's climate-resilience office. Roughly seventy people will be in the room: twenty-five academics who work on ancient societies (Mesoamerica, the eastern Mediterranean, the Pacific, northern Europe), twenty-five practitioners who write adaptation plans for towns facing sea-level rise, drought, and insurance withdrawal, and twenty graduate students split unevenly between the two camps. The workshop exists because the agency wants to learn something from the long record and the department wants its work to matter to somebody.

There is a problem with the word everyone arrived with. The agency's enabling statute uses "collapse" as the thing it exists to prevent; my department has spent a decade arguing that the word is a moral verdict dressed up as a description, and I am personally on record, in the grant proposal that created this workshop, calling the agency's founding language careless. I now have to open a room containing both sides, and I don't want to referee. I want to give them an instrument.

The material we're working from makes the difficulty concrete, and I'd like the address to work directly through these five cases rather than around them:

- The southern Maya lowlands in the ninth and tenth centuries: kingship by divine sanction stops, monuments stop being carved and dated, cities empty, and yet cities in the north are flourishing at the same time and Maya people and Maya languages are in the same region today, including among the descendants of the communities the department works with. A political order ended; a people did not.
- The eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age: interdependent palatial economies come down within a couple of generations, and what follows is worse by some measures (a script disappears from the Aegean for centuries, long-distance exchange contracts) and better by others (iron becomes widely available, alphabetic writing eventually spreads far beyond a scribal caste, land is occupied by people who had no stake in the palace system). Complexity fell; some measures of ordinary life rose.
- Rapa Nui: the deforestation is real and pre-contact, but the inference from deforestation to a society that consumed itself before Europeans arrived is precisely what the specialists in the room dispute, and the well-documented demographic catastrophe belongs to the contact period. This is the case where a moral about us got attached to an island, and the attachment outlived the evidence.
- Roman Britain: towns, villas, coinage, and administrative literacy largely disappear within a generation or two, while the farmland keeps being farmed and most of the people living on it are the ancestors of the people who live on it now, absorbed into a different political order. Almost nothing of the institutional fabric survives; almost nothing of the population is lost.
- The Norse settlements in Greenland: farms that were simply empty when ships came back. Nobody to absorb, no continuity, no successor order.

Here is what I want from you. Write the address, roughly fifteen hundred words, to be spoken aloud, in the voice it should be delivered in. Its central job is to give this room a working vocabulary it can actually share across tables for two days: a small number of named distinctions — I expect something in the neighborhood of separating the loss of a political-institutional order from demographic loss, from the loss of a people's continuity, from the reduction of social complexity as such, from the reduction in what ordinary lives were like — and then to run all five cases through those distinctions, showing which of them were never a single event at all and which of them still deserves the strongest word we have. The distinctions must be usable by someone who has never read the specialist literature: describe positions, don't cite them, and don't assume anyone in the room knows a single author's name.

Two constraints I care about most. First, the address may not dissolve into the platitude my colleagues would tolerate and the practitioners would despise — that "collapse" is merely loaded and we should say "transformation" instead. Some of these places lost the capacity to feed and govern the number of people they had been feeding and governing, and people died of it, and the address has to leave room for that to be said plainly and to count as failure rather than as change mislabeled. Second, it has to give the agency a straight answer: what the word in its statute should be taken to mean, whether the department should stop fighting it, and what the department gets in exchange for conceding. I want the address to end with an actual charge to the room — a concrete demand on how the two halves talk to each other for the next two days — not with a hopeful generality.

Make the choices rather than presenting me with options. Then, after the speech and in no more than a hundred and fifty words, defend the two decisions you think are most likely to be wrong: the distinction you are least sure earns its keep against the objection that it is just relabeling, and the concession you make to the agency. Say what would have to be true for the other side to be right, and why you still think it isn't.
