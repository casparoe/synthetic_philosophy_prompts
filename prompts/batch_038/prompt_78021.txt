I study narrative sources for the early first millennium BCE, and my case-by-case verdicts do not seem to come from any single rule I could state and defend. They run like this. Two authors centuries apart report the same event, and I count that as one report told twice rather than corroboration, unless I have positive reason to think the later one worked independently. A royal victory inscription that is our only account of a campaign: I credit it for the fact that the campaign happened, not for the casualty figures. Where a text and an excavation conflict I side with the excavation, except when the excavation's chronology was itself fixed by reading that text, and in that case I have no idea what to do. An eyewitness openly hostile to the people he describes, with nothing to gain by sparing them, earns more from me than a sympathetic eyewitness with a patron to please. Thucydides' admission that he composed his speakers' remarks to suit the occasion as he judged best does not lower those speeches in my estimation relative to his narrative, though I cannot say why it should not. Herodotus' habit of setting rival versions side by side and sometimes declining to choose looks like better epistemic behavior than it first appears, but I do not think it is available to us: we cannot simply publish both accounts of a battle and stop there. And oral material written down centuries after the events I treat as worthless for names and dates yet genuinely informative about the shape of what happened.

The pair that troubles me is this. A single, late, unsupported source reporting something extraordinary — a comet, a plague that carried off a king, a marriage to a foreign princess — I reject outright. A single, late, unsupported source reporting something routine — a levy, an unremarkable succession, the repair of a wall — I accept without hesitation. In every respect I can name, those situations are identical except for the prior plausibility of the content, which makes me suspect that I am letting my priors do all the work and dressing it up as source criticism.

Is there a principle behind all of this, or a small set of competing candidates? I want each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not yet met, with the verdict of mine that motivates each clause; I want to know what it delivers on the case I keep circling — a source demonstrably dishonest elsewhere in the same work whose claim here runs against his own interest; and I want a plain account of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would rather give up one verdict than buy a principle with six special provisions.
