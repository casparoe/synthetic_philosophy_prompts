For nearly three years I have been administrating a play-by-forum Nomic — Suber's game, which most of us first met through Hofstadter's Scientific American column — and I have run into what I take to be the paradox of self-amendment in a form that actually governs play. Our charter's core is two rules:

Rule A: "Any mutable rule may be created, amended, or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the members in good standing."

Rule B: "Rule A may be amended only by a unanimous vote, and no rule may be made immutable."

Two months ago three members moved to repeal Rule B by unanimous consent. I declined to put the motion to a vote, on the ground that nothing in Rule B authorizes its own repeal and that a rule cannot confer the power to eliminate itself. I was accused of ossifying the game; my own view is that I was applying the logic correctly.

My understanding of the literature — Ross's 1969 paper on self-reference and the puzzle in constitutional law, and Suber's *The Paradox of Self-Amendment* — is that self-amendment is formally impossible: a rule of change cannot validly repeal or alter itself, because the authority it would need to exercise is precisely what the amendment destroys. I have been thinking of it on the model of Gödel, where a consistent system cannot establish its own consistency, so that a rule set cannot validate the destruction of its own validating rule. It seems to follow that any rulebook permitting amendment at all must bottom out in at least one rule that cannot be amended, and that this unamendable rule is what keeps the system consistent rather than being the source of the paradox. Real constitutions appear to bear this out: Article V carries that proviso protecting each state's equal suffrage in the Senate against deprivation without the state's own consent, which looks exactly like the fixed point the logic demands.

So my question is a design question. Given that some rule must be entrenched, which of my two should it be? If I entrench Rule A, I forfeit every route to lowering the unanimity requirement and the game ossifies precisely as my members predicted. If I entrench Rule B, then a unanimous coalition can entrench anything it likes afterward, which makes the fixed point self-defeating. Is there a third way to write it — a fixed point that constrains the amendment power without falling to the two-step maneuver of first amending the entrenching clause and only then the provision it had been protecting? And what should the entrenched rule say about immutability, since Rule B's prohibition on immutable rules strikes me as the clause doing the real work here?
