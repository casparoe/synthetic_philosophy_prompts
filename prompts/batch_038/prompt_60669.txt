Forty years in stratigraphy, retired last spring, and now I read history of science for pleasure and get into arguments I loose. A historian friend of mine defends period talk — Middle Ages, Renaissance, Scientific Revolution — by saying it's just like stratigraphy: the layers are really there in the record, you draw your line where you can see a change, and the fact that the line is a convention doesn't make the layer unreal. She says the people who call those periods later inventions are making the same mistake as somebody who claims the Cretaceous is made up because a committee voted on where to put the golden spike. As a geologist I find the analogy flattering, and I think that's why I cant get traction against it.

So: audit it for me. I want the correspondence table — what plays the role of the rock sequence, the index fossils, the unconformity, the golden spike, the commission that votes — and then the disanalogies sorted into the ones that actually undercut the use she's making of it versus the ones that are just surface noise. What does the analogy smuggle in about boundaries being found rather than chosen, and does her conclusion survive once that's on the table? And if stratigraphy is the wrong source domain, tell me what analogy would do the job better. American English please, and don't go easy on it just because it's my own field in the driver's seat.
