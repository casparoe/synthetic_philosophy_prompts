I run a reading group of about fifteen people — two working neuroscientists, a handful of philosophy grad students, and several whose last ethics class was in high school — and next month we're doing moral status. I want to hand them a sorting quiz beforehand, "how much does a brain actually matter?": nine questions, each a concrete case with three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto named positions.

The reason I need this rather than a handout is that I think alot of the group has never noticed there are four different size claims in circulation and that they come apart: raw brain mass, brain-to-body ratio, encephalization quotient, and the count of neurons in the pallium or forebrain specifically. Every question should turn on a spot where at least two of those metrics disagree, so people are forced to say which one they're tracking and what they'd do when it points the other way. Cases to build from, with the facts stated so nobody has to look anything up: an African elephant's brain is roughly three times the mass of a human's and contains more neurons overall, but the surplus sits overwhelmingly in the cerebellum and its pallial count is well under ours; a Sheffield neurologist's famous patient was left with almost no cerebral cortex after hydrocephalus yet had an IQ of 126 and a first-class maths degree; children who've had an entire hemisphere removed grow up largely normal; a macaw has a walnut-sized brain packed with pallial neurons at a density no similar-sized mammal matches; an octopus runs something like two-thirds of its neurons out in the arms; a honeybee has on the order of a million neurons total and still navigates, learns individual faces and recruits nestmates to a food source. Then the human edge cases: an anencephalic newborn, a lab-grown cortical organoid, and something with no neurons at all that behaves indistinguishably from us.

Two hard constraints. First, no question may ask whether brain size matters, and the stems and options can't use words like sentience, personhood, or moral status — options have to be verdicts (who counts, who counts for more, who wins the tie-break, what gets done with the scarce resource). Second, the scoring key has to land on at least six leaves, including: a capacity-threshold view, a sentientist view on which status comes in degrees, a view where neuron count is itself the ground rather than a proxy, a view where size is only ever evidence about something else, a relational view on which none of this is the right question, and a deflationary view on which there's no determinate fact of the matter.

Then tell me what the instrument can't do. Which pairs of leaves come out identical across all nine questions, and what would a tenth have to hinge on to seperate them? And mark every answer-combination the quiz makes available that nobody actually defends, saying for each whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. I especially want to know whether "size is the ground itself" plus "status is binary" is a cell anyone can occupy coherently — my suspicion is that it isn't, and I'd rather the quiz exposed that than have me assert it over coffee.
