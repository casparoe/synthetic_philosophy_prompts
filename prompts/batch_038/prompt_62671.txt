I work in infrastructure engineering — mostly distributed systems, on-call rotations, incident review — and I have never taken a philosophy course. I've written a draft of an essay for a general-audience site about why conspiracy thinking is so hard to argue with, using ideas from my day job. I would like comments on the draft. I am not asking for a rewrite, and I am not asking for sources; what I want is diagnosis from someone who knows the relevant arguments better than I do.

Please read the draft below and then give me your comments.

DRAFT — "Trust Is a Topology, Not a Threshold"

1. When a service misbehaves, the first question we ask is what kind of failure we're dealing with. A crashed node is easy: it stops answering, and its silence is detectable. A Byzantine node is much worse. It stays up and keeps talking, but it sends different messages to different peers, and no amount of monitoring will catch it by noticing an absence. Our protocols treat the two cases completely differently, and for good reason: the second kind can make agreement impossible even when almost everything looks healthy. I want to suggest that discussions of conspiracy belief almost always describe the first kind of failure when the phenomena have the structure of the second.

2. The standard account treats belief as a scalar. A person has some threshold of evidence at which they accept a claim; conspiracy theorists have that threshold set badly — too low for stories that flatter their side, too high for anything coming out of official channels. On this picture the remedy is calibration: better media literacy, better statistics in schools, more fact-checking. I think this account is not so much wrong as misdescribed, and that the misdescription explains why the remedies keep failing.

3. Here is my proposal. A person's sources form a graph, and what matters is not only the weights on the edges — how much you trust whom — but the shape of the graph. In a healthy epistemic network the graph has structure: some sources are treated as authoritative over others, some are treated as genuinely independent of others, and there is a difference between one hundred witnesses and one witness reported a hundred times. Conspiracy belief, I suspect, is usually a change in shape rather than a change in weights. The hierarchy collapses into a flat graph in which every source is a peer of every other, and the only node left with any real authority is the believer and a small circle of people who already agree. I'll call this epistemic flattening. If that's what is happening, then supplying more institutional evidence makes things worse rather than better, because on a flat graph an additional institution is not an additional independent observation — it is the same node speaking twice, and the fact that it speaks in unison is itself counted as proof of coordination.

4. The independence point is the load-bearing part of the essay, so I want it stressed. In distributed systems, redundancy buys you confidence only when the copies are independent. Two replicas sharing a physical disk are not two replicas; they are one replica with a nice dashboard. When a hundred newspapers print the same wire story, that is one observation. And here is the part I am least sure of: institutions that have internalized this — science, law, engineering, auditing — build elaborate machinery specifically to manufacture independence. Blinding, replication, adversarial review, recusal, separation of duties. To someone whose graph has flattened, all of that machinery is visible and inexplicable on any innocent reading. The more sophisticated the independence procedure, the more it resembles a conspiracy. I worry this is too neat, and I would like to be told so if it is.

5. There is a technical detail I want to use, and I am aware I may be overreaching with it. In the Byzantine generals literature the classical result is that with ordinary, unauthenticated messages you can reach agreement only if more than two-thirds of the participants are honest; below that, liars can prevent consensus no matter what protocol you use. But the same literature shows that if messages are authenticated — signed, so that a recipient can tell who actually said what — you can tolerate any number of liars. Signatures are what convert an impossible problem into a solvable one. Now, institutional credentials are signatures. A byline, a court record, a deposition, a notarized document, a paper with named authors and a retraction policy: each is an authentication layer that lets a large network keep functioning in the presence of bad actors. On my account, the flattening move is precisely the refusal to accept signatures — attributing every claim to the person who uttered it rather than to the process that vouched for it. A signed message from a source you have decided is corrupt is, to the flattener, indistinguishable from a forged one.

6. The obvious objection is that sometimes the official account really is a cover-up, and that history has confirmed several. I do not want to dodge this, but I think it cuts my way. In almost every confirmed case I can think of, the confirmation came from the same institutional machinery that was supposedly compromised: reporters using legal process, archivists, inspectors general, congressional staff, courts, whistleblowers protected by procedure. The cover-up was broken by the signature system, not by the flat graph. So confirmed conspiracies are evidence for trusting authenticated channels, not against. I am aware that this paragraph may be doing something illegitimate — it may be set up so that nothing could count against it — and I would rather hear that from a reader now than after publication.

7. My conclusion is that nobody is at the wheel. Popper's old complaint, as I understand it secondhand, was that the large-scale outcomes of social life are mostly the unintended consequences of many people's intended actions, and that treating them as designed is a way of avoiding the harder work of understanding institutions. That is also what we teach in incident review: assume the outcome was produced by the shape of the system, not by a villain, and go read the logs. Conspiracy thinking, on this view, is a refusal to accept that the world's most terrible outputs are mostly unhandled exceptions.
