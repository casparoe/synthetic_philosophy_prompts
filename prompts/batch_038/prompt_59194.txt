I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and last month I moderated a panel at an interdisciplinary conference on cultural evolution that turned into a sustained argument between two of the participants: a cognitive anthropologist, whom I'll call Elena, and an evolutionary biologist, Marcus. The nominal question was whether the memetic framework — the idea that culture evolves by selection among self-replicating items, on the model of genes — is a substantive research programme or an elaborate metaphor. What follows is the exchange as it actually developed, in six moves.

(1) Elena opened by attacking the notion of copying. When a story is retold, a melody hummed from memory, or a craft technique demonstrated to an apprentice, nothing is duplicated: the recipient builds a new version out of fragments, inferences, and background knowledge, and different recipients build it differently. What persists across a population, she argued, is not a replicator but a set of attractors — regions of the space of possible variants that human minds reliably drift back toward. Calling that replication stretches the word until it also describes the way a river keeps returning to the same meander pattern, which nobody calls heredity.

(2) Marcus replied that attractors are a redescription, not a refutation. Genes are not copied by a homunculus either; DNA replication is itself a guided reconstruction, and its fidelity amounts to nothing more than the statistical fact that offspring resemble their parents more than chance would predict. What selection requires is heritable variation in fitness, not a copying mechanism. On any mechanism-neutral reading of that requirement — which is the only reading that does scientific work — a cultural variant that is reliably rebuilt in mind after mind, and that gives rise to further variants, qualifies as a replicator.

(3) Elena said mechanism-neutrality was precisely the defect, because it made the framework untestable: if statistical stability of any kind counts as replication, no observation could count against the theory, and a theory that cannot fail cannot explain. She added that whatever predictive content memetics had came from the gene analogy, and culture violates the analogy's substantive commitments. Particulate inheritance was what rescued Darwin from the blending objection; culture blends constantly. Someone who learns two variants of the same tale typically passes on a hybrid, and the hybrid does not segregate back into its parents. Under blending, selection is swamped unless it is very strong.

(4) Marcus answered that blending is an artifact of describing cultural items at too coarse a grain. Decompose a tale into motifs, plot functions, and character roles, and what looked like blending is the recombination of parts — no different in principle from reassortment or sexual recombination, where wholes are shuffled while parts persist. He noted a further asymmetry running the other way: cultural variants can be adopted after their performance has been inspected, so culture inherits acquired characteristics. That is a difference in dynamics, he said, not a failure of the analogy.

(5) Elena took this as a concession. If stability derives from what human minds reliably produce rather than from what was copied, and if transmission carries acquired improvements, then the explanatory work belongs to psychology — attention, memory, motor constraints, interest — which is what her field actually measures. The memetic vocabulary adds names without adding predictions: it says that the variants which persist are fitter, but fitness is defined by persistence, so the account redescribes the outcome it was meant to explain. She likened it to saying that markets select profitable firms.

(6) Marcus rejected the tautology charge, arguing that a population-level framework does work no individual-level psychology can do. Psychology can explain why a given person finds a given story memorable; it cannot explain why the distribution of variants across generations shifts in a non-random direction, or why some widely shared items are shared even though individuals find them unmemorable or actively dislike them. The market analogy fails, he said, because there the selecting force is trivially specified, whereas here the striking fact is that what gets transmitted and retained often fits badly with what individuals prefer.

Neither would yield and the session ran out of time. I owe my editor a sidebar making the dispute intelligible to non-specialists, and I left the room unable to say who had the better of it.

Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? For each of the six, give a rating on a scale running from indefensible to compelling, with a sentence or two of justification, and identify which of the individual claims inside it hold up and which do not. Then tell me where the real crux sits — whether they are disagreeing about the world, or partly about what a theory has to do to earn its keep — and name anything of substance that both of them left out of the argument entirely.
