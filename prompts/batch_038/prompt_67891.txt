I run grants at a mid-size private foundation. About a year ago our board moved from funding on expressed need to funding on expected wellbeing produced per dollar, and the change has started producing applications that break the model.

Every applicant now submits an estimate of wellbeing-per-dollar on our internal scale. The median this cycle is about 1.0. A handful of applications claim 40, 80, and in one case 300. Some of those are plainly just efficiency claims — a cheap procedure, high volume, durable effect, low overhead. But two of them claim the multiplier because of something about the recipients rather than the intervention: one serves a few hundred people who've had a particular neural implant and report that ordinary pleasures now feel dramatically more intense than they used to; the other serves about ninety people with a rare condition and claims per-person welfare gains that no ordinary population could match. And a third claims a big multiplier mostly because its recipients start from a very low baseline, so the same improvement is a much larger gain for them.

Our current answer is a blunt concentration cap — no single grantee gets more than 12% of the annual budget. Half the board thinks that cap is the only thing standing between us and something grotesque. The other half thinks its an unprincipled fudge we'd be embarrassed to defend in writing. I suspect both are right, and that the cap is standing in for three or four decisions we've never made explicitly.

So: write me a decision procedure my program officers can run on any application claiming an extreme multiplier. Constraints on the deliverable — the reviewers aren't philosophers, they get about ninety minutes per application with a spreadsheet, and the board ratifies at the end; every step has to leave a recorded answer, because a rejected applicant is entitled to see which step they failed at.

I want:

1. The questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure terminates in a no.
2. An early decomposition step that sorts the claimed multiplier into its sources — conversion efficiency, the recipients' capacity or intensity of welfare, and depth of starting deprivation — and says what turns on which box the claim lands in. Be explicit about whether a pure efficiency claim is even a monster claim, because right now our form can't tell them apart.
3. How to handle self-report, including the fact that people in chronically bad conditions often report doing fine. Does the procedure take adapted reports at face value, correct them, or refuse to? Whatever it does, it should say who is making that judgment and on what basis.
4. A clean separation between discounting a claim because we don't believe it and capping it because we believe it and think it shouldn't count for that much. These get confused constantly and the rejection letter is completely different depending on which one we did.
5. Which steps hand the decision up to a human or to the board because the question is about values rather than facts — and don't hide those behind a threshold number.
6. What an applicant learns to say once this procedure is public, and what we do about it.

Then run it on the three cases above: the high-volume efficiency claim, the implant cohort with the intensity claim, and the ninety-person low-baseline program. One of those should come out somewhere I personally don't like — I want to see whether the procedure forces that result or whether I've quietly built my own preferences into step 2 or step 4.

Finally, give me an honest list of which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume, and what this procedure cannot do no matter how carefully its run. I don't want citations or a reading list — I know the utility-monster argument and what it's supposed to show, and I'll handle that side myself. What I need is the operational version, including the places where the philosophy genuinely underdetermines the rule and we just have to pick something and own it.
