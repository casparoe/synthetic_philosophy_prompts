Here it is roughly: for a Bayesian expected utility maximiser, evidence that's costless and has no side effects on the decision problem is always worth waiting for, because maximising-then-averaging beats averaging-then-maximising over the partition the evidence induces — everyone grants that, and it's the standard proof. My claim is that the theorem still doesn't deliver the slogan even when you grant every hypothesis that normally gets listed, because there's an extra assumption doing quiet work that almost never gets stated: that the evidence is decision-independent, meaning the agent's posterior over the states given the evidence doesn't shift with which act she ends up taking. Savage is at least explicit that the states had better not depend on the acts; Good's theorem seems to inherit that silently. And the moment the free evidence bears on the agent's own choice — a sealed prediction of what she'll do, a report written by someone who knows her dispositions better than she does, that kind of thing — the two policies your supposed to compare are no longer scored with a common probability weighting, and an expected utility maximiser who conditionalises can end up worse off, by her own lights, for having looked.
