I teach a graduate seminar on the epistemology of cosmological reasoning, and in assembling the readings I have found that I repeatedly need a concept I cannot locate named anywhere. I would be grateful for help determining whether one exists, and if not, for help carving it out properly.

The instance that first fixed my attention is the fluctuation scenario in statistical mechanics: the worry that in a cosmos old enough, and near enough to thermal equilibrium, the combinatorial advantage of small departures from equilibrium over large ones makes it overwhelmingly likelier that any given observer with apparently rich memories is a momentary, randomly assembled one than an inhabitant of a genuinely ordered universe. I assume you recognise the scenario; my interest is not in it but in the structure it shares with quite unrelated arguments.

That structure recurs. Consider the genealogical argument against the trustworthiness of normative belief: our evaluative dispositions were shaped by pressures bearing on reproductive success rather than on truth, so they carry no presumption of accuracy. Consider likewise the argument, often pressed against naturalism, that if one's convictions are wholly the upshot of prior physical causes, then none of them is held on account of being true, and one therefore has no ground for supposing that any of them, including this one, is true. And consider critical theories which treat all moral discourse as the rationalisation of interest, a treatment they must extend to their own deliverances if they are to be consistent. In each case the reasoning begins from entirely ordinary evidence, and ends by pronouncing that the process which supplied the evidence, and the process by which the argument itself is assessed, cannot be relied upon.

What I want to know is whether this has a name. I have run through the candidates and each seems to me to fall short in a specific way, which is why I am not satisfied to file the thing under any of them.

It is not a straightforward refutation of itself, in either the logical or the pragmatic sense, since nothing here is inconsistent: one can coherently assert both the theory and the unreliability of one's evidence for it. It is not the familiar global sceptical scenario, since there the sceptic offers no positive case for the hypothesis, and the hypothesis does not impugn the reasoning by which it was reached; here the theory is reached by precisely the reasoning it condemns, and it is the reaching that generates the trouble. It is not the paradox of self-inclusion in the manner of the liar, since no sentence here refers to itself. It is not mere circularity, since I am not employing a faculty to certify its own reliability; I am employing it to deliver a verdict against it. And it does not behave like an ordinary reason for abandoning a belief, because such a reason survives the abandonment and continues to do work, whereas this one is generated by the belief and evaporates the moment the belief is relinquished.

The feature for which I can find no vocabulary at all is what the structure does over time. If I accept the conclusion, I must distrust the acceptance, since the distrust is warranted by the conclusion itself. But the distrust is likewise a product of the process under suspicion, so if I withhold the conclusion I have no warrant for the distrust either, and the original reasoning looks sound again. Belief, disbelief, and suspension each license a return to one of the others, and I cannot identify any attitude that survives scrutiny. My provisional and thoroughly unsatisfactory word for it is a wobble. I want to know whether there is an established term, in formal epistemology or in the cosmological literature or in metaethics, for an argument that leaves no stable doxastic attitude, whether or not every attitude it leaves is coherent. If the subfields use different words for what may be one phenomenon, I should like to be told whether it is in fact one phenomenon or several.

I am not after a label alone, so please do not stop at one. I want the carving: conditions necessary and sufficient for something to count, and an explicit ruling on the boundary cases. Does partial undermining qualify, as when a theory implies that a third of one's convictions are false, or that one's reasoning is sound inside a specified domain and unsound outside it, and if so where is the line? Must the conclusion concern the theorist herself, in a self-locating way, or does a conclusion about agents generally suffice? If the theory enjoys support independent of the impugned process — instrumental success, mathematical coherence, third-person data — does that rescue it outright, partially, and to what degree of independence? Is a theory that predicts its own rejection by its users without impugning their evidence the same phenomenon or a distinct one deserving its own name? And does the phenomenon attach to a theory's being true, or only to its being well supported?

Finally, if the honest verdict is that what I have described is no single phenomenon but a contingent conjunction of two familiar ones, please say so plainly and identify the two.
