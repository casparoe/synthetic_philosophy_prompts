I am a third-year philosophy PhD student working on conditionals, and I would like to settle what Lewis's 1976 triviality result actually commits me to before I finalize my prospectus chapter. I suspect the reasoning below goes wrong somewhere, but I have not been able to locate where, so a diagnosis would be more useful to me than a summary of the literature.

My understanding of the result is this. Lewis proved that Adams's thesis — the probability of an indicative conditional equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent — is false. The proof is a theorem of the probability calculus and turns on no assumption about what a conditional means: it establishes only that if the thesis holds for a pair A, C, then for every probability function P either P(A) = 1 or P(C | A) is 0 or 1. The thesis can therefore hold only for degenerate pairs. Being purely mathematical, I take the result to bear on every semantics that gives conditionals propositional content: on Stalnaker-style selection-function accounts, and equally on Lewis's own similarity semantics for counterfactuals, which assigns truth conditions in just the same way. That last consequence is the one I most want to avoid, since my chapter builds on the similarity framework.

What makes me doubt the reading is a toy computation of my own. Four worlds w1–w4, uniform P. Let A = {w1, w2} and C = {w2, w3}, so P(C | A) = 1/2. I give the conditional truth conditions directly: it is true at a world w just in case C holds at every A-world closest to w, with all A-worlds tied for closest and w's mass divided evenly among them. Each world then contributes 1/2, and P(A → C) = 1/2 = P(C | A). Adams's thesis comes out true, with a perfectly non-extreme conditional probability, in a model I can check by hand.

Two questions. First, since a theorem cannot have exceptions, where is the error in that computation? I have assumed it must be arithmetic and redone it several times without finding anything. Second, given that Adams's thesis is refuted outright, what should I put in its place as the norm governing which indicative conditionals may be asserted — a broadly Gricean pragmatic story laid over a truth-conditional core, or something else? I would especially like to know whether the answer differs for counterfactuals, since that is where my own project lives, and whether Lewis himself thought conditional probability had any residual role to play once the triviality argument was in hand.
