I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I also sit on the acquisitions committee of a university special-collections library. We have been offered the complete working papers of a community organizer who died last year: four decades of correspondence and case notes, and, from the late nineties onward, her email and chat logs, donated by her estate. Two problems have split the committee, and we vote Thursday.

The first is third-party exposure. The files name people who never consented to be in an archive and could be badly hurt by discoverability: women who wrote to her about abuse, tenants in unresolved disputes with landlords, and members of an informal network that helped undocumented neighbors reach clinics and schools. Our discovery layer runs entity extraction at ingest, so a name appearing once in a 2004 message becomes a permanent search hit attached to a living person. Restricting a folder does nothing about that.

The second is control. The estate will donate only under a deed giving a community review board — seven members named by her former colleagues, self-perpetuating, no sunset — veto power over description and access decisions, plus a covenant that we never deaccession and never impose restrictions tighter than the board allows. Our own policy says the institution, not a donor, must retain final authority over access. The board has already signaled it will resist restrictions on the network files, which its members regard as the entire point of the gift. A competing repository will take the collection on the estate's terms as they stand, and the offer lapses in six weeks.

You are a professional archivist who has chaired acquisitions committees and also teaches the philosophy of history; write with that double competence, and think the problem through before you commit to a recommendation. Begin with a short passage of reasoning in which you map the realistic structures of a compromise — including the option of declining — and say what each costs. Then draft the memo I will circulate to the committee: a clear bottom line in the first two sentences (accept, decline, or accept with specified amendments), amendments concrete enough that our counsel could draft against them, and an operational test for what the community board may and may not veto, phrased so a future committee could apply it without relitigating the whole gift. Address separately what we would actually have to do about name-level discoverability if we accept, and what we would owe the board's constituents if we do not. Finally, give the strongest honest case for the position you reject — I want the losing side to recognize itself in the memo rather than feel dismissed.
