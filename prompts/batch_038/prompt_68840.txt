I work as a software engineer on systems that make sequences of decisions; for the past few years that has meant an automated procurement agent that accepts and rejects offers over many rounds. I have no formal training in philosophy, though I read around decision theory on my own time. A pattern in that work has come to look to me like an unresolved conceptual problem rather than an engineering bug, and I want to turn it into a two-page project description for a workshop on the foundations of rational agency. I have roughly three months of part-time effort in which to do the actual work.

Here is the pattern. The money-pump argument, as I understand it, says that an agent with cyclic preferences can be walked through a sequence of trades it accepts one at a time and returned to its starting point with strictly less money, and that this demonstrates the irrationality of the cycle. Two features of the systems I build seem to loosen the argument's grip, or at least to raise a question it does not answer.

First, my agent's rankings are not standing states. They are recomputed from scratch at each decision point out of a model that has been updated in the interim. Between the first and last trade of a pump sequence, the thing doing the ranking has literally changed. It is not obvious to me that being pumped across such a sequence indicts the agent's preferences at any time, as opposed to indicting something else: the absence of a persistent plan, or the update rule.

Second, whether the agent is pumped depends on what it expects about its own later choices. An agent that foresees accepting the later trades should decline the first; an agent that does not is locally unimpeachable at every step and globally exploited. That makes the exploitable property look like it belongs to the policy across time rather than to the preferences at a time.

My half-formed thesis is that the argument treats irrationality as a single predicate applied to preferences, when there are at least three distinct objects that could bear the charge: the ranking at a time, the disposition to choose at a time, and the policy mapping histories to choices. Pumpability, I suspect, attaches only to the third. If so, some standard arguments establish less than they claim, and some standard replies, the precommitment reply and the foresight reply among them, are addressing a question that was never in dispute.

Please write the proposal. I need: a statement of the question precise enough to have a determinate answer, and in a form where I could tell from a short passage whether a proposed answer succeeds or merely restates the problem; an account of why the existing positions do not settle it, which I would like framed in terms of positions and arguments rather than names, since I cannot vouch for my recall of who held what and will supply the citations myself; the method, month by month, given that my comparative advantages are that I can specify a small formal model of sequential choice with recomputed rankings and that I can build and run the agents, together with an account of what that model must contain for the question to be genuinely at stake rather than settled by construction, and what I should do at points where the formalism and the philosophy pull apart; what a result looks like on each branch and what would count as failure, with particular attention to the negative result, since the three-way distinction collapsing under pressure is a live possibility; and a candid assessment of scope, including what to cut if three months is not enough, which parts of this will read as naive to a philosophy audience, and how to phrase them so that they read instead as an outsider's angle.

One constraint on tone: the proposal must work for readers with no interest in procurement software. The engineering should be the source of the question and the instrument for testing it, never the subject.
