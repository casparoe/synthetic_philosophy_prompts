I am in the fourth year of a doctoral program, and my advisor has spent two decades developing a contractualist account of wrongness in the tradition that runs through Scanlon: an act is wrong if it would be ruled out by principles no one could reasonably reject, where a reason for rejection is always some particular person's and is never summed across persons. She insists on what her commentators call the individualist restriction, and she objects sharply when anyone describes her view as lexical, though she has never said what else it is. For two years she has closed our weekly seminar by pronouncing on a case one of us brings. I have kept a record, because I believe she is running a rule she has not stated and perhaps could not state. Here it is, in the order I collected it.

1. A boat is sinking. You can pull either one passenger or five aboard the single raft. Save the five. She rejects the coin flip: where the complaints on the two sides are of equal strength, the fact that there are five of them is itself a reason.

2. The same boat, except that the five would each lose both hands while the one would drown. Save the one who would drown. We raised the five to five hundred, and then to five hundred thousand. She said the number was beside the point and that raising it changed nothing but our nerves.

3. To fund a treatment that saves the life of one identifiable child, the state levies ten dollars on each of forty million taxpayers. Not merely permissible: required. Asked whether five hundred dollars per taxpayer would be, she hesitated for the first time since I have known her, said the case was genuinely hard, and declined to rule.

4. Two industrial safety schemes with identical expected fatalities, five per year in each. Under the first, two hundred workers each face a one-in-forty annual chance of death. Under the second, four million residents each face a one-in-eight-hundred-thousand chance. The second is permissible; the first is not. We pointed out that the workers are adults, informed, well paid, and freely contracted. She said that changes nothing, and specifically that her objection has nothing to do with their information, their preferences, or their bargaining position.

5. A warehouse firm offers its staff a choice between a two-thousand-dollar annual bonus and a fifteen-hundred-dollar bonus plus a clause permitting supervisors to search lockers and read work devices at will. Nearly every employee would take the larger bonus, and in practice the clause would seldom be used. She says the firm may not make the offer at all — not that it may make it regretfully. When we said that nothing bad is likely to happen to anybody, she agreed that nothing bad is likely to happen to anybody, and said that was not the question. I note that this sits uneasily beside 3, and she has never addressed the tension.

6. A transit change returns four minutes a day to each of ten million commuters, and in exchange one technician faces a one-in-three annual chance of death on the job. Impermissible. The same change with the technician's risk at one-in-a-hundred-thousand: permissible, and not a close call.

As far as I can tell, all six are consistent with several quite different machines. The candidates I have written down are: a severity-threshold view on which burdens below a certain level count for nothing and burdens above it are compared person to person, with numbers settling ties among equals; an ex ante view on which each person's complaint is fixed by the risk she is made to bear rather than by what eventually happens to anyone, together with a ceiling on how much risk may be loaded onto one person; and a status view on which what matters is whether a principle puts anyone in a position where others may act upon her body, her home, or her prospects without her having any standing to forbid it, regardless of how much harm actually follows. I cannot tell which she has. My own suspicion is that 4 and 6 rule out the first in its simplest form, but I am not confident, since a defender might say the imposition of a risk is itself a harm of a determinate kind.

Next week I am bringing the following case, and I want to have predicted her verdict before she gives it.

A rare pediatric condition will kill thirty named, identified children in one region this year. A single preventive exists. Producing it requires one healthy adult to undergo a procedure carrying a one-in-four chance of killing her and, if she survives, a year of severe pain and permanent partial disability. There is a volunteer: forty years old, fully informed, uncoerced, uninduced, unpaid, unrelated to any of the children, who has read the consent documents twice and wants to proceed. The physiology does not permit the burden to be spread — there is no dispersed alternative at any cost, no third option, no delaying tactic, and no further technology on the horizon. If she does not undergo the procedure, the thirty children die. Nothing in the case turns on statistical anonymity.

I would like the following.

(a) State at least three candidate rules, each precise enough to yield a determinate verdict on the new case and each defensible as an account of 1 through 6. Where a rule must be amended to accommodate a particular verdict, say what the amendment costs it.

(b) Predict her verdict on the new case, with a rough confidence, and identify what in the record drives the prediction. I want a commitment, not a hedge. If you think she forbids the procedure, say also whether you think she would call it wrong or would call it a tragedy that no one may bring about, since she treats those as different judgments.

(c) Verdict 1 shows that she thinks numbers count; verdicts 4 and 6 show that she will not trade concentrated risk against dispersed. This is the first case I have managed to construct in which those two commitments collide with no exit, because there is no dispersed option and no threshold to retreat behind. Tell me which commitment you expect to give way, and what she would thereby be adopting: state the principle she would be committed to if she rules one way and the principle she would be abandoning, in each direction.

(d) Does the volunteer's consent matter on your reconstruction? Verdicts 4 and 5 suggest she does not think consent is doing the work in those cases. Say whether that generalizes here, or whether 4 and 5 have a feature — an employer, an offer, an asymmetry of power — that is absent from the new case, and, if so, whether its absence is sufficient.

(e) Name the single further case that would discriminate most efficiently among the surviving rules, and explain why it beats the obvious alternatives, such as a variant in which the volunteer is selected by lottery rather than stepping forward, or one in which her risk is smaller and the number of children larger.

(f) Finally, suppose a hospital committee has adopted her published framework, has no further guidance, and the case is mine to decide rather than hers to predict. Which ruling best coheres with 1 through 6, which of the six is the closest analog to the new case, and, where the record genuinely runs out, what principle would each available ruling commit the committee to?
