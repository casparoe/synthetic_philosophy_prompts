I write a science-and-ideas column, mostly about how the AI labs actually think, and I'm two weeks out from a long piece on the alignment strategy that goes roughly like this: take a human working on a hard question, let them farm out sub-questions to more humans, let those humans farm out sub-sub-questions, and what bubbles up to the top is its a kind of idealized deliberation no single person could run on their own; then train a model to imitate the whole tree, and repeat, so each round the student is a bit better than the teacher and the tree can be a bit shallower. There's a vocabulary around it — an assistant that answers questions about its own reasoning, factoring a cognition into pieces that fit in one head, the limit you approach as the tree gets bigger. I get the pitch. What I don't have is any sense of what it would take for the pitch to be *right*.

So before I quote anybody, write me the spec. What does an account of idealized deliberation of this shape actually have to deliver to count as adequate, as opposed to evocative? I want a numbered list of requirements, and for each one the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a nice-to-have. Some things I'd want covered — feel free to add to this and to tell me I've got the emphasis wrong:

- when the assistant is asked a question about its own reasoning, what exactly is it answering? if that isn't pinned down, "faithful" has no content
- why checking a sub-answer is supposed to be easier than producing it, and the cases where that just isn't true
- tasks that don't factor, where the sub-problems are as hard as the whole
- error: the humans aren't reliable, and mistakes compound down a deep tree
- what makes this more than "run more compute" — i.e. what's cognitive about the idealization rather than brute-force
- what the distilled student has to carry over, and what it's allowed to lose
- whether the idealization is a limit we never reach or an engineering target, and why it matters which one you mean
- adversarial material getting into the tree, i.e. a sub-answer that looks right

Also — and this is the part I actually care about most — mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp or another. My suspicion is that a bunch of things people demand here (that the tree be shallow, that the human be one person rather than a panel, that the reasoning stay readable after the fact) are definately just taste dressed up as principle. And if some subset of the requirements can't all be met at once, say so plainly and show me where the tension bites: my guess is the pair "produces output better than a human can check" and "correctness is still grounded in human judgment" is where it breaks, but tell me whether that's naive, survivable with some trick, or actually fatal. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate accounts.

Finish with a very short scorecard — the amplification story itself, debate-style setups, plain scaling plus feedback from human raters, and just letting a model think longer at inference time — two or three lines each against the list. Don't pad it, the list is the deliverable.

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list, I'll chase sources myself. Define any term of art the first time you use it, because my editor isn't a philosopher and neither am I. A table is fine for the scorecard.
