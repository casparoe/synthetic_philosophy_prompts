For eleven years I have helped organize a monthly reading group that began, in somebody's living room, as a slog through the Sequences and has since settled into a standing excuse for a dozen of us to argue about whatever the host of the month finds troubling. This term we did six weeks on infinity, in roughly this order: Zeno and the Aristotelian potential infinite; Dedekind's characterization of an infinite set as one that can be matched off against a proper part of itself; Cantor's diagonal; Hilbert's hotel, which Hilbert floated in a lecture on the infinite in the mid-1920s and which most of us first met in Gamow's 1947 book; the use Craig makes of the hotel in the Kalam argument, to the effect that an actual infinite cannot exist in the real world; and a closing week on finitism — Brouwer, Weyl's predicative reconstruction of the continuum, and what Gödel's second theorem did to the program of securing the infinite by finitary means. The final session is next Thursday in a library basement, and I have been asked to close it by reading something aloud. I would like a sestina: thirty-nine lines, six sestets and a three-line envoi, deliverable in about three minutes to a room containing two retired Bell Labs engineers, a philosophy-of-religion graduate student who takes Craig seriously and is not wrong to, an actuary, and four people who came for the coffee.

I want a sestina specifically because its machinery is the hotel's machinery inverted, and I would like the poem to be conscious of that. The six end-words return according to the fixed permutation 6-1-5-2-4-3, and that permutation is a single six-cycle: after the sixth stanza the end-words stand in exactly the order in which they began, everything has come home, and the form is a bijection on a finite set — a closed recurrence with nowhere left to go. The hotel's shuffles are the opposite kind of map. When the clerk sends the guest in room *n* to room *n*+1, or, to clear every odd-numbered corridor at a stroke, to room 2*n*, he is matching the whole hotel onto a proper part of itself, which on Dedekind's criterion is precisely what makes it infinite; no guest ever returns to the room he started in, and the map never closes. Let the form be the recurrence that comes home and the subject be the one that does not, and let the reader feel the mismatch without being told about it. One further lexical requirement, which matters more than it sounds: one of the six end-words should slide over the course of the poem from a verb into a noun — the clerk's counting, a process that is never finished, hardening by the sixth stanza into the count, the register, the completed list. The slide should never be flagged or pun-flagged, because that slide *is* the finitist's case against the hotel, and I would rather the poem commit it than diagnose it.

As for the arc: the first two sestets are the single late arrival at a hotel where every room is already taken, and the shuffling that follows; the third and fourth are the convoy — infinitely many coaches, each with infinitely many passengers — housed by prime powers and unique factorization, so that the guarantee the register offers turns out to be arithmetic rather than hospitality. Two things should be visible in that stretch: the cost, since whichever scheme the clerk chooses leaves whole corridors of room numbers permanently dark (numbers that are not prime powers, or that carry a prime other than two or three), and the quiet premise, since the method only works if each coachload has already been put into a sequence, seats numbered before anybody is housed — a favour the hotel has not earned and cannot itself supply. The fifth sestet belongs to the graduate student, and he must be given his best argument rather than a straw one: a hotel that can always accommodate one more guest has thereby confessed that its guests are never all present at once; what has been built is a rule for going on, not a finished building; the actual infinite is a way of speaking about a process, and treating the process as a thing is the original sin. The sixth is the clerk's reply, and it must not be a shrug or a mere quip — it should carry something like Hilbert's landlord confidence about a paradise built for us from which we shall not be expelled, without naming anyone. Then the envoi, all six end-words present, three of them closing the lines and three buried inside them, and this is where the guest arrives whom no scheme in the poem could have housed: not because the hotel is full, but because the diagonal of the register, with each digit altered, is a name no line of the list ever bore. Please honor these constraints strictly: exact repetition of the end-words, with no rhyme-substitutes, near-homophones, inflected variants, or singular-for-plural swaps, since the form's whole argument depends on the words being the same words; no proper names in the poem, though the specialists should be able to place each of them from what is described; *infinite* and *infinity* no more than once between them and never as an end-word; and none of the register of popular-mathematics wonder — nothing mind-bending, nothing about infinite possibilities, nothing that turns the hotel into an allegory for the human heart, for hospitality, or for grief. Finally, the poem should not adjudicate: the envoi should not decide between the clerk and the finitist, but should simply let the diagonal guest into the room with both of them, and stop.
