I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of history and I am finalizing the in-class exam. Please write one essay question on presentism in historiography that a strong student could answer in seventy-five minutes, together with a model answer of roughly 700 words and a marking scheme out of 25 points.

The question must be built around a specific case that the candidates will not have encountered in reading, so that reciting the standard anachronism debate earns nothing. Use this one: a seminar participant is working with the case notes of a provincial British physician from the 1840s who recorded a cluster of patients — nearly all recently confined women — as suffering from disordered nerves, and who prescribed extended bed rest, isolation from children, and bleeding. The participant wants to write that these women were suffering from postpartum depression and that the treatment aggravated it, but a supervisor objects on two separate grounds: first, that the diagnosis imports a twentieth-century nosology onto descriptions the physician and his patients would not have recognized; second, that calling the treatment harmful amounts to judging a practitioner by standards he had no access to. The exam question should force candidates to keep those two objections apart and to explain why answering the second does nothing to answer the first.

The crux I want tested is whether candidates can articulate an actual criterion for when retrospective description is legitimate, rather than naming the problem. A top answer should propose something like a continuity or recognition test — the historian may use a later concept only if she can show what in the agents' own conceptual and normative resources licenses it, and she should say what happens when nothing in those resources does. It should also confront the asymmetry that a strictly period-authentic vocabulary can make certain wrongs literally inexpressible, so the demand for purity is not neutral, and it should address the reflexive difficulty that the historian's own concepts, including the concept of anachronism, are themselves historically situated.

Please make the marking scheme discriminate sharply: name the specific moves that separate a first-class answer from a merely competent one, allocate points to each, and list at least three characteristic mistakes with the deduction each attracts — for instance, an answer that treats conceptual and evaluative presentism as a single issue, one that invokes the familiar formula about history being a dialogue between present and past as though it settled anything, or one that concludes relativism follows from the situatedness of the historian's vocabulary. Write everything in American English.
