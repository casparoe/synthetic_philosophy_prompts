I'm in a philosophy-of-law seminar working through Lemkin and the 1948 Genocide Convention, and somebody built an argument that seems to prove far too much. Our tutor just smiled at it and said we should sort it out ourselves, so here I am. I can't find the exit.

Setup, so you know what we're working with: Lemkin coined the term in 1944 and understood a genocidal plan much more broadly than the Convention eventually did — for him it ran across every field of a group's life, its language, schools, courts, churches, economy and family structure, not only its members' bodies. The Convention as adopted is narrower: four protected groups (national, ethnic, racial, religious — political groups were cut out), an intent to destroy the group as such in whole or in substantial part, and five listed acts. Oddly, the one act that looks cultural rather than bodily — forcibly transfering children of the group to another group — survived into the list.

The argument:

(1) The distinctive wrong of genocide is a wrong against a group and not just the sum of wrongs against its members; what gets destroyed is a people, and that is why it's treated as the crime of crimes rather than as a very large number of murders.
(2) A people exists only in so far as it can reproduce itself as a people — i.e. pass on its language, its practices, its institutions, its patterns of marriage and childrearing to the next generation.
(3) Whatever reliably stops that transmission destroys the people, no matter what means it uses and no matter whether anyone aimed at that destruction or merely at unity, progress, salvation or administrative efficiency.
(4) Assimilationist state policies — residential schools that take children away, bans on a language in school and public life, suppression of religious institutions, removal of children into out-group adoption — reliably stop that transmission.
(5) So those policies are genocide, and their survivors were right to call them that.
(6) But (3) makes no reference whatever to the agent's aim or to the means used, so the same reasoning covers policies not aimed at any particular group: compulsory schooling in a single national language, standardisation of dialects by bureaucrats, secularisation, urbanisation-driven language shift — the ordinary homogenising work of any modern state.
(7) Each of those destroys smaller peoples as peoples, on (2) and (3), just as surely as a residential school does.
(8) So either the French Third Republic schoolmaster in the Breton- and Occitan-speaking regions, the Prussian district administrator and every state that ever ran a national curriculum are guilty of genocide, or (1)–(3) are wrong.
(9) The first horn is intolerable: it drains the word of any discriminating power, and it insults the victims of actual extermination campaigns by filing them under the same heading as a school inspector.
(10) Therefore genocide has to be defined by the destruction of members' bodies plus an intent directed at the group as such — roughly the Convention line — which leaves Lemkin's broader conception without a coherent foundation.

I don't want to accept (10). I'd much rather keep (5) than (10): I think the residential-school claim is right and I don't want to be shunted back into a body-count view, which is exactly what Lemkin was arguing against. But I also don't want to keep (9) only by biting a bullet, and I can't see how to do both.

So: tell me whether the chain actually holds. If it does, which premise is cheapest to give up and what do I pay for it? If it doesn't, which step breaks, and why did it look fine when I first read it — I want the equivocation or scope shift named explicitly, with both readings spelled out, not just a wave at "destruction is doing double duty". Then, because I don't trust abstractions on their own:

- Run your diagnosis on three cases and say whether it delivers the verdict I want in each: (a) a state that bans a minority language in schools and courts but otherwise leaves its speakers free to live, marry, worship and raise children as they like; (b) a state that runs boarding schools which remove children from their families for most of the year and punish them for speaking their own language, but kills and sterilises nobody; (c) a language that dies because everyone moves to a city for work and nobody legislated anything.
- Price the alternatives properly, not in one line each: the Convention/intentionalist reading, the Lemkin-inspired cultural-genocide revision, and the view that keeps group-level harm but drops the requirement that anyone intended it. What does each have to give up to escape this argument?
- Tell me what the child-transfer clause does to your diagnosis. It's sitting in the Convention's own list of acts, which looks like the Convention is half-committed to (2) and (3) already, and I can't tell whether that rescues Lemkin or undermines the narrow reading from inside.
