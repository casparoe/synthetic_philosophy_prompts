I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, but I wrote up some notes for a blog post after an incident review at work and I want a sanity check before I post it. Tell me where it's wrong or just confused.

The incident: a service was down for about four hours. Nothing "did" it in the obvious sense — no bad deploy, no traffic spike, no hardware failure. A background job that was supposed to renew a TLS cert had silently stopped running three weeks earlier, and the alerting on that job had been deleted in a refactor. So the postmortem says the outage was caused by the job not running. That bugged me: how can a non-thing cause a thing?

My notes, roughly the thesis I want to argue:

1. Omissions aren't just a funny way of talking about events, but they're not free-floating either. There's a real difference between "the cert job didn't run" and "the cert expired because nothing renewed it." The second one has a subject and a job description attached to it.

2. My proposal: an absence is a cause only when there's something like a live watchdog in the situation — some standing arrangement that was set up to bring the event about, that was still running or available at the time, and that failed to fire. I dont mean that as a metaphor, I mean it structurally: a real process, still instantiated, whose function was to produce the event. The cert job was scheduled and the machine was up; the watchdog was live and just didn't do its thing. So the omission is a cause. Where no such arrangement exists, the absence is just background noise about the world.

3. This is supposed to fix the obvious objection, which is that if absences count as causes then everybody's failure to water my neighbor's tomatoes while he's away caused them to die, including mine, and I've never even met the guy. On my view none of those are causes, because none of us had a live watchdog aimed at those tomatoes. The neighbor who set up a sprinkler timer and let it break does, and he's the one we blame. So expectations, norms, and who-was-supposed-to-do-what drop out of the analysis entirely — it becomes a fact about the structure of the situation, not about obligations.

4. Bonus: it handles the case where the omission is itself produced by an earlier event. My teammate deleted the alerting. That's a positive event that removed the watchdog, and then the missing watchdog let the cert lapse unnoticed. So you get an ordinary causal chain running through a gap, which is exactly what I want to be able to write in a postmortem.

Where I'd like your comments:

- Does 3 actually solve the problem or just relocate it? My worry is that "live watchdog" is doing secret work — something had to set the watchdog up in the first place, and maybe that's where norms and obligations sneak back in through the back door.
- Give me the sharpest counterexample you can, preferably from ordinary non-software life, so I know whether the view is just an artifact of my day job.
- Is 4 even consistent with 2? I think I'm letting the absence of a watchdog matter, which looks like I've re-admitted arbitrary absences one level up.
- Is any of this interesting to someone who thinks about causation for a living, or is it a rediscovery with worse vocabulary? Be blunt, I can take it.
