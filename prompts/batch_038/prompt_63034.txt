For a paper on punishment theory I have been developing a reductio against the unfair-advantage account of retributivism: the view that punishment is justified not by the harm it repays but by the restoration of a fair distribution of liberty, since law-abiding citizens restrain themselves, the offender declines to, and punishment takes back the extra freedom he arrogated. My argument seems to show that this account makes people punishable for their characters rather than their acts, and I am fairly confident something has slipped, but I cannot locate where.

Here is the argument.

(1) On the unfair-advantage account, what punishment annuls is the offender's extra share of liberty, and that share is measured by the liberty he claimed rather than by the harm he caused.
(2) The liberty an agent claims is determined by the maxim he acts on: what he takes is exactly the freedom that maxim requires, since it is the maxim, not the world's cooperation, that expresses his will.
(3) Therefore desert is fixed by the maxim, and two agents who act on the same maxim deserve the same punishment however differently things turn out. (This is the step I most want to keep: it yields the verdict that a thwarted attempter and a successful killer deserve equally, and it makes constitutive and resultant luck irrelevant to sentence.)
(4) But an agent who is firmly resolved to act on a maxim at the first opportunity, and who is prevented only by circumstances he does not control, already acts on that maxim: resolution plus a settled disposition to execute it is the same practical state as the attempt, differing merely in the timing of its outward expression.
(5) By (2) and (3), the resolved agent has therefore already claimed the same share of liberty and already deserves the same punishment.
(6) The same reasoning reaches the agent who has formed no resolution but is such that he would offend if he could do so with impunity: his maxim, in the sense that (2) requires, is the offender's maxim, and (3) makes the outward expression irrelevant to desert.
(7) Hence desert attaches to character and not to conduct: whoever would break the law if he could get away with it has already taken the extra share and already deserves it removed.
(8) Since nearly everyone is like that with respect to some prohibition or other, nearly everyone is liable to punishment, and the amount each deserves is set by the most permissive maxim he would be willing to adopt.
(9) A theory that renders almost the whole population punishable, in amounts fixed by hypothetical choices they never made, is not a theory of punishment. So the unfair-advantage account is false.

My attempted patch is to insert a manifestation requirement between (3) and (4): a share of liberty is claimed only when the maxim is exercised in conduct, because before that there is no unfair distribution to annul — the compliant have not yet been restrained on his behalf, and he has taken nothing from them. But I worry that this either collapses into a harm requirement, in which case the account loses its distinctive appeal and lets moral luck back in at (3), or else is a stipulation that blocks the reductio without showing why (4) is false, since (4) is offered as a claim about the identity of practical states rather than about timing.

Which step do you reject, and where exactly is the pressure coming from? I would like an answer to four things. First, name the step and explain why it looked innocent when I wrote it down. Second, say whether (8) genuinely follows from (7) or whether I have run together what an agent would do under some possible circumstance with what he has taken under this actual scheme. Third, price the repairs: what does the account pay if it rejects (2), if it rejects (4), and if it accepts (5) but constrains punishment by an independent principle limiting when the state may act at all? Fourth, tell me whether the patch above is a repair or a relocation of the problem. I would rather keep (3) than any other premise, but if that preference is what is distorting the argument, I want to hear that instead.
