I'm an observational cosmologist. My working life is completeness corrections — selection functions, V/Vmax tests, inverse-probability weights, volume-limited subsamples when I don't trust my selection function. Basically zero philosophy training.

A friend who works in philosophy of physics keeps trying to get me interested in the Sleeping Beauty problem, and every time I tell him how I'd handle it he says I'm begging the question. Here's my version. Tell me where it breaks.

Setup as I understand it: Sunday, a fair coin is flipped. Heads, she's woken Monday only. Tails, she's woken Monday and Tuesday, with a drug in between that wipes her memory of Monday, so the Tuesday waking is from the inside indistinguisable from the Monday one. Each time she's woken she's asked her credence that the coin came up heads, then put back to sleep.

My read: this is a selection effect and I already have the machinery. Write down the selection function. Conditional on heads there's one waking at which she's asked; conditional on tails there are two; she knows both numbers before the experiment starts. Her being-asked is a single detection out of a pool whose size depends on the quantity she's trying to estimate. That's Malmquist bias in a different hat — the chance an object makes it into my catalog depends on the luminosity I'm trying to measure. Same fix: weight by 1/P(selection), get odds 1:2, credence 1/3. And I've got the frequency check I'd actually run: bet a dollar at every waking, and heads wins only a third of the wagers, because tails hands her two bets to heads' one. Finally, when I don't trust a selection function I retreat to a volume-limited subsample, and the analogue here is conditioning on her knowing which day it is — at which point both sides apparently agree it's 1/2. So I'd like to say the whole fight is about which sample your conditioning on, and that the two positions are just two estimators, neither of them wrong about the coin.

His objection is that I've assumed the disputed thing: that wakings constitute a sample at all, that there's a population being drawn from, that she's a random draw out of it. He says self-location isn't a sampling process, so there's no selection function to write down, only the appearance of one.

So restate the halfer/thirder dispute in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage, without smuggling my assumptions in and calling it translation. Specifically: does the 1/P weighting actually reproduce the thirder's argument, or am I flattering myself? What has to be distorted to fit — especially the halfer's insistence that waking up gives her no relevant new evidence, since I can't express "no new evidence" without already talking about detection? And what has no counterpart in my framework whatsoever? My guess is it's that the two tails-wakings are the same person: if I detect one galaxy across three epochs I deduplicate and call it one object, and I've got an external fact — a position on the sky — licensing that move, whereas all she's got is amnesia. Tell me if that guess is right or if the real residue sits somewhere else.

Then give me the verdict. Is my framework missing something genuine, some kind of indexical information that isn't a measurement on a sample? Or is their disagreement less substantive than it looks — two estimators over two samples and a lot of noise? Be blunt about where the analogy is doing sneaky work, including in the betting argument, since I know a halfer can object that those wagers aren't independent.
