Background first: I run a two-person bookkeeping practice, no academic training whatsoever, but I've been reading AI-strategy stuff online for years and one idea has lodged in my brain. It's the commitment races thing — roughly, that when two sides both gain from locking themselves into a stance in advance (we retaliate if you do X, we fold if you do Y), there's pressure to be the one who commits first and most visibly, and that pressure can end up worse for everyone even when all parties would've preferred a world where nobody committed to anything. I've been explaining it badly to friends for about a year.

Our library does a Thursday "big ideas" night and the librarian asked me to take 45 minutes next month. Whiteboard, maybe twenty people, retired folks and a few shift workers, no maths beyond school. I don't want you to write the explanation for me — I want the plan for it.

So:

Order. What has to be in place before what, and why. My problem is I always open with "what makes a threat believable" and the room goes flat, and I think it's because they don't yet see why tying your own hands could ever be an advantage. Tell me the actual dependency chain and where my usual opening is going wrong.

The example. I want one running case for the whole session, not three. My candidates are two supermarkets on the same road, a landlord versus a tenant, and the old story about a commander burning his own boats so his men can't retreat. Which one, and what does it have to contain and — more useful — what must it NOT contain? I'm worried a war-flavoured example makes everyone immediately think nukes and then I've lost them to a different topic.

Here's the wrinkle I can't resolve and I need you to handle it in the plan, not dodge it. About six years ago the two big grocers in my town both publicly announced they'd match any competitor's price cut. I expected a death spiral. Instead prices just sat high and stable and everybody seemed happy. That looks like a counterexample to the "commitment races make things worse" story, but I suspect it's actually the same machinery pointed somewhere else. Tell me whether to use it in the session, at what point, and what I have to have already established or it'll just confuse them.

Misconceptions. I'm already braced for "isn't this just an arms race" and "so a commitment is just a promise." Walk me through the ones I'm not anticipating, and for each one say exactly where in the sequence I should head it off — and say which ones I should actually encourage, because I get the feeling one of them is a necessary stepping stone rather than an error.

The check. One question I can put to the room at the end that tests whether they grasped the race part and not just the commitment part. It has to be a question where "got it wrong" looks different from "didn't follow at all," otherwise I learn nothing.

And what to cut. I have a bad habit of bolting on the whole advanced-AI-agents version at the end and I suspect that's the first thing to drop — but tell me if you'd cut something else instead to buy the room for it.

No reading list and no titles, they won't read anything and I couldn't remember them anyway.
