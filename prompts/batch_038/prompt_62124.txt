I am drafting a handout for a graduate seminar on aggregation, and the argument I have sketched has slipped out of my control. I intended it as a reductio of non-additivism about harms across persons — the view on which no number of dust specks, distributed among any number of people, can be worse than one person's torture — but it now appears to establish something I do not believe, and I cannot locate the step at which it escapes me.

(1) If harms fail to add across persons, the explanation must lie in how they are distributed rather than in what they are intrinsically like; and the only candidate explanation available is that in the interpersonal case there is no single subject for whom they sum, no one whose experience contains the aggregate.

(2) I accept a reductionist account of personal identity, on which a person is a series of momentary stages bound together by psychological continuity and connectedness; and no one stage's experience contains the aggregate of a life's harms either.

(3) So the reason identified in (1) applies with equal force to additivity across the stages of a single life.

(4) Hence, for any severe harm H and any strictly lesser harm h, no number of instances of h spread across a life is worse than one instance of H. A rational agent should therefore prefer eighty years of continuous dull toothache to one minute in the dentist's chair.

(5) The natural reductionist reply — that connectedness supplies the missing subject — is unavailable. Connectedness comes in degrees and can be arbitrarily weak between distant stages; if additivity tracked it, prudential concern would have to be graded accordingly, and once grading is admitted the difference between two persons and between two distant stages of one person is a difference of degree rather than of kind.

(6) So (4) stands; and by parity of reasoning the same holds for benefits, which makes the additive structure of prudential reasoning systematically mistaken.

(7) Therefore nothing can explain the failure of additivity across persons: non-additivism is not merely unmotivated but incapable of being motivated, and the dust-speck intuition has no legitimate ground whatsoever.

I would rather keep (2) than any other premise, since the reductionist picture is doing independent work for me elsewhere in the course. Which step should I give up, why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it down, and what does giving it up cost? I am especially keen to know whether any available rejection leaves me able to say something about the grip of the dust-speck intuition, or whether I must simply concede that it has none.
