The idea. The "taxation is theft" argument and most of the standard replies to it seem to assume there's a determinate fact about how much of my paycheck was mine before the state touched it. I don't think there is, and I think that's where the debate is actualy stuck. My salary number is downstream of a huge amount of legally constituted stuff: the currency it's denominated in, whether my employer is a legal person with limited liability, whether the equity component is worth anything at all, contract enforcement, IP, the fact that I learned to program somewhere that somebody paid for. So pre-tax income looks less like a natural quantity and more like the output of a model with a lot of free parameters. What made this click for me comes from ML: when you do feature attribution on a model's prediction you have to pick a baseline to compare against — all-zeros, the dataset mean, some reference input — and different baselines give genuinely different attributions for the exact same prediction. Nobody thinks there's a baseline-free answer to "how much did this feature contribute." My hunch is that the tax debate has the same shape: there's a joint product, and the real argument is over which decomposition principle gets to carve it up, not over a fact of the matter the state is violating. So the question I want to chase is whether the indeterminacy of the pre-tax baseline makes "taxation is theft" unfalsifiable, or merely expressive, or flatly confused — or whether it just relocates the dispute onto a second-order question about which decomposition principle is justified, in which case the slogan could be perfectly coherent and my whole angle is deflated before it starts.
