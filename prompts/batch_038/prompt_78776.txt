Right, so I've been asked to write down my welfare-economics commitments for a workshop where everyone else's job is to attack them, and I'd like a dry run first. I work on allocating a fixed health budget, so all of this is meant to be operational rather than armchair. Here's the list, roughly as I'd defend it:

1. Welfarism, plus expected utility all the way down. Only individual wellbeing matters morally. Each patient's wellbeing over the prospects we can offer them is represented by a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function, unique up to a positive affine transformation. And my own ranking of prospects satisfies the same axioms — completeness, transitivity, continuity, independence. I'm not interested in lexicographic or rank-dependent escapes.

2. Ex ante Pareto, both halves of it. If every individual is indifferent between two prospects, so am I; if everyone weakly prefers one and somebody strictly prefers it, I strictly prefer it. My reason is that its the minimum condition for taking each person's own good seriously instead of substituting mine.

3. Anonymity. Permuting who gets which utility leaves my ranking untouched.

4. I accept Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and I think it applies to my setup. Given 1–3 the social evaluation is a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, and the weights are pinned down by the Pareto condition rather than chosen by me. I lean on that quite a lot when people accuse me of smuggling in my own values: the theorem does the ethical work and I just read the answer off it.

5. I'm a Robbins-type sceptic about interpersonal comparisons of utility. I don't think there's any fact of the matter about whether a unit of your utility is the same size as a unit of mine, and I don't think my framework needs one, because I never compare anyone's utility with anyone else's — I only ever add them up, as 4 tells me to.

6. But I'm not a utilitarian. I'm a prioritarian: I put each person's utility through a strictly concave increasing transform before summing, so that gains to the worse off count for more. I moved over to this from egalitarianism precisely to avoid the levelling-down problem. People sometimes say 6 clashes with 3, on the grounds that weighting by level is just a way of weighting some people over others. I don't think that's right, but I've never been able to say why in a sentence.

7. A case I'm confident about. Same budget, two identified patients. Programme SURE gives each of them 100 units of wellbeing. Programme TOSS flips a fair coin: heads, patient A gets 200 and patient B gets nothing; tails, the other way round. Each patient is exactly indifferent between the two programmes. I rank SURE strictly above TOSS, and I regard that as a clean application of 6 rather than as a difficulty for it.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what has to be given up and what the cheapest fix costs me — including anything I'd lose that I haven't thought of. I'd particularly like to know wether my verdict in 7 is something 1–6 jointly deliver, something they permit but don't require, or something they flatly rule out; if it's the last, tell me which commitment the verdict is really resting on and whether there's more than one way to rebuild it. And please don't give me a general lecture on Harsanyi or on prioritarianism — I want the audit, not the survey.
