I'm the series producer on a three-part documentary about disease and climate in human history, and I should declare that I'm no longer a neutral party: I came to this project after a decade reporting on drought-driven displacement, and I think the non-human side of the ledger has been systematically underweighted. Our two academic consultants have been exchanging notes for five months and have now stopped replying to each other entirely. One is an environmental historian who wants a pathogen, a volcanic aerosol plume, and a monsoon failure to carry real explanatory weight in the narrative. The other is a social historian who believes that framing, whatever its intentions, reinstates the determinism the field spent decades dismantling, and who objects that we're using the word "actor" to smuggle in a causal claim we haven't earned. Before I can lock the outline, I need the document they should have written together: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration that both could sign.

Please draft it. I want five things.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims about non-human causes in history — what both can honestly sign, phrased so that each side recognizes it as their own view. Make these substantive rather than platitudinous; "epidemics changed what people faced" is too thin to be worth writing down.

2. A disagreement table. For each genuine disagreement, state it in each side's own preferred wording, and then classify it. I suspect they've been collapsing three rungs of a ladder into one, and I want each dispute sorted onto exactly one: is it about causal weight (how much of the variance in outcomes a non-human factor accounts for), about explanatory primacy (which factor a correct explanation of this event must cite first), or about narrative salience (which factor a story should make its protagonist, given that explanation and storytelling are different tasks)? Say so explicitly if you think one of the rungs does no work.

3. A short section on whether calling a virus or an aerosol an "actor" or an "agent" is doing real work or is merely verbal. If part of their dispute is verbal, say precisely which part and why, and say what survives once the vocabulary is neutralized — including whether goal-directedness produced by selection rather than by intention can qualify as agency under any criterion both would accept.

4. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of the two. For each, name a concrete episode or piece of evidence both would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Serviceable episodes include the 1815 eruption of Tambora and the harvest failures of 1816; the potato blight and the Irish famine of the 1840s, where the pathogen and the political economy of relief are hard to prize apart; and the post-Black Death movement in real wages and labour bargaining in England. Add better ones if they occur to you.

5. A closing section in which they state the social historian's actual worry about determinism, and decide whether it is epistemic (monocausal inference, unfalsifiable post-hoc attribution) or moral and political (the historical deployment of climate-and-geography arguments to justify domination). Then require them to say whether that moral worry has any bearing whatsoever on whether the causal claims are true.

Don't include a bibliography, footnotes, or citations — I want the structure of the disagreement, not references. If you'd have to guess at a work or an author, leave it out.

Two further requests. Tell me where this document is most likely to be wrong: which agreed claim you suspect one side would in fact refuse to sign, which crux you've mislabeled as genuine, and what consideration would make you move a line from the agreements column into the disagreements table. And flag anywhere you've leaned on a contested reading of the historiography rather than a settled one, since I'll have to defend those choices to a standards editor.
