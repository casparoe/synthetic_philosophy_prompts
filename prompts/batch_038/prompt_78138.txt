I run a multidisciplinary design-optimization group in aerospace. Our daily vocabulary is design spaces, feasible regions, Pareto dominance and trade fronts, scalarization and weighting schemes, existence-of-an-optimum questions, and the optimizer-versus-satisficer distinction. I am fluent in that vocabulary and nearly illiterate in early modern philosophy: my exposure to Leibniz amounts to the slogan that this is the best of all possible worlds, encountered mostly as a punchline.

I would like that doctrine translated into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can manage, and then audited. Below are five claims that make up my own reconstruction; they may be mis-stated, in which case correct them before translating. For each, give me (a) the closest formulation in optimization terms, (b) a verdict on the mapping — faithful, faithful only under distortion, or inexpressible — and (c) an account of what the distortion or the gap consists in.

1. The space of possible worlds functions as the design space over which God's choice ranges.
2. Compossibility is a constraint: not every combination of good things can be jointly realized, so the choice is over the feasible region rather than over the unconstrained space. If compossibility is something other than a constraint set — a matter of internal relations among substances, say — tell me, because that would change item 1.
3. Best is a maximum, so the doctrine requires the objective actually to attain one. I suspect Leibniz needs a compactness argument he never supplies, and that the familiar objection — that there may be no best world, only an endless series of better ones — is precisely the unbounded-objective case.
4. Leibniz characterizes the best as the greatest richness of effects obtained through the greatest simplicity of means. To me that reads like a ratio objective, or a scalarization of two competing criteria. If it is a scalarization, where do the weights come from, given that Leibniz denies that goodness is whatever God happens to will?
5. God's choice is determined by the best and yet free. I have nothing here. In my vocabulary a unique determined optimum means there is no choice left to make.

Two constraints on the exercise. First, do not smuggle in the assumptions my framework brings by default — that an objective function is handed to the optimizer by a client, that the feasible region is fixed prior to the search, that best always means best-for-some-stated-purpose — and then present them as translation. Wherever such an assumption is doing work in your rendering, flag it as an importation. Second, treat the residue as the deliverable rather than as a coda. I want to know whether what fails to translate exposes a limitation of engineering vocabulary — perhaps it simply has no way to speak of a goodness that is not goodness-for-a-purpose — or shows that Leibniz's claims were less determinate than the slogan suggests, for instance that best is used as though a single ordering of worlds were already settled when no measure has been given.

If you think the assignment cannot be done honestly — that any optimization rendering will collapse into my assumptions dressed in Leibniz's clothes — say so at the top rather than complying politely, and say what a better framing would be.

Commit rather than hedge: one verdict per item, the two or three places where your confidence is genuinely low identified and explained, and where interpreters disagree in a way that changes the translation, take a side and sketch in a sentence what the other side would do to it. No bibliography or reading list; I cannot look anything up. Please write in American English.
