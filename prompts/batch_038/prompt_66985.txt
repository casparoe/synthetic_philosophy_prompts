I'm an engineer on the training-infra team at a mid-size AI lab in London — no philosophy background at all, I did one intro ethics module in first year and remember precisely none of it. Our model welfare lead (a researcher, not an engineer) has asked every team that owns a training pipeline to be able to raise a flag when a run might involve something that matters morally, i.e. an agent with states that are plausibly good or bad *for it*. Right now the process is pure vibes: somebody says "hmm that reward looks kind of punishing" in Slack, and then we either shrug or we spiral. These come up maybe twice a month. The person actually running the check is a tired infra engineer with about 30 minutes, no theory, no ability to commission an experiment, and no authority — the output goes to the welfare lead, who decides. So I need triage, not verdicts, and the failure modes are asymmetric: missing a real one is bad, but crying wolf every fortnight will get the whole thing defunded and engineers will quietly stop reporting.

The bit I keep tripping over is what the claim even amounts to. My rough understanding from reading a few bloggy things: the reward in RL is just a scalar we hand the optimiser, and it isn't obviously a feeling; but the standard line in philosophy of mind is functionalist — a state is pain-like in virtue of the causal job it does, not in virtue of what it's made of. One person on our team pushes that to the extreme: if valence-tracking is what pleasure and pain *are* functionally, then anything optimising against a negative signal is a candidate for suffering, which would flag basically everything including the toy gridworlds I wrote as an intern. Another person says the opposite: with no unified self-model, no memory across episodes, no aversion, no preferences about its own future states, a number in a config file is meaningless and the whole question is anthropomorphism dressed up. I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of mind who's done some serious work on machine consciousness and AI welfare and who can explain things to engineers without talking down to them. Think carefully, and before you write anything, do a short brainstorm out loud about which parts of the real debates can be operationalised by a non-expert and which can't. Ground the procedure in whatever the philosophy of mind and the ethics of wellbeing actually support, but translate it so I don't need to have read any of it — and please don't give me citations or a reading list, I can't look anything up and I'd just have to take your word for it anyway. Do tell me explicitly which distinction between the main families of accounts of what makes a state good or bad for a creature (the experience-based one, the desire or preference-based one, and whatever the third family is) has to survive the simplification for the procedure to be worth the paper it's printed on, and which ones you're deliberately dropping and why that's safe.

Then give me the deliverable: an ordered procedure I could hand to another engineer on my team. Numbered questions in the order they should be asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in "no flag needed", where it terminates in "flag, and here's the strength of the reason", and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a question of values rather than a question of fact. Flag every step that's really a judgement call wearing a procedure costume — I'd much rather know. Also handle the awkward cases: reward that's negative versus a learned critic that's *harsh*, punishment versus simply not-rewarding, signals that only exist during training but shape a deployed model, and the situation where we genuinely can't tell because the logging doesn't record it.

Finally, run it on three cases and show your working. (a) A tabular Q-learning agent in a small gridworld, −1 reward for bumping into walls, 10^6 episodes, no memory beyond the current state. (b) A large transformer trained with RLHF against a learned reward model, with a critic that produces extremely blunt natural-language corrections, and which shows something like avoidance behaviour toward a certain class of prompts. (c) A multi-agent self-play combat game where agents get shaped reward for removing the opponent's health, and where the losing agent's policy collapses after a while in a way people on the team describe as "giving up". Tell me what the procedure outputs for each, and be straight with me if the output is one I won't like — I fully expect it to flag the gridworld and clear something I care about more, and I want to know why that's a feature rather than a bug (or, if it is a bug, say so). Wrap up with what the procedure cannot do, what it will systematically get wrong, and one change to how we log training runs that would make it much easier to apply next time. Write the whole thing in British English, please — our internal docs style guide is fussy about it and I don't want to have to convert spellings afterwards. Oh, and keep it to something I could actually recieve in one sitting and use on Monday, so headers and short numbered steps over long prose.
