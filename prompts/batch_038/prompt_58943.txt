I write features on science and ideas for a UK broadsheet, and I am currently drafting a long piece about a dispute in moral philosophy that my editor has rightly described as unprintable in its present form: how much a theory of morality may demand of a comfortably off person before the demands themselves count against the theory. Rather than another he-said/she-said conference report, I want to publish a document — specifically, the joint statement that an adversarial collaboration between the two camps ought to have produced and, so far as I can tell, never has. I should like you to draft that statement. The two parties are these. Camp A, whom I shall call the Threshold Objectors, hold that the burdens a normative theory places on ordinary agents are evidence bearing on the theory's acceptability: a theory that instructs people to keep transferring resources until their own marginal sacrifice weighs as heavily as the marginal benefit thereby effaces the distinction between what morality requires and what it merely commends, estranges agents from the projects and attachments that make a life theirs, and offers no point at which anyone can truthfully say they have done enough. Camp B, whom I shall call the Revisionists, hold that this objection covertly treats the current, remarkably undemanding shape of commonsense morality as the baseline against which "too much" gets measured, when that baseline is precisely what is in dispute; that the intuitions of the affluent are contaminated by an obvious asymmetry of interest, since agents have a standing motive to believe morality asks little of them and no comparable motive in the other direction; and that a theory's being demanding is therefore at most a prompt to recheck one's reasoning, not a defeater — with genuine excesses to be diagnosed through justice, integrity or futility rather than through sheer magnitude. Please write each camp's position in language its own members would sign, not in the unflattering paraphrase its opponents prefer.

I have also been running a case of my own devising past both camps, and I should like it built into the document as a shared test rather than left out as journalist's garnish. Call it the Graduated Giving Trial: a public-sector employer operates a payroll scheme in which every employee's donation rate is visible in an internal register, the recipient organisation publishes the marginal effect of each tranche, and the scheme comes in two variants. In the flat variant, an additional one per cent of salary buys the same amount of benefit regardless of what colleagues do. In the threshold variant, the money funds a coverage-sensitive intervention — a vaccination campaign, say — so that the marginal value of any individual's contribution depends sharply on whether aggregate giving clears a level that, as it happens, the workforce has not reached. From this I want the collaboration to work with two notions I have found useful and which I suspect are where the real fault line runs. The first is the coupling function: the demand a theory places on you expressed as a function of others' compliance. My conjecture is that the camps may agree about what is owed in a world of full compliance and in a world of near-total non-compliance, and disagree almost entirely about the shape in between — in which case a great deal of the published argument is about the middle of the curve while both sides talk as though it were about its endpoints. The second is the ratchet intuition: the suspicion that Camp A's complaint is not that a theory asks for a large quantity but that it never asks for a final one, since for any contribution satisfying the theory there is a marginally larger one the theory requires instead, leaving the agent's situation without a resting point. If that is right, the objection should still fire in cases where the demanded sacrifice is trivially small but structurally open-ended — and the Revisionists' habit of asking "how much, exactly, would be too much?" would be a category mistake rather than a rebuttal. I want both conjectures treated as hypotheses the document tests, not as settled framings, and I want each camp allowed to say if it thinks my formulation misdescribes its own view.

The deliverable should have three parts. First, a numbered section of agreed claims: statements both camps could initial, covering the empirical background (for instance, that for at least some interventions additional funds yield substantial additional benefit, and that aggregate private giving sits far below what any candidate moral theory would require — though I expect the exact wording of these will need care), what the dispute is not about, and what each camp concedes the other is entitled to assume for the sake of the exercise. Second, a disagreements table, with columns for the dispute, Camp A's own formulation of its side, Camp B's own formulation of its side, a judgement as to whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal (and if verbal, what is gained by saying so), and what that camp would have to give up were it to lose. Third, a cruxes section: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side — I have in mind the coupling question, the ratchet question, and whether the contamination charge applies symmetrically to the Revisionists' own intuitions, but please prune these, sharpen them, and add at least one crux of your own — and, for each crux, a concrete case, argument or empirical consideration that both camps would accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance, before anyone knows how the case will come out. I am not asking for references, a reading list or a bibliography; I will add names myself later, so please attribute views only to Camp A and Camp B rather than to particular philosophers. Write the whole thing in British English, in spelling and idiom alike, since it will run in a UK title.

Finally, step outside the document for a closing paragraph in your own voice and tell me where it is most likely to have failed. Which of the agreed claims is really one camp's view dressed in neutral clothing? Which pre-registered prediction is, as you have stated it, unfalsifiable or else trivially satisfied? And — the part I most want — what would change your mind: what consideration would lead you to reclassify a disagreement you have marked substantive as merely verbal, or to abandon my coupling and ratchet conjectures as redescriptions that do no explanatory work? I would much rather print that caveat than a statement that reads tidier than the dispute actually is.
