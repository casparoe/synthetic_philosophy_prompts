I write about science and ideas for a general interest magazine and roughly a third of my beat is machine learning now. Problem: every piece I file that says "the model represents X" pulls in the same two letters. One is from a cognitive scientist who says I've used the word as decoration — that nothing in a network is a representation of anything until someone with a decoder and a task shows up, and that every interpretability result is an artifact of whatever method produced it. The other is from a researcher who says the thing is right there: they find the direction, they ablate it and the behaviour changes, they add it back and the behaviour changes, and if that isnt a representation then nothing in a brain is either. I've run both. Neither convinced me, and I dont think either convinced the other, so I've stopped trying to referee the fight and started trying to work out what I should have been demanding in the first place.

So: write me the desiderata for an account of what it's for a network to represent something. The purpose is narrow and I want you to keep it in view — an account good enough that when I write "the model represents the layout of the maze," that sentence says something that could have come out false, and isn't just a redescription of the training data or of my own choice of decoder.

Give me the requirements with the case behind each one. At minimum I want it to handle: a model representing something falsely, and what that costs you; a direction that's there and steerable but never shows up in any behaviour the model actually produces; a behaviour the model reliably produces with nothing decodable behind it; the same concept recovered in two models with incompatible geometries; units carrying several unrelated contents at once; and contents that survive only in one narrow band of layers and get wiped by fine-tuning on paraphrases. And tell me which distinctions it cant collapse — representation versus use, existing versus being relied on, content versus cause, decodable-by-us versus decodable-in-principle.

Then the part I actually care about: which of these demands are real requirements on the concept, and which are one camp's preferences wearing a labcoat. And whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable — my hunch is that non-arbitrariness, decodability-in-principle, and the fact that weights plus data plus architecture fix everything can't all be kept at once, but I may be talking nonsense, and there's the old objection that if you get to pick the decoding a wall can be said to run any program, which might already settle it against the whole enterprise. Say which single requirement does the most work ruling candidates out, and finish with a few lines on which of the families of account people actually offer — causal-role ones, decoder-relativist ones, ones grounded in what the training process was for — clears the bar. A table is fine for that last bit.
