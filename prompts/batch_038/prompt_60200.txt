I sit on the appraisal panel of a national transport authority, which must settle the evidence base for valuing travel time savings in the case for a new intercity rail link. Our guidance permits either source but does not rank them. On the table: over a decade, households along the corridor have traded longer journeys against cheaper housing at a rate implying a value of time well below our survey estimates, and when a faster, dearer peak service was introduced, uptake was thin. Meanwhile, the same respondents say in surveys that they would pay a considerable premium to cut twenty minutes from the trip. One wrinkle complicates the choice data. Long-standing commuters report being content with a ninety-minute door-to-door journey, while households that arrived in the corridor within the last three years describe the identical journey as a serious loss — so it is unclear whether the veterans' revealed valuation records what that time is worth to them or only an adjustment to what they can afford.

Half the panel wants choice data alone, on the ground that only choices show what people actually value; the other half holds that choices are contaminated by such adjustment and wants the survey numbers. Draft the panel's decision memo: recommend one basis, or an explicit rule for weighting the two, with reasons the losing side could actually answer; take seriously the objection that stated preferences are cheap talk; and state which conception of welfare the recommendation presupposes — what people choose, or how well off they are. Close with a brief note on how confident you are in the recommendation and on the single point in it you are least sure of.
