An agronomy co-op kept two seasons of records comparing two seed varieties, classing each field as dry upland or wet bottomland. "Success" means reaching target yield. Varieties were not assigned at random; growers chose what to plant.

- Dry region: X succeeded on 175 of 350 fields (50%); Y on 20 of 50 (40%).
- Wet region: X succeeded on 45 of 50 fields (90%); Y on 280 of 350 (80%).
- Pooled: X succeeded on 220 of 400 (55%); Y on 300 of 400 (75%).

A member of my reading group built the following argument out of these numbers. I cannot accept its conclusion, but I have not been able to say where it breaks.

(1) What a decision requires is the difference the option makes for the case actually being decided.
(2) A grower choosing a variety is choosing for a field that lies in one region or the other, so what she requires is the within-region difference.
(3) X beats Y in the dry region and X beats Y in the wet region; therefore X is better for every field she might own.
(4) The pooled figures nevertheless rank Y above X.
(5) A pooled figure that contradicts the correct answer for every case it pools cannot be a noisier estimate of the same quantity; it is an artifact, recording nothing but the fact that this co-op happened to plant X mostly on dry land.
(6) Which figures count as pooled and which as within-group is merely a matter of how finely you slice, and you can always slice finer: soil depth, slope, previous crop, season.
(7) By (5), every coarser figure is an artifact of how the finer categories happen to be distributed across treatments.
(8) So no rate computed from observational data measures the effect of anything, and the quarrel between X-growers and Y-growers can never be settled by evidence, since each side can point to a slicing that favors it.
(9) Therefore "X is better than Y" is true only relative to a choice of slicing, and there is no such thing as the effect of a variety.

One further datum he did not use: in a second trial the co-op recorded whether each field developed a mid-season fungal infection, a condition itself partly caused by which variety was planted. Conditioning on that record reverses a comparison the agronomists regard as settled.

I would rather keep (5) than (9) — the pooled number does look as though it measures nothing here — but I see that (5) is what drives the rest. Which step should I reject? Why did it look unobjectionable on a first reading, what does giving it up cost, and does the infection variable in the second trial change the diagnosis or confirm it?
