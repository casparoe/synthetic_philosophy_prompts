I convene a reading group in the philosophy of mathematics, and for two terms now its two most vocal members have been arguing past one another. One is a classical set theorist of broadly Cantorian persuasion: infinite totalities exist completed, all their members there at once; the natural numbers form a definite collection and the reals a strictly larger one; and the fact that a proper part of an infinite collection can be matched one-to-one with the whole is a theorem to be accepted rather than an absurdity to be fled. The other is a potentialist with finitist sympathies: there is no completed infinity, only a process that can always be continued; claims ostensibly about infinite totalities are to be read either as claims about the finite stages we have so far reached, or modally, as claims about what would hold in any possible extension of the universe as it stands. Each regards the other as either confused or evasive, and neither has ever written down what the two of them actually share. I should like you to draft the joint statement they ought to have produced.

Begin with the claims both can sign. Please do not pad this section with trivialities such as that both accept modus ponens; I want substantive shared ground, and I want it stress-tested. Candidates I would like you to consider, and to reject with reasons if you think one camp cannot honestly accept them: that there is no greatest natural number; that any particular finite computation can in principle be carried out; that the diagonal argument establishes what it establishes only relative to granting the totalities over which it quantifies, so that both camps accept it as a conditional even while disagreeing about whether the condition is satisfied; that the behaviour of Hilbert's hotel is a restatement of Dedekind-infiniteness rather than a contradiction, and that a set may nonetheless be infinite in the plain sense, failing to correspond to any natural number, without being Dedekind-infinite, since that equivalence is not provable without some weak principle of choice; that Goodstein's theorem is true, is not provable in first-order Peano arithmetic, and is proved by transfinite induction up to the first epsilon number, each camp glossing that cluster of facts in its own way; and that the incompleteness theorems do not by themselves decide the dispute.

Then set out the disagreements one by one, each phrased so that its own camp would accept the wording of its own side. That is the difficult part of the exercise, and I would rather have three well-drawn disagreements than a dozen straw men.

Then the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody. I suspect at least three. First, whether the potentialist's modal idiom yields a genuine alternative foundation or is a notational variant of the actualist's practice, and what each camp says about whether the two positions agree on every first-order arithmetical consequence. Second, whether an indispensability argument can be run from finite combinatorics, of the kind surveyed in reverse mathematics, where a statement about finite trees or finite sequences resists every weak system but falls out immediately from axioms quantifying over infinite sets or well-orderings; here each camp should record a prediction about how far up that hierarchy the phenomenon reaches and what would follow if it stopped early. Third, whether the actualist, by refusing a completed totality of all sets and retreating to proper classes, is a potentialist at the second order without admitting it. For each crux, name a case, argument, or result both would agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction in advance, as one pre-registers a hypothesis.

Please also state what each camp would count as evidence or argument against its own position, not merely against the other's, and say whether there is anything at all that both agree would move the debate, or whether the emptiness of that answer is itself the finding. Record, briefly, whether either camp can say anything coherent about the more extreme view that doubts whether very large numerals denote, and whether the finitist's denial leaves them with an unacceptable vagueness about which numbers there are. Finally, if you judge some part of the dispute to be merely verbal, say which part and give your reason, and distinguish it clearly from the part that is not.

One presentational request. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a communiqué to be signed or a letter to the seminar, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text and no tables. Paragraph breaks are of course welcome, but I should like to be able to read it aloud without announcing the formatting.
