You're a professional philosopher who also happens to be good at writing speeches, so please think this through carefully before drafting anything.

I'm giving my aunt's eulogy on Saturday. She died at 84. She was a historian of decline — her whole career was on the cyclical side of the argument about history, the Spengler-and-Toynbee end of it, and she used to tell grad students that "progress" is just a provincialism with good PR. Her own twist, the thing she was still arguing in her unfinished last book, was that every cyclical theory secretly borrows a linear premise: to say that Rome fell and that the West will fall the same way, you have to stand outside both cycles and measure them against one common timeline. She called it the survey problem, and she thought it made cyclical history a kind of honest defeatism rather than a prediction.

Here's my problem. The room will have my cousins and her siblings — small-town Ohio, a lot of them churchgoing — plus maybe twenty of her former students, several of whom are now working historians. I can't say she's watching over us; she was an atheist and would have definately hated it. But I also can't fall back on the usual consolation that her work will outlast us, because her entire point was that nothing outlasts its civilization, and the students would catch me faking it in a second. So the speech has to comfort people who loved her without asserting anything she'd have called false, and I'd like the survey problem to do real structural work in it — not show up as a cute reference, but actually generate the consolation, something like: standing outside the cycle and being able to see the whole shape of a life is exactly what the people who loved her are now in a position to do, and she knew she'd be handing that over.

Two things before the speech: a short brainstorm of what is and isn't sayable given her views, and one or two alternate consolations you considered and rejected and why. Then the eulogy itself, 700–900 words, spoken-aloud rhythm, American English. Not reverent in a generic way, no "in these difficult times," and please don't make her sound like a fortune cookie — she was funny and a bit mean.
