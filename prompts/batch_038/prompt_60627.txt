Had a two hour argument with my friend Nina last night about when a theory actually gets credit for fitting the data, and we both ended up talking past each other, so I want a clean principle. Here are my gut verdicts, case by case:

1. Mercury's orbit was off from Newton's predictions, everyone knew it for decades, and then Einstein's theory falls out of it exactly. Strong confirmation. The fact that the discrepancy was already old news doesn't seem to matter to me.
2. A lab announces a surprising result, and a theory gets quietly patched the next week with two new tunable parameters until it produces that same result. Fitting it now confirms the patched version barely at all.
3. A theory predicts some specific eclipse effect that would have been a disaster if wrong, and it comes out right. Huge confirmation.
4. A theory predicts a rare event that was probably going to happen anyway just from background rates, and it happens. Almost nothing, for me.
5. Same setup as 4 but with a second, simpler theory that also predicts it — I'd say the simple one is confirmed more than the baroque one, even though they're both right and the evidence is identical.
6. The eclipse case, but where I later learn the theorist had been told the answer in private before publishing. The prediction still came out of the theory and it still came true — but I don't feel nearly as good about it.

Nina's line is that what matters is only whether the evidence raises the probability of the theory, so 1 and 6 should feel the same and 3 and 4 differ only in how surprising the outcome was beforehand. Mine is that something about the theorist's own epistemic situation sneaks in, which she says is incoherent because a theory's support can't depend on who knew what.

What I want: the principle that actually generates my list, or if none does, the two best candidates and what each one costs me. Then give me a table with these columns: verdict_id (1–6), verdict_predicted (does the principle say confirmed strongly / weakly / not at all, matching my gut or not), driver (the single feature of the case doing the work), and note (one line, and if it doesn't match my verdict, say what I'd have to give up to keep the principle simple). After the table, settle 1 and 6 explicitly — do they really come apart, or is one of my verdicts just a bias about people rather than about evidence?
