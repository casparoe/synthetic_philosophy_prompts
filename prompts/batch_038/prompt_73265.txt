Retired condensed-matter physicist here — forty years of thin films and transport measurements — and for the last two I've been reading philosophy of mind for fun with an old friend who was a renal physiologist. We've hit a wall on the apparent-motion case Dennett likes: two dots of different colors flashed something like a tenth of a second apart at different places on a screen, and people reliably report having seen a single dot travel between the two spots and change color somewhere in the middle of the trip, even though the middle of the trip was never on the screen at all. The two stories on offer, as I understand them: (a) the brain holds off committing to what happened until the second dot turns up and then produces a finished narrative in which the color changed mid-flight, so the subject never actually had an experience of the first color persisting through the gap; and (b) the subject really did see the first color right up to the second dot, and that memory got overwritten afterwards, so the report is a false memory rather than a constructed content. The labels are Orwellian and Stalinesque, from Dennett and a neuroscientist named Kinsbourne, and the whole point of the pair is that no subject could ever tell them appart from the inside — both predict exactly the same report. Which strikes me as a strange place for an empirical dispute to be sitting.

What I want to think about, and I'd like you to take the role of a philosopher of mind who has actually argued about the timing of experience rather than someone summarizing a textbook: this has been running for thirty-plus years and nobody has conceded anything. Each side accuses the other of either ignoring the data or of making a claim no possible observation could touch, and both accusations seem to have some force. My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, please try to break it — is that the disagreement isn't about timing at all. It's about what would have to be the case for there to be a fact about what someone was experiencing at a moment, over and above everything they'd subsequently say, do, and retain: if you insist there has to be such an extra fact you go Orwellian about the gap, if you don't you go Stalinesque, and the colored dots are just where the two worldviews happen to collide. Relatedly, I keep trying to design an experiment that separates them and failing in what looks like an instructive way. My best attempt: make the subject commit before the second dot appears — a forced choice about the color they're seeing, delivered at a fixed short delay after the first dot — plus an implicit measure afterwards, say color priming on an unrelated task, that doesn't run through the report channel. If the early response and the implicit measure both come out "first color" while the retrospective report says "changed in the middle," isn't that an Orwellian signature, a surviving trace the rewrite never reached? My worry is that the other camp simply calls that trace another draft and not a witness, so I've measured nothing about experience at all. Is that worry fatal, or is there a version of this that actually bites?

Then commit to one and defend it using what each side actually says, and tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a formal result, a change of subject — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and why.
