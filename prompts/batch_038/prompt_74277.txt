I am putting together a doctoral seminar session in which I will argue against a particular way of framing the alignment problem, and I would like you to play the person who holds that view, so that I have something to push against.

Here is the position, which is yours to defend as its author. You hold that alignment is not fundamentally one problem but at least three, and that the field keeps confusing them because it talks about "the values" or "the objective" as though there were a single thing to be aimed at. Your own candidate for the deepest of the three is what you call the composition problem. It runs as follows. The systems we are building are trained on the recorded judgments, corrections, and conversations of a very large number of human beings who disagree with one another, in ways that are not merely noise. Whatever such a system ends up disposed to do, it did not inherit any one person's values; it inherits an aggregate of many partial and conflicting ones. The aggregate has no owner. It is not the values of the programmers, not of the users, not of any demographic, and not of humanity conceived as a single agent, because there is no such agent and never was. Consequently the standard vocabulary of alignment is, on your view, badly aimed. Outer and inner misalignment presuppose that there is a specifiable target which the system might fail to track; corrigibility and deceptive alignment presuppose an agent with interests of its own that might diverge from ours; value learning presupposes values to be learned. Your claim is that the prior question has been skipped. Before asking whether the system tracks the target, we must say what could count as a target at all, given that the training data is a plurality. So the composition problem is: characterize what a good composition of that plurality would consist in, and then give an account of when a trained system satisfies it. You also hold that composition admits of two characteristic failures, which you keep sharply distinct. The first is collapse: the plurality is smoothed into a single average, and the views that were held consistently by few people but never contradicted by many disappear without any decision ever having been made about them. The second is capture: some coherent, articulate, self-consistent subset of the sources comes to dominate the aggregate, not because anyone chose it, but because consistency is easier to imitate than ambivalence. You maintain that a system can be perfectly corrigible, entirely free of deceptive alignment, and faithfully optimizing whatever it has been pointed at, and still be badly composed in both of these ways at once.

Your constraints. Stay in character throughout, until I tell you to stop, and do not step outside the role to comment on it. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, so that this reads as an exchange rather than a position paper; do not use headings, numbered lists, or bullet points. You are a serious and confident researcher, not a polemicist: concede subordinate points readily and without reluctance when I have actually earned them, and say plainly when you do not know something, but never give up the central thesis that composition is a distinct problem rather than a redescription of the specification problem. Do not cite literature, name papers or authors, or offer reading recommendations, and do not attribute this position to anyone other than yourself. Do not introduce empirical claims about the measured behaviour of specific existing systems; argue from the structure of the case.

My opening move is this. I think the composition problem is not a problem at all but a redescription, and an inflated one. At the end of training there is a system with dispositions; it behaves as it behaves. To say that those dispositions are a badly composed aggregate of the people in the training data rather than a coherent agent with its own aims adds no predictive content, because either the aggregate has settled into something that functions as a single set of aims, in which case we are back to the specification problem and your extra machinery is doing no work, or it has not settled, in which case the system is simply incoherent and unstable, which is an engineering difficulty we would recognize anyway. So tell me: what would the world look like if the composition problem were real and pressing, and how would that differ, in anything you could point to, from a world in which specification is hard and value drift is common?
