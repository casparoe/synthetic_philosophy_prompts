Next week I run a fifty-minute section on the St Petersburg paradox for first-year philosophy students. They can compute an expected value but have no measure theory, and in week nine they meet Savage's axioms, so my section has to leave them able to say what problem those axioms are meant to fix. What I want from you is the design of the explanation, not the explanation itself.

Set out the sequence and its dependencies — in particular, whether the divergence of the expectation should be established on the board before I ask what they would pay to play, or after, and what is lost either way. Choose the single version of the game that ought to carry the section, and say what it must contain and what it must not: I have been toying with a capped variant, and I need to know whether the cap belongs in the carrying case from the outset or should arrive late as a complication. Then map the points at which students will believe the difficulty has been removed when it has not — I expect some version of 'it only shows that people are hopeless with large numbers', 'take logarithmic utility and you are done', and 'no payer has unlimited resources, so there was never a puzzle' — and place an intervention at each. The crux I care about most is that the diminishing-marginal-utility move and the claim that any acceptable utility function must be bounded above are two different moves, and students will fuse them into one; order things so that they cannot.

Finish with a single question I can put to the room that would reveal whether they now see a problem for the theory of expected value rather than for gamblers' prices, and list what I should leave out at this level and announce that I am leaving out. Please write in British English.
