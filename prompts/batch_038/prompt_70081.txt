I'm the only paid staff at a small nonprofit foundation — me plus a volunteer treasurer — that maintains an open technical standard and its reference implementation. Companies join at three tiers: Contributor (free, no vote), Member ($5k/yr, a seat in the general assembly), Sustaining ($50k/yr, a board seat). I get maybe four or five proposals a year to change the requirements or the fees, and right now I evaluate them on vibes and then the board argues about it for two hours.

I want a procedure we can actually run on a proposed requirement. The question is always some version of: does this cost enough, and cost in the right way, to tell us something we couldn't otherwise know about whether a company will still be here in five years and still be contributing code? I know the rough shape of the idea — a signal only works if its cheaper for the type you want than for the type you don't, and there's a real difference between requirements that can't be faked at all and ones that are merely expensive — but I can't get from that to steps.

So give me the ordered questions to ask about any proposed fee or membership requirement, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which answers kill the proposal outright, and where the procedure has to stop and hand the decision to the board because it's about who we want to be rather than about information. It has to run in about an hour, by me, with no economist on call, and end in one page I can put in front of the board. Mark clearly which steps look like procedure but are really judgment. And tell me what it can't do — what kind of proposal it will wave through that's actually bad.

Then run it on three live ones:

1. Drop the Sustaining fee to $10k but require the company to second one full-time engineer to the project for at least 12 months. I love this one. Don't soften the verdict if it fails.
2. A $2,500 non-refundable application fee at every tier, "to filter out the tire-kickers," plus mandatory in-person attendance at the annual meeting.
3. Waiving all fees for companies under 20 people. Half our actual commits come from two such companies and we'd loose that signal entirely.

As a check on whether the procedure is any good: two years ago a large firm paid the $50k, took the board seat, contributed one commit in fourteen months, and left. Would your procedure have flagged that in advance? If not, what would have to be added — and would the addition break case 1 or case 3?

One more thing it should be honest about. The fees aren't only read by us; the community reads them too, and a big corporate check can cost us credibility with the individual contributors who do the actual work. Fold that in if it belongs in the same procedure, but say so plainly if it's a different question than the one I'm asking.
