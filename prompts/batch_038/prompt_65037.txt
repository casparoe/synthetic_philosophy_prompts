I'm a research assistant on a comparative-politics consortium that maintains a hand-curated registry of regime change. For each country-year a five-person coding committee assigns one of three labels — no flag, backsliding (erosion while elections still decide who governs), or regime break (the point past which the old regime is simply gone and "erosion" is the wrong description) — and publishes a short written rationale for every decision, including the decisions not to code. Candidate cases circulate internally under pseudonyms until the note is published, so I'll call the one now in front of us Aurelia. The committee is split two to two, the chair is on medical leave, and under the registry's standing rule a tie leaves the previous year's code in place, which in Aurelia's case means no flag. Before I go back to them, I want to work out what the precedents actually commit them to.

The precedents I've transcribed. Hungary from 2011: a party that had just won a two-thirds supermajority in a free election replaced the constitution, redrew the electoral map, expanded and re-staffed the constitutional court, and consolidated most private news outlets under allied ownership; coded backsliding, on the reasoning that the legal changes mattered "not because they were irregular — nearly all of them were perfectly regular — but because each one moved a referee institution into the governing party's hands and made the next election less able to undo the one before it." Poland 2016 through 2019: the governing party paralyzed and then re-staffed the constitutional tribunal, merged the justice ministry with the prosecutor's office, replaced public-broadcaster management, and created a disciplinary chamber for judges, while leaving the electoral law, the electoral commission and the private press alone; coded backsliding, with the note observing that the opposition kept winning big-city mayoralties and that everything done so far could be undone by ordinary legislation. Turkey 2017: after two years of emergency rule, mass dismissals by decree, the imprisonment of an opposition party's co-chairs, and a constitutional referendum held under that emergency rule whose narrow result turned on a mid-count decision by the electoral board to admit unstamped ballots, the committee moved Turkey from backsliding to regime break, writing that once emergency decrees were no longer reviewable and opposition campaigning was curtailed by force rather than by rule, the erosion vocabulary described the wrong thing. The United States 2020–21: a sitting president's refusal to concede, the pressure campaign on state election administrators, the competing slates of electors, and the attack on the Capitol; coded backsliding for a single country-year and then allowed to lapse, even though not one statute was amended and the count was completed, because the breach was one of forbearance and "conduct, unlike structure, can stop." In that same note the committee expressly declined to flag partisan gerrymandering, the malapportionment of the Senate and the Electoral College, voter-identification laws and polling-place closures, on the ground that these are long-standing structural features of the regime rather than recent departures from it. Two declines matter just as much. Ecuador, where the legislature used the constitution's own amendment procedure to lift presidential term limits, the incumbent stood down anyway, his party's candidate won and then broke with him, and a referendum three years later put the limits back: declined, as "incumbent-advantageous rather than anti-democratic, and reversed by the ordinary machinery inside one electoral cycle." And the United Kingdom in 2019, where a prime minister advised the head of state to prorogue Parliament for five weeks ahead of a major deadline, the Supreme Court unanimously held the prorogation unlawful and void, and Parliament resumed sitting the next day with no resistance from the government: declined, because "a breached convention and a court that could reverse the breach inside a fortnight are not the same datum."

Aurelia is a presidential democracy of some forty million people that has held competitive elections since the early 1990s and changed governing parties four times, never disputed. In 2021 a newly elected president's party won a slim legislative majority. Since then: a statute moved the electoral commission's administration under the interior ministry and let the president name four of its nine members — the constitutional court struck down the appointments but upheld the administrative transfer; a constitutional amendment, carried by the required two-thirds and ratified in a referendum the court had authorized, permitted one consecutive presidential re-election where consecutive re-election had been banned; the state's advertising budget was redirected overwhelmingly to friendly broadcasters and two critical stations lost their licenses on stated technical grounds; a public-security emergency decree has been renewed by the legislature every ninety days for three years, suspending certain pretrial protections in designated districts, and the court has upheld each renewal; and a former interior minister turned opposition figure was prosecuted and convicted of corruption in a trial that observers called procedurally regular but conspicuously timed. Against all of this: no judge has been removed, disciplined or retired early; the court has struck down two further government measures; the leading opposition candidate is on the ballot; and in last year's municipal elections the opposition won three of the five largest cities including the capital, on the highest turnout in two decades, with the ruling party's losing mayors conceding on the night and no credible allegation of fraud.

So: which precedent is Aurelia's closest analog, and what does that analogy actually license? Reconstruct at least three candidate coding rules each of which fits every precedent above, Hungary through the United Kingdom, and say what each yields for Aurelia — I expect them to diverge, and I want the divergence located precisely, not gestured at. Then predict the committee's verdict and defend the rule you think they are really running, paying attention to which phrases in their rationales are load-bearing rather than decorative. Tell me what principle the committee would be adopting if it defaults to no flag on the tie rather than deciding, and whether that default is neutral or is quietly one of the candidate rules in disguise. I'll also confess the worry that brought me to you: every decline on this list protects a practice that is old, and every flag attaches to one that is new, and I cannot tell whether that is a principled baseline-relative rule or a standing arrangement for only ever scrutinizing countries we expected to scrutinize. Say whether the precedents support that suspicion, and finish by describing one further country-year, concretely enough that I could put it in front of the committee, that would come out differently under your top two candidate rules.
