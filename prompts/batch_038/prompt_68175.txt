Twenty times a year, my clinical ethics consultation service is asked the same question, and we have no standing way to answer it. A patient with advanced dementia faces a treatment decision — feeding tube, dialysis, transfer to the ICU, amputation — and there is a signed advance directive, but the patient is presently content: she eats with apparent pleasure, hums, brightens when family visit, and shows no sign of suffering. The family and the attending disagree about whether the document should govern. I need a decision procedure my consultants can actually run, not an essay on why the question is hard.

The consultants are a social worker, a chaplain, two nurses, and a hospitalist on rotation. None has studied philosophy, none will look anything up, and a typical consult allows forty-five minutes at the bedside plus a same-day write-up that the attending — and, if the family contests, the committee chair — must sign. Do not name authors, cite studies, or recommend further reading; write for someone who will use this standing in a doorway.

Our board has adopted a distinction I now need made operational. It sorts directive language into three kinds: **authority-bearing** (the writer was exercising decision-making power over a future she anticipated and described), **evidence-bearing** (the writer was reporting what she took herself to want, without claiming to bind anyone later), and **aspiration-bearing** (the writer was voicing a hope or a value she did not expect to be tested). The distinction is meant to do work: only authority-bearing language overrides the present patient's welfare; evidence-bearing language counts alongside everything else we know about her wishes; aspiration-bearing language may be honored only if honoring it costs her nothing she can now register.

Deliver:

1. The questions to ask, in order, about the document and about the patient, with what counts in the text or the history as a sufficient answer to each, and how a consultant tells the three categories apart when a single sentence seems to sit in two of them.
2. The two exits — where the procedure stops at "the directive governs" and where it stops at "it does not" — plus the points that are judgment dressed as procedure and must be handed to the attending or the chair rather than resolved.
3. The procedure run on both of these. *Case A:* one handwritten paragraph, written six weeks after her husband died, saying she does not want to be kept alive the way her mother was; witnessed and filed, never mentioned again to anyone; she is now six years into dementia. *Case B:* a lawyer-drafted directive with a named surrogate, a detailed paragraph refusing artificial nutrition, and a separate closing sentence about wanting to die at home; she now swallows poorly but brightens at hand-feeding, and the surrogate wants the tube placed.

In one of the two cases the procedure should reach a result I expect to dislike; say which and why it comes out that way. Close by naming two real situations in which following this procedure will produce a decision you believe is wrong.

Open with two sentences stating what the procedure can settle and what it cannot, before anything else. Number the steps and keep each to four lines or fewer. Hard limit: 1,100 words total.
