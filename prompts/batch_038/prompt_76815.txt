I sit on the twelve-member panel that advises our finance ministry on how wellbeing enters cost-benefit analysis. Our appraisal guidance is modelled closely on the UK Treasury's supplementary Green Book guidance on valuing wellbeing, and it uses the wellbeing-adjusted life year as its unit — one point on a 0–10 life-satisfaction scale, for one person, for one year. Benefit is then summed linearly and without weighting across everyone affected, and monetised at a single value per unit. That linearity has just stopped being a background assumption. A patient organisation representing roughly 2,400 carriers of a rare hereditary phenotype — markedly elevated positive affect, near-absent negative affect, unusually high pain tolerance — has filed a consultation response against the draft rule I wrote last spring, which would have capped the recognised wellbeing level used in appraisal at the ninetieth percentile of the national distribution, so that any gain accruing above that threshold counts only up to it. The test case is a proposed £180 million neuromodulation and psychosocial service that would treat about 6,000 people a year, of whom roughly 400 would be carriers. On the uncapped metric those 400 account for about 58 per cent of appraised benefit; under my cap, about 7 per cent, which is to say the service would be redesigned around everyone else. The organisation's response argues that the cap is a discount applied to a trait rather than to a measurement, and therefore penalises people for how much they are capable of feeling. I drafted the rule, I've been turning the problem over since my younger brother was identified as a carrier eleven years ago, and I have declared the interest and will abstain from the vote — but the chair has asked me to write the background opinion anyway, because I'm the only member who has worked with both the instrument and the petition. I want to be able to hand that memo to my brother without embarrassment, and to the panel's disability nominees without their concluding I've written my family's case.

The panel will not converge on its own. Five members are economists, two of them health economists who came up in cost-per-QALY technology appraisal, think subjective-wellbeing money-metrics are soft, and will insist that if we monetised properly through willingness to pay the whole dispute would dissolve. Two are statisticians from the national statistics office who think we are deciding far too early: we have only eighteen months of experience-sampling data, no evidence that the carriers' scores are not a scale-use artefact, and our own validation work found that their advantage is large on momentary-affect measures and small on life-evaluation measures — so which instrument we appraise with changes who counts as the claimant draining the budget. Two are disability nominees, one of whom uses a wheelchair; they object to capacity-to-benefit adjustments as a class because of how such adjustments have behaved inside health technology appraisal, and they will say that if reported intensity is allowed to count, the framework permanently favours whoever produces the largest numbers. One animal-welfare representative will ask whether capping intensity also caps our valuation of non-human animals, whose affect may be more concentrated per unit of anything we currently measure. One academic ethicist keeps insisting that we are treating Nozick's utility monster as though it were an empirical claimant when it was a reductio against aggregative views, and that the analogy is doing more work than anyone has examined. And the chair, a former treasury official, needs something he can defend to the finance minister on Friday.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting — about 1,200 words, decision-ready, with the recommendation stated in the opening paragraph and the rest in numbered sections. It has to give the panel wording it could actually vote on, and it should compare at least the four live options (no adjustment to linear summation; a hard cap at the ninetieth percentile; a smooth concave transformation of intensity with a floor; prioritarian weighting by baseline wellbeing level), explaining why the one you choose beats the others on grounds the economists, the statisticians and the disability nominees could each engage with rather than on grounds that appeal to only one of them. Include a short section on what evidence would establish a genuine intensity multiplier rather than a scale artefact, since the organisation has offered to co-fund a validation study and the chair will want conditions attached to any approval. Give the non-human precedent one paragraph, not a lecture. Then state the strongest case against your own recommendation in the voice of the member most likely to make it, and say plainly whether you think my instinct to cap is wrong. Work through the sketch I'd bring, correcting it where it fails: intensity claims are not self-authenticating, but a cap isn't either, since a truncated gain has no coherent interpretation in the willingness-to-pay metric we monetise with; any rule must be phenotype-neutral in form while remaining evidence-sensitive in application; the choice of instrument does more work than the choice of cap, because on life evaluation the carriers' advantage largely disappears; adaptation cuts both ways, since the carriers' scores may reflect adaptation to a life without pain while the people we rank as moderately satisfied may have adapted downward, so the distribution we are capping is not the distribution of welfare; and whatever we adopt will be invoked by every subsequent claimant group, including ones nobody has identified yet. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I want reasons a panel of non-philosophers can argue with on Thursday morning.
