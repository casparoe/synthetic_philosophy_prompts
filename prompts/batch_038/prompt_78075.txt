Here's the situation. I run a reading group, six of us, and we've been going through Eliot's treatment of religious people for a couple of months. We've ended up with what we think is a pretty consistent track record of her verdicts, and now we've hit a character in a contemporary novel that we can't agree about, and half the group is arguing by analogy to Bulstrode while the other half says that's cheap. Her rulings as we've compiled them (I'm giving you enough context on each that you shouldn't need to go hunting):

- Her own case, 1842: she tells a close friend she can no longer in conscience attend chapel and calls it a solemn renunciation of what she's come to think is false; her father is wrecked by it; she ends up going on attending with him for years anyway, out of love for him rather than belief. Her implied verdict on herself: honest unbelief plus willing participation in the form for someone else's sake is acceptable, maybe better than acceptable.
- The fashionable evangelical preacher she takes apart in one of her review-essays (the Dr Cumming piece): devout, rhetorically powerful, apparently generous. She argues his theology turns virtue into a means to a reward, glories in the damnation of other people, and treats this life as a waiting room. Verdict: morally corrosive, and worse than plain honest worldliness.
- Amos Barton, the first of her clerical sketches: vain, dull, a bad preacher, no spiritual gifts whatsoever, faintly ridiculous to his own parish. She insists we're obliged to pity and love him anyway. Verdict: approved, tenderly, in full awareness that he's no use.
- Mr Tryan and Janet Dempster, in the same collection: the sickly ascetic curate who turns around a lawyer's alcoholic wife. She honours the result and his self-sacrifice, and stays distinctly cool about the harsh creed and the self-mortification that came with it. Verdict: mixed — approved for the effect, kept at arm's length from the theology.
- Adam Bede: Dinah Morris, the Methodist preacher, tender and self-denying, is the moral centre of the book; and the easygoing old rector Irwine, who has no dogma and no enthusiasms, gets a defence from her too — notably when the young hero says he can't abide people who think their notions make them better than their neighbours, and the undogmatic rector pushes back on behalf of the enthusiasts. Verdict: both approved, on different grounds, and the man who wants to sort everyone's doctrine out loses the exchange.
- Romola and Savonarola: the sincere, self-sacrificing reformer is revered by her heroine early on, and she endorses the heroine leaving him once it's clear he'll subordinate truth and particular human claims to his cause — while never letting us forget that he was genuinely great. Verdict: reverence withdrawn on a very specific ground, not a dismissal.
- Bulstrode in Middlemarch: pious banker whose doctrine becomes a machine for flattering himself. Exposed and ruined. And yet she gives him a scene of real tenderness with his wife at the end and refuses to let the reader jeer. Verdict: condemned, pitied, and the one genuine thing left in him is an un-doctrinal human moment.
- Farebrother, also Middlemarch: hardly a creed to speak of, plays whist for money, entirely candid about his own weaknesses, and actually functions as a pastor. Her most likeable clergyman. Verdict: approved.
- Daniel Deronda: the young man raised without a faith or a people is portrayed as genuinely damaged by it, rootless, and finds a vocation only by taking on an inherited communal religious life; the believing Jew who lives inside the tradition gets a respect she gives essentially nobody Christian in the book. Verdict: religion as a shared inherited form of life is a good; rootless private sensibility is a deficit.

Now the new case. It's a novel published in the last few years, so nothing Eliot could have ruled on; I'll describe the character fully so you don't need to know the book.

A woman in her late fifties, raised Catholic, who says plainly and often that she believes none of it. Every Sunday she goes to Mass at her old parish, sits at the back, does not take communion, does not mouth the creed. She's there for the music, the smell, and because her mother and her brother are buried in the churchyard. She told the priest exactly what she's doing years ago and he tolerates her. She runs the parish food bank, gives away money she can't spare, and is reliably kind to people who can do nothing for her. But she has joined nothing, holds no role, will not be counted a member, and won't say the words even silently. She also writes a widely read column in which she describes herself as the only honest person in the building and refers to the congregation as her deluded flock — affectionately, and yet it's plain that she enjoys the position. Her grown children say she is warmer than she was ten years ago. That's the whole character; there's no secret past and no hidden hypocrisy, and the novel doesn't punish her or reward her, it just ends.
