I write about ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am reporting a long piece on how nineteenth-century liberalism squared its universalism with empire. My editor wants to open with a passage from a recent essay collection that frames the whole question through Mill. It runs roughly as follows:

Mill spent thirty-five years in the East India Company's London office, eventually heading its correspondence department, and he defended British rule in India without embarrassment. Some societies, he argued, had not reached the point at which self-government is possible, and for those the correct arrangement was administration by a more advanced power, carried out by specialists who knew the country. Democracy at home and despotism abroad were therefore perfectly consistent for him: the liberty principle governs civilised nations only, and peoples at an earlier stage of history simply fall outside it. Because he held that the entire point of such rule was the improvement of the governed, he fought the 1858 transfer of India to the Crown on the grounds that Parliament would be ruling a country it understood nothing about. His Indian writings thus show that liberal universalism ranked human beings from the outset, and that its offer of freedom was always conditional on being the right kind of people.

I would like an accuracy audit before any of this goes near my lede. Please take it sentence by sentence: what a specialist on Mill would sign off on, what is a defensible simplification, and what is wrong or misleading — with particular attention to whether the passage runs together two claims Mill kept distinct, or overstates a thesis he held in a narrower form. Then tell me whether the closing claim survives the corrections, and if it does not, what the strongest version of that framing claim is that the evidence would actually bear.
