I have no academic training in philosophy. I read David Lewis's *Convention: A Philosophical Study* on my own a few years ago — slowly, and twice — and I have been turning it over ever since, largely because I live in a housing cooperative where I edit the monthly circular and where getting forty-odd households to do the same thing without being told is a weekly practical problem rather than an abstraction.

Here is the situation I would like satirized. For eleven years our lobby noticeboard followed a settled practice: new notices went at the top and older ones were pushed down until they fell off the bottom. A new resident, entirely innocently, began posting at the bottom and pushing upward, and the board now reads in two directions at once. The Committee on Custom held a vote, which passed 21–19, and the vote has made things worse rather than better, since the twenty-one now know that the nineteen do not expect to be doing what they are doing. The committee's response is to advertise for a replacement practice — literally to post a job advertisement, as though a convention were a vacancy that could be filled by hiring.

Write that advertisement. I would like roughly 600 words in the flat, earnest, over-explained voice of a real cooperative circular, consisting of exactly five numbered clauses and nothing else: no masthead blurb, no sign-off from me, no closing paragraph telling the reader what the joke was. The clauses should run, in this order — (1) about the post, including its eleven-year history and the committee's insistence that the post is not a job but a regularity; (2) the successful candidate, who must be self-sustaining, arbitrary, and conditional, and who must be capable of being commonly known without ever having been announced; (3) applicants the committee cannot consider; (4) remuneration, tenure, and the selection procedure; and (5) a note on the recent vote.

The humor has to come from Lewis's actual apparatus rather than from generic bureaucracy jokes, and clause (3) is where most of it should live: the applicant whose proposal is simply better than the alternatives and who is therefore disqualified rather than hired, since a convention has to be arbitrary; the applicant who promises to be a lone exception and does not understand that everyone's preference for conformity is conditional on everyone else's; the applicant who arrives bearing a signed undertaking, a covenant, or the committee's own majority resolution, all of which defeat the point, since if a promise could do the work there would be nothing left for the convention to do; and the applicant who claims to be already in force and to have been so the whole time, on the strength of beliefs nobody has ever consciously entertained, which the committee discovers it has no procedure for. Clause (4) should reveal that the post is unpaid and permanent, that payment would turn conformity into an exchange, and that the committee cannot in fact select a successful candidate at all — that eleven years of precedent is now the only salience on offer, which is to say that the previous postholder has reappointed himself. Clause (5) should end on the committee's appeal to residents to observe the new practice in the hope that observing it will give them reason to expect it, and on the admission that the board will stay as it is until somebody posts at the top, at which point everybody will.

Please keep the piece affectionate rather than sneering. The target is the committee's instrumentalism — the assumption that a self-sustaining regularity is the sort of thing one can order — and, secondarily, the bathos of deploying an analysis designed to show that driving on the right needs no social contract upon a cork board in a stairwell. A reader who knows Lewis should be able to tell that the piece has read him closely; a resident who has never heard of him should still laugh at the committee.
