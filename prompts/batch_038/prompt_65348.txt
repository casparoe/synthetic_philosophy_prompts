I chair the evidence-review panel for a national mental-health classification. Our terms are used in primary-care coding, insurance adjudication, decisions about school accommodations, and forensic risk assessment. The schedule is revised on a triennial cycle; in the coming one we expect roughly ten proposals for new categories and three proposals to rename existing ones. A positive recommendation is close to irreversible. Once a term enters the coding schedule it is absorbed into statute, clinical guidelines, and the research literature, and withdrawal requires a separate multi-year process over which we have no jurisdiction. The panel is eleven clinicians, one methodologist, and two lay representatives; we allow six weeks from receipt of a dossier to recommendation, and the assessment section of each recommendation is capped at four pages.

Our standing dossier format covers the usual ground: construct definition, delimitation from neighbouring categories, prevalence estimates, reliability across raters, prognostic value and treatment-response evidence. What it does not cover is the feedback the term generates once it is in circulation, and that omission has cost us. A category adopted ten years ago has since tripled in recorded prevalence while every population-level risk indicator we track has been flat. Our critics say we manufactured the condition we counted. At present we have no method for deciding whether that is so, whether the growth reflects improved case-finding, or whether incidence genuinely rose. Several members have been reading Hacking on the way classifications of people differ from classifications of things: the classified can learn the classification, describe themselves in its terms, and change accordingly, so that the class described in the dossier and the class that exists a decade later need not be the same class. I want that idea turned into something we can run, not into a cautionary paragraph at the end of a report.

Please write the following.

1. The procedure itself, as numbered steps of no more than a paragraph each. For every step state what counts as a sufficient answer, what part of the dossier it draws on, what the step can conclude on its own, and where it terminates the review with a no. It must be runnable by people with no training in philosophy and no time for theory, and it must not come with a bibliography or a reading list.

2. An early screen that identifies proposals where feedback risk is genuinely low — for instance where the term is technical, is never spoken to the person classified, and functions only as an index to an intervention that already exists. The procedure must be capable of returning a verdict of low interactivity and proceeding on the ordinary evidential grounds. If it treats every label as a hazard, the panel will stop using it.

3. A requirement that the expected feedback be stated in advance in a checkable form. For each proposal: what signature would adoption leave on the data — in the shape of reported symptoms, in the severity distribution of newly diagnosed cases, in the demographic composition of the class, in the geographic and institutional pattern of uptake — and how would that signature distinguish change induced by the label from better ascertainment and from a real change in incidence. Specify what the panel must record at the moment of adoption so that the next cycle can audit its own prediction.

4. A treatment of feedback as two-sided. The mechanism that produces identity foreclosure, lowered expectation, and diagnostic overshadowing is the same mechanism that produces access to services, exculpation, community formation, and a research base. Show which parts of that balance are factual questions the panel can investigate and which are value questions that must be handed to a named decision-maker, with the trade-off stated in terms a lay representative could follow.

5. A step that separates harms attaching to a label without moving the kind — stigma directed at a trait that would be equally stigmatized under any other name — from harms arising because the kind itself has changed. Last cycle we conflated the two and prescribed renaming where what was needed was a change in the criteria.

6. An account of how the procedure applies to renaming, since a euphemism is not obviously a free improvement: it changes who finds the term applicable to themselves, changes the threshold at which clinicians become suspicious, and orphans the prognostic knowledge accumulated under the old name.

Then run the procedure on three cases and give the verdict and reasoning for each. First, a proposal from my own department for Prolonged Situational Distress: persistent distress of more than twelve months following a documented life disruption, offered as distinct from depressive disorder, in language that is immediately legible to patients and to the press. Second, a proposal for Biphasic Response Pattern, defined solely by two consecutive assay values, predictive of response to a treatment already in use, with a term confined to specialist reports. Third, a proposal to replace our existing and widely resented category Personality Deviance Disorder with Adaptive Rigidity Pattern, which our lay representatives regard as an unqualified good. At least one of the three must come out against the preference of the party that proposed it; I would rather see the procedure bite than see it deliver three comfortable yeses. Where it bites, say what the proposer could change in the definition or in the rollout to obtain a different verdict.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do. We have no jurisdiction in which a label can be withheld at random, the feedback is entangled with simultaneous changes in treatment availability, and a published statement that a category is self-altering is itself liable to alter behaviour. Say how far those limits weaken the audit in point 3, and identify the places where the panel should nevertheless act without knowing.

Format: the numbered steps, a one-page form the chair can complete for the public record, the three worked verdicts, and the limits section. Roughly 2,500 words in total.
