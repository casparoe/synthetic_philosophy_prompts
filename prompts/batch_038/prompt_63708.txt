I have just begun a two-year term on the Civic Engagement Standards Panel of my state's election administration office. There are seven of us: two election administrators, two technologists, an attorney from the Secretary of State's office, a political scientist, and me. I was appointed from the disability access advisory board, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance of our meeting in three weeks. I would like that opinion drafted.

The case. A vendor supplied our office, at no cost, with a mobile turnout application for the last general election. Three of its features are at issue.

First, it displayed in real time which of a user's phone contacts had already cast a ballot, and it sent a push notification whenever a contact voted. Second, it showed a countdown — "Early voting ends in 5:12:40" — beside a claim that appointment slots at the user's nearest vote center were nearly exhausted. The appointment system itself was genuine; the scarcity framing was the vendor's own copy, and our office has since confirmed that there was never an actual shortage of slots. Third, it sent messages presented as coming from "someone in your neighborhood who has already voted." These were composed by the vendor's language model. No neighbor wrote any of them.

On effects: verified turnout among first-time registrants under twenty-five rose roughly four points relative to comparable counties. In the post-election survey, 71 percent of users who said they had noticed these features reported being glad the application used them; 22 percent of that same group reported feeling "steered"; 7 percent said they would decline the application if offered it again. One further fact bears on the case: the vendor markets a functionally indistinguishable feature package to a consumer budgeting application whose revenue depends on users remaining subscribed to a service they rarely open.

Our current draft standard defines manipulation as any technique that produces a change in a person's attitudes or behavior by means other than the rational force of the evidence presented to the recipient. My difficulty is that the standard appears to prove too much. It would condemn listing early-voting sites in order of distance rather than alphabetically, using a photograph of a long queue to illustrate a deadline, and writing a ballot measure summary in plain language rather than in the statutory text's own clauses. If the standard condemns those, it condemns communication as such, and the panel will simply stop applying it.

I should be candid about why I asked for this assignment. Last October I installed the application myself. On the morning after a countdown notification I drove thirty-eight minutes to a drop-off center, and I am still glad I voted. But when I later tried to locate the moment at which I decided to go, I could not find it. I do not know whether that failure to find it marks something illegitimate in the technique, or is only what an uneventful decision feels like from the inside, and the memo should not settle that question by assumption on my behalf.

What I need is a memo of roughly 900 to 1,200 words that the panel could act on. It should state a clear recommendation — approve continued use, decline it, or approve it subject to conditions specified concretely enough that the administrators and the attorney could write them into a contract. It should supply criteria the panel can actually apply to this feature package and to the next one, rather than a definition. It should confront the ends-versus-means problem squarely: whether the same features can be forbidden to the subscription application and permitted to us without the distinction collapsing into nothing more than our approval of our own objective. And if it recommends conditional approval, it must give a real answer, not a sentence, to the objection that reported gladness after the fact cannot serve as authorization, since the preference being reported was itself produced by the technique under evaluation.

Two constraints on manner. The memo will be read by people who have no philosophical training and no texts in front of them: no footnotes, no bibliography, no appeals to named authorities. Argue from the features of the case. And please mark your epistemic footing as you go. Some of what you will assert is settled among people who study this subject; some is genuinely contested — for instance, whether disclosure cures an influence technique, whether a person can be manipulated into doing what they would have done anyway, and whether autonomy is set back only when the influence is noticed or resisted. Distinguish the two explicitly, and do not write as though a contested position were the professional consensus merely because it is the one your argument needs.
