so, no math degree here, but I've spent the last couple months down a rabbit hole about infinity. started with are some infinities bigger than others and ended up at the continuum hypothesis, and now I cant stop thinking about it. not the proofs, I cant follow those, but the situation: Gödel built a universe of sets where the answer comes out yes and Cohen built one where it comes out no (forcing is what my library book calls it), and both are equally legitimate models of the exact same axioms. so the sentence never changes, only the world its being said inside of. to me that feels more like a poem problem than a math problem.

please take on the role of a set theorist who also writes good poetry — someone who could give a seminar on forcing in the morning and a workshop in the afternoon. think carefully before drafting: first sketch out 3 or 4 candidate lines you could recyle through the poem, say which one has the most room to change meaning, and only then write it.

what I want is a pantoum, four or five stanzas of four lines, and I want the form actually kept rather than approximated — the 2nd and 4th lines of each stanza come back as the 1st and 3rd of the next, and the very first line of the poem returns as the last line to close it, ideally with the first stanza's third line sitting as the second line of that final stanza if you can do it without it sounding like homework.

the whole point: one of those returning lines has to be a claim about the sizes of infinity that reads as true in one stanza and as false, or at least unsettled, in the next, with the wording identical. only the punctuation or the neighbors get to move. the repetition should be doing what independence does. a reader who actually knows set theory should come away feeling the writer understood what forcing buys you, not that they looked up two famous names and sprinkled them in.

stuff to avoid: no awe about how huge and mysterious infinity is, no hint that math failed or gave up or that nobody cares anymore, no lecturing, no verse footnotes, and dont rhyme it into greeting-card territory. it still has to work as a poem for someone who's never heard of the continuum hypothesis — the feeling I'm after is something like claustrophobia about a question that cant be wrong, only elsewhere.

after the poem, give me two or three sentences in plain english about which line you flipped and how, so I can check I'm not just being dazzled for nothing.
