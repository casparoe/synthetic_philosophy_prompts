You are a writer of philosophical fiction in the tradition of Borges, Stanisław Lem, Ursula Le Guin, and Ted Chiang, and you have also spent years teaching the foundations of probability to undergraduates. I would like an original short story for a literary magazine whose readers enjoy conceptual fiction but have no technical training.

The story should dramatize the reference class problem — the question of which collection of past cases a probability claim is secretly a claim about — by embodying it in an institution whose entire function is choosing that collection. Please do not write an essay in fictional dress. The concept must be carried by procedure, paperwork, and argument between people who want different things from it.

**The setting.** An unnamed country in an indeterminate period, roughly our mid-twentieth century in its technology. A public agency — name it as you see fit — issues legally binding probability certificates. A citizen petitions for a number attaching to some future event that concerns them, and that number decides whether an operation is authorized, a loan is granted, a sentence is suspended. The agency has exactly one method, fixed by statute: locate the class of recorded past cases relevantly like the petition, and report the observed frequency of the outcome within it. Three rules govern, and the reader must see all three working as machinery:

1. The Narrowing Rule — the clerk must make the class more specific for as long as a further step is available.
2. The Sufficiency Rule — a class may be used only if at least thirty of its members have recorded outcomes; otherwise the clerk must broaden again.
3. The Schedule — narrowing may proceed only along an enumerated list of admissible attributes, consulted in an order of precedence set down when the statute was passed and never revised.

Choose what the petitioner is asking to have predicted; it should be something that must be settled this week and cannot be undone.

**The plot.** A clerk who is very good at his job, and who believes the Schedule is a neutral instrument, receives a petition the machinery cannot digest. Build the story so that all of the following are present:

- At least four successive narrowing steps with real sizes and real frequencies, arithmetically honest: sizes fall as the class tightens, the threshold is checked at every step, and the emerging number moves non-monotonically, so that narrowing sometimes rescues the petitioner and sometimes ruins her.
- A hearing at which the petitioner's representative and the institution's representative each press for a different narrowing, each with a genuinely respectable principle behind them. Neither is a villain or a fool.
- A tempting but inadmissible attribute: one that correlates beautifully, clears the thirty-member threshold with room to spare, and yet strikes the clerk as no way to carve the world. Let him feel its pull.
- Something found in the archive showing that published certificates change the frequencies they report — a class whose rate shifted after its number was posted, so that the certificate was false at the moment it was accurate. Let a character draw the obvious and uncomfortable conclusion about whether the classes are out there to be found rather than made by the finding.
- A scene in which the petitioner asks what her number says about her, alone, on the morning in question, and not about people like her, and no one can give her an answer that satisfies her, because there is not one.

End with the clerk resolving the petition. The resolution must come from *using* the machinery rather than overriding it: some combination of the three rules, applied literally and in good faith, yields a lawful certificate that no drafter of the statute intended and that the reader could state precisely afterward. It should read as both a relief and a small horror.

**Craft and format.** Roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words. Third person, close on the clerk, with the petitioner fully voiced rather than pitied. Bureaucratic dryness as the default register, allowing one or two moments of unguarded feeling. No named philosophers, no lectures, no dialogue that exists only to state a thesis, no closing paragraph that explains the moral. Give the story a title.

Before you write, plan privately: settle the classes, their sizes and their frequencies so that every number you print survives scrutiny, and decide exactly what the clerk's final move is and why the statute permits it. That planning must not appear in your reply. Your response should begin with the title and the first line of the story and contain nothing else — no preface, no summary of the ideas, no note on your choices.
