I spent thirty-one years running trace-metal analysis for a geological survey — mostly ICP-MS, some of it field work in places you wouldn't want to visit — and I retired four years ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy properly, mainly because I got tired of hearing people at dinner parties say things like "well, those old numbers were true at the time," as if accuracy were a fashion.

**The essay question (1,200 words, take-home):**
Explain why Popper thought he needed a notion of verisimilitude. State his original formal defintion of it, and say what Tichý, Harris and Miller established about that definition in 1974. Does the failure of Popper's definition undermine the claim that science progresses toward the truth? Support your answer by reference to at least one post-Popperian account.

**The student's answer:**

Popper started from a problem most stories about scientific progress have trouble with. If truth is the aim of science, and if — as Popper insisted — we can never be certain we have reached it, then nearly every great theory in the history of science turns out to be strictly false, Newtonian mechanics included. That leaves an unappealing choice: either science never gets anywhere, or progress just means piling up confirmed truths. Popper wanted neither. What he needed was something that could make sense of the intuition that Einstein's theory is a genuine advance on Newton's even though both are false — that one false theory can be nearer the truth than another. That notion is verisimilitude, which he introduced in the early sixties and defined formally in Objective Knowledge (1972).

The definition is stated in terms of content. Every theory has a class of consequences, some of which are true and some of which are false. Popper called the true ones the theory's truth-content and the false ones its false-content, and defined the verisimilitude of a theory as its truth-content minus its false-content. So theory A is nearer the truth than theory B just in case A's truth-content less its false-content exceeds B's. Two features are worth stressing. First, the notion is meant to be objective: verisimilitude is a relation between a theory and the world, not a measure of how much evidence we have or how confident anyone is entitled to be. Second, it runs in the opposite direction from probability. Because the definition rewards content, a tautology scores nothing at all, and the more a theory says the more it risks — so the improbable, highly informative theories Popper favoured are exactly the ones capable of being high in verisimilitude.

The definition did not survive. In 1974 Tichý, Harris and Miller showed independently that it carries an unintended consequence: no false theory can be nearer the truth than any other false theory, except in cases where the theories stand in a special logical relation to one another. Popper accepted the criticism almost at once. This was less damaging than it looks, though, because Popper already had the notion he needed in degree of corroboration; corroboration and verisimilitude are really the same quantity under two names, one belonging to the logic of the situation and the other to the practice of testing. On that reading, a theory that has survived severe tests has thereby been shown to be nearer the truth than the theory it replaced, so the collapse of the formal definition only removes a piece of scaffolding.

Later work took three routes. Miller and Kuipers kept to content, treating truthlikeness as a function of a truth factor and a content factor. Schurz and Weingartner kept the consequence approach but restricted it to relevant consequences. Tichý and Hilpinen, developed especially by Niiniluoto and Oddie, took the likeness in truthlikeness literally: how near a proposition is to the truth depends on how similar the possible worlds in which it would be true are to the actual world.

That last route does not rescue the realist, though. Similarity gets measured against a chosen set of questions — a cognitive problem, in Niiniluoto's terms — and which questions we care about is a matter of our interests rather than of the world. So on the likeness accounts there is no fact about whether Einstein was nearer the truth than Newton, full stop; there is only that he looks nearer from inside the problem specification we happen to have adopted. Which is quite enough to keep the pessimistic meta-induction running: every theory looks nearer the truth than its predecessors to the people who hold it, and the fact that Popper's own account of progress toward the truth proved incoherent only strengthens the point.
