I have sketched a scenario for this year's paper on our final-year undergraduate module in democratic theory, and I would like you to turn it into a finished examination item along with the assessment apparatus that goes with it.

The scenario is invented, and the numbers are there only to make it concrete. A self-governing island territory of roughly half a million inhabitants is to hold a referendum on whether to extend its franchise to non-citizens who have been lawfully resident for more than five years — some 60,000 people, most of them employed in fishing and care work. Two features make the case awkward. First, the outcome will itself determine who counts, in future, as bound by and as having a stake in decisions of the same kind: if the franchise is extended, the newly enfranchised will help settle the questions that produced the referendum, and if it is refused, they will continue to live under laws in whose making they have no say, so that the decision reshapes the very constituency whose interests a subsequent decision of the same type would have to weigh. Second, the two criteria of inclusion that my students have been taught come apart here. On the affectedness criterion, the resident non-citizens are plainly affected by the referendum, but so are the flag states of the fishing fleets, the employers who recruit abroad, the relatives who depend on remittances, and migrants who have not yet arrived and whose arrival the outcome will make more or less likely; nothing in the criterion says where the class of the affected stops. On the subjection criterion, the class is determinate — everyone within the territory's jurisdiction — but only because it takes the territory's existing jurisdictional boundaries as given, and the standing of those boundaries is precisely what the legitimacy of the decision was supposed to be established by.

Please write the examination question itself. It must be self-contained: a student should be able to answer it in forty-five minutes using nothing beyond what the question supplies, with no expectation of citations, since the paper is closed-book and students are told in advance that naming authors earns no marks. It must not be answerable by reproducing the taxonomy of criteria of membership, which is what nearly everyone will do if given the chance. What I want it to press on is the reflexive step — that whoever applies a criterion of membership is either inside or outside the group whose membership is in question, and that the criterion cannot be applied at all without already assuming a body entitled to apply it. The question should require students to say which of the following that reflexivity amounts to: a vicious circle, in which case a democratic decision about its own boundaries is impossible in principle; a benign regress that terminates in practice at some historically given unit; a fixed-point problem, which might in principle admit of a stable solution even though no first step is justified; or a sign that the whole enterprise of looking for a criterion of the correct boundaries is misdirected, because the demos is not something to be identified but something that institutions bring into being. It should then require them to draw the consequence for the territory in the scenario: on the diagnosis they have defended, what, if anything, could the referendum legitimately be made to turn on.

After the question, give a model answer of about 700 words, written as a strong answer would actually be written under examination conditions rather than as a survey of the options.

Then give a marking scheme out of 25, allocated across four or five identifiable moves. It should say explicitly what separates a first-class answer (70 per cent and above) from an upper-second one (60 to 69): which specific steps the first must contain that the second characteristically omits, rather than a general remark about clarity or depth. List three failures that should cost marks, with the deduction for each; the ones I expect are treating the two criteria as rival answers to a well-posed question about a fixed population, declaring that the affectedness criterion is too broad without either proposing a stopping rule or saying who is entitled to impose one, and dissolving the circle by appealing to a pre-political people or to historical continuity without noticing what that concedes about the source of the boundary's legitimacy.

Finally, please append a short note separating what is settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the problem. My own suspicion — and I may well be wrong about it — is that the shape of the taxonomy is settled while the diagnosis of the reflexive step is not, and I do not want either the model answer or the scheme to imply that one of the four diagnoses above enjoys anything like a professional consensus. Accordingly, the scheme should attach marks to the reasoning rather than to the conclusion reached, so that a student who defends the misdirected-search diagnosis competently is not penalised for departing from the model answer. Where the model answer itself leans on a contested reading of one of the positions, please flag it as such.
