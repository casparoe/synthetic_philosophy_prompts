I direct a small forecasting unit that produces long-horizon survival estimates for large political orders. I am writing to request your judgment on a set of reference classes we have assembled, not on the target case itself.

The target question, stated precisely: what is the probability that T exists, in substantially its current constitutional form, thirty years from now?

T is a supranational union of twenty-four member states, founded sixty-eight years ago by a treaty ratified by all of them. It has a directly elected assembly with genuine budgetary powers; a single currency used by nineteen of the twenty-four; a court whose rulings bind member-state governments; a unanimity requirement for treaty amendment; and no military force under its own command. One further stipulated fact about T's environment: throughout its entire sixty-eight-year history, no member state has fought a war against another, and no external great power has had an interest in T's dissolution.

I have deliberately not named T. Please treat the description above as complete, do not supply additional facts about it from memory, and do not try to identify it; the exercise concerns the classes.

Our coders propose nine candidate reference classes. For each, the base rate is the fraction of class members that were still in existence, in substantially the same constitutional form, thirty years after reaching the age T has now. Take the figures as given and do not audit them. Audit the classes.

1. Supranational unions with a directly elected assembly and at least fifteen member states, founded since 1800. n = 9; 7 survived; 0.78.
2. Compound polities, federal or confederal, with twenty or more constituent units that share a single currency while lacking a central authority with independent power to tax. n = 13; 5 survived; 0.38.
3. Voluntary interstate organizations of any size that outlived the death or retirement of the generation of leaders who founded them. n = 52; 38 survived; 0.73.
4. Polities whose aggregate economic output was within a factor of three of T's, measured at the same age. n = 34; 24 survived; 0.71.
5. Multilateral institutions of any kind with at least forty member states and at least forty years of age at the start of observation, including standard-setting bodies, treaty secretariats, and postal and telecommunications unions. n = 61; 55 survived; 0.90.
6. Monetary or customs unions that our coders marked as having experienced sustained internal fiscal or trade friction at some point during their history. n = 17; 6 survived; 0.35.
7. Firms and commercial corporations in the top size decile of their era that reached age sixty-eight. n = 120; 73 survived; 0.61.
8. Polities that appeared in this institute's own earlier forecasting rounds. n = 15; 11 survived; 0.73.
9. Compound polities with a unanimity amendment rule, no military of their own, and the background environmental condition described above. n = 4; 1 survived; 0.25.

Please produce the following, in this order.

(a) A score from 0 to 10 for each class on evidential weight for the target question, presented as a table, with one or two sentences per class giving the reason for that score and no other.

(b) A set of non-negative weights across the nine classes, summing to exactly 1.00 and stated to two decimal places, expressing how much of the final forecast each class should carry. Show one line of arithmetic per class and give the resulting blended probability to two decimals. Zero weights are permitted and should be used where you think them deserved.

(c) An ordered ranking of the classes from most to least trustworthy. Wherever this ranking diverges from the weights in (b), explain the divergence in a sentence; in particular, distinguish a class that is well constructed but evidentially redundant given another from one that is both weak and redundant.

(d) The single class you would discard outright, with the defect located in its inclusion rule rather than in its size; and the single class you believe we have under-weighted, with the same discipline.

(e) The class whose base rate is most fragile to reasonable changes in how its inclusion rule is applied, together with the direction in which a re-coding would push it.

(f) Two of our coders disagree about the background environmental condition. One holds that it is a structural feature of T's situation and therefore that every class should be restricted to cases sharing it, which in our coding leaves only class 9. The other holds that it is a period effect of the last seventy-five years and should enter only as an adjustment to the blend, never as a restriction on class membership. Take a side, and show concretely what it does to your weights.

(g) You may add at most one class of your own. If you do, state its inclusion rule, estimate its n, give it a base rate, and explain what it captures that the nine do not.

(h) A self-rated reliability for your blended figure, from 0 to 1, and a statement of what would have to be true for that rating to be 0.70 or higher.

Two constraints on the response. First, assume we already know the standard objections about small samples, survivorship, and heterogeneous outcome definitions; we want your scores, your weights, and your reasons, not a survey of how reference-class reasoning works or a list of caveats. Second, do not cite literature, name authors, or recommend readings.

If your considered view is that no convex blend of these classes is the right way to answer the question, say so plainly — and still produce the blend, with two sentences on what it fails to capture.
