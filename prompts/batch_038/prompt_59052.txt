I'm a second-year philosophy of mind PhD student working on what makes pain bad, and our reading group has been sitting on pain asymbolia for about six weeks now and hasn't moved an inch. Quick sketch of the phenomenon for context, since its the whole reason the argument exists: patients with lesions around the posterior insula / parietal operculum and the projections toward cingulate report sharp, well-localized pain when you prick them, and they describe the quality and location accurately — but there's no withdrawal, no grimace, no guarding, no guarding of the limb afterwards either. They'll leave the hand on the hot surface, they'll let you repeat the stimulus out of what looks like curiosity, some of them seem faintly amused by the whole business, and they rack up injuries and burns they never noticed. Schilder and Stengel's original case report is from the late 1920s and Schilder classified it as an agnosia, which already tells you something; it got into philosophy mainly through Grahek's book in the mid-2000s. And meanwhile clinical pain research routinely asks patients to rate intensity and unpleasantness on two separate scales, which quietly assumes the dissociation is real and measurable.

Here's where the stalemate is, as I see it. Camp one says the dissociationists have it exactly right: these patients genuinely feel pains, the pains genuinely aren't unpleasant, therefore unpleasantness is a separate dimension from the sensory-discriminative character and it — not the sensory quality — is what makes pain bad. Camp two says the whole description is incoherent: either the reports are unreliable, because what the patient is doing is classifying a bodily sensation with the only word he has for it (note that Schilder's own framing was a failure to *recognize* the stimulus as pain, not a failure to mind it), or else being in pain just is being disposed to dislike being in it, in which case asymbolics aren't in pain by definition and the interesting question never arises. Camp three wants to keep the phenomenology but relocate the mechanism: pain is a felt command to protect a body part, or a representation of bodily damage as bad, and the asymbolic has lost the imperative/evaluative component while retaining the bodily content — so it's still a pain, just not a bad one, and the explanation is about content rather than about a separate affective channel. The stakes aren't purely academic for me, because camp two's picture ties the badness of pain to the subject's minding it, and then it bites on animal pain, on neonates, on cingulotomy and dissociative-analgesia patients who say the pain is still there but it doesn't bother them, and on whether treatment should target the sensation or the suffering. And I keep going back and forth on the asymbolic himself: I'm inclined to say his pain isn't bad *for him* while he's having it, but I also think he's badly off in some way that shows up in the accumulated tissue damage, which makes me suspect badness-of-pain and badness-for-the-person have come apart — and I can't tell whether that's a discovery or a confusion on my part.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the argument isn't actually about the patients. Both camps read the *same* case reports and each finds them decisive, which suggests the clinical evidence isn't doing any work; what's doing the work is a prior disagreement about whether unpleasantness is a property of an experience or a relation between a subject and an experience, plus a disagreement about which kind of evidence is admissible at all — first-person report versus lesion and behavioural data. And I suspect "asymbolics feel pain" is being recruited to answer two different questions under one heading, one about phenomology and one about value, with each camp answering one while claiming to have settled the other. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement hiding behind the impasse — an empirical one about what these brains are doing, a conceptual one about the individuation of experiences, or just a hard instance of the problem of other minds? Please don't give me a survey of the accounts of unpleasantness, I've read them all twice. What I want is the structure of the disagreement: where the two sides actually differ once you strip out the rhetoric, why the case reports fail to discriminate between them, and what — if anything — would count as progress, whether that's a new kind of study, a better operationalization of unpleasantness, an argument from the nature of introspection, or a change of question. And if your answer is that no progress should be expected, I want to know whether that's because the question is ill-posed or only because it's very hard, since those feel like they should lead somewhere different and I can't see where.
