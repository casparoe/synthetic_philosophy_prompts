I'm a fourth-year philosophy graduate student, and next week I'm running the section of our graduate rational-choice seminar on updateless decision theory — the idea, originally Wei Dai's, that an agent should settle on the policy that maximizes expected utility relative to its initial prior and then carry that policy out, rather than re-deciding from scratch every time new evidence arrives. Two of my students, one of them an economist who keeps insisting the whole literature is mechanism design in a costume, have independently objected that updatelessness has no distinct content: whatever it recommends, they say, an ordinary updateful agent could get by binding itself early, so the view is just precommitment with extra steps. I think they're wrong, and I think the cleanest way to show it is with a case that is boring enough that they can't dismiss it as science fiction but pointed enough that they have to say where their objection breaks. I'd like you to build that case for me, to specification.

Here is what I need. Construct a decision problem in which updateless and updateful reasoning come apart inside an entirely ordinary institution — a municipal retrofit program, a hospital network, a research funder, a lender, a utility, a conservation district, that sort of thing. The constraints: no boxes, no deserts, no demons, no superintelligent predictors, no simulations of the agent, no amnesia drugs, no duplicated agents, and no anthropic self-location puzzles; the randomness must come from an ordinary institutional lottery, and the only predictive element may be a competent but fallible human being forming a judgment about someone's dispositions, with an accuracy figure you state explicitly and keep below anything implausible. The engine of the case must have two parts: a sealed assessment, made before the lottery is drawn, of what the agent would do in a situation that has not yet arisen; and a benefit that is awarded in one arm of the lottery and keyed to that assessment, while the burdensome situation actually arises only in the other arm. Give me the numbers — probability of each arm, the assessor's accuracy, the value of the benefit, the cost of complying — and show me the arithmetic for the two candidate policies, in a small table, so that I can check that no verdict is resting on a suspiciously tuned parameter.

Then the analysis, which is where I need the real work. First, track the agent through three moments: before the lottery is drawn, after being told which arm they landed in, and after being shown the content of the sealed assessment but before being asked to act. For each moment, state what updateless reasoning and what updateful causal and evidential reasoning respectively recommend, and flag every moment at which they coincide — I expect at least one coincidence, and I want you to draw the lesson from it: is updatelessness best understood as a refusal to condition on news generally, or as a refusal to condition only on a particular species of news, and which species? Second, tell me what the agent is actually choosing at the first moment, and if in your design nothing is — if their dispositions are fixed before the interview and cannot be revised by an act of will — say so plainly and explain what updateless reasoning is recommending instead, since that is exactly the point where my students will say the view has quietly stopped being a theory of decision and become a theory of character. Third, name the single structural feature doing the work, and report what happens to each verdict if you delete it. Fourth, since no precommitment mechanism, no audience, no repetition, and no reputational stakes are permitted in your design, explain in a sentence or two why the "just bind yourself early" redescription genuinely fails here rather than merely going unmentioned. Fifth, tell me which familiar case yours most resembles — the counterfactual mugging is the obvious suspect — and if the honest answer is that yours is a relabeling of it, say so and then tell me what the realistic institutional instantiation buys that the canonical version does not. Finally, write the strongest objection one of my students could press from the updateful side, which I expect to run something along the lines of "your agent is being asked to pay for a benefit that is already closed, which is a sunk-cost error dressed up as insight," and either answer it or concede it and say what survives of updatelessness. Keep the scenario itself to about 150 words and the rest under headings. No bibliography and no reading list, please — I want the case and the reasoning, not the literature.
