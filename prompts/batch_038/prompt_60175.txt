I teach a fifty-minute evidence-based-medicine session to second-year emergency medicine residents. They are already fluent in the likelihood-ratio idiom: a pre-test probability, a positive or negative finding, a ratio that carries them to a post-test probability, and most of them can drive a Fagan nomogram without conscious effort. I want to use the hour to teach Jeffrey conditionalization — updating when the evidence itself is uncertain — and I want a plan for how to teach it, not an explanation of the rule addressed to me.

Two commitments the residents arrive with, and which I need dislodged, are worth stating precisely. The first is what I have started calling binary collapse: the habit of snapping an ambiguous observation to positive or negative before reasoning about it, so that the likelihood-ratio machinery has something to bite on, and then treating the snap as costless. The second is the belief that uncertainty about a finding is a logistical problem — if you are not sure what you are seeing, get a better test or telephone the consultant — rather than a state one can reason from directly.

The case I intend to build the hour around is a sixty-two-year-old with forty minutes of crushing chest pain, a noisy tracing in which V2 through V3 may or may not show roughly 1.5 mm of elevation, and a decision about activating the catheterization laboratory that has to be reached in under ten minutes. I want the partition over the tracing to be three-way — clear elevation, no elevation, and an equivocal reading — because the three cells license three different actions, and because I want them to see that the rule is not merely a weighted average of two terms. Tell me whether this case can carry the idea. In particular, tell me whether equivocal is a legitimate cell of a partition over the tracing at all, or a report about my own confidence that has been smuggled down to the object level; if it is the latter, tell me where in the sequence that confusion has to be surfaced rather than papered over, and whether a substitute case — I have considered a lung examination in a noisy resuscitation bay, and a rapid test whose colour change is faint — would avoid the problem or reproduce it in a less visible form. I would rather not fall back on the familiar textbook illustration of a coloured cloth seen under odd lighting, since nothing in that story turns on the answer.

What I want from you is the following. First, the order in which the components must be introduced, with the dependencies made explicit: where the reduction to ordinary conditionalization as a limiting case belongs; whether the convex-combination picture — the updated probability as a point on a line whose endpoints are the old conditionals on each cell, with the prior sitting somewhere along it — should precede or follow the formal statement; and how to render the procedure executable mentally in ten seconds rather than as a piece of apparatus to be consulted afterwards. Second, the point at which rigidity must be named as a substantive assumption rather than a technicality, given that in this very setting what the resident notices next — diaphoresis, a second look at the tracing, a collateral history — can plausibly change what an elevation would have meant. Third, the misconceptions I should expect, each attached to the exact place in the sequence where it should be headed off rather than corrected at the end; I predict at least that this is only a sloppier likelihood ratio because the test characteristics already absorb observational noise, that the rule licenses being less confident about everything, that the order in which one revises several findings is immaterial, and that it amounts to a rationalization of gestalt since the new weights can be chosen freely. Fourth, a single question I can put to them in the final five minutes that would separate a resident who has grasped the structure from one who has memorized an arithmetic recipe.

Finally, tell me what to refuse. I have no intention of teaching the general measure-theoretic formulation, of settling where the new weights come from, or of entering the dispute over whether the rule is uniquely rational, and I would prefer to announce those omissions rather than gesture at them. Tell me what to say in their place, and where in the hour, so that the refusals read as scoping and not as evasion.
