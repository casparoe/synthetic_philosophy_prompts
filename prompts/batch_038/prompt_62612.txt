**Question** (one hour, closed book): Karma is standardly presented as a theory of desert that needs no dispenser: deeds are said to ripen into fitting experiences on their own, across lifetimes. (a) Explain how such an impersonal order is supposed to ground desert, and how it differs from a theodicy built on divine judgment. (b) Assess this objection: even granting a causal continuum that connects a deed to a later suffering, it does not follow that the sufferer *deserves* it, since the continuum tells us who inherits the fruit, not why inheriting it is a matter of justice rather than mere transmission. Does the Buddhist denial of an enduring self (anātman) help with this objection, or sharpen it? (c) On your account, what does the tradition you are drawing on say an agent should therefore do about action itself?

**Answer**: Karma is the view that actions carry their own moral consequences: a deed leaves a trace that ripens, sooner or later, in an experience proportionate to it. Because the trace outlives the body, karma works as a theodicy. Suffering that looks undeserved — a child born into famine, an honest trader ruined by fraud — is in fact deserved; the ledger is simply longer than one lifetime. That is what makes karma attractive as a theory of desert: it preserves the intuition that the world is morally ordered without requiring anyone to do the ordering. Unlike a Last Judgment, there is no judge, no weighing, no clerk. The fruit follows the deed the way a sprout follows a seed, and no school of classical Indian philosophy needed a divine supervisor of the process, which is precisely the point.

The obvious cost is the identity of the desert-bearer. If my present misery is the fruit of deeds performed by someone who died two thousand years ago, in what sense is it *my* desert? Buddhism is usually said to make this worse, because it denies any enduring self: persons are just causal series of momentary events, so there is no one who persists to inherit anything. I think that gets the direction of the objection backwards. The objection assumes a substantial self that Buddhism never posited, and once the assumption is dropped the problem dissolves, because what karma requires is exactly what anātman supplies: a causal continuum running from deed to fruit. Nāgasena's answer to Milinda — that the one reborn is neither the same as nor another than the one who died — gives all the continuity desert could need. Identity was never the relation to look for.

The same logic governs conduct. Since every act deposits a trace that must ripen, the Bhagavad Gītā concludes that the wise person withdraws from action altogether: the only way to stop accumulating karma is to stop acting, so the contemplative who sits still is safer than the soldier who fights. Desert, on this picture, is something to be escaped rather than satisfied, which is why liberation is release from the whole apparatus instead of a favorable verdict inside it.
