Context. I'm coordinating a small working group — six people, about two years of part-time effort — whose deliverable is not a book about anyone. It's a written framework for adjudicating claims of the form: this outcome depended on this particular person vs. this outcome was over-determined by the structure. We want to be able to take a real case (would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler; would Soviet industrialization have gone the same way without Stalin; would Singapore have turned out as it did without Lee Kuan Yew; would relativity have happened without Einstein, given how many people were circling it) and say what would have to be true for any answer to that question to be defensible, and what kind of confidence in an answer is even licensed. Right now our arguments are stalemates where one side says the structural conditions make the individual irrelevant and the other says the structural conditions were there for everyone and only one guy acted. Neither side has stated what they think a good theory owes them.

So: write the desiderata. Give me the list of requirements any adequate account of individual-vs-structural historical explanation has to meet in order to be usable by us, and for each one give the case or consideration that motivates it. Then mark each as non-negotiable or as a prejudice belonging to a particular camp — great-man enthusiasts, the longue-durée structuralists, and the people who think counterfactual history isn't history at all and should be dismissed rather than constrained. I want you to be opinionated about which of the things people demand here are real requirements and which are just one side's taste dressed up as method.

Specifically, I want you to include and evaluate at least these, though don't feel limited to them:

- whether the account has to license numeric probabilities over counterfactual outcomes, or whether ordinal/confidence-band output is all anyone can honestly claim;
- whether it has to say what the relevant similarity metric between the actual and counterfactual worlds is, and how fine-grained that metric has to be for a historian to actually apply it;
- whether it has to avoid trivializing long-horizon counterfactuals — call it the drift problem: perturb anything at all, run the clock thirty years, and every world differs from every other world, so any long-range counterfactual comes out trivially true and tells you nothing;
- whether it has to handle the multiple-discovery cases (several people independently converging) and the reverse cases (an idea available and ignored for decades) without special pleading;
- whether it has to be applicable with only the evidence historians actually have — surviving documents, no experiments, no controls, heavy selection on outcomes — rather than in an idealized setting;
- whether it has to give a principled account of what counts as the same position or role, since "who would have been in his place" depends on how you individuate the place;
- whether it has to distinguish an outcome not occurring from an outcome occurring differently — delay, milder form, different agent, different timing — because almost nobody actually claims the counterfactual world is empty of the thing.

Two homegrown proposals we keep fighting about, which I want you to explicitly rule in or out as genuine requirements rather than treat charitably by default:

1. The substitution audit. For an individual X occupying position P at time t, the core measurement target is replaceability: how many and what quality of alternative occupants of P would have produced outcomes that are relevantly similar, judged before the fact rather than after. Some of us think this is the whole theory in one number. Others think it's circular, because you can't assess the alternatives without already assuming how much individuals matter.

2. Order-invariance. A framework should return the same verdict whether you hold the structure fixed and vary the person, or hold the person fixed and vary the structure. If the verdicts come apart, one of the two factors is doing the real explanatory work and the framework is smuggling in a prior. Is that a requirement, a theorem you can derive from something better, or a category mistake?
