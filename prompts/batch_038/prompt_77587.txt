The bit they both accept going in: if two agents share a common prior, and their posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, then those posteriors must be equal.

1. **Arun:** So the upshot is that rational disagreement is basically impossible. Anything that survives full mutual disclosure of credences means somebody is broken — not conditionalizing, or shielding themselves from evidence, or reasoning motivatedly. Politics, philosophy, religion: in each case at least one side is making a mistake, and the theorem lets us say that without looking at the subject matter at all.

2. **Bea:** Only if common knowledge of the posteriors ever obtains, which it essentially never does. The theorem needs the full infinite iteration, not "we both know that we both know." You can have mutual knowledge to arbitrarily high finite order and still disagree, and when you swap common knowledge out for common p-belief the way Monderer and Samet do, agreement survives only up to a slack that widens as p falls. So the crisp theorem is a limiting case of a result that says agreement is a matter of degree. It has no grip on actual disagreement.

3. **Arun:** That's the wrong lesson to draw. "Nobody has numerical credences and nobody reaches common knowledge" is the same complaint you'd make about Bayes' theorem, and it doesn't make Bayesianism useless — it makes it a norm. The theorem is diagnostic: if you aren't converging, exactly one of three things is failing — incomplete pooling of information, non-Bayesian updating, or the absence of a common prior. That's a research program, not a tautology.

4. **Bea:** The third disjunct is a cheat. You can always manufacture a common prior by fattening the state space — throw in states that encode which prior each agent has, and any belief profile you like becomes a pair of posteriors derived from some common prior. If the assumption can't fail on a rich enough space, it can't explain anything, and the theorem reduces to: if two agents' beliefs happen to be conditionalizations of one shared prior, pooling information makes them converge. The genuinely contestable claim is the one you assumed instead of argued — that a rational agent's credences are pinned down by her total evidence. That's uniqueness. Permissivism denies it.

5. **Arun:** Your fattening trick is empty in exactly the same way, and it costs you your escape route. If "we lack a common prior" can never be established, then "we're rationally permitted to differ" can never be established either. And there's positive pressure toward uniqueness: Harsanyi's line is that real differences in belief trace to differences in information, because everything else — taste, temperament, style — isn't belief-forming in the first place. Permissivism is the exotic thesis here, not the common prior assumption.

6. **Bea:** Permissivism is the default and uniqueness is the exotic thesis. Real agents differ in how heavily they weight testimony, in how cautious they are, in what they'll accept as a reason, and none of that is an error. Uniqueness implies that in every philosophical dispute somebody is screwing up, which is a reductio rather than a premise.

7. **Arun:** And permissivism costs you the entire epistemology of disagreement. If two people can be equally rational holding opposite credences on the same evidence, then finding out that an equally informed, equally smart person disagrees with you carries no evidential weight at all, and "reasonable people disagree" stops being a reason to moderate anything. You've bought coherence at the price of never having to take anybody else's view seriously.

8. **Bea:** No — permissivism is perfectly compatible with conciliation. Learning that an acknowledged peer disagrees is fresh higher-order evidence, and I can be required to respond to it without that requirement being derived from a shared prior. All I deny is that the response has to amount to splitting the difference.
