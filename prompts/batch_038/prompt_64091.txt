I'm a staff engineer on the risk-analytics platform team at a mid-sized specialty insurer. I build and maintain the pipeline that turns our internal forecasting panel's submissions into the single probability we publish each quarter, and because I own that code I've been asked to sit on the Forecast Governance Committee, which has five members: the CRO as chair, the head of actuarial, two senior analysts who are themselves on the panel, and me. I should say up front that I have no formal training in philosophy, statistics, or decision theory — I write Scala and Python and taught myself enough probability to be dangerous — so I'm going to describe our problem in operational terms and let you supply the conceptual structure. The committee meets Thursday and members expect a written opinion circulated the day before.

Here is the situation. Our panelists all condition on a single corporate baseline distribution that actuarial rebuilds every January, so in a literal sense the institution imposes a common prior on everyone. Six analysts submit a probability for each question on a fixed quarterly list; submissions are confidential and only the aggregate is published. The handbook specifies an accuracy-weighted arithmetic mean, and it also contains what everyone calls the reconciliation clause: if two submissions for the same question differ by more than fifteen percentage points after both analysts have received the same evidence packet, the gap is logged as an update failure, the pair must meet and either converge or file a joint statement of why they cannot, and no number is published for that question until they have done so. As far as I can tell from the minutes, the clause was justified by an appeal to the idea that rational people who share a prior and have seen the same evidence cannot simply keep disagreeing forever. One of my colleagues observed that the result people have in mind also requires each person's beliefs to be common knowledge, which our confidentiality rule deliberately prevents; another observed that it assumes agreement about what counts as evidence in the first place, which we lack — our lowest estimator treats a remark from an unnamed contact at the regulator as evidence, our highest excludes the same remark as unattributable and therefore inadmissible. That debate stayed academic until this cycle, and I want the memo to resolve it rather than re-stage it.

The live case is Question 7, which asks whether a proposed federal rule takes effect within eighteen months. The six submissions are 0.15, 0.28, 0.30, 0.32, 0.35, and 0.60. The accuracy-weighted mean the pipeline reports is 0.33; the equally weighted log pool — the multiplicative rule the head of actuarial wants to adopt, on the grounds that it is what falls out if you assume the panel shares a prior and simply pools everyone's evidence — comes to a little over 0.01, because it is dominated by the confident 0.15. Our reserve model has a tier break at 0.20 and underwriting's go/no-go threshold is 0.50, so the choice of pooling rule determines whether we hold capital against this exposure, and the reconciliation clause determines whether we publish anything for Question 7 at all. Three further facts complicate the decision, and I don't know what to do with any of them. First, the analyst who submitted 0.15 builds her estimate from a model whose inputs include the other analyst's published numbers from the previous four quarters; I discovered this while debugging the feature store, and it means their submissions are not independent, so I suspect the log pool is counting one opinion twice, though I can't prove it. Second, the head of actuarial's other argument is that linear averaging is unstable under revision — if one panelist learns something mid-quarter and we re-average, the published number moves even though the group's total information has not changed, whereas the log pool doesn't drift that way — and I have no idea whether that argument is sound or whether it would survive contact with the first fact. Third, we ran both rules in shadow over eight quarters: the weighted mean scored 0.19 Brier and the log pool 0.24, but only three of those eight cycles contained a spread wider than twenty-five points, so the backtest barely samples the situations in which the two rules actually disagree. Meanwhile the update-failure log holds forty-one open items with a median age of five quarters; not one has ever been closed by a pair converging, and nine were closed only because the question resolved itself or an analyst left the company — while the analysts tell me privately that, knowing the clause exists, they message each other before submitting, which may be why recorded spreads look narrow and the calibration of the published number has not improved in two years.

Draft the memo I will circulate. It should recommend one course of action on both questions — keep, amend, or repeal the reconciliation clause, and keep, amend, or replace the pooling rule — with a transition time frame, and it should not present a menu of options with their advantages and disadvantages: the chair has said plainly that a memo which declines to take a position comes back unread. Give reasons the two analysts on the committee can actually respond to, and treat the strongest argument for keeping the clause seriously rather than as a straw man — as I understand it, the worry is that without it the panel degrades from a panel into a poll, and that a wide published spread invites business units to cherry-pick whichever number suits them at the moment the aggregate feeds a capital decision. State explicitly what your recommendation commits us to about whether people who share a prior and the same evidence packet can rationally continue to disagree, since I expect that question from the CRO and I will not be able to answer it off the cuff. Tell me what to do about the contaminated estimate now that I know about it, including whether it should change this quarter's number or only the rule. Give the operations lead a criterion she can apply next quarter without me, one that distinguishes a spread that is a defect from a spread that is legitimate information. Say what we should start recording now so that this decision can be revisited on evidence rather than on another argument in a conference room, and say what a future cycle would have to look like to show that your recommendation was wrong. Please do not send me to the literature or suggest papers for the committee to read; I cannot circulate a bibliography to the CRO and the document has to stand on its own. Assume your reader is technically literate but has never taken a course in epistemology or decision theory, define every term you use, and keep it to roughly 1,200 words.
