I teach a graduate seminar on the middle books of the Republic, and I have come to distrust a habit my students pick up almost immediately: they treat the allegory of the cave in Book VII as a narrative rendering of the divided line from the end of Book VI, and when asked to relate the two they produce a confident four-station-to-four-section correspondence and consider the matter settled. I want to press against that habit, and I would like your help in comparing the two devices as instruments doing different work rather than as one diagram presented twice. Please do not reproduce the standard mapping and then note its rough edges; the interesting content lies precisely where the mapping fails, so I want the failure exhibited first and explained second.

To keep the comparison anchored, let me fix the two sequences as they stand. The line is cut into two unequal sections, the visible and the intelligible, each then cut again in the same ratio, yielding four: (i) images — shadows, and reflections in water and on dense, smooth, bright surfaces; (ii) the originals of which those are likenesses — the animals about us, all plants, and the whole class of things made by human beings — grasped by belief rather than knowledge; (iii) a section in which the soul reasons from hypotheses down to a conclusion, making use of the visible figures and diagrams as images while its thought is directed at the square as such and the diagonal as such, that is, at the objects of the mathematical arts; and (iv) a topmost section in which dialectic treats hypotheses not as first principles but as hypotheses, as rungs for ascending to something unhypothetical, and proceeds without images, from forms to forms. The cave runs differently: shadows on the wall; the vessels, statues, and figures carried along behind the screen; then, after the release and the painful dragging up the steep ascent, shadows again first, then reflections of men and other objects in water, then the objects themselves, then by night the light of the moon and the stars and the spangled heaven, and last the sun seen in its own proper place rather than in reflection — whereupon the freed man goes on to reason that the sun gives the seasons and the years and governs everything in the visible world, and is in some way the cause of all that he and his fellows used to behold. And then, unlike the line, the cave turns around and sends him back down into the den.

Here is the comparison I want, organized around three points of non-coincidence rather than a general summary. First: the cave contains more stations than the line contains sections, so any four-to-four correspondence must either collapse two stations or promote one to the rank of a section. Say which you would do, and name the cost — in particular, whether the collapse or promotion is licensed by anything in the text or is merely convenient for the interpreter. Second: mathematics occupies an entire section of the line and seems to have no station in the cave at all, unless one reads the reflections in water that greet the prisoner on his arrival outside as the image of something like it. Take that reading seriously, then set against it the rival reading — that the cave simply omits a tier the line insists upon — and tell me which survives better and what turns on the answer. Third: the asymmetry at the terminus. What the line reaches as an unhypothetical first principle and a stopping point, the cave presents as a cause that generates seasons and makes visible things visible, and then immediately re-enters the political world through a descent that has no counterpart on the line at all. Does the good, as these two devices portray it, occupy a different metaphysical role in each?

On the basis of those three, adjudicate among the following accounts of the mismatch, which I will put to you as a disjunction: that the two are images of different subject matters — the line of the states of the soul together with their correlative objects, the cave of a conversion and of a life that must afterwards be lived in a city — so that no clean mapping should ever have been expected; that the cave is the line plus an ethics of return, continuous with it but not coextensive; that Plato is simply being loose, and the discrepancy is an artifact of composing two images in sequence rather than one theory twice; or some fourth account you regard as better. Commit to one, argue for it against at least one named rival, and state what in the text would count most strongly against your choice. Close with the payoff I actually want for the seminar: whether the theory of forms is more or less determinate once the two devices are read together — that is, whether the cave supplies content about the forms (their plurality, their causal efficacy, their relation to the good) that the line withholds, or whether the line supplies content the cave suppresses.
