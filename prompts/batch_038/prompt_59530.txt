In September I am delivering the inaugural address for a new master's programme in Risk and Foresight at a British university. I am a philosopher of science, not an economist, and I was asked precisely because I am not one; the dean's brief was that the students should hear something about the limits of what they are about to spend two years learning to do, from someone with no stake in the profession.

The context matters. The programme was endowed in the aftermath of a flood that the region's own hazard models had treated as a once-in-two-hundred-years event, and the endowment comes largely from a reinsurer whose chief risk officer will be seated on the platform and whose bursaries are paying for a third of the students in the hall. So the address has to acknowledge why the programme exists without indicting the people who fund it, and it has to leave incoming students neither cynical about quantification nor comforted into thinking that every number they will ever produce measures something real.

I want the spine of the talk to be Keynes's two-tier view of probability: the domain in which frequencies or symmetry genuinely license numerical calculation, and the much larger domain — the long-run future, novel events, most of what actually matters in decision — where, on his account, there is no basis for any calculation at all, only a state of ignorance that we paper over with convention, with the assumption that the present situation will persist unless there is some particular reason to expect change, and with a spontaneous urge to act rather than to abstain. Alongside that, I want the talk to make audible a second and less famous part of his apparatus, from his early work on probability: that the quantity of relevant evidence behind a judgement is not the same thing as the probability that evidence licenses, so that a precise estimate resting on thin data can be epistemically worse than a vaguer judgement resting on more.

Please write the address to these specifications:

1. British English throughout. Roughly 1,500 words, speakable aloud in twelve to fifteen minutes: varied sentence lengths, no headings, no parenthetical hedging, nothing that requires a footnote.
2. Do not put any words in quotation marks and attribute them to Keynes, and do not supply page numbers, chapter numbers, dates of works, or any bibliographic detail. Render his ideas in paraphrase. There is a historian of economic thought in the second row who checks such things, and a fabricated quotation would be the one unrecoverable error available to me. Refer to his writing descriptively if at all — the early book on probability, the chapter on long-term expectation, the later journal restatement of his argument.
3. No technical term may appear unless the sentence that follows it unpacks it for a lay listener. Assume roughly forty per cent of the room are parents, university officers and people from other disciplines who have never heard of him.
4. Build the central distinction out of one worked example that is not financial and not a flood. Something small, concrete and recognisable to the whole room, which lets the two domains be felt rather than defined.
5. Handle the obvious objection that a modern Bayesian would raise — that there is no second domain, only weak priors and wide posteriors, and that Keynes's category of irreducible uncertainty is just a refusal to assign a number. Take it seriously and answer it, in language the parents can follow. Similarly, note briefly that his early account of probability as a logical relation between propositions came under sustained attack from the subjective-degree-of-belief side and that he never squarely answered it; then say what nevertheless survives that attack.
6. Do not let the talk pretend that Keynes is simply vindicated by current practice. Some of the modern apparatus — sensitivity analysis, scenario work, deliberately coarse or imprecise estimates, stress testing — genuinely absorbs his point; some of it merely decorates a single point estimate with an interval nobody believes. The address should hold both halves of that judgement without turning into a survey of methods.
7. If you use his well-known phrase for the spontaneous urge to act rather than to do nothing, use it once, and make it carry weight; the students will meet it later in fund managers' marketing copy.
8. End with something the students can actually do differently in their first term, without letting the ending curdle into exhortation or a list of virtues.

Avoid the standard registers of the genre: no opening joke at economists' expense, no remark about how long I have been given to speak, no invocation of the future belonging to them.

After the text of the address, append a note of no more than 120 words, clearly marked as not to be read aloud, telling me which two or three sentences are doing the load-bearing work for the reinsurer on the platform and which are aimed at the parents, and flagging any place where a pause or a change of pace is needed for the argument to land.
