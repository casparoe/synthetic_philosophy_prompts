I'm presenting Peirce's "The Fixation of Belief" (1877) at my phil-of-science reading group next week and I've gotten completely stuck on a stretch near the start where he does the history of logic. We've spent the whole term on Popper and demarcation so I keep trying to read it through that, which may be the problem. Here's the bits I mean.

On Bacon:

> "But, superior as Lord Bacon's conception is to earlier notions, a modern reader who is not in awe of his grandiloquence is chiefly struck by the inadequacy of his view of scientific procedure. That we have only to make some crude experiments, to draw up briefs of the results in certain blank forms, to go through these by rule, checking off everything disproved and setting down the alternatives, and that thus in a few years physical science would be finished up — what an idea!"

On Kepler:

> "He accomplished this by his incomparable energy and courage, blundering along (to us) in the most inconceivable way, from one irrational hypothesis to another, until, after trying twenty-two of these, he fell, by the mere exhaustion of his invention, upon the orbit which a mind well furnished with the weapons of modern logic would have tried almost at the outset."

And then, the paragraph I actually want to build my talk around:

> "The Darwinian controversy is, in large part, a question of logic. Mr. Darwin proposed to apply the statistical method to biology. ... In like manner, Darwin, while unable to say what the operation of variation and natural selection in any individual case will be, demonstrates that in the long run they will, or would, adapt animals to their circumstances. Whether or not existing animal forms are due to such action, or what position the theory ought to take, forms the subject of a discussion in which questions of fact and questions of logic are curiously interlaced."

Three things I can't work out.

1. That Bacon sentence. He's laughing at a procedure of "checking off everything disproved and setting down the alternatives." But isn't that... falsificationism? Eliminate the refuted, keep the survivors. Popper's whole pitch is that this is the only thing that ever actually happened in science and that Baconian induction is the fantasy. So what precisely is the target of the mockery here — that it's supposed to be mechanical and rule-driven, that it would wrap up physics "in a few years", or something about "briefs" and "blank forms" as a picture of what evidence is? I need to be able to say what distinguishes Bacon's elimination-by-tables from Popper's elimination-by-refutation, because someone in my group will definitely say Peirce is attacking a proto-Popperian method and I won't have an answer ready.

2. Kepler. Peirce calls the Mars work maybe his "greatest service to science" but then describes the actual route as blundering from one irrational hypothesis to another until he got there by "the mere exhaustion of his invention," and adds that a properly equipped logician would have hit the right orbit almost immediately. Is he praising wild conjecturing (which sounds a lot like bold conjectures and refutations) or saying Kepler only landed it by luck-plus-stamina because his logic was bad? What is "the mere exhaustion of his invention" doing there — is exhaustion a virtue or a confession?

3. The Darwin paragraph, which is the one I don't understand. "they will, or would, adapt animals" — I can't parse that little "or would." And what is it for questions of fact and questions of logic to be "curiously interlaced"? My reading is that he's saying the dispute over Darwinism is partly a dispute about what kind of claim the theory even makes, because it's a statistical claim about the long run and not about individual cases, so you can't settle it by pointing at any particular organism. Am I right about that, or is he just noting that Darwin borrowed a technique from gas theory?

Which brings me to the thing I most want you to poke at. A friend in the group has a take: Peirce is here flagging a theory whose predictions are only long-run frequencies, and no finite run of observations can strictly contradict a long-run frequency claim, so Darwinism as Peirce describes it is a case that breaks Popper's criterion outright and can only be made usable by an extra methodological decision about how much deviation counts as a refutation. Her conclusion is that Peirce spotted the falsificationism problem with statistical hypotheses decades early, and that this paragraph is therefore a demarcation argument in disguise. Pressure-test that for me: does the passage actually carry that much weight, or is she importing a 20th-century worry into a paragraph about the statistical method? And if the statistical objection is real, is it a problem for demarcation (does it push Darwinism out of the scientific on Popper's own test?) or only a problem for falsification as a rule of method, leaving the boundary intact? Pick one and tell me why it's that one rather than the other.

Last thing, and be blunt: the essay's real business is ranking four ways of settling belief — tenacity, authority, the a priori taste, and the method of science — and I'm worried that dragging the Darwin paragraph into a falsifiability debate is exactly the kind of anachronistic abuse Peirce would hate, since for him the interesting thing about the scientific method is social and about what constrains belief from outside, not about what makes a single sentence refutable. If that's right, say so, and tell me what the paragraph is actually there to do in the essay's argument.
