I served six years as president of a small homeowners' association, and for most of that time I was the person who mailed out the violation notices: bins left at the curb past collection day, house numbers obscured by ivy, a boat parked on the street over a weekend. I was quite good at it. Then one August I cited a woman two doors down for her recycling bins, and she answered with a photograph of my own bins, taken from the street that same morning, still on the curb at four in the afternoon. I remember that the only thing I could think to say was that the photograph wasn't really about her case, and that my bins were a separate matter. She said that if my bins were a separate matter, then so was hers, and I had no answer to that at all. I've been turning the thing over ever since — the thought that an accusation can be entirely accurate, and still not something the accuser is entitled to make, and that the defect has nothing to do with the truth of it.

Working it through on my own, I've ended up with a set of questions that I can't resolve. Does admitting your own identical lapse put you back in a position to complain, and if so, does it take a day or a decade? Does it matter who you are to the person you're accusing — the one who was actually harmed, versus a neighbor who merely happened to notice? Is there a separate disqualification for people with spotless records who simply have no business inquiring into the matter at all? And if every complainant has to be screened this way, does anyone get to complain about anything, ever?

What I'd like is a piece of satire that answers those questions by pretending they've already been answered administratively. Please write a formal adverse-action letter of roughly 700 words — the kind an insurer or a credit bureau sends when it has decided against you, complete with a letterhead, a file number, a date of determination, and instructions for disputing the finding. The issuing body is a Bureau of Clean Hands, and the recipient is a resident who filed a complaint against a neighbor. The letter denies the complaint, not because it is false but because the complainant lacks standing to bring it.

The humor should come entirely from the bureau taking this view seriously and building paperwork around it. So: a numbered list of reason codes written in the flat diction of the real thing, covering matters like materially identical conduct within the reporting period, selective enforcement among similarly situated neighbors, and self-exemption. A paragraph stating plainly, and without embarrassment, that the accuracy of the underlying complaint was not examined and is not among the factors considered. A restoration procedure that turns on the complainant formally acknowledging their own conduct, with a waiting period and some note about which other parties may not benefit from it. A separate short section addressed to complainants against whom no findings exist, whose complaints are nonetheless rejected because they have no relationship whatsoever to the matter. And at least one dry figure or line — a processing statistic, a caseload note, a remark about the standing of the bureau's own adjudicators — in which the reader suddenly sees where the whole scheme must end, without the letter ever noticing. Keep it deadpan throughout, with no wink and no narrator stepping outside the document; a reader who has never thought about the subject should find it funny on the surface, and someone who has should recognize exactly which machinery is being run. No real organizations, no philosophers named, no citations of any kind. End with a signature block and a short list of enclosures.
