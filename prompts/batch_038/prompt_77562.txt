So I run — well, "run" is generous, I'm one of three volunteers — a small hardship fund for members of the trades council here. Real money, small scale: maybe forty claims a year, a few hundred to a couple thousand each. Two claims landed on my desk this week and they've wrecked my sleep, because they're the textbook pair. One is a guy in his 50s who put most of his savings into a leveraged crypto thing a coworker talked him into, and now can't make rent. The other is a woman in our 30s cohort who just got a genetic diagnosis that will knock her out of work within a few years, and she has no safety net beyond whatever we give her. Our written criteria says we can't discriminate on the basis of how someone got into trouble, but the committee keeps leaning one way anyway, and I keep finding myself defending the leaning out loud, in increasingly explicit terms.

Which has moved me from vaguely egalitarian to explicitly luck egalitarian over the last few months: inequalities that trace to brute luck are the ones justice has to fix; inequalities that trace to option luck — gambles people actually took, with the odds roughly in view — aren't in the same category, and a fund that pretends otherwise is just underwriting risk-taking with other people's dues.

But I hold a specific version of this and I want you to stress-test the version I actually hold, not the straw one. I don't cash out the brute/option split in terms of desert, or free will, or whether someone "really" chose. I cash it out menu-relatively: a disadvantage counts as option luck for a person just in case there was some other choice on that person's actual menu which they could reasonably have been expected to see as better, and taking it would have avoided the disadvantage. If the menu didn't contain that choice, or if they couldn't reasonably have been expected to pick it out — bad information, cognitive load, time pressure, the way the pitch was framed — then on my account it's brute luck and the claim is in scope. So the crypto guy and the woman with the diagnosis might both be in scope, and not because I've gone soft, but because his menu arguably contained only the pitch he was handed.

Two things I want from you.

First: what would count against this? Lay out the defeaters and rank them, most fatal first, and be honest about the gap between an argument that should make me abandon luck egalitarianism and one that should make me add a caveat. I want especially the ones that bite on my menu-relative version, since that's the piece I'd actually have to surrender — including whether making the menu-relative move just relocates the problem rather than solving it, since now everything turns on an assessment of what someone could reasonably have been expected to see, and I have no idea how to keep that assessment from smuggling in either brute determinism or plain old desert. Say which of these a committed luck egalitarian would concede as a real hit with a straight face, versus which we'd wave off as beside the point. And say what should count as evidence from running a fund like mine: patterns in the claims themselves, patterns in how committees talk about claimants, the way people narrate their own histories in intake interviews. If there's a kind of data I should start collecting over the next few years that could genuinely settle this for me, tell me what it is and what shape of result would count which way.

Second: I've already got one qualification in mind and I want to know if it insulates me into meaninglessness. I'd pair the luck criterion with a floor — nobody goes below some threshold no matter how they got there, because the fund is small and its not a justice system, it's a mutual aid pot. So the view becomes: allocate above the floor according to luck, never let anyone below it. Is that a coherent two-tier position or a retreat that leaves luck egalitarianism with nothing to do? Would someone who thinks equality is about relations between people rather than distribution patterns just say the floor is doing all the work and my criterion is decoration — and would they be right? Tell me whether being that well insulated is a genuine problem for a view of this kind or just what a view that has to survive contact with a committee looks like.

One calibration note: what would move me most personally is a concrete case where the menu-relative test spits out the wrong answer and there's no further qualification available that doesn't also break the cases I care about keeping. If you can build one out of a fund like mine — real-ish numbers, two claimants, a decision the committee has to make — build it.
