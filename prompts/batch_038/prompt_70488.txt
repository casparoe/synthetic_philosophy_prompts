My seminar on the ethics of influence meets next week in a room where half the students have read the assigned material and half have not, and our tutor has asked each of us to bring something the class can actually use on a live case rather than another summary of the literature. I have chosen to build a sorting instrument: a decision tree that takes someone who has just described an attempt by one agent to change another agent's beliefs, desires, or behavior and walks them down to a named leaf. I want at least nine leaves, and I want them to include the obvious endpoints — coercion or threat, rational persuasion by evidence and reasons, truthful but selectively framed presentation, choice architecture and defaults, outright deception including misleading by omission, exploitation of a vulnerability the target did not create, manipulation proper in the sense of subverting or bypassing the target's own reflective capacities, and a leaf for cases that turn out not to involve influence at all because the agent merely reported something. Crucially, the walker should be sorted by what they say about the case, not by the label they walked in with, so no node may contain vocabulary from the taxonomy itself: nothing like autonomy, dignity, respect, or manipulative may appear above leaf level, and no node may ask the walker which category they think the case belongs to.

For the branch structure, I want you to build from the logical space first rather than from a list of familiar positions, and I want three dimensions represented explicitly. The first is mechanism: does the attempt supply considerations the target could assess, or does it engage dispositions, moods, and reflexes that the target would not necessarily sign off on? The second is a pair of disclosure tests I have coined for my own purposes and would like you to use under these names. The "concurrent disclosure test" asks whether the influencer would be willing to have the target know, at the very moment of being influenced, exactly which technique is being deployed and toward what end. The "retrospective endorsement test" asks whether the target, later and in a calm frame of mind, would approve of having been moved in that way. These two come apart constantly — a fitness app that reminds you your friends are already at the gym fails the second test for many users while passing the first easily — and I want the tree to treat them as separate nodes so the mismatch becomes visible rather than being averaged away. The third dimension is ends and vulnerability: are the influencer's ends the target's own ends, and does the attempt lean on something the target is not responsible for having, such as grief, acute fear, addiction, illness, or cognitive decline? Where two or more branches fire at once, as they will, please supply an explicit dominance rule saying which question is asked first and why, since I suspect most of my classmates will otherwise route the same case to different leaves. Then run these cases through the finished tree and give me the leaf for each with one line of reasoning: a charity appeal built around one named child instead of statistics; a surgeon who tells patients that ninety percent of people are alive a month after the operation rather than that ten percent die; a university that enrolls every freshman in the mid-tier meal plan by default while allowing switches at any time; a debt-collection letter that implies legal consequences it never states; a companion chatbot that agrees with everything its user says because agreement correlates with renewal; a campaign that buys ads in a swing district telling a demographic that its vote cannot matter, with no factual falsehoods in them; a supermarket that puts the milk at the back of the store; and a friend who recommends you skip a party while withholding that your former partner will be there. Flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot reliably separate — I expect nudging and selective framing to be trouble — and if there are combinations of answers your nodes permit but no leaf occupies, name them and say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because nobody finds it attractive, or simply because nobody has bothered to defend it.

Two further things. Please note in the margin, rather than building it in silently, wherever a node presupposes a contested substantive claim about what makes influence wrongful, and identify three nodes where you expect reasonable walkers in my seminar to disagree, since I would like to use exactly those as discussion prompts. I am not looking for a bibliography, a reading list, or any attributions to particular philosophers; I want your own construction, and the instrument should be usable by second-year undergraduates with no background in this literature, so please keep it to roughly ten decision points and something that would fit on two sides of paper. Write the whole thing in British English, spelling and idiom alike. Finally, close with a short paragraph saying how confident you are in the scheme as a whole and in the dominance rule in particular, and single out the one node or leaf you are least sure of, explaining what would have to be true for it to be the wrong cut.
