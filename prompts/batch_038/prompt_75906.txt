I belong to a philosophy-of-mind reading group in which one member defends a hard line on machine mentality, and the analogy he reaches for every time is the dictionary. His argument goes like this. The ink marks of a printed entry, and the paper they sit on, have no aboutness of their own: they are about a horse only because lexicographers put that there and readers take it back out. Replace the community of readers and the same marks are about nothing at all, or about something else. A machine's internal states are strings of 0s and 1s, physically no different in kind from ink marks, and they are about bananas, or about a blocked path, only because engineers designed them and we interpret them that way. So machine aboutness is always borrowed and never original, and no amount of architectural complexity changes that, because complexity in the marks is still just marks.

He also has an answer for Dennett's two-bitser, which I raised. As I understand that example, a state made of two bits can be read one way as 01 and, just as legitimately, the other way as 10, and more generally any representation carries an indefinite swarm of further aboutnesses for free, depending on the frame in which it is read — which is roughly how the frame problem gets into focus. His reply is that this confirms rather than undermines his case: a state with two readings is like a word with two senses, an ambiguity resolved by stipulation, so the readings remain ours to confer.

I don't believe him, but I cannot yet say why. Could you audit the analogy?

First, the correspondences: what in the machine plays the role of the ink marks, the lexicographer, the reader, the entry-to-referent relation, the act of looking something up, and the language in which the definition is written? Which of these are one-to-one, and which have no counterpart at all in the target?

Second, what the source quietly imports — for instance, that aboutness is a single relation between a mark and a thing, fixed at the moment of authoring; that there is a reader standing outside the system doing the interpreting; and that the several readings of a two-bitser are equally optional, when the point may be that only some of them are causally load-bearing for the system that has the state.

Third, please separate the disanalogies that actually threaten his conclusion from those that are merely cosmetic, such as the difference between silicon and paper, or the fact that the machine changes state on its own.

Fourth, does the conclusion survive the failure of the analogy, or was the analogy carrying it? If the dictionary picture goes, does the derived-intentionality thesis still stand on independent legs — a Chinese-Room-style argument, or the general claim that syntax is not semantics? And is his stipulation reply about ambiguous senses the strongest form of the objection, or is there a better one? I suspect it gains force if the target is a Dennett who is himself interpreter-relativist about intentional ascription, and I would like to know whether that suspicion is right or whether it cuts the other way.

Finally, propose a better analogy for what the two-bitser is meant to show, and, separately, the best analogy available to him. I keep reaching for a thermometer or a fuel gauge, whose reading seems to be about the temperature because it is caused by the temperature rather than because anyone assigned it — but I can see that this imports problems of its own, misrepresentation and the disjunction problem among them. Compare the candidates on their merits rather than assuming that mine is the improvement.
