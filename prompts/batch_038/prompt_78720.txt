Some background so you know where I'm coming from. I spent forty years in condensed matter physics, mostly computing lattice sums for ionic crystals. Retired three years ago, started reading philosophy for fun, and that's how I ran into the Pasadena game — the coin-flip one from Nover and Hajek where the payoff on the n-th branch is plus or minus 2^n/n, so the expectation comes out as an alternating harmonic series. What hit me is that I spent my entire career on this exact problem in a different costume. The electrostatic energy of a crystal is a conditionally convergent lattice sum too: sum it over expanding spheres and you get one number, over expanding cubes you get another, and the gap isn't rounding error, it's a genuine physical question about the shape and surface of the sample. Nobody in my field called that a paradox. We fix a summation prescription — Ewald, or an explicit neutrality condition, or a stated boundary — write it down, and then the number means something. So when philosophers announce that the Pasadena game has no expectation, my reflex is: naturally it doesn't, you never specified a prescription. The interesting question is whether any prescription here is defensible in the way neutrality is defensible in electrostatics.
