I cover science and ideas for a general-interest weekly. This autumn we are running a three-part series on the fortunes of meritocracy: what behavioural genetics has and has not established about the heritability of conscientiousness and of the capacity to sustain effort over long horizons; what education and labour-market research shows about how much of measured "merit" is inherited advantage wearing a different coat; and what political philosophers have made of the claim that people deserve what they earn.

The letters we received on the pilot piece were worse than hostile — they were uninformative, because at least five distinct claims were being made with the same two words. So the desk has approved an interactive to run alongside the series: a sorter that tells a reader which position on merit and desert they actually hold. I would like you to build the instrument. Please treat this as an exercise in mapping a logical space rather than in labelling people.

**1. Space first, names second.** Identify four to six axes on which the positions genuinely divide, say in a sentence or two what turns on each, and only then locate actual positions in the space you have drawn. At least three of your axes should be ones a general reader has never considered. Candidates I would expect you to weigh, though you may substitute better ones: whether a claim that someone deserves something requires a basis in something the person did, and whether a disposition the person never chose can serve as such a basis; whether merit claims are inherently comparative or can hold of one person without reference to anyone else; whether unequal rewards can be justified at all without anybody deserving them; and — closest to my beat — whether any available measure tracks merit closely enough to distribute by, or whether measure and thing come apart the moment the measure is put to that use.

**2. The tree.** Nine to eleven nodes. Every node is a concrete case with two or three options a reader could engage with without knowing where it leads. You may use conditional branching, but in no more than two places, and only where a later question is genuinely meaningless for readers who took a particular earlier path. Give each leaf a unique path string — 1B, 3A, 5C, 7A — because my interactive team will implement this as a flow.

**3. Vocabulary discipline.** No stem and no answer option may contain the words desert, merit, deserve, fair, just, entitlement, or luck, and no option may name a position. Cases should be domestic and unpartisan: a family garage with two siblings, a nurse and a derivatives trader, a scholarship committee, an inherited farm, an algorithm that scores job applicants, a medal that can only go to one person. Nothing that requires the reader to know which country's politics I am alluding to.

**4. Two nodes I need regardless.** One must turn on the science I have been reporting: if the capacity to grind out effort is itself substantially heritable and substantially shaped by early circumstance, does the claim that someone earned their position lose its force, survive untouched, or survive with a changed meaning? Phrase it so the reader need not settle whether the science is right. The other must be a scarcity case in which two people are equally deserving of a reward only one can receive, and the options must test whether the reader thinks something has then gone wrong or whether they think the question simply stops applying.

**5. Required leaves.** Include, as distinct positions where distinct they are: the view that desert is real and pre-institutional, so that institutions should track it; the pluralist view of David Miller's kind, on which desert is a genuine principle of justice but only within certain sorts of relationship — the workplace rather than the family or the polity; Rawls's separate claim that distributive shares should not be allotted according to moral desert, which is not the same as the claim that nobody ever deserves anything; the historical entitlement view that never asks whether anyone deserves anything; the desert-sceptical position on which effort is as much a matter of luck as talent; the position that a perfectly realised meritocracy would itself be objectionable, of Sandel's and Markovits's kind; the position that the only defect is that the mechanism is broken and can be repaired; the ideological position that believing in meritocracy is itself socially harmful, which I suspect readers will conflate with the previous two; the relational or status-equality position on which the injury to those at the bottom is one of standing rather than of shares; and the view that rewards should track contribution to others rather than effort or virtue. If any two of these collapse into one under your questions, say so and merge them, telling me why.

**6. Vacancies.** List the answer combinations your space permits that no position occupies, and for each diagnose whether the cell is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but nobody finds it attractive, or because nobody has articulated it yet. I want at least two flagged in the last category; I would rather write about an empty cell than about an occupied one.

**7. Separation failures.** Tell me which pairs of leaves my tree cannot actually distinguish. I expect trouble in three places: Rawls and Nozick on the desert questions; Rawls's difference principle against a pure incentive justification for unequal rewards; and the Cohen-style objection that an incentive justification fails when the talented would not need the incentive. If an extra node splits any of them, add it and mark it; if one pair cannot be split by anything a reader could answer in ninety seconds, say so plainly.

**8. Reveal copy.** For each leaf, forty to sixty words for a general reader stating what they have committed themselves to and what it costs them, followed by one sentence containing the hardest question a critic could put to someone who landed there. I will print that question as a teaser for part two.

I do not want a reading list or citations; where a named thinker is the standard reference point for a leaf, a single name is enough.
