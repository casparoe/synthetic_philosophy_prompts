So I got into an argument at a dinner thing last weekend and I still haven't gotten it out of my head. My friend Maya is four years into a PhD project, the hypothesis she started with has basically been picked apart by her own data at this point, and she's staying anyway. Everyone at the table did the standard move — sunk costs are sunk, the four years are gone whether she quits or not, so only the expected value of the remaining years should enter the decision. Maya said something like "that's all true but it's not actually why I'm staying" and then couldn't articulate it, and I couldn't either. I want the conceptual issue spelled out properly, because I suspect the standard line is doing a lot more work than it lets on.

Here's what I'm after: what exactly has to be true for "ignore the sunk costs" to be correct as a piece of decision theory rather than just decent folk advice? My guess is that its quietly assuming a whole stack of things — that the decision is made once and only once, by a single agent whose beliefs and preferences the spending didn't change, in a world where nobody else can observe what you already put in, and where the project's value isn't structured so that nearly all of it only materializes on completion. Take those apart one at a time: say which assumption is load-bearing for the "irrational" verdict and give a concrete case where each fails.

Then the harder bit, which is the one I actually care about. In most of the cases where continuing looks like honoring sunk costs, isn't the past spending just a *cause* of a fresh, entirely forward-looking reason — you learned something from the failures, or quitting in year four costs you a credibility that quitting in year one wouldn't have, or the half-finished thing is worth less than the pile of parts? If that's right, is there any case left where the expenditure itself, and not its downstream consequences, does justificatory work — where "but I already gave four years to this" is a real reason and not a rationalization in a nice jacket? I want a straight answer on that one, not a both-sides shrug.

Two sentences of bottom line up front on whether honoring sunk costs can ever be rational, then the argument. Hard cap of 600 words for the whole thing. And skip the nonrefundable concert ticket and the season-pass-to-the-theatre examples, I've read those a hundred times — assume I know them.
