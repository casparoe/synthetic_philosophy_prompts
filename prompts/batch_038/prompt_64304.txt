I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and for the past eighteen months I have been assembling a long piece on the so-called peace-versus-justice debate in transitional justice: the argument, running now for roughly three decades, over whether prosecuting atrocity deters its recurrence and consolidates fragile democracies, or whether amnesties, truth commissions and hybrid arrangements end wars that prosecution would prolong. I have read the cross-national statistical work that codes transitional justice mechanisms across dozens of countries and finds prosecution-friendly outcomes associated with better human rights records and more durable democracy, and I have read the rejoinders insisting that those studies compare things that are not comparable. I have spoken to mediators who describe amnesty as the price of getting armed men to a table, to human-rights lawyers who regard that framing as a form of blackmail dressed as realism, and to two people who sat on truth-commission-style bodies. The cases everyone cites are the same ones: South Africa's amnesty committee, which granted relief only on full disclosure of a politically motivated act; Argentina's Full Stop and Due Obedience laws, annulled by Congress in 2003 and struck down by the Supreme Court in 2005, followed by years of trials; Rwanda's community courts, which disposed of something on the order of two million genocide cases in about a decade; the Ugandan indictments, where people still disagree about whether international arrest warrants prolonged or shortened the war they were meant to end; and Colombia in 2016, where an accord widely praised for its justice architecture was rejected at the polls by less than half a percentage point, largely on the claim that it was too soft on the guerrilla leadership. Thirty years in, nobody has moved anybody, and each side reads the same evidence as confirming its position.

My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than flatter, is that this is not a hard empirical question but a badly posed one, and that at least three different disagreements are being run together under a single label. First, a problem of admissible counterfactuals: amnesty is on offer mainly where the state was losing or the war was mutually hurting, so treated and untreated cases differ in kind rather than degree, and no amount of data will yield the comparison. Second, a measurement problem: a mechanism coded identically in a database may be a negotiated blanket amnesty in one country and a truth-for-immunity procedure with subpoena powers and public hearings in another, so the sides may be estimating different treatments and reporting them as estimates of one thing. Third, and possibly the load-bearing one, a difference in the question being asked: the quantitative literature is almost entirely prospective — what happens to violence and to the political order afterwards — while much of the advocacy is backward-looking, holding that victims are owed accountability as a matter of what they are due, and that this is not hostage to a coefficient. If that is right, the debate looks empirical because the prospective camp answers in numbers and the backward-looking camp has adopted number-answering rhetoric for tactical reasons, not because the two are engaged with the same claim.

Tell me whether that diagnosis holds, or whether I have it wrong. I want the structure of the disagreement identified and defended with reasons drawn from what each side actually argues — where the load really sits, whether any part of it is genuinely empirical or formal, whether some of it is bedrock moral intuition that neither camp can argue for, and whether the two camps are, as I suspect, answering different questions under one heading. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a research design or natural experiment that could break the selection problem, a formal model, a distinction that dissolves something, a reframing of the dependent variable, or a persuasive case that no progress should be expected and why. If your answer is that the question is ill-posed, say exactly which question is ill-posed and what the well-posed replacement would be. Two constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, and no tour of the cases, which I have already done — and no even-handed hedging that ends by saying both sides have a point. Roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words, written for an educated non-specialist, in a register I could adapt as the spine of a column.
