I teach an undergraduate course in the philosophy of history, and every year or so a well-prepared student arrives with a version of the following argument, which I have never managed to answer to my own satisfaction. It is offered in defence of Anatoly Fomenko's new chronology: the thesis, advanced by a Moscow topologist on the strength of what he describes as an empirico-statistical analysis of narrative sources, that most of what passes for ancient history is a medieval phantom reflected backwards — that the Trojan War and the Crusades are one and the same episode, that the life of Jesus belongs to the twelfth century, and that dendrochronology and radiocarbon dating are circular because they are calibrated against the very chronology they are supposed to test. The student knows perfectly well that historians reject the theory; the argument is designed to show that their rejection cannot be a principled one.

(1) When an observation conflicts with a hypothesis, the conflict is never with the hypothesis alone but with the hypothesis together with the auxiliary assumptions that link it to the observation; logic therefore permits rejecting the hypothesis or rejecting any of the auxiliaries.
(2) Confronted with tree-ring sequences, radiocarbon dates, and eclipse records that do not fit a compressed chronology, Fomenko rejects the auxiliaries — the calibration of the dating methods and their alleged independence from the received chronology — rather than the chronology itself.
(3) This is exactly what the paradigm cases of good science did. Le Verrier and Adams saved the orbit of Uranus by positing an unseen perturbing planet, and Neptune was found within about a degree of the predicted place. Pauli saved conservation of energy in beta decay by positing a particle nobody could then detect, and the neutrino was eventually detected. Astronomers saved the orbit of Mercury by positing Vulcan for half a century, and Vulcan was never found.
(4) In each case, what settled the choice between rescuing the core and rescuing the auxiliary was nothing other than how the bet turned out: auxiliaries that paid off were vindicated, and auxiliaries that failed left the core exposed to revision.
(5) Hence there is no logical or methodological difference between Fomenko's procedure and the procedures that produced Neptune and the neutrino — only a difference in the state of the wager, and Fomenko's wager has not yet been closed.
(6) Therefore the line between pseudohistory and history cannot be drawn by appeal to method at all. Any criterion we propose will either convict mainstream historical science of the same vice or acquit Fomenko of it, and the actual selection between the two is made by whichever community controls the journals, the prizes, and the syllabi.

I want to keep (1). I cannot see how to deny it without lapsing into a crude falsificationism I have spent the course arguing against, and my treatment of underdetermination depends on taking the Duhemian point seriously. I am also convinced that (6) is false, and I regard it as the most damaging idea in the student's paper. What I cannot locate is the step that does the damage.

So: which premise or inference would you reject, why did it look innocent on a first reading, and what exactly does rejecting it cost — in particular, what does it cost someone who wants to retain (1) intact? I want a direct answer to the sharpest form of the student's challenge: is there anything that distinguishes the Neptune case from the Fomenko case other than a report on which wager paid off? If the only available reply is that the mainstream happened to be right, then (5) stands and my course is built on an equivocation. Please also say where your diagnosis turns on something philosophers of science genuinely disagree about — the standing of the demarcation project itself, the reach of the Duhem–Quine thesis, whether ad hocness can be cashed out non-circularly, whether cluster or gradational criteria count as a real answer to (6) — and mark those points as contested rather than presenting one camp's position as the settled view.
