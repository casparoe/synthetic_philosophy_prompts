I am writing an essay for my school's philosophy journal on whether there is a moral duty to vote, and rather than another summary of the positions I would like help diagnosing why the argument seems to have gone nowhere for decades. Both sides grant the same facts: the probability that one ballot decides a large election is negligible, voting costs an afternoon, and who governs matters enormously. Yet one side insists that an obligation must be grounded in what your act actually causes, so its only remaining fallback is the generalization test — what if everyone abstained — which critics dismiss as a confusion, since that antecedent can never obtain and the generalization of voting is harmless in any case. The other side insists that obligations need not be causal at all: fair play toward those whose political labor one free-rides on, or voting as the way a person takes a public stand, can bind her without any decisive ballot in it. Between the two keeps surfacing a third move, that abstainers are not randomly distributed, so someone inclined not to vote should reckon the odds against others like her, where those odds are nowhere near negligible.

My hypothesis is that the real dispute concerns what an obligation must be made of — causal efficacy, or a relation such as fairness or integrity — and that both the generalization test and the correlation point get used to paper over that. Is my hypothesis right, or is this a verbal quarrel about the word duty, two camps answering different questions under one heading, or a hidden empirical question in disguise? Please say what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, or a better-posed question.
