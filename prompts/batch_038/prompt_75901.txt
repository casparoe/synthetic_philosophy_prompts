I am a high school junior in the United States, and I am writing a research paper for my government class on how different legal systems arrange the relationship between religion and the state. My case study is England. I have collected several features of its system that I understand one at a time but cannot fit together into a single picture, and I have not found anyone stating the underlying assumption explicitly — which makes me suspect it is invisible to people inside the arrangement. I would like help working out what it presupposes.

The features, as I understand them. First, the Church of England is the established church, the monarch is its supreme governor and must be in communion with it, and the coronation is an Anglican liturgy. Second, up to twenty-six of its bishops sit in the House of Lords by virtue of their office — seats reserved for the clergy of one particular church, and not for any other religion or for nonreligious worldviews. Third, the church governs itself through a synod, but the rules that synod passes do not become law until a joint committee of Parliament reports on them and both Houses approve them and they receive royal assent, so members of Parliament who are Catholic, Muslim, Jewish, or atheist vote on the worship and internal discipline of the Church of England. Fourth — and this is the part I find hardest to place — the basic unit of the church is the parish, which is a piece of geography rather than a congregation: everyone living inside its boundaries belongs to it whether or not they believe anything, and the parish church owes them services, most obviously weddings, which it cannot refuse to residents merely because the couple is indifferent or hostile to its doctrines. Meanwhile the same church also presents itself, and is often treated, as an ordinary religious body: it seeks converts, it disciplines its clergy, and it insists on the freedom to settle its own teaching without interference.

My questions are these. What conception of religion, or of religious belonging, does this arrangement presuppose? Does establishment treat religion as something like a public utility that must be supplied to a whole population whether or not there is demand, the way water or roads are supplied — and if so, does that analogy actually hold, or does it break down because the thing supplied is supposed to be about the inward faith of the person receiving it? Or does the arrangement instead presuppose that the nation as such is a religious body, of which individuals are members by residence rather than by consent? I would also like to know which rival conceptions the practice quietly rules out — for example, a picture on which a church is simply an association of consenting members and therefore can owe nothing to outsiders — and whether the practice is internally coherent at all, since a body does not easily function both as an autonomous self-governing community and as an organ whose rules Parliament must approve and whose clergy help legislate for everyone.

Please be careful to separate what the arrangement strictly presupposes from what it is merely consistent with, and tell me where it is philosophically underdetermined — where a purely ceremonial or historical reading would account for the same facts without committing anyone to any theory of religion. Finally, if England adopted something like the American model, in which no church has duties to nonmembers and no clergy hold legislative seats, what exactly would have to be surrendered: only the political privileges, or also the parish's obligation to serve everyone in its territory? And does the American arrangement itself presuppose that religion is essentially private belief and voluntary association, or is it underdetermined in the same way?

Please write your answer in American English. I am not looking for sources, citations, or a reading list — I want the reasoning itself laid out.
