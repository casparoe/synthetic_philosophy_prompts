Fine for normal instruments. Then I fell down a rabbit hole on a class of gambles where the expected value doesn't just blow up to infinity (the St. Petersburg thing everyone's heard of) but where it flat out isn't a well defined number, because the series only converges conditionally and you can re-order the terms to land on whatever you like.

Concretely: flip a fair coin until it comes up heads. If heads first appears on flip n, the holder receives (-1)^(n+1) * 2^n / n dollars, and that happens with probability 1/2^n. Add the terms up in the natural order and you get something like 69 cents. Re-order them and you can get any real number you want, or make it diverge outright.

The part that won't let go of me is that in my day job "summing in a different order gives a different answer" is just floating point noise — you paper over it with Kahan summation or pairwise adds and move on. Here it isn't an artifact of finite precision. At infinite precision the order still changes the answer. Which means for a contract like this, whoever controls the order the terms get enumerated in — the settlement schedule, the truncation cutoff, the way the legs get written into the ledger — effectively gets to choose the price. And our engine truncates at term N purely for compute reasons, so two services in the same building quote two different numbers for the identical contract and neither one is exactly wrong.
