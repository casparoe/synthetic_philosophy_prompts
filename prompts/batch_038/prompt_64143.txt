Ok so this isn't really a philosophy-class question, I'm a nurse and I read this stuff at night because my brother does a lot of arguing about it and I want to be able to hold my own, which so far I can't. There's a move I keep seeing in debates about when a war is justified, and I can't find a word for it. I want to know whether it already has one, or whether I'm just describing something everyone already knows under a different label.

The pattern. A war starts with a stated aim — disarm something, push forces back behind a line, whatever. A year in, that aim is clearly not going to be reached with the means being used. Rather than stopping, the government announces a bigger aim: remove the regime, make sure it never happens again, "dismantle the threat" as a whole. And here's the part that bugs me — the new aim seems to change how you're supposed to judge the first year. Strikes that looked wildly disproportionate measured against "disarm the program" look fine measured against "remove the government that will rebuild the program," and since the bigger aim is now the official one and now looks achievable, the earlier casualties get re-filed as contributions to a war that can actually be won. No phase of the war ever has to answer the question "was this worth it given what you said you were doing at the time?" The justification just slides forward and picks up the past on the way. A second, smaller version: an intervention that starts as protecting civilians in a zone, and once the local armed group starts shooting at the interveners, becomes "defeat the group," at which point the civilian harm caused during the protecting phase is described as part of a security operation whose point was always stability. Related worry I can't quite separate out: does the soldier on the other side change status when the aim changes, or was he a legitimate target the whole time regardless of what his government was calling the war?

I know some neighbors and I want to be told how this differs from each. It isn't mission creep, because mission creep is a complaint about the aim getting bigger, and what I'm describing is the way the enlargement cleans up the record of what came before. It isn't sunk cost reasoning either, at least not out loud — nobody says "we have to keep going because we've already paid," they say "we have to keep going because the goal is now larger and now reachable." And it isn't plain goalpost-moving, because in the goalpost case you're just dodging a defeat, whereas here the re-description arguably changes something true about the war's proportionality. Or does it? Honestly that's a big part of what I want an answer on. My vague sense is that there's a live question about whether the justice of a war is assessed once at the outset or continuously as it goes, and something about "last resort" being a standing condition rather than a box you tick before you launch — if the thing I'm describing is really just a corollary of one of those, tell me so and tell me which.

So: name, or no name? I'm not after a reading list and I can't chase down references anyway — I want the concept. If there's no established term, coin one and define it tightly enough that I could tell the legitimate version apart from the illegitimate one, because there has to be a legitimate version: a war genuinely learning what it's fighting for as the intelligence improves, revising its aims in good faith, ought not to be the same as this. Where's the line, and can it even be drawn? Give me the edges and the borderline cases, not just a label — if I come away with only a word I'll consider the whole thing a bust.
