For about eighteen months I've been co-convening a small working group on paid family leave at a foundation that funds state-level policy work, and I want to understand why it isn't going anywhere. Three positions, and everyone in the room knows all three cold. The first is what I'll call the symmetry camp, mostly the labor economist: employers discriminate against women of childbearing age because they rationally expect long absences, so if you make leave entitlements individual and non-transferable and roughly equal in length, the expected cost of hiring a woman converges on the expected cost of hiring a man and the statistical basis for the discrimination evaporates; bonus, fathers who spend solo time with an infant form habits that persist, which is why they keep pointing at the Nordic reserved-months reforms (Norway carved out four weeks for fathers in 1993, Sweden followed in '95, Iceland went to a three-months-each design). The second camp, the two political theorists, says this accepts a baseline that is itself the problem: the goal of symmetry is to make mothers more like the ideal worker, the one with no care responsibilites at all, and it treats care as a cost to be equalized rather than an activity worth valuing; on their view the quotas bought a cosmetic ten or fifteen weeks of stroller-pushing while the default-parent role and the second shift stayed exactly where they were, and the real levers are restructuring the standard work week, putting a price on long-hours norms, building public childcare, and paying for care directly so that choosing it isn't a penalty. The third camp, one fellow who is closer to the funder's board than he probably should be, says both of them are paternalists: households know their own comparative advantage, give the cash and let families split it however they want, and note that non-transferability actively destroys value for the households that would rather bank the months or trade them for a longer, lower-paid leave.

Here's the part I can't get past. All three appeal to the same Scandinavian natural experiments and the same take-up and earnings numbers, and everytime one side produces a study the other two say it measured the wrong thing — the economist says the theorists' outcome variables (share of housework, self-reported sense of who's the default parent) aren't welfare measures, the theorists say his variables (mothers' earnings relative to childless women, or relative to fathers) bake in the ideal-worker standard he's supposed to be arguing against, and the choice guy says both of them are measuring the effects of a policy that shouldn't have been compulsory in the first place. Nobody has moved an inch and I've stopped believing this is an argument about facts. My working hypothesis is that they aren't disagreeing about what happens when you reserve months for fathers, they're disagreeing about which of several different quantities is the one that matters — the mother-versus-nonparent earnings gap, the within-couple division of labor, the social status of care work, household utility as the family itself defines it — and on top of that they're using different baselines (the unencumbered worker, the male-breadwinner era, a frictionless household optimum), so no study could ever settle it. I also suspect there's one genuine empirical question hiding underneath that would at least discipline the fight: whether employer discrimination in this setting is statistical or taste/status-based, because if it's the latter then making leave symmetric could backfire by making both sexes look expensive rather than making women look cheap — and separately, whether the Nordic effects came from the non-transferability or just from the money and the public signal.

So: diagnose this. What kind of disagreement is it really — a verbal one about what equality is doing in each mouth (equal treatment, equal outcomes, equal freedom to be different), two or three groups answering different questions under the single heading of leave policy, a hidden empirical question doing the work, or bedrock intuitions about the value of care that nobody in that room can argue for and everyone just performs? Use what each camp actually says rather than re-describing them at a higher altitude. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves part of it, a study design that would be genuinely decisive about the hidden question, a reframing of the target quantity, or an argument that no progress should be expected and the honest move is to split the group into two conversations. And the bit I most want: tell me if one of the three camps turns out to be a disguised version of another, or whether the framing itself — that leave design is the lever worth fighting about — is the thing they should have been arguing about all along. No bibliography and no lit review, I've read the obvious stuff and I'm not chasing references.
