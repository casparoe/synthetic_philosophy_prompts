I am teaching a third-year seminar on the ethics of collective harm — the familiar terrain of climate change, industrial animal agriculture, and voting, where the central puzzle is that any single person's contribution seems too small to make a perceptible difference to the harm, so it is hard to see how the standard act-based arguments for changing one's own behaviour get off the ground. Most of my students have seen all four seasons of The Good Place, and I want to build one session around it, because its afterlife runs on an explicit point system: every action a person performs earns or loses a determinate number of points, the points are recorded in a cosmic ledger, and admission to the Good Place is a matter of crossing a threshold. That is, in effect, a metaphysics of individual moral contribution — the very thing my students find hardest to believe in the real world — and I want the session to press on it rather than treat the show as light relief.

What I am after is a reading that takes both the show and the debate seriously. First: what does the point system commit the show to? It seems to presuppose that individual acts carry discrete, additive, agent-relative scores, that a global ledger is in principle computable, and that moral assessment is a matter of the total one's acts produce — a fairly pure act-consequentialist moral mathematics. Then: does the show ever actually stage the inefficacy objection, or does it stage the mirror image of it? As I recall, the season-three turn is that the ledger has effectively broken down — no one has been admitted in centuries, because in an interconnected modern economy every purchase and favour sets off consequences too tangled for the accountants to score. If that is right, I want to know whether the show is objecting that individual acts make no difference (a metaphysical claim) or that we cannot know what difference they make (an epistemic one), since those come apart in the literature and my students routinely slide between them. Third, the two characters who respond to the ledger: Doug Forcett, who has devoted his whole life to optimizing his score and is a wreck because of it, and Chidi, whose paralysis in the face of an ordinary purchase is the show's running joke. Is Doug a reductio of the point system or its vindication — does the show think his moral mathematics is wrong, or right but unlivable? And is Chidi's paralysis cured by anything that counts as a philosophical answer, or merely by Eleanor's insistence on relationships and small acts, which looks more like therapy than argument? Fourth, the ending: Michael's speech comparing a person to a wave, visible and measurable for a moment before it merges back into the ocean, and the final image in which one of Eleanor's sparks nudges a stranger to deliver a misdirected letter. Do these answer the inefficacy objection, or do they quietly change the subject from whether my act makes a difference to the harm into whether my act is meaningful to me and to those near me — a conflation I want named if it is there? And is the spark image an argument at all, or does it simply stipulate the causal chain that the objection denies? Finally, I want the honest critic's part: where does this reading strain? The point system is a comic device and, in the first season, the mechanism of a torture experiment; the show's real target may be moral philosophy as self-help rather than the metaphysics of contribution; and its moral universe contains desert, judgment, and an afterlife that the actual debate lacks. If the show resists being read as taking a position on individual contribution, say so plainly and say what it is doing instead — and then tell me what it does add to the debate that argument alone does not, since I suspect the phenomenology of the calculation problem is something the papers assert but never dramatize.
