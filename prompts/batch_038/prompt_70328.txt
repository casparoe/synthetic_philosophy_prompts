I have spent the better part of two decades reading rationalist blogs and forums, and I have become convinced that the word "satisficing" is doing at least four incompatible jobs at once. In a thread about which charity deserves a donation, in an argument about whether a heuristic is a bug or a feature, and in a discussion of whether an artificial agent should be built to aim at "enough" rather than at "most", the same term covers a claim about the price of looking, a claim about what practical reason demands, a claim about cognitive architecture, and a claim about temperament and well-being. The resulting arguments never end, because two people can agree on every recommendation and be on opposite sides of all four claims. I want a printed decision tree that sorts people by which claim they are actually making. The audience is a reading group of about a dozen: half from decision-theory culture, where the local assumption is that a bias is a bias and the only live question is how to debias it, and half from psychology and public policy, who find that assumption naive.

Take the following as the trunk and do not argue for it: in any real choice I make, the alternatives I weigh are a small and decidedly non-random sample of the alternatives open to me, the sample having been drawn by whatever happened to occur to me, and I know this.

Between seven and nine nodes, no more than three branches at any node, legible on one side of A4, because the point is that the group can walk it out loud in a single evening. Every node must be a concrete case or a claim one can accept or reject from one's own reactions, with no hint of what lies down either branch: nothing of the form "do you think rationality is bounded?". Nodes I would expect, in your own phrasing and order: a job search called off after the second acceptable offer, where the options force a choice between a saving, a mistake, and a third thing; a prediction contest in which the model that ignores two of three variables does better out of sample, where the branches turn on whether the ignored information marks a bias in the richer model or a symptom of something else; an aspiration level lowered after a year of failure, where the question is whether the lowering is part of the procedure or evidence that it has broken; a case in which I know another available option is better and take the worse one at no cost to anyone, with nothing about search, memory, time or attention doing any work; and a case in which I must choose among options I have estimated rather than observed, so that the winner is precisely the one I rated highest — is my rating of the winner evidence about the option, or evidence about the rating? Two further topics must appear somewhere on the tree: whether the agent is a person, an organisation running on standing procedures, or a system I am in the business of specifying; and whether the alternatives admit of a single ordering at all.

The leaves must include: Simon's bounded rationality on its own terms; the position that satisficing is not a rival to optimising but optimising once the act of looking has been priced; the ecological-rationality position, on which the bound is a fact about the structure of environments rather than a deficiency in the head; the trait-psychology position, on which maximising is a disposition that makes people miserable without making their choices better; Slote's claim that it is permissible not to produce the most good available; the converse claim, that there are choices in which taking the best is itself the objectionable thing; Popper's incrementalist position, that it is the ambition to optimise which makes social projects dangerous; the position in policy analysis that abandons prediction and seeks strategies which stay acceptable across many plausible futures; the position that specifying a threshold-based agent is a safety measure, together with the objection that an agent below its threshold still wants whatever helps it reach the threshold; and the dissolving position, on which alternatives can be on a par or betterness can fail to be transitive, so there is no maximum to have missed.

The diagnostics matter to me more than the tree. Flag any pair of leaves that no sequence of answers can separate, and say whether that is a defect in the instrument or a fact about the positions. Give at least one pair of leaves whose occupants would answer every node identically and still disagree, and at least one pair who would answer differently and act alike. Say which nodes are empirical rather than conceptual, and what observation would settle them. Then list the combinations your tree opens up that nobody occupies — I have a private suspicion about a view on which the aspiration level is fixed in advance and binding for that very reason, since it was not chosen in response to what is on offer — and for each, say whether the cell is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has looked.

For the leaf key: a code, the name of the position or "unnamed", and one sentence naming what its own advocates would least like to be reminded of. No bibliography, no reading list, no page references; a name attached to a leaf is quite enough.
