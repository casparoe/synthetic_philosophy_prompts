Last term I sat in on a philosophy of mind seminar as an auditor, mostly to keep my reading habit honest, and over the Easter break I picked up a popular book on consciousness in an airport bookshop — the author is a well-known science writer rather than an academic philosopher, and the chapter on dualism contains the following summary of epiphenomenalism:

> 'Epiphenomenalism is the view that conscious experiences are caused by the brain but cause nothing in return: the neural activity set off by a hot pan produces both the recoil and the pain, yet the pain does not produce the recoil, and would be missing from the causal story altogether if it could be removed. It is where you end up if you accept that physics is causally closed — that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause — but are unwilling to deny that there is something it is like to be you. The experiences are real; they are simply, in Huxley's image, the steam whistle on the locomotive, a by-product that contributes nothing to the work. On this view nothing mental ever makes a difference, so beliefs and desires are as causally idle as pains. And the position is fatal to itself, which is not a subtle point. If my pain plays no causal role, then my belief that I am in pain is produced entirely by neurons and not by the pain, so that belief is no better connected to what it is about than a coin landing heads is connected to the weather. It follows that no belief about consciousness can amount to knowledge — not the belief that epiphenomenalism is true, and not the premises of any argument anyone has ever offered for it. The theory does not merely lack support; it removes the possibility of its own support, and that is a contradiction rather than a difficulty. Which is why it survives only in textbooks, as a dialectical stepping stone, and why no working philosopher defends it.'

I found the chapter persuasive at the time, and I am still inclined to think epiphenomenalism is a dead end. A friend of mine who has done considerably more philosophy than I have dismissed the passage as confident nonsense, but would not say why beyond muttering that the author had 'turned an embarrassment into a refutation'. Our seminar leader, incidentally, referred to the difficulty the author describes by a different name — the paradox of phenomenal judgment — and I am not certain whether that is the same objection under a more technical label or a genuinely different claim.

Could you audit the passage for me sentence by sentence, or at least claim by claim, and sort its contents into three piles: what a philosopher of mind working on consciousness would accept without demur, what is a fair compression that a specialist would nonetheless want to phrase differently, and what is simply wrong? In particular I would like to know whether the author's slide from 'epiphenomenalism makes our beliefs about consciousness causally disconnected from their subject matter' to 'epiphenomenalism contradicts itself' is licensed, and what the difference amounts to between a theory that undermines the warrant for believing it and a theory that is internally inconsistent. Please also set out, in the voice of a sympathetic defender, the standard reply that qualia and the reports about them share a common neural cause, so that reliability can survive the absence of causation — and tell me candidly whether that reply actually answers the paradox or merely relocates it, and what the answer turns on (for instance, whether knowledge requires a causal connection, and whether the worry is about justification or about explanation). Finally, two loose ends: whether the generalisation that beliefs and desires are causally idle follows from the argument the author has given, and whether the closing claim that nobody defends the position follows from anything true in the passage, or is doing independent work. If, once the errors are corrected, some smaller and more defensible conclusion remains — something along the lines of 'epiphenomenalism is an unstable place to stop' — please say exactly what that conclusion is and what would have to be added to the argument to earn it.
