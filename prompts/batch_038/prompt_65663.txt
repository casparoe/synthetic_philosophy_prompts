I sit on the exhibits committee of a small city history museum — we mostly get photographed for our 1890s steam fire engine — and we've deadlocked on the wall text for a new permanent gallery about the 1912 streetcar strike. Here's the material we actually own: the streetcar company's payroll ledger covering the strike months (wages going up right before the walkout, and no women's names anywhere in it), the diary of a striker's wife kept in a reused account book, and about forty issues of the local paper whose largest advertiser was the company. They don't line up. The diary says her husband hadnt been paid in five weeks; the ledger says otherwise; the paper mostly frames the strike as a public-order problem. There's also a descendants' association that wants sign-off on any panel naming their ancestors, and our accreditation reviewer keeps citing an interpretive balance standard that nobody at the museum can define for me.

The committee splits three ways and I want all three taken seriously rather than split down the middle. Dana, our registrar, thinks the labels should present the documents and shut up — no framing sentences, no adjectives, let visitors conclude, because any frame we add is just propaganda wearing a lanyard; she's forever invoking Ranke's dictum that the historian's only job is to show how it actually was. Priya, who has a literature PhD and does are oral history work, argues the opposite end: you can't put three sources on a wall in some order without choosing a plot, and the choice among romance, tragedy, comedy and satire happens whether we admit it or not — our current draft is a tragedy with a hopeful coda, which she calls the worst of the four options because it buys uplift we haven't earned. And the director, who has to face donors, says the past only earns gallery space where it speaks to the present, so we should write from where we stand now and say so on the wall; he's fond of Carr's point that the facts of history aren't the same thing as the facts of the past.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting: approve, reject, or approve-with-conditions on the current draft, plus a concrete recommendation on (a) whether we run a framing panel at the gallery entrance and if so what exactly it commits us to, (b) how the wall handles the ledger/diary contradiction when we can't resolve it and one of the three sources was financially compromised, (c) whether the descendants' association gets editorial sign-off or only consultation, and (d) one thing we should cut from the draft entirely. Then give Priya's objection in its strongest form and say why it doesn't get the whole gallery. Around 700 words, plain prose, recommendation up top — no reading list, no bibliography, and don't invent sources we don't own. One more constraint: we cap labels at 45 words, so whatever you propose for the wall has to survive that; flag it if it can't.
