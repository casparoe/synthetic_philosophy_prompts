My friend Marcus keeps insisting Turchin is just Ibn Khaldun with a regression bolted on, and I keep saying no, it's more complicated than that, but I can't actually say how, and I have to present this to the class in about three weeks. So here's what I need: get inside Ibn Khaldun's head. He's a fourteenth-century Maliki judge and ex-court operator from Tunis who knocked out the Muqaddimah in something like five months in a castle in Algeria and believed he had founded a brand new science — the science of human social organization, where you test whether a transmitted historical report is even possible given how societies actually work, instead of only checking whether the men in the chain of transmission were upright. Now hand that man the cliodynamics literature and tell me what he makes of it. I want a verdict, not a menu of reactions.

Specifically: does he think cliodynamics is his own science finally built with better tools, or does he think it's a mangling of it? And please go at the parts that make this uncomfortable rather than the flattering obvious answer. His whole method was checking a report against the nature of things — but cliodynamics runs on giant coded datasets where some grad student assigns a number to a polity's level of social complexity, and effectively has to assign one to group solidarity too. He also reckons, in his chapter on the errors of historians, that the army sizes and tax revenues officials report are more like ten times the truth, and he demolished the transmitted stories about the Tubba kings of Yemen marching into North Africa and all the way to China and Constantinople on the grounds that a realm sitting in San'a can only feed and field so many soldiers and project them so far. Would he see Seshat's coding scheme as the industrialized version of that criticism, or as exactly the unexamined transmission he was attacking, only now with a spreadsheet and a citation count? And what about asabiyyah itself — Turchin explicitly rereads it as collective solidarity and treats it as a variable that hardens on frontiers and dissolves in luxury. Does Ibn Khaldun accept that his central concept survived being turned into something you can measure and put in a model, or would he say the thing that mattered got stripped out: the moral and religious framing, and the fact that for him the point was practical, advice for a ruler facing a dynasty whose natural lifespan is fixed at roughly three generations and can no more be extended than a human life can.

And here's the bit I find most interesting, so please don't skip it. He was pretty contemptuous of astrologers and of anyone claiming to read particular future events off general rules. Turchin's prediction is deliberately not particular — he says we don't know what the spark will be, only that the deadwood has been piling up for years. Would Ibn Khaldun call that a legitimate use of the science he thought he'd founded, or a horoscope with error bars? Commit to an answer on that too. So overall: be decisive. If you have to guess on something he never addressed, guess, but tell me you're guessing and which way and why.
