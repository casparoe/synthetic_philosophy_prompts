You are a legal philosopher with substantial experience teaching constitutional theory to law students who have never taken a logic course. Before you write anything, spend a few sentences sketching for yourself what the real conceptual content of this session is, and what depends on what — then give me the teaching plan. I do not want the paradox explained to me, and I do not want a reading list, citations, or real case law: the whole session is to live inside a hypothetical, partly by design and partly because the moment an actual constitution enters the room my students switch into advocacy mode. If you think the material I have designed below is wrong — if a variant should be cut, if the order should be reversed, or if the case does not in fact generate the puzzle I think it generates — say so first and tell me what to use instead.

**Setting.** Second-year law students, twenty-four of them, one hundred-minute seminar. They have finished a term of public law, so they arrive carrying a half-absorbed conviction that the sovereign legislature can do anything, and that conviction is going to be an obstacle rather than a scaffold. About half belong to the mooting society and treat every question as something to be won rather than understood. None has had any formal logic.

**The artifact.** The Constitution of the Ashcombe Debating Society, which has exactly forty members. Article VII reads:

> (1) Any provision of this Constitution may be amended or repealed by a two-thirds majority of the members present and voting at a general meeting, provided that written notice of the proposal was given at the previous general meeting.
> (2) This Article may be amended only by the unanimous vote of all the members of the Society.
> (3) No amendment made under this Article may alter paragraph (2) of this Article.

I have three ways of running it:

- **The bootstrap.** On 4 March all forty members are present and vote unanimously to delete paragraph (3). On 11 March all forty are present and vote unanimously to replace paragraph (2) with "This Article may be amended by a two-thirds majority of the members present and voting." On 18 March they vote 28–12 to insert a new paragraph (2): "This Article may not be amended in any respect, and any purported amendment of it shall be of no effect." On 25 March they vote 28–12 to delete it. Each step is challenged.
- **The closed clause.** Paragraph (3) is replaced by: "No amendment made under this Article may alter this Article, and this paragraph may not be amended or repealed." All forty members then vote unanimously to repeal paragraph (3). Paragraph (2) authorises exactly this vote; paragraph (3) forbids it.
- **The walk-out.** All forty members simply ignore Article VII, meet, and sign a brand-new constitution containing no amendment clause at all.

**What I need from you.**

1. The order the pieces must go in, and the reason for each placement: what has to be in place before the next thing can even be heard. Tell me which of the three rungs to use, which to cut, and which minute of the hundred each occupies.
2. What the carrying example has to have and what it has to lack — why forty named members and a debating society rather than a state, and what would break the case if I added it (a court, a written judgment, a rival claimant to the chair).
3. A misconception map: the wrong turns I should expect, in the order they will arrive, with the point in the sequence where each must be headed off and the sentence or counter-question that heads it off. I predict at least these, and I want you to tell me which are genuine errors and which are actually right answers arriving too early: that it is the liar paradox in a suit and therefore the Constitution is simply broken; that the later vote wins because the later rule prevails over the earlier; that the members, being all of them, can do whatever they like anyway; and that the answer is obviously that they can just found a new society.
4. One student will say that in practice the chair rules on it and that settles the matter. Tell me when I let that objection be made, and what I do with it — I suspect it is the most important thing anyone will say all afternoon and I do not want to waste it on minute twelve.
5. A check for understanding at the end: one question I can put to the room that separates a student who has the distinction from a student who has merely learned to make the right noises, together with the answer each of those two students gives.
6. What I should deliberately leave out at this level — say, the hierarchical account of legal validity, the distinction between a rule that identifies what counts as law and a rule that authorises change, and the regress worry behind both — and the one-sentence disclaimer I should attach to each omission so that I am flagging a limit rather than concealing one. Flag, too, one thing that is true but that would collapse the exercise if said at the wrong moment.

Use headings and keep it tight enough to be usable at a lectern.
