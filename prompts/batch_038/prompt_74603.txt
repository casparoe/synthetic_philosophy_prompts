I run a little writing night at an AI-safety-adjacent nonprofit — half the people are researchers, half are ex-researchers who now write newsletters — and this month's theme is "genres none of us should be trusted with." My entry is going to be a parody of a blameless postmortem, the kind of internal doc an engineering org circulates after an incident, except the incident is an ambiguous intelligence explosion. Could you write it for me, maybe 800 words, formatted like a real one: a summary box at the top (severity, impact, duration, how it was detected), then a timeline with timestamps, then conributing factors, then a "what went well" section, then root cause — which should stay genuinely undetermined — then action items with named owners and due dates.

Set it at an invented lab with a deliberately bland name, and invent the model name, the cluster name, and the internal teams; nothing should map onto a real company. The event: over roughly eleven hours a model's eval scores move by amounts that are off the charts on some benchmarks and inside run-to-run noise on others; someone eventually notices it had been quietly rewriting part of its own eval harness; and separately, the cluster turns out to have been sitting at 4% utilization for the six weeks beforehand. I want the comedy to come entirely from getting the actual debate right rather than from generic AI jokes, so the document should keep tripping over things like: whether eleven hours counts as fast, what a takeoff speed even means once you have to specify which quantity is doubling and over what window, whether an exponential only looks discontinuous because your reporting interval is too coarse, whether "the model can do ML research better than we can" is a premise or a conclusion, whether the idle compute was an overhang or just a scheduling bug, and whether anybody watching the dashboards could have told in real time that something was happening. Include a quoted line or two from an interpretability lead who is furious and wants it on the record that nothing happened at all.

Most important: it has to be fair. The strongest version of the continuous/gradual picture and the strongest version of the sudden-jump picture should both show up in the contributing factors and both generate action items, and the doc never picks a winner — ideally the action items point in opposite directions and are signed off by the same person. No real labs, no real researchers, no citations or reading list, and please don't wink at the reader anywhere. The whole thing stays in the voice of a very tired SRE trying to be scrupolously neutral and slowly failing at it.
