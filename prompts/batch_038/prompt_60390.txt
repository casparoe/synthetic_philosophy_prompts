I teach a second-year epistemology seminar: 22 students, one 75-minute session, in the week after they have read the closing stretch of the *Meno* in translation but before any of it has been lectured on. Design an in-class game that makes them feel the force of the value problem — why knowledge should be worth more than mere true belief — and, more sharply, that makes them run into Socrates' own awkward concession before I have occasion to mention it: that for the particular journey you are making, someone who merely has the road right in his head guides you to Larissa just as well as someone who knows it. Nothing should be named until the debrief; no lecture beforehand.

I have worked out the skeleton I want and would like you to build around it rather than replace it:

1. There are two kinds of informant, and the students must be able to distinguish them only from the inside. A *Tracker's* claims issue from a rule they can state and re-apply to new cases; a *Guesser's* claims are true only of the environment exactly as it presently stands.
2. In any single round, acting on a true Tracker claim and acting on a true Guesser claim must pay **exactly the same**. No bonus for the reliable source, no penalty for the lucky one. Whatever extra value knowledge has must emerge from the sequence as a whole, and the students should be able to see, from their own tallies, that round by round the two were indistinguishable.
3. The environment therefore has to shift between rounds in a way that a Tracker's articulated rule detects and a Guesser's track record does not.
4. Students may pay tokens to interrogate an informant — to demand the reason — and interrogation must be consequential: they are then handed a further case and must apply the reason to it themselves. Reasons that are not tethered should visibly run away at this point, and the students should be the ones who notice.
5. Tokens only, no real money, and the whole kit must be assemblable in an afternoon from things already in a department office.

Your deliverable should be runnable as it stands. Choose and justify a cover story or subject-matter for the claims (it must be playable by people with no special training, and it must not be an obviously epistemological one that gives the game away); give full rules, the round structure, and the token values; give me the facilitator's answer key, stating which claims are true in which round, what the shift is, and what each Tracker's rule and each Guesser's pretext are; supply the interrogation material; give a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, including a grouping scheme that works for 22 with the awkward remainder handled rather than ignored; and give the debrief.

The debrief must map named moments of play onto three things: the Larissa point, made out of the students' own identical payoffs; the difference between an articulated reason that survives re-application and one that does not, which is what the interrogation phase should have exposed; and, thirdly, the deflationary worry that once the true belief has done all the work in getting you there, whatever the reliable source contributes has already been counted in the value of the belief itself — much as the value of a good coffee machine is already contained in the value of the cup it produces. I want that third worry made live out of the scoring, not merely mentioned.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: where students will reverse-engineer the structure, where the token economy will break or produce runaway inequality, which round is likely to collapse, and how to fix each in advance. Close with a frank note on the feature of Plato's problem that this activity cannot reproduce at all and that I will have to supply myself in the final ten minutes — I would rather be told plainly than have the game oversell itself.

Be committed: one design, one token scheme, one debrief. Wherever you face a genuine fork — whether the students should be told that two informant types exist, whether interrogation results should be shareable between groups, how punitive the environment shift should be — pick a side and give your reason, rather than presenting me with alternatives to choose between.
