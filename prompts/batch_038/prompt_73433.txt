I'm TA-ing the social choice week of an intro political philosophy lecture and I get the 25-person section for 80 minutes on Thursday. Every year my professor does the same thing: writes the four conditions on the board, states Arrow's result, and the room just nods along, nobody actually believes it bites, and the section is dead for the rest of the hour. I want to run a game instead where they walk into the problem themselves, and I specifically don't want anyone to hear the words "Arrow," "impossibility," or even "theorem" until the last ten minutes. Most of these kids haven't done anything formal since high school, so nothing can require notation beyond filling in a ranking of three things on an index card.

The shape I have in mind — tell me if it's bad — is that they sit in committees of five, and each committee has to write down, in plain English on a card that gets posted on the whiteboard, a rule for picking one of three options (I'm thinking where the section party goes, low stakes and a little silly, three fixed choices I supply so nobody can dodge by adding or removing options). Then a pair of "challengers" from a different committee gets to build a ballot profile — they literally assign all five voters their full rankings — and their goal is to make the committee's own rule produce an outcome that's objectionable in whatever way is written on the challenge card they drew. The cards are the conditions in disguise, so I need you to write that deck: something like "make the committee pick a place that every single one of the five voters ranks below some other place," or "make the winner change even though not one voter changed their mind about which of the two front-runners they like better," or "show that one person's ballot is doing all the work here" — each with the plain-language script a challenger can read aloud to explain why the result is bad without ever naming the condition, and then, separately for me, a key mapping each card onto Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, non-dictatorship, and whatever else you think deserves a card. I also need a library of killer profiles: for plurality, for a two-round runoff, for Borda-style point counting, for pairwise comparisons with the chair breaking ties, and for consensus-with-a-single-veto, give me the actual 5-voter/3-option preference tables that break them, which card each one corresponds to, and the one sentence the challenger says afterward. If for some rule there just isn't a profile with only five voters and three options that pulls off a given failure, say so and tell me which card to pull from the deck instead of fudging it — I'd rather know going in than get caught at the board. Committees score a point for surviving a round, challengers score for breaking a rule, roles rotate, and there's no real money involved, just points and maybe candy for the winning committee.

Then the debrief, which is where I need the most help. I want it to end with the class reading their own challenge cards back off the board and realizing they've been demanding all of them at once, and I want them to write the theorem's hypotheses as their own list of complaints rather than mine. So: exact timeline with minutes per phase, what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this (I already suspect committees will write "the chair decides if there's a tie" and think they've won, or that the challengers will just fail to find a breaking profile and the whole thing stalls out — how do I keep the pace up when that happens, and do I feed them a profile from my library without giving the game away), how I handle a hypercompetitive section versus a flat one, and a quick adjustment if I'm handed 40 students or only 45 minutes. And the part I most want you to be straight with me about: a game can only ever produce counterexamples to particular rules, never the result itself, so some clever committee could survive every round I throw at it and walk out thinking "see, totally fine, you just need a good rule." Give me the exact line I say when that happens, and tell me plainly which features of the actual theorem the activity structurally cannot reproduce — the universality over all possible profiles, the fact that it's about social orderings and not just a chosen winner, whatever else — so I know what I have to supply by hand in the last ten minutes.
