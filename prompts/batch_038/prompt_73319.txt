I am a senior in high school, and this spring I have been working through a popular trade book about mathematics and consciousness that I found on my older brother's shelf. It has a great many italics and almost no formulas, which is why I liked it, but its closing chapter has set off an argument in my school's philosophy club that we cannot settle. The chapter's summary of Gödel runs as follows:

"Gödel's 1931 result destroyed the dream of a complete mathematics once and for all. He proved that any sufficiently complex formal system contains statements that are true but can never be proved, and that such a system cannot even establish its own consistency. He managed this by writing an arithmetical sentence that says, in effect, this sentence is not provable, which converts the liar paradox into a rigorous theorem. Because a computer is nothing but a formal system grinding out proofs, it is permanently sealed inside its own Gödel sentence; a human mathematician, by contrast, can step outside the system, look at that sentence from without, and simply see that it is true. That is why no machine will ever equal a mind at mathematics, and why general artificial intelligence is a fantasy: whatever program you build, there are arithmetic truths it cannot see and we can. The theorem also shows that no formal theory of anything — physics, economics, theology — can be both complete and consistent, so there will always be questions that no amount of evidence can settle. Indeed, nobody can ever know that arithmetic is consistent, only hope. In this sense mathematics itself proves the existence of mystery."

My club is split down the middle: half of us think this is a fair popularization, and the other half think it is what people say when they want to feel superior to computers. I am afraid I am a biased judge, since I would rather like the argument to work and would also rather like strong AI to be possible, and I have not yet discovered which of those I want more. Could you go through the passage and sort its claims into three piles — what a working logician would sign without qualification, what is a simplification that quietly changes the content, and what is false as stated? Four things especially. First, which hypotheses do the incompleteness theorems actually require, and which of them does this passage drop, since the phrase "any sufficiently complex formal system" seems to be doing enormous unexamined work. Second, is the locution "true but can never be proved" licensed at all, and true from what standpoint, given that the system by hypothesis cannot prove the sentence in question? Third, does the step from the theorems to the comparison of minds with machines go through; if it does not, what would a defender have to add in order to repair it, and are we in fact entitled to the additions? Fourth, does the sweeping claim about physics, economics, and theology survive contact with the theorems as they are actually stated? Please explain the technical points in terms a bright high school reader can follow, define any term the passage leaves vague, and write in American English. I am not after a reading list or a catalog of who has argued what — I mainly want to know which of these sentences I can repeat at club on Thursday without embarrassing myself.
