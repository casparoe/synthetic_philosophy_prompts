In Book IV, Chapter VII of *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*, after a long critique of the monopoly of the colony trade, Adam Smith turns to the question of what Britain should actually do about its American dominions. He sketches two broad courses: a gradual relaxation of the commercial restraints, undertaken deliberately and in a manner that would let Britain and the colonies separate as friends rather than as enemies; and a constitutional union, in which the colonies would be admitted to send representatives to the British legislature in proportion to their contribution to public revenue, would assume a share of the national debt, and would thereby be incorporated into a single political community. Smith devotes considerable attention to the obstacles facing the second course — British unwillingness to share offices and patronage, the reluctance of colonial men of standing to exchange local prominence for a minor place in a much larger assembly, and the further consideration that, as American wealth grew, the center of gravity of such an empire would naturally migrate westward across the Atlantic.

Readers disagree about how to take the union proposal. Some treat it as Smith's genuine, if pessimistic, reform program: a serious third way between mercantilist subordination and independence, defended with the same concreteness he brings to other questions of policy. Others treat it as a rhetorical device — an unworkable scheme introduced precisely so that its unworkability can be exhibited, thereby demonstrating that no constitutional arrangement can preserve an empire whose commercial logic is monopoly.

Write an argument for the second, reductio reading. Your case should meet the following requirements.

1. Argue from the internal architecture of the text only. Do not appeal to Smith's correspondence, his subsequent career, his private views on the American conflict, or what happened after 1776.

2. Concede and then explain away the strongest evidence for the sincerity reading. At minimum: the plan's fiscal and apportionment detail, which looks like design rather than parody; its presentation as one of two remedies rather than as a foil; and Smith's general practice elsewhere in Book IV of offering cautious, gradual, implementable advice while openly predicting that it will not be adopted. If you invoke parallel passages to make that last point, identify them by book and chapter and paraphrase them rather than quoting.

3. Give a positive account of the work the device does. Explain why Smith would need to demonstrate the impossibility of union at that point in the chapter, given what he has already argued about the monopoly of the colony trade, the cost of colonial defense, and the divergence between the interest of merchants and the interest of the public. A reading that says only "the plan would not work" is not sufficient; you must show what conclusion the impossibility is meant to compel.

4. Settle the relationship between the two courses. If the union proposal is a reductio, what is the operative recommendation of the chapter, and does Smith's discussion of gradual relaxation function as that recommendation or as a second, softer version of the same impossibility? Commit to an answer.

5. State the interpretive principle you are relying on — the principle that licenses inferring an ironic or self-defeating intent from the features of the text you have identified — and defend it against the charge of uncharity. Say why your principle does not license reading any sufficiently detailed but unpopular proposal as covert satire.

6. Supply a diagnostic. Specify at least one textual signature that would, if present in the chapter, refute the reductio reading, and explain why you think the chapter lacks it. Be concrete enough that a reader could check your claim against the text without access to your reasoning.

Where you refer to Smith's wording, paraphrase; do not reproduce quotations, and locate passages by book, chapter, and part rather than by page or paragraph number.

Present the argument as a sequence of numbered claims, each supported rather than merely asserted, and close with a short paragraph stating your conclusion together with the single strongest objection that survives your treatment of it. Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,500 words.
