Question 3 (25 marks). Bradley's regress is usually introduced as a problem about the reality of relations, but structurally similar regresses are said to arise for the unity of the proposition. Set out the regress concerning relations. Then explain how an analogous regress is supposed to arise for propositions, and say what a successful account of propositional unity has to deliver. And the answer as submitted (typos are the student's, obviously don't mark for style):

"A proposition like 'a is F' has ingredients we can list: the object a, the property F, and whatever ties them together. Bradley's argument in Appearance and Reality is that if the tie is itself one more ingredient, then you need a further tie to attach that first tie to a and to F, and a further one to attach that, and so on forever. So any account that treats the tie as another item on the list is doomed, and Bradley concludes that relations are not real — they belong to the way thought carves things up, not to reality, which is a single all-inclusive experience. The same argument works for propositions, because a proposition is supposed to be more than the list of its parts: its the difference between a sentence and a heap of words.

Frege basically solved this with unsaturated concepts. An unsaturated item doesn't need a further tie to hook onto a name, because hooking onto a name is what it is. So the regress stops, and Bradley would have had to accept that, since all it makes explicit is that the tie was never a thing to begin with. Which goes to show the whole regress was a verbal dispute about whether you count ties as items.

If you don't want to go the Frege route, the way to block it is to say relations are internal: aRb holds in virtue of the monadic natures of a and b, so there's no relational ingredient left over that needs tying. That's Bradely's own preferred view and it does the job.

The regress is vicious rather than benign because you could never finish the infinite sequence of ties, and an account that requires an infinite task to be completed explains nothing.

Russell drew the obvious lesson: since the regress refutes unitary propositions, he dropped them and went in for the multiple relation theory, where judging is a relation between a subject and several items that never get fused into a single thing. That's the right move, but it has a cost — the multiple relation theory can't say what makes one ordering of relata into a judgement rather than a different one, which is the unity problem coming back for Russell."
