The dogmatism paradox, as Kripke posed it, runs roughly like this: if I know that p, then I know that any future evidence against p will be misleading; and knowing that, I seem rationally entitled to disregard whatever evidence against p turns up, which is absurd, since it would make knowledge immune by construction to counter-evidence. Restate that argument as faithfully as you can in my vocabulary, reading "knows p" as some state of a clinician's posterior and "further evidence against p" as the result of another test. Then give me the residue report. Which premises survive intact — is there a genuine counterpart in threshold reasoning to the claim that some further test is worth nothing and may rationally be skipped? Which claims survive only if their meaning is quietly altered, and what does the alteration cost? And which claims have no expression at all in a framework where every doxastic state is a number and every revision is conditionalization? I'm especially interested in whether the move from "knows p" to "is 0.99 confident in p" preserves or destroys the argument's force, since that seems to be where everything turns. Finally, commit to a verdict with reasons rather than surveying the options: is the residue a real blind spot in decision-analytic thinking, something it structurally cannot represent about knowledge, or does it reveal that the paradox was trading on a distinction between knowledge and very high confidence that does no substantive work?
