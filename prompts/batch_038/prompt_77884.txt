You are a philosopher who works on the normative foundations of public policy and on intergenerational justice, and who also has a working grasp of how future losses are valued in civil litigation. I should like a careful reconstruction of what two official British practices presuppose about the moral weight of the future. Think hard throughout about the difference between what a practice cannot do without, what it merely sits comfortably with, and what it leaves open.

**The two practices**

(1) *The courts.* In England and Wales, when a claimant has been catastrophically injured, her future pecuniary losses — the lifetime cost of care, of aids and equipment, of lost earnings — are converted into a single lump sum payable now by applying a discount rate that is fixed centrally: the Lord Chancellor sets it under the Damages Act 1996, and it binds across the jurisdiction. From 2017 until the end of 2024 that rate was negative; it stood at −0.25% immediately before being raised to 0.5% in January 2025. Under a negative rate the lump sum is larger than the simple nominal total of the costs it is meant to cover: the law proceeds as though money set aside today, invested as safely as a claimant must invest it, will not merely fail to grow in real terms but will shrink. The rate is flat, moreover — a cost falling due in fifty years' time is discounted at the same rate as one falling due next year, with no step-down for remoteness. The official rationale has centred on the claim that a claimant, unlike an ordinary investor, cannot afford to take risk and must invest prudently; the appropriate benchmark is therefore the real return available on the safest assets, net of tax and of the cost of administering the fund.

(2) *Appraisal.* The same state, in the guidance it gives departments for appraising public spending, requires future costs and benefits to be discounted at a social time preference rate of a few per cent in real terms, with the rate stepping down at long horizons so that effects falling many decades out are discounted less heavily than near ones. The official story is that the pure rate of time preference — the discount applied to a future good simply because it is later — is very low or even zero on ethical grounds, and that the bulk of the rate comes from expecting future people to be better off, combined with the assumption that an extra unit of consumption is worth less to someone who already has plenty.

**The puzzle**

In the courtroom, one identified stranger's care in year fifty counts at close to face value — indeed at more than face value while the rate was negative — with nothing subtracted for its remoteness. In the appraisal of a flood defence or a low-carbon scheme, an anonymous stranger's identical need in year fifty counts at a fraction of its nominal size. Neither treatment is an oversight; each is administered by people who take themselves to be applying the correct treatment of time, and each has been reviewed and re-endorsed many times over.

**What I want**

Begin, before any analysis, with an explicitly exploratory pass: sketch the candidate presuppositions you can see in each practice, flag which strike you as load-bearing and which as incidental, and note any place where my description is too thin to bear the conclusions I am asking for. Only then commit. Specifically:

1. For each practice separately, reconstruct the view of the significance of future well-being that it presupposes. Sort the commitments you identify into three classes — those the practice could not survive without; those it merely fits comfortably with; and those on which it is neutral, so that rival views would license it equally well. A table is the right format for this.

2. Decide whether a single underlying account makes both practices coherent, or whether they rest on incompatible commitments. Consider at least the following candidate loci of the disagreement, and say which of them actually does the work: the weight of future welfare as such; who bears which risks; the standing of market returns as a normative fact; the identity and separateness of the person whose welfare is in question; the difference between compensating an identified individual after the event and choosing a policy before it; and the difference between restoring someone to the position she would have occupied and maximising aggregate welfare. If you conclude that part of the apparent inconsistency is verbal — that the two practices use 'discount' for different operations altogether — say precisely which part, and say what is left standing once the verbal component is removed.

3. On practice (1): does the statutory rate express anything about the value of the claimant's future, or only a prediction about her portfolio? Press the question whether a discount rate can be purely descriptive here, and whether a negative rate is best read as the law's judgement that this claimant's future goods are worth more in present terms than a positive rate would imply, or as a judgement about assets, tax and fund management. Consider what follows from the fact that the identical arithmetic would apply to a claimant who intended to spend the entire award in the first year.

4. On practice (2): the declining schedule is standardly motivated by uncertainty about what the discount rate will turn out to be. What does that presuppose about whose uncertainty this is? If the pure rate of time preference is an ethical parameter rather than an empirical one, in what sense can the state be uncertain about it, and what would it have to believe about how ethical questions get settled for the schedule to be anything more than a technical device?

5. Say which rival positions on the treatment of the future each practice rules out — for instance, a view on which the pure rate must be exactly zero as a matter of ethical necessity; a view on which the social rate simply is the market rate of return; a view on which the welfare of future people is not comparable with ours; a view on which only effects on identifiable contemporaries matter. For each, state which of the two practices it would render incoherent and which it would leave untouched.

6. Trace one reform. Suppose a single uniform rate were imposed on both practices, composed of a zero pure rate of time preference plus a growth term reflecting expected increases in wealth and the declining marginal value of consumption. What would happen, concretely, to the arithmetic of a catastrophic-injury award and to the arithmetic of a long-horizon scheme appraisal? Would courts have to cut lump sums on the ground that the claimant's future care will be delivered by a richer and more productive economy? Would appraisal have to abandon its declining schedule? Say whether the resulting picture is a reductio of the unified theory, of one of the practices, or of neither — and if of neither, what the two practices must have been doing all along.
