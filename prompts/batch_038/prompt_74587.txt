I'm TA-ing an intro ethics discussion section — 25 students, 50 minutes, week is on aggregation and moral status. Most of them come in with the reflex view that bugs probably don't feel much and that settles it. I want an in-class activity that makes them feel the pressure of the scale argument — huge numbers of tiny short-lived creatures swamping a very low probability of sentience — without me saying any of that up front. They should hit it through their own numbers: commit to some exchange rate early, then discover what they signed up for.

So build the whole thing. Rules, what goes on the board, cheap materials, a points or scoring system that doesn't need real money, and a timeline that actually fits 50 min with 25 people (small groups are fine). Then a debrief that maps what happened onto the argument — specifically which parts of the squeeze came from the aggregation and which came from the probability, since those get blurred together.

Two things I care about extra. (a) I want the activity to at least gesture at the worry that the probability isn't a sharp number at all — that "somewhere between basically zero and 10%, genuinely no idea" might be the honest state. Can you stage that, or does any workable game force them to a point estimate and thereby misrepresent the situation? Be honest about which, rather than papering over it. (b) Tell me what predictably breaks the first time I run it and how to handle it, especially the student who games the scoring by writing down an absurdly low figure on purpose.

Also: don't let them walk out thinking we proved insects matter. The point is the structural bind, not the verdict.

Where you're inventing numbers for the sake of the game, mark them as invented; where you're leaning on a real order-of-magnitude fact, tell me if you're shaky on it. And if you think the setup is just a bad idea — that a point-scoring game trivializes exactly the thing it's staging, or that 50 minutes with 25 people can't carry it — say that plainly instead of handing me something polite.
