Right — for this one I want you to put on two hats at once: a poet who makes erasure/blackout poems, and someone who has spent years sitting in a record office getting to know what a clerk's handwriting tells you. Think it through in stages rather than lunging at the poem; the stages matter and I'll want to see all of them.

Context, briefly. I'm putting together a little pamphlet for the last session of a masters module on archives, evidence and power. The thing I keep failing to get across to the group is that a source isn't a window that happens to be dirty — it was made by someone, for a purpose, and the people it is mostly *about* never held the pen. Every column of a record is a decision about what was worth writing down. I'd like a centrepiece that makes that felt rather than argued. Just the document and the poem and a short note: no bibliography, no named scholars, I'm doing the reading list myself.

**Stage one: the page.** Write me a page from an invented estate register of enslaved people on a British Caribbean sugar estate, 1820s — fifteen to eighteen entries. Columns roughly: name or designation (first names only, or things like "woman belonging to the boiling house"), age, sex, occupation, condition or state, value in pounds, and a remarks column. Make it properly bureaucratic. The clerk is not being cruel for entertainment, he is being tidy: he is noting what his employer will want to check against the last return, and he totals the page at the bottom. Two or three remarks should be disciplinary (running away, insolence, refusing work), one or two should record a birth or a death, and the values should be dull round numbers. No editorialising, no winks at the reader — the flatness is the point. And keep it spare: no gratuitous detail, the arithmetic does the work.

**Stage two: choose a spine.** Before writing anything, list me four to six candidate spines for the erasure — e.g. the recurrence of one adjective across rows; two entries adjacent on the page who turn out to be mother and child; the way a disciplinary remark reads differently once you strip the words around it; the total at the foot of the page. A line each on what the poem would be doing. Then pick one and say in a sentence why it earns its place.

**Stage three: the erasure.** Rules, and please be strict with them, because the constraint is the argument:

- Deletion only. No added words, no respellings, no reordering, no squeezing a letter out of a longer word.
- Line breaks are yours, and this is where the work happens. Each row keeps one person seperate from the next; your lineation is allowed to let words from different rows share a line. Make that crossing deliberate — the opening line should draw on at least two entries, the closing line on a different set again.
- Keep no more than about one word in six. At least one entry must contribute nothing whatsoever, and I want that absence to be a decision with a location in the poem, not an oversight.
- Twelve to sixteen lines.
- Banned from the poem: silence, voice, memory, ghost, history. No capitalising nouns for solemnity either.

**Stage four: the note.** A hundred and fifty words or so on what you kept and why, where you placed the empty entry, and what a deletion rate that high says about how much of a person a record like this can carry. Your own account, not a survey of anyone else's.

One last thing: the reader of the pamphlet is a historian who reads poetry, not a poet who reads history. So the poem has to stand up as a poem first — if it only works as an illustration of a thesis, I've failed the session and so have you.
