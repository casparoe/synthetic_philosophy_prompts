**The question as set:**

> State the paradox of deontology. Then explain why the paradox is thought to threaten the *justification* of agent-centered restrictions, rather than merely restating the disagreement between deontologists and consequentialists. Finally, identify what you take to be the most promising response available to the deontologist, and say whether that response answers the paradox or dissolves it.

**The candidate's response:**

> The paradox gets its grip from the structure of agent-centered restrictions. A restriction of this kind tells me not to perform acts of a certain type — killing an innocent person, breaking a promise, torturing a prisoner — even in circumstances in which performing one such act would prevent a larger number of acts of the same type by other agents. The restriction therefore forbids me to minimize violations of the very norm it exists to protect. If the point of prohibiting killing is that killings are bad, a theory that forbids me to prevent five killings by committing one appears to frustrate its own point; and if that is not the point, the theory owes us some account of what the point is. The case that makes this vivid: a militia will murder three villagers unless I shoot one of them myself. The restriction says do not shoot. The value the restriction supposedly protects says shoot.
>
> Two things distinguish the paradox from other familiar complaints about deontology. It is not the demandingness objection, since the argument runs just as well on restrictions that cost the agent almost nothing. Nor is it merely the consequentialist's assertion that restrictions are irrational, for the paradox is meant to be internal: it recruits the deontologist's own apparent commitment to the badness of what the restriction forbids. That internality is what makes it a threat to justification rather than a restatement of the dispute. A theory cannot complain that its opponent has simply announced a different first principle; but here the opponent is using the theory's own principle to show that the theory forbids the promotion of it.
>
> There are three standard lines of response. The first keeps the maximizing structure and changes the object of value: what matters is not that killings not occur but that they not occur at my hands, so my shooting one villager is not a sixth violation to be weighed against the other five but something of a different kind. The second denies that practical reason is maximizing at all: rationality does not require the promotion of the good, and once that is conceded the question "why not minimize?" is seen to presuppose the contested premise. The third is threshold deontology, on which restrictions hold up to some point beyond which consequences take over, so that in the village case I may shoot.
>
> The first line looks to me like a relabeling. Either the agent-relative value in question is simply the restriction described in the vocabulary of value, in which case it explains nothing, or it is a genuine value, in which case it is a value I have reason to promote and the paradox returns one level up. The third line is a retreat. A threshold theorist must say where the threshold sits and why it sits there, and every candidate answer — a number of lives, a magnitude of harm — looks stipulated; and since the paradox can be run at any point below the threshold, the threshold does not so much resolve the difficulty as relocate it. The second line is, I think, the right direction, but it is usually stated too bluntly, as though denying maximizing were itself an argument. It is only a permission to look elsewhere for an account of what restrictions are for.
>
> So the paradox is in the end a problem only for deontologists who try to justify restrictions by appeal to their consequences. The deontologist who treats restrictions as foundational, and who declines to say that they exist in order that violations be minimized, is untouched by it, and I suspect this is why the difficulty has been overstated in the literature: the argument is run against a hybrid position and then charged to the whole family.
>
> What is needed is an account of what restrictions are for that does not make them instruments. I want to call it the custodianship account. On this account, a restriction does not hand the agent a share of the world's causal output to manage; it assigns her jurisdiction over one determinate thing, her own agency. What happens inside another agent's jurisdiction is not morally diminished — it is addressed differently, by responses that do not require me to convert myself into an instrument for its management. The paradox then commits a category mistake: it treats the value protected by a restriction as a quantity that is available for aggregation across jurisdictions, when the whole point of the jurisdictional structure is that it is not so available. On this picture the militia's three murders are a horror that I have every reason to oppose, but they are not a deficit in my accounts, and my shooting a villager would not be a way of settling it. What dissolves the paradox is not the denial that killings are bad but the denial that badness of this kind sums.
>
> This is, I take it, the same predicament that the literature on dirty hands describes: an agent who must violate a restriction to prevent catastrophe will have done something wrong even where she did what she ought, and her wrongdoing is not erased by its necessity. Once that is granted, the paradox stops looking like an inconsistency inside deontology and starts looking like a permanent feature of moral life, which is where a theory should expect to find it. The custodianship account explains why the threshold theorist's stipulation feels arbitrary: the threshold is being asked to do a job — mark the point at which aggregation begins — that the jurisdictional picture says was never available to be done.
