I am the standards editor at a non-partisan fact-checking organisation. For a decade we rated only claims about the present and the past. Eighteen months ago we opened a second desk that rates forward-looking claims — what officials promise, what agencies project, what economists forecast — and it has outrun our rulebook.

Our board fixed four verdicts years ago and will not add a fifth before the next funding cycle, roughly fourteen months out: Accurate, Broadly Accurate, Inaccurate, Unverifiable. The last was designed for present-tense claims we cannot check — a private conversation, a sealed record. Our writers now apply it to anything about the future that has not come out yet, and readers object with some justice: the label says we could not check, when what is really going on is that there is nothing to check yet, or perhaps nothing there to be accurate or inaccurate about at all. I can create internal sub-statuses that do not print as verdicts, control the wording of the analysis paragraph, and make a recheck date mandatory on every item.

The desk publishes about a dozen forward-looking items a week. They feed a public commitment tracker that two state legislatures' oversight staff and a partner newsroom rely on; twice a year a subject's counsel writes to us about a rating. The writers are journalism-trained, have no philosophy, and get roughly twenty minutes per item at intake.

Deliverable: a procedure a writer can actually run — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which it terminates in a verdict, the points at which it instead sets a recheck date and publishes something short, and the points at which it stops and hands the item to me because the question is one of editorial policy rather than fact. Keep that procedure to something a new writer could be handed on their first day: two pages at most. It should also settle, or explain why it cannot: how to treat claims that are explicitly probabilistic; whether a promise and a forecast are the same sort of thing to rate, since our writers currently treat them identically; what we do when a prediction's window closes and the event occurred partly, late, or in a form the speaker never described; and whether a published rating can ever be revised, by whom, and at what cost.

Then run it on three live items.

1. A senator said a tax package would pay for itself within three years. Fourteen months in, with revenue tracking below every projection then available, we rated it Inaccurate. The window is still open. If the procedure says that rating was premature, or simply wrong, or must now be reopened, say so and tell me what reopening will cost us.
2. A mayor said that if a referendum passed, property taxes would fall within a year. The referendum failed. The item has sat in Unverifiable for eight months.
3. An economist said the metro area would be in recession by the end of next year. Two standard operationalisations of "recession" disagree about the region's current state, and the one our partner newsroom uses is not the one I would have chosen.

I want the procedure to state plainly what it assumes about a claim whose window is still open: whether such a claim is already either accurate or inaccurate and we merely cannot tell which, or whether there is as yet no fact for it to match. Half my writers think one thing, half the other, and I have been telling both camps that it makes no difference to the work. Tell me whether I am right. If it does make a difference, the procedure has to handle it rather than smuggle in one camp's assumption as though it were house style.

Mark every step that is a judgment call dressed up as procedure, and say what the procedure cannot settle. I am not asking for references or a survey of the literature; nothing here should depend on my writers reading anything. Where you are extrapolating from a dispute with no settled answer, say so and say what the alternatives would cost us; where a step is forced by what the claim itself says rather than chosen by us, distinguish that clearly from convention. And if you think the premise of my request is confused — if rating forward-looking claims is a category error that no workflow can repair, and the honest recommendation is that we close the desk — say that directly and make the case, rather than writing me a tidy procedure around it.
