I run a county heritage grants program — around 150 applications a year, mostly small festivals and processions, decided by an elected commitee that hates being told its town's ancient custom is 60 years old. I get 25 minutes a file and nobody on staff who's read much history, so it has to be something they can actually run without me.

Build me a decision procedure out of Hobsbawm on invented traditions and Anderson on imagined communities: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the call to a human because it's a values question rather than a factual one. Tell me which distinctions have to survive the dumbing down — invention versus custom versus plain routine, the claim of continuity versus the fact of it, the point that invented doesn't mean worthless — and which ones I can drop. Also flag where the procedure misfires, especially when the only evidence about a tradition's origin is evidence the tradition itself produced.

Then run it on three files: a 'medieval' harvest rite first documented in 1948, apparently started by a schoolteacher; a genuinely 17th-century procession that lapsed for sixty years and was revived in 1990 off a printed account, whose current custodians won't admit women or newcomers; and a street carnival invented thirty years ago by immigrant families that makes no antiquity claim at all. Make sure one of the three comes out in a way the committee will hate.
