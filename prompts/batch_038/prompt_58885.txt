We spent the last two weeks of my epistemology seminar on Wittgenstein's *On Certainty* and the hinge-proposition literature, and every time someone presses the external-world sceptic's argument, our instructor cuts it off with the remark that the sceptic's doubt is not real doubt — doubt, we are told, only makes sense against a background of what is not doubted. I would like to examine that one-liner rather than take it on trust. Please lay out, as an itemized list I can bring to office hours, the distinct theses it could be expressing (for instance, claims about the grammar of "doubt"; about whether a doubt must have grounds, or must be capable of being settled; about whether doubting is an inner state or something shown in inquiry and action; and about what is psychologically possible for a thinker), and for each say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or simply false. Then tell me which readings, if any, would actually entitle someone to refuse to engage with the sceptic's argument instead of merely redescribing her position — and whether any of them is something the sceptic could cheerfully accept while keeping her argument intact.
