Construct a thought experiment engineered to force three theories of emotion into a three-way disagreement about *which* emotion a single subject is feeling — not about whether she is feeling one. The theories: (i) a judgment view, on which an emotion is an evaluative judgment or set of beliefs about one's situation; (ii) a perceptual view, on which an emotion is a perception-like presentation of value, non-doxastic and so able to persist against the subject's better judgment; (iii) a Jamesian feeling view, on which an emotion is the subject's awareness of her own bodily changes.

Specifications. One paragraph, ordinary life — a workplace, a family, a courthouse corridor — with no phobias, diagnoses, drugs, or unusual physiology. All three components must be genuinely present and discordant, such that the judgment view reads the episode as guilt, the perceptual view as shame, and the feeling view as fear. The narration must never name an emotion and never report the subject's own introspective characterizations in emotion-words; the verdicts must be generated by the theories from the raw materials you supply — what she judges, what appears to her, what her body is doing. Describe the bodily profile specifically enough that a feeling theorist can identify it, rather than leaving it as undifferentiated arousal.

Then: give each theory's verdict with the reasoning that yields it; say which verdict you find most defensible and why; identify the single feature of the case doing the separating work, and show what happens to the disagreement if you remove it, stating the revised scenario and the resulting verdicts; and explain how a defender of each theory that comes out badly would attack the construction, including whether she can absorb the discordance as a mixed emotion without abandoning her view. The case is meant to demonstrate that these theories are separable in ordinary, non-pathological settings, so stipulating a verdict or importing an exotic condition counts as a failure.
