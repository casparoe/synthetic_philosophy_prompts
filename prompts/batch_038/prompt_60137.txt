Seminar on film and epistemology, and next week is Antonioni's Blow-Up (1966 — the London fashion photographer, the pictures taken in the park, Jane demanding the negatives, the enlargements, the mime tennis at the end). Instead of the usual photography-and-skepticism reading I want to pair it with Otto Neurath on picture languages, which I know mostly secondhand, so correct my gloss if it's off: Neurath's Isotype held that pictures could state social facts across language barriers, but only under rules — you count a quantity by repeating an identical symbol rather than by making the symbol bigger, you keep icons flat and stripped of detail, no perspective — because a picture left to itself doesn't fix whether it means one thing or a class, a claim or an illustration. Words come with that discipline built in; pictures need it bolted on.

Write the reading. The obvious move is that the enlargement sequence is Isotype backwards: Thomas keeps adding resolution and the candidate corpse dissolves into grain, so more pictorial detail yeilds less determinate content, which is precisely what Neurath's reduction rules were there to prevent. Does the film really make that point, or is it just showing what happens when someone interprets raw photographs with no conventions at all — a case Neurath never claimed to cover? And please use a distinction I've invented for this unit, taking it as stipulated rather than arguing for it: a picture is self-policing when the conventions that fix its content are visible on its surface (axes, a legend, a repeated unit), and free-floating when they live in the viewer's head. Sort the film's images into those two buckets — the park shots, the contact sheets, the fashion shoots, the blown-up grain, the mime tennis — and say which ones the film lets produce knowledge and which ones it treats as fantasy.

Be faithful in both directions: don't reduce Antonioni to a Neurath case study, and flag where the pairing strains, the ending especially. Lead with two or three sentences giving your verdict — does the film undermine the picture-language project, or only punish someone who never used it — and then give the rest. Hard cap 750 words.
