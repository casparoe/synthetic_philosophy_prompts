I am a graduate student in the last seminar Professor Halloran will teach — a close reading of Braudel's Mediterranean — and the department has asked me to give the toast at her retirement dinner in April. Roughly eighty people will be in the room: early modernists who know the book line by line, three decades of her former students, and a table of family who have never read a page of history and know her mainly as someone who was always working. Four to five minutes, delivered standing, without notes.

Her career was spent arguing, in Braudel's wake, that the event is the least explanatory level of historical reality — that what happens to particular people in a particular year is surface disturbance, and that anything deserving the name of explanation sits in the slow structures: the sea, the routes, the price cycles. She also insisted, against readers who take Braudel to be a causal pluralist, that the three time-scales are not three kinds of cause but three resolutions of a single instrument: you get the geographical level by lowering your temporal resolution until centuries look simultaneous, and you get the event by zooming all the way in. On her own account, a retirement dinner is an event in the strict sense — one evening, one person, no explanatory weight whatsoever.

I want the toast to take that seriously rather than flatter her by quietly abandoning it. So it cannot simply celebrate a great individual who transformed her field, which she would hear as bad history wearing a sentimental costume; and it cannot escape by announcing in the last thirty seconds that events turn out to matter after all, which she would hear as a concession bought cheap. Let the tension be the structure instead: the affection has to reach her at whatever level she thinks is real. Make the early modernists laugh twice at jokes that are genuinely about the argument, and make it work as a speech for the family at the table who have never heard the term. Please write the whole thing.
