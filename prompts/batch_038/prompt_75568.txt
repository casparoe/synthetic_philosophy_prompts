I'm TAing a required medical ethics block for second-year nursing students — 24 of them, one 90-minute session, in a room with desks bolted to the floor and no working wifi, so nothing digital and no rearranging furniture into circles. What I want them to walk out having *felt* is the doing/allowing distinction: that people reliably judge an agent who does a thing as much worse off than an agent who lets the very same thing happen, even when you've matched the outcome, the agent's intentions, the cost to the agent, and the victim's prospects either way. The usual intro move is a pair of matched cases read aloud and then a poll, and honestly that's never worked for me — half the room just reports whatever the person next to them reported. So I want an actual activity where they're making choices under some mild pressure, and where the pattern only becomes visible once I tally what the whole room did.

Here's the thing I'm specifically interested in, and it's more in the weeds than the intro version. The cases that break people's intuitions in this area are all withdrawal cases — ones where the agent's body moves, but what the movement does is stop something that was already going on, or hand something back that the agent had been supplying, rather than start anything new. Turning something off versus never turning it on; letting go of a grip versus never grabbing hold; unplugging yourself from an arrangement you'd already agreed to for an hour; declining to restart a process that stopped on its own and that you'd been keeping alive manually. I've got a suspicion — call it a hypothesis I want the students to test on themselves without being told — that what's really doing the work in these judgments isn't the act/omission line at all but three things that get tangled up in every textbook example: (a) whether the lethal sequence was already underway before the agent turned up, (b) whether the thing being removed was the agent's own ongoing provision or somebody else's, and (c) whether removing it returns the situation to a state that was there before the agent got involved. I want the activity structured so those three factors can be varied one at a time while everything else is worded identically, and so the students end up looking at their own verdicts flipping across rounds and having to explain why. If the room's pattern actually supports my suspicion, great; if it contradicts it, that's even better and I want the debrief to handle that outcome rather than paper over it.

So: give me the full package. Rules and roles for groups of four, materials that are just paper, index cards and whiteboard, and some payoff that isn't real money but that they'll actually care about losing. A minute-by-minute timeline that fits 90 and leaves ten minutes of slack. The rounds themselves written out in the exact plain language I'd put on the cards — keep them concrete and non-clinical so nobody feels ambushed, because in a cohort of 24 nurses there is almost certainly someone with a relative who was ventilated and I don't want the first time they meet this material to be a public vote about it. The reveal should come late: they shouldn't hear the words doing and allowing, or any of the jargon for the withdrawal cases, until after they've seen the tally. Then a debrief that maps specific moments of the activity onto specific pieces of the distinction, tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover, and is straight with me about which feature of this distinction a classroom game just cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by talking — my guess is the stakes, but tell me if you think it's something else.
