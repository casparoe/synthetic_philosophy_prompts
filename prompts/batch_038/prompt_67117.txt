I handle desk decisions for the articles desk at a mid-tier history journal, and anything touching the French Revolution goes to the same outside reader — a retired professor who's been doing this for something like thirty years. I've now got two years of her reports saved up and she's a terror in both senses. Here's the record:

- A book arguing the Terror's escalation in '93–'94 is explained by the war emergency, the assignat collapse and the subsistence crisis bearing down on the machinery of revolutionary government: reject. Her phrase was that it describes a machine with no operators — by the last chapter you know exactly what the situation required and nothing at all about who did anything.
- A book arguing that Jacobin virtue-talk was strategic cover for the property interests of a new owning class, and that the language of disinterest did useful work for people who needed it: reject. She complimented the archival digging and the prose, then said a book that treats revolutionary language as an alibi has picked its verdict before it opened a single register.
- A book on the summer of 1791 — Varennes, the Champ de Mars, the split in the patriot camp — arguing the constitutional monarchy survived by inches and that radicalization was one bad fortnight from never happening: strong accept, best thing she'd read in ten years.
- A microhistory of one department's revolutionary tribunal showing the local severity curve tracked family feuds, denunciations and committee seating far more then any instruction from Paris: reject. Her reason: it proves the provinces were a shambles but never says who authorized the shambles, and nobody in the book ever decides anything about the Terror as such.
- A study of Convention speeches tracing how the vocabulary of conspiracy and unity narrowed what deputies could see as an option, so each purge made the next one thinkable (her report kept returning to the spring of '94, when the assembly ate its own left and its own right within a fortnight): accept, with one chapter cut.

Next month I have to send her a manuscript and I want to know what I'm walking into. It's a quantitative study of the representatives-on-mission — arrest orders, correspondence, the timing of thier missions. The headline finding is that the number of death sentences in a department is predicted almost entirely by whether a given representative was present and how much latitude he'd been handed, and that these men's decisions were erratic and often reversed within weeks. The part I can't read her on is the conclusion the author draws: that the Terror had no coherent center at all, and that the ideological categories the Convention supplied did no explanatory work, because the same categories produced wildly different outcomes depending on which man happened to be in the room.

So — will she send it forward for review, and with what kind of report? Which rule is she actually running: politics as its own causal register that can't be reduced to economics or to local sociology; a flat anti-necessitarianism that rejects anything making the Terror look automatic; a demand that the actors' own reasons stay load-bearing; an insistence that the national political center be doing explanatory work rather than just existing; or something I haven't thought of. Tell me what each candidate rule predicts about this particular manuscript, give me your single best guess at her verdict plus the strongest reason to prefer it over the runner-up, and then name the sort of manuscript I could send her afterward that would cleanly separate the two readings you find most plausible. And flag which of her five verdicts, if any, your favourite rule has trouble with — I'd much rather hear it now than in her report.

Please keep the settled stuff separate from the contested stuff. Where you're leaning on things historians of the Revolution broadly agree about, say so; where you're taking a side in a live fight — social interpretation versus the political-culture turn, whether the Terror is the kind of thing that can be explained at all, how much of the devouring-your-children business was structure and how much was choice — don't hand it to me as though the field had closed the question. Same goes for your reconstruction of her rules: mark which parts are actually forced by the five reports and which parts are you filling in gaps.

Sections, in this order, please: "The pattern" (~200 words); "Candidate rules" (~350 words, one paragraph per rule, each ending in what that rule predicts for the representatives-on-mission manuscript); "My call" (~150 words); "The discriminating manuscript" (~150 words); "Where this is a guess" (~120 words).
