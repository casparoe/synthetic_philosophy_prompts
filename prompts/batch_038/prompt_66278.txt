I teach an annual graduate seminar on the ethics of long-run policy, and I open it with a piece of fiction rather than an article, because the distinction I most need my students to feel is one they arrive having already collapsed. The distinction is this. When a society settles on the rate at which it discounts the welfare of future people, three quite different things are being bundled together: a bare impatience toward later welfare as such; the fact that resources set aside now will grow before they are spent; and the expectation that future people will be richer than we are, so that a given sum does less good for them than it does for us. Only the first of these is a claim about how much we care. The other two are claims about productivity and about the distribution of income across time, and they would persist unchanged in a society that cared about its descendants exactly as much as it cares about itself. My students hear any discounting whatever as selfishness. I would like a story that makes that error impossible to hold.

Please write an original short story of roughly 2,500 to 3,500 words, in American English, that carries this material in its plot and characters rather than in its exposition. I have set out my requirements below, and I would rather you follow them strictly than please me with a looser and prettier piece.

1. Setting. Not a committee room, not a hearing, not a laboratory, not a courtroom, and not a world after the collapse. Choose an ordinary institutional setting in which people make binding decisions about money that will be spent after they are dead — a water district, a fishing cooperative, a family timber holding, a small college's endowment office, a municipal bridge authority, a cemetery fund, a shipping concern — a place where nobody would describe what they are doing as philosophy. The setting should constrain the shape of the story rather than decorate it.

2. Characters. Include one character who understands the arithmetic exactly and is wrong about what the arithmetic entitles them to, and another who understands the arithmetic badly and is right about what it entitles them to. Neither may be a villain and neither may be a saint. The reader should not be able to say which is which until after the final paragraph.

3. Vocabulary. No character may say "discount rate," "time preference," "utility," "utilitarian," "sustainability," "the future of humanity," or "externalities." No formula may appear on the page. The conceptual structure should be recoverable by an attentive reader and invisible to an inattentive one.

4. Turn. Somewhere past the midpoint, a revision in what looks like a purely technical assumption — most obviously an assumption about how much richer or poorer the eventual beneficiaries will turn out to be — must reverse who is being generous to whom. The reversal should register as an ethical event for the characters and as an arithmetic event for the reader, or the other way around.

5. Optional, but welcome. If you can find a way to embody, without explaining it, the thought that uncertainty about how fast people will get richer makes the distant future weigh more rather than less, do so, but only where it earns its place and does not read as a fact inserted for my benefit. Likewise the older intuition, most often associated with Ramsey, that discounting a person merely for being later is a failure of imagination rather than a principle: I would far rather see it tested by a character who genuinely lives by it and pays for doing so than stated by anyone.

6. Ending. No reconciliation, no speech in which someone articulates the lesson, no framing narrator reflecting afterward, no epigraph, and no title that names a concept. The story should end with the question it raised still open and the reader slightly the worse for having understood it.

After the story, add a note of no more than 300 words addressed to me as its editor. Say where the conceptual content is being carried structurally; identify the one or two places where you had to distort the material in order to make it narratable; and name something about the ethics of valuing later people less that your story could not express and had to leave out. Write that note without citations, a reading list, or named sources — I am interested in your judgment, not in your references.
