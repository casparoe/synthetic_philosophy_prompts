I help run a tuesday evening philosophy night at our local library — twelve-ish regulars, mostly retired, plus one guy who works in logistics and always wants things spelled out in numbers. This month we're reading a trade book about happiness and the good life, and chapter four has one of those boxed sidebars where the author pauses to define terms. The box is on hedonism and I typed it out because I read it three times and kept getting the feeling that something in the middle gets swapped without me noticing:

"Hedonists hold that pleasure is the one thing that makes a life good for the person living it. The trouble is that pleasures look nothing like each other: a cold drink on a hot day, the satisfaction of finally getting a proof to work, sun on your shoulders, being complimented in front of people you respect. What could these possibly have in common? Bentham's answer was that they differ only in amount, never in kind — pleasure is one measurable feeling, and all the variety we notice is just variety in what produces it. Mill rejected this with his higher and lower pleasures: someone who has tried both prefers the higher even when it comes with more discontent, and that preference shows the higher pleasure is worth more. Sidgwick tried to end the quarrel by defining pleasure as any feeling that, when we attend to it, we find desirable — a purely formal definition that dodges the question of what pleasure feels like. Most contemporary hedonists have given up on there being a common feel at all, and instead say that you take pleasure in an experience just in case you want it to keep going. Either way the critic has a point: define pleasure by desire and you've stopped saying anything about pleasure, and the theory quietly turns into the claim that we benefit from getting what we want. So hedonism is caught on one of two horns — a shared feeling that isn't there, or a redefinition that isn't about pleasure anymore."

My hunch, for what it's worth, is that the box slides between two different questions — what pleasure is as a kind of experience, and what makes a life good — and that the closing dilemma only looks forced because it treats the felt-quality view and the want-it-to-continue view as rivals for the same job when they maybe aren't. But I could easily be wrong about that, and I don't want to walk into Tuesday and confidently mangle it in front of the logistics guy. So: go through the box roughly sentence by sentence and tell me which bits a hedonist would happily sign, which are legitimate simplifications for a general audience, and which are actual errors or would make a value theorist wince. I especially want to know what's fair and what's subtly unfair in the three-name history — is that Mill paragraph right about what Mill was actually doing with quality, and is it fair to call Sidgwick's definition a dodge rather than an answer? And then, seperately: once the errors get fixed, does the two-horns conclusion still stand up, or does it evaporate? If part of it survives, say which part and why. No reading list needed, I can't get hold of the papers anyway — I just want reasoning I can actually use out loud.
