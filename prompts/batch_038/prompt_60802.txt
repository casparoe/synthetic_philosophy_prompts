Consider the following claim, which I will label (V): *the world is substantially less violent now than it was in 1800, and this is a matter of fact rather than an artefact of how "violence" is operationalised.* Read "substantially" as at least a halving, so that (V) is determinate enough to be true or false.

I want your credence in (V) as a single number, together with the derivation that produces it. Please decompose it into at least the following components, each with a figure and one or two sentences of justification: the probability that the narrow physical measures — homicide, deaths in war, deaths attributable to state coercion — have genuinely fallen by at least half, given how thin and locally biased the pre-1800 records are; the probability that per-capita normalisation is the right one rather than absolute counts, given that the world's population has grown so much that the two can diverge; the probability that the categories added to the concept over the last two centuries — domestic coercion, psychological harm, industrial animal suffering, structural harm — have improved broadly in step with the older ones rather than offsetting them; and the probability that some decline holds across every defensible operationalisation, so that the second half of (V) is true at all. Then show how you combine these into your final number, state which component the total is most sensitive to, and say what evidence or argument would move it furthest.

One consideration I would like you to assess rather than take for granted: any operationalisation of "violence" that stays fixed in meaning across two centuries is plausibly one that people in the earlier period would themselves have applied, and those are precisely the narrow physical ones — so determinacy might be secured by continuity of usage rather than by anything in the world. My own credence in (V) is 0.55, largely because I do not know how much weight to give that thought. Please write your answer in British English.
