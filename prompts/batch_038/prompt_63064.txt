My brother-in-law is an economist and over the holidays he told me that anyone who thinks 'better than' might not be transative is just confusing themselves, and that the whole spectrum-argument business evaporates the second you sit down and write an actual utility function. I don't think he's right, but I also couldn't say much more than hand-wave at it, so I want to try to get clear on what I actually believe by writing out my gut verdicts on a bunch of concrete cases and seeing whether any single principle fits them. Context that maybe matters: I do data work part-time for a palliative care network, and there's a standing committee that has to rank symptom-burden scenarios to prioritize protocols, so a lot of these are semi-real rather than invented. Pain is on the usual 0–10 scale. I'm not after names or references or a tour of the literature — I want the principle itself, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen.

Here's what I say when I go through them one at a time.

1. A1: one patient at 10/10 for a day. A2: two patients at 9/10 for a day each. A3: four at 8/10. Keep doubling the headcount and dropping one point of intensity, out to A10: 512 patients at 1/10 — a dull ache — for a day. Each of these is worse than the one before it, and worse in the full-blooded sense, not "equally bad." Doubling how many people are hurting in exchange for one point of intensity is a bad trade every time.
2. But A10 is better than A1. Five hundred-odd people with a dull ache for a day beats one person at 10/10 for a day, and I'm not remotely ambivalent about that.
3. Adding one more sufferer to an outcome always makes it worse, however slightly. There's no rung on the ladder at which an extra person's ache stops counting.
4. Sixty minutes at 10/10 versus thirty minutes at 10/10: the thirty is better, obviously. Sixty at 4/10 versus thirty at 8/10: the sixty-at-4 is better. Sixty at 8/10 versus thirty at 9/10: now I flip and say the thirty-at-9 is better. So somewhere between 4 and 8 the trade between duration and intensity reverses, and I don't know where, and I don't want to be told it's vague — the committee has to actually pick.
5. I refuse the escape hatch about imperceptible differences. A patient at 5.00 for an hour is really worse off than one at 4.99 for an hour, and in exactly the same sense in which 5.0 is worse than 4.0; I don't think "nobody could tell them apart" has anything to do with how bad a state is. But — and this is the part I can't reconcile with the previous sentence — if you ask me to compare 5.00-for-an-hour against 4.99-for-an-hour head to head, I don't have a verdict. Not "equally good," not "the first is worse." Just nothing there.
6. Then this trio, which might be the worst one. X: two patients at 6/10 for an hour. Y: two patients at 6.5/10 for an hour. Z: three patients at 5/10 for an hour. X is better than Y — same two people, half a point less pain. Y is better than Z — I'd rather have two people at 6.5 than three at 5. But X and Z strike me as exactly equally good, I'd genuinely flip a coin, and I don't think that's just me failing to notice a difference. If betterness is transitive, X-better-than-Y-better-than-Z should hand me X better than Z, and it just doesn't feel that way.
7. Same shape outside pain, which makes me think this isn't about pain being weird. One hearing of the best piece of music ever written is worth more than a lifetime of elevator music, I think. But for any two pieces, if one is better it's really better, and a million forgettable-but-pleasant songs are worth more than 999,999 of them. So the chain from the masterpiece down to the million jingles has to break somewhere and I can't point to where.

So: is there an account of what makes one outcome better than another that gets all of this, or most of it? For each clause of whatever you land on, tell me which verdict above is doing the work motivating it. Then run it on two cases I haven't listed — (a) the same spectrum with probabilities instead of intensities, i.e. an ever-smaller chance of a catastrophe traded against a certain small cost spread over everyone, which is roughly what our safety-budget fights actually look like: does your account predict my verdicts there go intransitive too, or does it rule that out? and (b) whether it comes out differently when the people in the two outcomes are different people versus the same person at different times. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts it has to overrule: if the honest answer is that a simpler principle forces me to give up 2, or 5, or 6, say which one and why that's the cheapest sacrifice rather than the one that just annoys me least. Last thing — my brother-in-law's fallback is that I'm not talking about betterness at all but about what I'd choose under uncertainty, and that once you seperate those two the puzzle dissolves. Is that a real solution or is it relabeling?
