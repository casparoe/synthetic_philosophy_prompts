I am a senior traffic engineer in a mid-sized American city's transportation department, and for three months I have been drafting our written response to a public comment filed by a coalition of disability and elder-services organizations about the signal-timing plan for a seven-lane arterial. Their comment concerns the pedestrian clearance interval. Our design manual computes that interval from an assumed walking speed of four feet per second, a figure inherited from the national traffic-control standards; the coalition asks us to retime those crossings at three feet per second or slower, so that their members can actually finish the crossing. I have run the numbers, and the result is awkward: at any walking speed slow enough to accommodate them, the pedestrian phase required exceeds what the corridor's vehicle volumes permit within the maximum cycle length our state's congestion standard allows. So on the design space as we have defined it, every feasible option excludes somebody. And nobody in my building chose any of the relevant inputs. The four-feet-per-second assumption came down from a standards body; the crossing distance was fixed by a road-widening decision made in the 1960s; the cycle-length ceiling arrives from the state as a given. The only thing we chose was to reproduce all three of them in this plan.

Two of my colleagues disagree about what our response may honestly say, and I cannot adjudicate between them. One is a standards engineer with thirty years in the field. She says the politics is in the artifact — that the number in the design manual is what does the excluding, that a signal controller is a standing decision re-made every ninety seconds whether we notice it or not, and that a response pointing only to the funding envelope and the state mandate is a way of mislocating the decision so that no one has to own it. She is fond of Winner's account of the deliberately low Long Island overpasses, though I have told her our case is nothing like it, since Moses intended his effect and we intend ours for no one. The other is a sociologist of technology we retain as a consultant. He says she has it backwards — that artifacts have no politics of their own and are only durable records of choices made by identifiable groups with interests, and that describing the clearance interval as itself political is precisely the rhetorical move that turns politics into physics and forecloses the argument about who decided and who could decide otherwise. He adds that my "every feasible option excludes somebody" result is not a discovery about the corridor but an artifact of how we framed the problem as signal retiming in the first place.

Please write a dialogue between these two, staged at my desk over the draft response, in which each tries to convince the other of what the single sentence describing the clearance interval may truthfully say.

Requirements:

1. Exactly five exchanges, where an exchange is one turn by each speaker, for ten turns total.
2. No introduction, no conclusion, no scene-setting, no narration, and no stage directions: only the two speaker labels and their words.
3. Write in American English.
4. Neither speaker may name a theorist, cite literature, or appeal to any case other than this corridor. The argument must be carried entirely by the specifics I have given you.
5. Neither may simply assert their general view: each must say what their position implies about a case in which no design in the feasible set could have done otherwise, and each must address the other's answer to that problem rather than their own preferred version of it.
6. Both must be at full strength, and each must concede at least one substantive point and then press the concession for something it costs the other side.
7. The two positions must not read the same in the fifth exchange as in the first — by the end each should have been forced into a more precise formulation.
8. End on the sharpest statement of the unresolved crux, not on a resolution, a compromise, or a summary of where they landed.
