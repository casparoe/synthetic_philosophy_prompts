I am the executive director of a small grantmaking foundation — call it the Ninth Generation Fund — which formally closes at the end of this quarter, and on Saturday I give the closing remarks at our last donors' dinner. For eleven years the fund's entire case for existing rested on a single estimate in the founding memo: that the problems we worked on concerned on the order of ten to the sixteenth people who have not yet been born. We made grants on that basis. The largest went to a four-person lab developing a cheap assay for engineered respiratory pathogens in airport wastewater; the memo justifying it ran to the astronomical figure on page one and to a budget of $2.4 million on page nine. That assay now works, it is in service in three countries, and it is the only thing we ever funded that a stranger can independently verify. Two years ago the board commissioned an outside review of our methodology, chaired by a decision theorist who has sat with us for seven years, which concluded that our arithmetic was not merely optimistic but structurally broken: a case whose whole force comes from multiplying an enormous number by a probability nobody can estimate is not a case at all, because anyone who wants more money need only name a larger number. The board accepted the review, the remainder is committed, and I have been asked to explain all of this to about forty people — donors and their partners, four staff, three board members including the reviewer, one regional journalist who covers philanthropy, and our founder, who is eighty-four, who wrote the memo, who will be seated at the head table, and who has said nothing in public since the review came out. Almost nobody in the room is a philosopher, and the tone cannot be a funeral.

I spent three hours last night arguing about this with a friend who prices catastrophe risk for a reinsurer, and I can no longer see which sentences are safe to say aloud. Her first point is that the review is Pascal's mugging redeployed as governance, and that Pascal's mugging proves far too much to be a foundation's epitaph: an insurer routinely sells protection against events with probabilities of one in ten thousand and consequences nobody can bound, and no one calls a hurricane policy a mugging, so the defect in our work cannot have been that our numbers were large. Her second point is that the real fault was that our numbers were never checkable against anything — no frequency data, no feedback loop, no mechanism by which we could be wrong in public — and that the assay is the one thing we funded which eventually became checkable, which is why it is the one thing that worked. Her third point is the one I could not answer. On either diagnosis I am not entitled to the story I most want to tell: if the reviewer is right, the assay was a lucky accident of a broken method, and if she is right, the assay only became a defensible bet once it became verifiable, which was fortune rather than design. So "we aimed at ten to the sixteenth and hit something real" is not an achievement on anybody's account, and a speech built on it is survivorship bias wearing the clothes of gratitude.

Write the speech — about six minutes, deliverable aloud by someone who is not an orator. It must (1) thank forty people for eleven years of giving without telling them, directly or by implication, that they were taken in; (2) neither assert nor hint that the astronomical estimate was or might have been right; (3) refuse the modest fallback — that we were really an ordinary public-health funder all along — because it is untrue and the donors know it; (4) not present the assay as vindicating the method, or anything else; (5) contain no decision-theoretic vocabulary, and let the only large number in it be the founder's, appearing exactly once, with no other quantity stated beyond plain counts of years and of the people present; (6) contain exactly one sentence that states the reviewer's objection accurately enough that he would recognise it as his own, in words his neighbours at the table can follow, while ensuring that nothing else in the speech depends on that objection being correct, since my friend's diagnosis and his remain unresolved between us and I will not say anything at that podium I would have to retract if she turns out to be right; and (7) not solicit money, promise anything about what comes next, or close on a note of renewal, because nothing is being renewed. Bear in mind also that the journalist will quote a single sentence out of context, so no sentence may stand alone as an admission that the donors were duped or that the money was wasted, and the founder must be able to sit through the whole thing without being publicly contradicted. Before the speech, give me in no more than five lines the set of claims you judge to be true on both my friend's diagnosis and the reviewer's, since that is the only ground I am permitted to build on; if you think no such set can carry six minutes, say so plainly rather than quietly adopting one side. After the speech, tell me in two or three sentences which line was written for the reviewer, and where you suspect you may have cheated.
