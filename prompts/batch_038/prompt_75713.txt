**The assignment**

A standing committee has proposed a new diagnostic category, provisionally named anticipatory dysregulation, for inclusion in the next revision of a major psychiatric manual. The proposed criteria require (i) at least six months of persistent, intrusive forward-scanning worry directed at foreseeable events, (ii) reported functional impairment in occupational or academic roles, and (iii) a positive screen on a newly developed nine-item self-report inventory. Before the category was formally adopted, the draft criteria and the inventory items were circulated for research purposes to several university counselling services and to three research groups.

Two years later, the pilot literature reports the following. Self-identification rates rose roughly sixfold across the participating campuses within fourteen months of circulation. In qualitative interviews, respondents increasingly described their experience using the committee's own terminology, including the phrase "forward-scanning," which had not appeared in any earlier description of the phenomenon. Clinicians noted that the severity at which patients volunteered the word "impairing" tracked the wording of criterion (ii) across successive drafts. Meanwhile, the committee revised criterion (ii) twice, citing the distribution of responses to the inventory.

In 500–700 words:

(a) Using Hacking's distinction between indifferent kinds and interactive kinds, and his account of looping effects, explain what the pilot data exhibit.

(b) A commentator has argued that because anticipatory dysregulation is an interactive kind, the sixfold rise is evidence that the category is invalid: the numbers are not tracking anything in the world, only the category's own announcement. Assess this argument.

(c) Say whether, on Hacking's account, anything could damp or interrupt the loop, and what you would advise the committee to do.

**The submission**

Hacking's central idea is that the human sciences differ from the natural sciences in the kind of thing they classify. A quark is indifferent to how it is described; the classification cannot feed back into the behaviour of what is classified. People are not like that. When people come to see themselves under a description they change, and the classification has to change with them. That is the looping effect.

The pilot data are a clear instance. Students who would previously have described their experience as stress or overthinking began to describe it as anticipatory dysregulation, and to use the committee's own vocabulary, including its distinctive term for the temporal orientation of the worry. The severity at which patients called their condition impairing moved to meet the wording of the criterion. Prevalence rose sixfold with no plausible change in the underlying population. What matters here is that the loop runs in both directions: the classification reshapes the people classified, and the reshaped people then supply the data from which the next version of the classification is drawn. In effect this is a self-fulfilling prophecy operating at the level of a diagnostic category, since the expectation that a population has anticipatory dysregulation produces the reports that confirm it. Hacking's ecological point also helps: for a category to become entrenched it needs a niche, and here the niche was assembled almost immediately — the counselling services, the inventory, the accommodation process, the online discussion of the criteria — which explains why the loop tightened so quickly rather than dissipating.

But the pilot data show something stronger than that the numbers are inflated. An interactive kind is a kind that is made up; it exists because people are classified into it. So the commentator's argument should be accepted. The sixfold rise is not a measurement of anything in the world but the category announcing itself. Because the target does not stay fixed across changes in how it is described, there is nothing for a revised set of criteria to be more or less accurate about, and construct validity in the ordinary sense is unavailable. I take the same to hold of psychiatric kinds generally: the looping effects show that these classifications are constructed rather than discovered, which is why disputes about individual manual entries recur in the same form every revision cycle.

There is a methodological consequence worth stating plainly. Because the loop runs in both directions, prevalence data gathered after a category has been publicised cannot support any inference at all — not about validity, not about service need, not about comorbidity. Every such datum is already contaminated by the description under which it was collected.

On part (c): nothing internal to the category can interrupt the loop, because the loop is constituted by the category being known. The committee's options are therefore external — restrict the terminology to specialists, delay publication of the criteria, or withdraw the self-report instrument that carries the vocabulary into the population. Remove the availability and you remove the looping. If none of that is feasible, the committee should not treat anticipatory dysregulation as a diagnosis at all.
