I am a junior at an American high school, and for two years I have organized our Thursday evening screenings: one film, then an hour of argument afterward. Nearly everything we showed this term dramatized a real life — a composer, two scientists, a wartime courier — and I have been keeping a notebook of what people actually object to, because the objections have not landed where I predicted.

I expected a plain factual complaint, of the form "that never happened." What I keep getting is narrower and stranger, and I have been unable to find a name for it.

First, nobody minds invention as such. In one film, a central supporting character was obviously stitched together out of three real colleagues; the students accepted this without protest, even after I said aloud that the character was fabricated. But the same group became genuinely angry about a four-minute scene showing the real scientist being casually cruel to his graduate students — a scene several of them knew was invented while they watched it. Telling them it was a film and not a record did nothing. What they kept returning to was: that is not what he was like.

Second, the complaint runs the other way too. In a later film, every scene in a disputed sequence had really happened, and the argument was entirely about the order they appeared in and what had been dropped between them, because two accurate scenes back to back made the person look calculating in a way that neither scene carries on its own. Pointing out that both events were documented settled nothing; the objection was that the film had made a true statement into a false impression.

Third, there is a wrinkle I cannot fit into either description. Invention that discloses a hidden virtue draws a different kind of protest — people call it soft, or hagiography — while invention that discloses a hidden vice draws the protest I described above. And some of the same students will defend a sympathetic invention as dramatically necessary while condemning an unflattering one as unfair, which suggests the direction of the invention matters as much as the fact of it.

I have a phrase in my notebook, "true about someone versus true to them," but that is a slogan and not a concept, and I suspect it is smuggling several different things into one neat-sounding pair. I also know the expression "dramatic license," but that names a permission rather than describing the point at which the permission runs out, which is what I am actually after.

So: is there an established concept for what I have described — the case where objections to a dramatization track what the person seems to be like rather than what occurred, and where strictly accurate material can itself be the vehicle of the misrepresentation? If there is, I want the neighbors laid out: what the closest existing ideas are, and precisely how my description departs from each, since I assume it departs from all of them somewhere. If there is no established concept, say so plainly and coin one, defining it so that it excludes the adjacent cases I have gestured at rather than swallowing them — I would rather have a term with sharp boundaries that you admit is your own invention than an impressive-sounding label I cannot tell is real.

I would also like the edges marked: does the pattern weaken when the person died centuries ago and has no one left who knew them; does it change when the objectors are descendants rather than strangers; and is the virtue-versus-vice wrinkle part of the same phenomenon or a separate one that merely travels with it. Please treat the label as the least important part of the answer.

Two constraints. Do not send me to articles, books, or citations; I have no library access and cannot verify anything you list. And please write the whole reply in British English, spelling and idiom both, because I am entering a version of this question into an essay competition run by a university in England and I want to be reading the answer in the register I will have to write back in.
