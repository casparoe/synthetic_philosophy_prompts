I am a third-year undergraduate in a seminar that combines formal social choice theory with democratic theory, and I would like your help reconstructing the political philosophy buried inside an ordinary administrative procedure. Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher with genuine technical command of social choice — someone comfortable with the definitions of a universal domain, surjectivity, dictatorship, and strategy-proofness, and with what the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem does and does not establish. Address me as an advanced undergraduate: I have met the theorem and can follow a formal argument, but I have not read the surrounding literature. Think carefully rather than answering quickly, and do not supply a bibliography, a list of relevant papers, or any citations — I want your own reconstruction, not a survey.

Here is the practice I want examined. In a large number of U.S. states, and in a number of other democracies, an exact tie in an election is settled by lot: a coin is flipped, straws are drawn, a name is pulled from an envelope, cards are cut. The procedure is written into law, carried out by an election official in public, and recorded in the official result. The loser concedes; the outcome is treated as fully legitimate, exactly as legitimate as a one-vote margin, and nobody proposes recounting the coin. Meanwhile the same polity uses, for everything else, rules that it knows perfectly well are manipulable — plurality, runoff, ranked-choice — and it spends an enormous amount of reform energy worrying about spoilers, wasted votes, and sincere expression, while never worrying that its rules invite strategic misrepresentation.

My questions, in order:

First, what theory of political legitimacy and of fairness between persons does settling a tie by chance presuppose? Before committing to one reading, sketch three or four candidate theories the practice could be resting on — for instance, one on which the tie means the electorate has genuinely said nothing and the choice is therefore not a political choice at all; one on which lotteries treat persons as exact equals in a way counting cannot; one on which the practice is merely about administrative finality and has no theory behind it. Then say which of these the practice strictly requires, as opposed to merely fitting comfortably, and tell me where I have over-read it.

Second, I have a hypothesis I want you to test rather than endorse. A lottery is a rule that ignores every reported preference, and a rule that ignores every reported preference is trivially strategy-proof: there is no ballot anyone could change to get a better outcome. If that is right, then the one place where our electoral system permits an explicitly preference-ignoring decision procedure is precisely the case in which preference aggregation has run out, and the impossibility theorem is doing quiet work at the margin of the practice that reform discourse never acknowledges. Is this a real structural parallel or a superficial one? In particular, does the impossibility even apply to randomized procedures, or is it confined to deterministic ones — and if it is confined to deterministic ones, what does that say about why a polity will tolerate chance at the margin but never at the center?

Third, test the practice against a variant I have invented. Suppose my county abolished the tiebreak and instead selected the winner uniformly at random from the ballot in every election. The two procedures use identical apparatus and identical reasoning about symmetry between persons; one is universally accepted and the other sounds insane. Identify which of the presuppositions you reconstructed in the first part actually does the separating work, and say whether the difference is principled or merely psychological.

Finally, tell me what would have to change in the practice — in the legal language, in what officials say when they announce the result, in the loser's concession — if the presupposed theory were replaced by a straightforwardly aggregative one on which the only thing that can confer legitimacy is the sum of actual preferences. And flag any point at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that rival theories would equally license it.
