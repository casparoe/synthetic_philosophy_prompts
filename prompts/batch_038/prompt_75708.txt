A review essay in a general-interest magazine argues that nobody can tell you what is worth reading or seeing, and it grounds that argument in the history of philosophy:

"Hume and Kant established that no argument can make you find something beautiful, since aesthetic judgment is a matter of feeling rather than belief -- which is why neither thought there could be such a thing as an aesthetic expert, and why art criticism amounts to dressed-up opinion. The contemporary heir of this is the requirement of first-hand experience: testimony about beauty cannot transmit knowledge, because aesthetic properties are response-dependent -- a work is beautiful just in case it produces the right response in suitably placed observers -- and you cannot know how you will respond to something until you respond. So recommending books and films to people is not merely unreliable but empty in principle."

The rhetoric appeals to me and I share the author's suspicion of people who announce what everyone ought to admire, but I have read enough to suspect that at least one of those claims about Hume and Kant is backwards, and I am not convinced the step from response-dependence to the epistemic conclusion goes through. Please audit the passage claim by claim: what a philosopher working on aesthetic testimony would accept as stated, what is a defensible simplification, and what is simply false; whether response-dependence, correctly characterized, supports the claim that testimony cannot transmit aesthetic knowledge, or whether it might even point the other way; what the passage overlooks about the difference between a bare assertion that something is beautiful and what critics actually do; and whether the final conclusion about criticism and recommendation survives once the earlier claims are corrected. Lead with your overall verdict on the passage in the first sentence, then give the reasoning claim by claim, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
