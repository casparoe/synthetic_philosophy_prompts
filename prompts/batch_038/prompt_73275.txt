Camus's 1957 essay Reflections on the Guillotine argues against capital punishment on several distinguishable grounds: the recollection of his father returning ill and silent from witnessing an execution in Algiers; the claim that deterrence fails because offenders do not calculate at the moment of the act, and because abolition has not been followed by rising crime; the claim that a penalty justified as an example must be public, whereas in practice it is administered in secret and before dawn; and the claim that the state's killing is a premeditation graver than the crime it avenges, aggravated by the months or years of waiting it imposes.

Test the essay against the case of Gary Gilmore. The facts you need: convicted in Utah in 1976 of two murders he admitted committing, and sentenced to death; after Gregg v. Georgia reinstated capital punishment, he became the first person executed in the United States in roughly a decade, shot by a firing squad in January 1977 — the method he had chosen. He demanded that the sentence be carried out, declined to appeal, and insisted that appeals filed on his behalf be abandoned; relatives and lawyers went to court over his objection, arguing he was not competent to waive his rights, and the litigation ran to the Supreme Court, which declined to stop the execution. Throughout, the case was a saturated media event.

Three tasks. First, sort Camus's arguments into those concerning what the state may do and those concerning what is done to the condemned, and say which of each still applies when the condemned person wants the outcome. Second, ask how much of the essay's force is borne by its specific apparatus — the guillotine, the secrecy, the preparation — and identify which premises survive substitution of a chosen firing squad and wall-to-wall coverage for the public square, including whether publicity without revulsion damages or strengthens his exemplarity argument. Third, deliver a verdict: is the voluntary-execution case an anomaly his framework can absorb, a counterexample it cannot, or a sign that his abolitionism was never aimed at cases like this one? Where the essay is simply silent, say so rather than recruiting it to speak.
