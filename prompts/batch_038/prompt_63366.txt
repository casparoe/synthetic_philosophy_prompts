I cover law and the social sciences for a monthly magazine of ideas, and next month's long interview is with a legal philosopher whose new book defends a modernized legal moralism: that the criminal law may properly prohibit conduct a society's conventional morality condemns, on the ground that shared norms are load-bearing for the institutions that make ordinary cooperation possible. She knows Hart and Devlin well, and she is adept at absorbing the objection that the disintegration thesis is an untested empirical assumption by saying that hers is a sociological claim about trust and social capital rather than Devlin's claim about the reasonable person's feelings. I have forty-five minutes and do not want to spend them on restatement.

Draft nine or ten questions, each with a sentence or two on the point it is meant to expose, covering at least the following:

- Hart's charge that the disintegration argument slides between a definitional claim (a society just is its shared morality, so any moral change is a change of society) and an empirical one (moral change causes institutional collapse), and her likely retreat to the position that only some norms are load-bearing while others are decorative — with a follow-up demanding a test, applied in advance rather than in hindsight, for telling the two apart, given that we usually identify load-bearing norms only after they have been removed.
- The treason analogy: what magnitude of threat the comparison requires, and whether private conduct can meet it.
- The epistemology of collective indignation and disgust: what such feelings are supposed to track, how she distinguishes a settled shared conviction from a vocal minority or a moral panic, and what threshold of agreement must be crossed before coercion becomes admissible.
- The asymmetry problem: if the value is cohesion, why does enforcement in practice target sexual conduct rather than infidelity, greed, or the refusal of ordinary duties of mutual aid — and a demand that she name one norm whose enforcement would cut against her own sympathies.
- The dynamism problem: the view entrenches whatever morality is in force, yet the changes she herself welcomes required the law to run ahead of, or at least to decline to police, settled opinion. How is a legislator to tell an emerging morality from a dissolving one?

Reserve the final question for whether her sociological version collapses back into a long-run harm principle, and have it ask her to state the empirical claim she will accept testing, the measure she will accept, and the result that would force her to abandon the view. Add two questions of your own choosing at whatever you judge to be the position's softest point, and flag any question where you expect a rehearsed answer together with the pivot that should follow it.
