I have no academic training in anything. I left school at sixteen, worked in the trades for most of my life, and have spent the last several years teaching myself French and, more recently, teaching myself to think about translation — mostly by attempting it and doing it badly. I have read almost none of the literature and I am not looking for a survey of it. What I want is close, patient attention to a single text.

Some months ago I picked up a popular book about translation, written by a scientist, which takes a short sixteenth-century French poem as a test case and collects a great many English versions of it. I deliberately stopped reading before I got to those versions. I found the French original myself — a modernized-spelling text posted on a French poetry website, titled "A une damoyselle malade" — and I have been living with it alone. Here it is, with line numbers I added myself:

3  Le bon jour;

My school French gets me the gist of some of this and leaves me genuinely lost in other parts, and I have no confidence at all in my instincts about sixteenth-century usage. Before I tell you about the private theory I have built around translating it, I need help simply understanding what it says. Please work through the following, and be blunt wherever my reading is just wrong.

1. Give me a plain, literal prose gloss of the whole poem, line by line or in small groups, keeping my line numbers. Where the French word order has been inverted, say so and tell me what the natural order would be — I suspect lines 6–7 are a case in point.

2. I have been scanning this as four-syllable lines, because "Ma mi-gnon-ne" plainly has four sounds in it. A person I argued with online insists every line here is three syllables, because a final mute e at the end of a line does not count. Settle this: state the counting rules that actually apply, apply them to a few of the trickier lines (say 9–11, 14, 19, 26), and tell me whether all twenty-eight lines are the same length or whether any of them is irregular.

3. My copy has no stanza breaks at all. Is that how the poem really goes, or do other printings divide it? Give me the rhyme scheme letter by letter. I made out nothing but couplets, but the last four lines seem to behave differently, and I cannot tell whether lines 27–28 are deliberately echoing lines 1–2 or whether I am inventing a pattern out of a coincidence.

4. What exactly is "doint" in line 26? I cannot find it in my dictionary. Give me the modern French equivalent of the whole clause, tell me what grammatical form this is, and say whether it was already an archaism or a fixed devotional formula in Marot's day.

5. The poem starts by addressing the lady as "vous" and at some point switches to "tu." Tell me exactly where the switch happens, whether it is complete or whether the two forms coexist afterward, and what it would have signalled to a reader of the period — a change in intimacy, a change in who is being addressed, or a change in the kind of speech act being performed. Is there anything else in the text that identifies her or fixes her social standing relative to the speaker?

6. Lines 14–19 are where I am most at sea. "Va, friande / De ta bouche" — what is "friande" doing here, and what does it govern? Does "Qui se couche" refer to the lady or to her mouth? And what is the accusation being made: is he saying her illness is her own fault, brought on by eating preserves in bed? Please unpack the syntax rather than just paraphrasing the sense.

7. Two smaller puzzles. In line 13, does "Le vous mande" mean "he sends you word of it," "he commands it of you," or something else, and how do I choose? And in line 10, who is the "on" in "Et qu'on sorte" — the lady, or someone else?

8. In line 25, is "l'embonpoint" a compliment about her figure or a warning about wasting away? I assume the sixteenth-century valuation of plumpness was not ours, but I would like to be told rather than to assume. Relatedly, how affectionate or how forward is "Ma mignonne" as a form of address from a poet to a lady who is presumably his superior?

9. Some editions call this an "épigramme." What did that word mean for Marot and his readers, as distinct from what it means now, and does the poem end with the kind of pointed turn the form was supposed to deliver? If so, where is the point — and is the poem, all things considered, a courteous get-well note or a teasing, flirtatious one? Point me to the lines that decide it.

10. Since my text is modernized, I worry I am reading rhymes that Marot's contemporaries did not hear. Are any of these pairs richer or poorer than they now appear? I wonder especially about "embonpoint"/"doint," "vitement"/"Clément," and "recouvez"/"ouvrez." And is there anywhere in the poem a pair that rhymes only to the eye?

Now the theory, which you should feel free to attack once the above is settled. I keep what I call a ledger. Every formal feature of the original is a debt the translation owes: line length, the position of each rhyme, whether the poem comes back to its opening rhyme at the end, the register of each archaic word, and — if that distinction is a real feature of French verse and not something I have made up — the difference between rhymes ending in a mute e and rhymes that do not. When I can pay a debt on the spot, in the same line and the same currency, I do. When I cannot, I record it and settle it later, but only in kind: if I lose a rhyme at line 15, I owe a rhyme somewhere after it, not a pretty image I happen to like.

Where I am stuck is that three features of this poem appear to have no English currency whatever, so there is nothing to record and nothing to pay against: the shift from "vous" to "tu," which English has lost entirely; "doint," which is archaic in French in one very particular way and would force me into some English archaism that is archaic in a different way; and "embonpoint," which is a single word doing work that English needs a clause for, and whose connotations have drifted. So I want three things from you on this. First, audit my list of features: what have I missed, and is the masculine/feminine rhyme distinction one I should be tracking at all? Second, in actual fact I have been cheating — I dropped "confitures" completely in my draft, and two stanzas later I invented an image that is nowhere in Marot to make up the weight. Under my own rule, is that a repayment or a default dressed up as bookkeeping? I want you to argue the question rather than list options. Third, if a translator "pays" for the lost vous/tu distinction by importing an archaism somewhere else in the poem, has the debt been discharged, or has the ledger simply been cooked?

Please do not write me a full translation. If you need to make a point concrete, quoting back three or four lines of English is plenty.
