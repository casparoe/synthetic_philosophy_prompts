I'm a data analyst, not a lawyer — I spent six years at a housing authority cleaning up lead-inspection records — and three months ago I got hired as the staff person for a city reparations commission. The ordinance that created us sets aside $40 million over five years and says the fund is for harms caused by the city's own past actions and their continuing effects, language that everyone on the commission agrees is doing a lot of unexamined work. We start taking claims in about eight weeks. Realistically I'll be preparing thirty to fifty recommendations a week for a seven-member commission that rubber-stamps most of them, I get maybe an hour per claim, and the claimants don't have lawyers. Two commissioners are former public defenders who think anything short of a large check to every Black household in the city is a betrayal of the point; one is a retired contractor who thinks we can only pay for things we can put a receipt on. Nobody has given me a way to decide between them, and I keep noticing that whichever account of harm I lean on mentally in the morning gives a different answer than the one I leaned on in the afternoon. Here's the piece I want you to take seriously rather than paper over: I read about the non-identity problem on a long flight and it lodged in my brain. For descendant claims — the redlining and urban-renewal sort, where the people directly harmed are dead — the counterfactual test, would you be better off if the injustice hadn't happened, seems to fail, because the specific claimant wouldn't have existed at all. But there are other accounts that don't need the counterfactual: one where the wrong is that you were deprived of something that was already rightfully yours or your family's, and one where the obligation comes from benefits the payer is still sitting on rather than from harm it caused. Those give different eligibility rules, different amounts, and different answers on whether the city's continuing corporate identity even matters, and I dont know which one our ordinance's wording actually commits us to.

What I need is a decision procedure I can run at a desk, in order, per claim: the questions to ask in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer to each, including what stands in for documentation when there is none, since a lot of these families have nothing on paper before 1960; where the procedure stops with a no; where it stops with a yes but no number, because sizing the award is a values call the commission has to make; and where I should mark a step as a judgment call wearing a procedure costume instead of pretending it's mechanical. Build it on whichever account of harm and liability you think survives the most objections, and say out loud what you had to drop to make it usable by someone with an hour and no legal training — I'd much rather be told the procedure is quietly collapsing a distinction now than discover it in year two. Then run it end to end on three claims. Claim A: a woman with a deed her grandmother signed in 1957 for a house in the neighborhood the city cleared for a highway, where the family was paid the then-assessed value and relocated to a rental. Claim B: a man in his fifties with no documents at all whose grandparents were turned down for a business loan through a city-run program, and who is himself doing fine financially. Claim C: a claim from the adult children of a man who died in 2019 of a condition tied to a city dump that the commission has already admitted liability on, where the family settled a tort suit in 2005 and received money. At least one of these should come out no and I want you to say so flatly, without softening it, and I expect C is going to embarrass us — if it is, tell me why and what the least-bad version of the answer looks like.

Please use exactly these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths.
