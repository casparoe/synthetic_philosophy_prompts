I am working on the question of whether reality has a fundamental level, and I have become convinced that two claims which are usually run together in discussions of grounding are in fact distinct. The first concerns the grounding relation: that grounding is well-founded, meaning that there are no infinitely descending chains of grounds. The second concerns the world: that there is a fundamental base, consisting of facts which are not themselves grounded, such that everything else is ultimately grounded in them. I would like a careful explanation of how these come apart and of what turns on the difference.

Please take up the following points, in roughly this order.

First, well-foundedness as it is standardly defined is a property about subsets: a relation is well-founded when every nonempty subset of its domain has a minimal element. That is not obviously the same as the absence of infinite descending sequences, and the equivalence depends on choice principles. Explain what this gap amounts to when the relation in question is grounding rather than membership, and say which formulation is doing the real work for a philosopher who insists on well-foundedness. Is the underlying worry that an infinite regress would leave some fact ungrounded, or rather that it would leave nothing available to answer the question of why things are the way they are at all? Those seem like different complaints, and I would like them teased apart.

Second, separate two structures that are frequently collapsed into one another. In the first, every fact has a ground and the grounds descend forever. In the second, a single fact is grounded by infinitely many facts at once, jointly, with no descent anywhere in the picture. Explain why the second can look like a threat to the existence of a finite base while leaving the no-descending-chains condition perfectly intact, and whether it ought to worry a proponent of fundamentality more or less than the first does. Relatedly, say whether well-foundedness on its own secures a fundamental base in any interesting sense: I take it that if grounding is well-founded there must be ungrounded facts, but I am not persuaded that this gives you a single base level rather than merely a scattered collection of minimal elements, and I suspect the inference turns on whether the base is supposed to be finite, unique, or simply the set of minimals.

Third, situate the two live rivals to fundamentality within this framework — infinite descent, and coherentism about grounding, on which the relation can loop. For the looping case, identify precisely which formal constraint on grounding a loop violates, since a pair of facts grounding one another and a fact grounding itself do not obviously fail in the same way, and irreflexivity and asymmetry are not independent constraints once transitivity is assumed. Tell me whether mutual grounding is better or worse than self-grounding, and why.

Finally, consider the analogy with metaphysical gunk, a world in which nothing has an ultimate part because every part has a proper part. Say whether this is a genuine parallel for infinitely descending grounds or a misleading one, and explain what has to be true of grounding for the parallel to hold.

I am not after a survey of who has defended what; I want the conceptual structure. Assume the reader is comfortable with the usual formal constraints on grounding and with elementary set theory, but has not thought about the choice-theoretic wrinkle at all.
