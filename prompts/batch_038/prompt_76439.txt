Two people I heard arguing after a departmental workshop on assurance cases for autonomous systems would not let the matter drop, and I'd like an adjudication. Iris works on formally verified control software; Daniel is a logician. Neither is a crank, which is what made it hard to follow.

**(1) Iris:** Verification of critical software is at bottom a proof: you formalize the code and the property, and derive the property inside some theory T. Any theory rich enough to talk about programs is rich enough to encode its own syntax and its own proofs. If T is consistent and recursively axiomatized, Gödel's second theorem says T cannot prove Con(T). So there is a claim about T's own reliability that T can never settle from the inside. Assurance must therefore rest on something outside the system being assured: self-certification is impossible in principle, not merely impractical.

**(2) Daniel:** Con(T) is one particular arithmetical sentence — the formalization of "no derivation of a contradiction exists." It says nothing about whether a controller keeps a vessel inside its envelope, whether a scheduler meets its deadlines, or whether an update rule preserves an invariant. Those are different sentences, and T can prove plenty of them outright. You have slid from "T cannot prove its own consistency" to "T cannot certify its own correctness," and consistency is not correctness. The incompleteness theorems concern a theory's proof-theoretic self-description; specifications live at the object level. The two coincide only if the specification literally is Con(T), and no specification anyone writes is.

**(3) Iris:** Take the label back if you like; the structural point stands. The first theorem yields, for such a T, a sentence G that T cannot prove and that is nonetheless true — true because it asserts its own unprovability and T is consistent. So within any sufficiently expressive theory there is a gap between truth and provability, and it is a gap about the theory itself. A verification enterprise that must describe the verifier inherits that gap. When people say a system cannot fully justify its own reasoning, that is the content of the claim, not a slogan.

**(4) Daniel:** "Any sufficiently expressive theory" is doing unearned work. The construction requires that T represent enough syntax, and the second theorem requires that T's provability predicate satisfy the derivability conditions. Weaken arithmetic far enough — to systems that cannot prove multiplication is a total function and cannot contain even Robinson arithmetic — and you get consistent theories that prove their own consistency. So the phenomenon tracks a specific and delicate formal capacity, not richness in any loose engineering sense, and certainly not self-modeling. It is also not a wall but a ladder: PA together with Con(PA) proves Con(PA), and ZFC proves Con(PA) outright. Add the right principle and the previously unprovable sentence becomes provable.

**(5) Iris:** The ladder is my argument. PA + Con(PA) proves Con(PA) because you handed it Con(PA) as an axiom; that relocates the trust rather than discharging it. Every rung needs an assumption from outside, and each new assumption carries its own consistency question. Assurance is a regress that never terminates in self-contained proof — precisely what I claimed. And your self-verifying systems certify themselves only by being too feeble to state the interesting properties: you may have self-certification or expressive power, not both.

**(6) Daniel:** The regress terminates; it just does not terminate in an internal proof. Gentzen established the consistency of arithmetic by transfinite induction up to ε₀, an ordering arithmetic cannot prove well-founded, and we accept the argument because we see that it is correct. Knowledge of consistency bottoms out in informal mathematical insight, and it is finite: you need one good argument at the metalevel, not an infinite stack of theories. "There is always an unverifiable trust assumption" is a thesis about justification, not a consequence of anything Gödel proved.

**(7) Iris:** Then you have conceded the operative point. At the top of every assurance case sits something that is not a proof inside the system — a judgment accepted because it seems right. That was my practical claim all along; Gödel's contribution is to show that this is not a temporary deficiency of our tools.

**(8) Daniel:** Gödel shows no such thing. What he shows is a precise, local fact about a precise class of theories and a precise sentence. If you want a theorem to the effect that a system which trusts its own outputs thereby becomes unsound, name it. Otherwise you are borrowing a famous name for a general skepticism about self-knowledge that the theorem neither states nor implies, and the theorem is doing decorative work.
