I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in formal epistemology on the question of how an agent should revise her credences when what she learns is itself a probabilistic conditional — the family of puzzles initiated by van Fraassen's Judy Benjamin case, in which a reliable informant reports that a conditional holds to some stated degree, and the naive route through Adams' thesis seems to let testimony about an "if" shift probability between hypotheses the conditional says nothing directly about.

Please build a decision tree that takes a student from "I have just been told, by someone I trust, that a conditional is probable" to a named proposal for updating. Begin by fixing the concrete case you will use, in a sentence or two, so that every node is anchored to it. Each node must be a question the student could answer by reflecting on that case or on a normative intuition about revision, without already knowing the labels — for instance, whether the testimony is allowed to move probability between hypotheses relevant to the antecedent; whether prior ratios among the hypotheses it leaves untouched must be preserved; whether the answer changes if the conditional is supposed rather than asserted, or inferred from statistics rather than reported; whether your rule must commute with later updates.

The leaves should include at least: probability kinematics applied to the supposition in the Adams–Jeffrey style; van Fraassen's own resolution; Skyrms's conditional-event algebra; Kaufmann's Bernoulli-space or random-variable semantics; the possible-worlds camp that rejects Adams' thesis; and a suppositional or contextualist position on which there is no single correct update. Give each leaf one sentence naming the constraint it gives up. Note any combination of answers that leads to no defended position, and say whether the gap is due to incoherence, unattractiveness, or mere neglect; flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot actually separate.

Finally, since this is a classification instrument rather than a verdict: identify the branch question you think is least well posed, say how it is likely to mis-sort students, and state what consideration would lead you to redraw that node or to merge two of the leaves.
