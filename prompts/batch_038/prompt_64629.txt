Pretend you're a professional epistemologist who has moderated a bunch of these things, and think hard before you draft — maybe jot down some brainstorming first about what the two camps genuinely agree on, then write the polished document.

my school philosophy club has two members who've been arguing since september and i'm pretty sure they're talking past eachother. one is a contextualist: "knows" behaves like a gradable word ("tall", "flat"), so the bar moves with the conversation the report happens in, and what's going on for the believer is irrelevant. the other is a pragmatic encroachment person: the meaning of "knows" is fixed, but whether you actually know depends on what's riding on the belief for the believer — high stakes make knowing harder, period. each of them claims the other is just redescribing their own view in different words.

the case i want them to fight over: lena checks one weather app and says it won't rain. in version 1 nothing is at stake, she's just deciding whether to walk home. in version 2 she's coordinating an outdoor wedding and being wrong costs her thousands. in version 3 her own stakes are as high as in version 2, but the person who says "lena knew it wouldn't rain" is her roommate, in a lazy conversation where nothing at all turns on it.

write the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between them could actually produce: a numbered list of claims both would sign, including about what version 3 shows and what ordinary speakers would say about each version; then a table of the disagreements, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording of its own position; then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would move at least one side — and for each crux a case, test, or argument both agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. if part of the fight is only verbal, say which part and why.
