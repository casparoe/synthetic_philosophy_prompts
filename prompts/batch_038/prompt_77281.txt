Question as set:

"Kant distinguishes maxims that cannot even be conceived as universal laws of nature from maxims that can be conceived as such but cannot be willed as such. Explain the distinction, and show how it accounts for the difference between strict and wide duty in his examples of the false promise and of refusing help to others. What kind of argument does the second case require?"

Student's answer:

Kant's test is applied to maxims, not to actions or to types of action, which matters because the same act can fall under different maxims and come out differently. He divides failures of the test into two kinds. In the first, the maxim cannot even be thought as a universal law of nature without contradiction: a world in which everyone acted on it is not a possible world. In the second, such a world is perfectly conceivable and people could go on living in it, but a rational agent cannot consistently will to be in it.

The false promise belongs to the first kind. The maxim is something like: when I need money, I'll borrow it on a promise to repay that I know I won't keep. If that were a universal law of nature, promising as such would collapse, since no one would take a promise at face value, so the maxim cannot even be thought as a law — it destroys the condition of its own success. This yields a strict duty to others, and strict duties admit of no exception in favour of inclination.

Refusing help belongs to the second kind. A world in which nobody ever helps anybody is not inconceivable; the species could get along in it, as Kant concedes. But I cannot will it, because there will be situations in which I need the love and sympathy of others, and in willing such a law I would be depriving myself of what I myself will to have. So the failure is in the will and not in the concept, and the duty that results is wide: the law cannot fix how much help I owe, to whom, or on which occasions, and how far one goes beyond the letter is left open. Wide duties are the ones other moral theories treat as supererogatory — praiseworthy to perform, not blameworthy to omit.

The second case, then, does not run through the concept of a promise or any other social practice. It runs through what an agent necessarily wants, which is why it is the weaker of the two arguments. In effect the reasoning is: I cannot will universal indifference because I might one day be on the receiving end of it, and that is close to a prudential calculation — Kant knows this, which is why he makes the duty wide, to leave room for how much self-interest is at stake. Both failures can also be restated under the formula of humanity: to act on a non-universalisable maxim just is to treat someone as a mere means, which is what the false promiser does. The formulations are the same principle in different words, so passing one of them guarantees passing the other.

The same goes for the remaining two maxims. The self-love-and-suicide case fails because one cannot will a world in which the feeling whose function is to further life destroys life; the neglected-talents case fails at the conception stage, since a world of permanently undeveloped capacities cannot be thought at all.
