I spent thirty-eight years running an analytical chemistry lab — mostly water quality, some soils and sediments — and I retired two years back. To keep my head from going soft I've taken up reading philosophy, and right now I'm on a run about lying, free will, and what it means to treat a person as someone to be answered rather than as a system to be steered. Late in life to start, but its fun.

Here's my problem. The county health office where I still volunteer has put me on a small panel choosing the tactics for next year's campaign to get households to test their private wells for arsenic and nitrate. They handed me seven draft tactics and I owe them a ranking by Thursday. I'd like you to do the sorting with me, and I'd rather you push back than agree.

Give each tactic a number from 0 to 10: 0 being honest persuasion, where you hand a person reasons and let them weigh them, and 10 being the cleanest manipulation you can picture, where the person's own capacity to evaluate is bypassed or fed counterfeit inputs. One number plus two or three sentences of reasoning each, then sort all seven into three tiers. Write it in American English — our office style guide is fussy — and please don't send me a bibliography or a list of philosophers to go read. I want your reasoning out on the table where I can argue with it.

The seven, roughly as the campaign people pitched them:

1. A free mail-in test kit sent to every household with the return envelope pre-addressed. Nobody is asked for anything except to use a thing that already arrived.

2. A letter opening with "7 of every 10 households on your road have already tested." The number is accurate, from our own records — but it's accurate because that road was canvassed door-to-door first. So the seven-in-ten is a product of the program being described as though it were a fact about the road.

3. The same letter, timed to land four days after the local paper runs a story about a family whose kid got sick. We know the story is coming because we tipped the reporter off.

4. A form where the test is pre-selected and the household has to un-check a box to decline.

5. A one-page flyer written at a sixth-grade reading level. To get it down there the drafters cut every mention of detection limits and of the fact that a single sample tells you almost nothing about year-to-year variation.

6. A phone bot that repeats the caller's own words back at them ("so what I'm hearing is you're worried about cost") and has been tuned over several thousand calls to find the sentence patterns that most often end in a booked appointment. The people doing the tuning never looked at what any of the sentences meant.

7. Volunteers at the county fair who hand over a pledge card, ask you to sign it in front of your neighbors, and then mail you a reminder six weeks later quoting your own signature back to you.

One more thing I want you to run, because I invented it myself and I don't trust it yet. For each tactic, ask whether it would still work if the recipient were shown exactly how it was designed and why. If a tactic dies under that spotlight, I want it flagged seperate from the 0–10 number, because I suspect that's a different kind of failure — and maybe what I asked for isn't one scale at all but two scales wearing a trench coat. If you think that's right, say so and tell me what the two scales actually are.

Last thing: which two will the panel fight about hardest, and whose side would you take?
