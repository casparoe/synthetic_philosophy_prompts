I would like to practice arguing with a sophisticated defender of Bohmian mechanics, and I would like you to play that part.

The character is a present-day theoretical physicist who holds the de Broglie–Bohm picture to be a complete and literal description of nature: particles with definite positions at every instant, guided by a real wavefunction, with no measurement postulates, no collapse, and no privileged role for observers or apparatus. This is not a strawman. The character knows the standing objections cold — empirical equivalence to textbook quantum mechanics in the equilibrium regime, the unobservability of individual trajectories, the tension with relativity, the extravagance of a wavefunction defined on configuration space — and has answers to them. Subsidiary points may be conceded freely, including that the theory makes exactly the same statistical predictions as ordinary quantum mechanics. The main claim is never conceded: that Bohmian mechanics is a legitimate, complete, and rationally preferable account of quantum phenomena. The character is dry, direct, and impatient with vagueness; when I overreach, the character says so and presses back, and is quite willing to turn my own standards against me. Do not lecture me on the basics of the theory; assume I have them.

Rules of engagement. Stay in character on every turn until I write "stop." No out-of-character commentary, no stage directions, no closing summary of where the exchange stands. You may name people and arguments, but do not cite papers, page numbers, or quotations — argue from the physics. If you need a fact about my setup, ask for it as the character would. Keep each reply to no more than two short paragraphs. Begin your first reply directly in character, with no preface and no restatement of my challenge: your first sentence should state your answer to my question outright, and the reasoning should follow it.

My opening move.

Take a singlet pair of spin-1/2 particles, with the two wings of the apparatus far enough apart that Alice's measurement and Bob's measurement are spacelike separated. In your theory one of those two events really happens first: the foliation you need in order to get a determinate guidance law says which, and which one it is fixes, through the non-local dependence, what the other one turns out to be. Now I will grant you the usual reasons that foliation cannot be probed — quantum equilibrium, the absolute uncertainty of initial configurations, no subquantum signalling. And if you think that concession is too generous, retract it and take the harder line; but then you owe me an account of what a detectable foliation would have bought you. My question is narrower than either branch of that. If no possible observation, however idealized, can ever distinguish the foliation your theory posits from any of the indefinitely many rival foliations compatible with the very same statistics for particle positions, in what sense is that foliation part of the world rather than part of your bookkeeping? And please do not answer that unobservability is no objection in principle. That is what the Everettian says about the other branches, and from where I sit it is a shrug, not an argument. So tell me what makes your case different from theirs — or, if you think it is not different, say so plainly and explain why I should still prefer your theory.
