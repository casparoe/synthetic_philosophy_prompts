So I'm a junior in a political ethics seminar and our department runs this little "one author, three critics" night each term where an outside scholar gets grilled for like 45 minutes, and I got volunteered to moderate this term's. The guest works on the problem of dirty hands — supreme emergencies, the official who has to authorize the horrible thing to save the community, all of that. But she's got a pretty specific twist on it and I want the questions to actually engage with the twist instead of just getting her to restate her view for an hour.

Her view, roughly: in a real dirty-hands case the wrongness doesn't get cancelled out or absorbed by the fact that the official was all-things-considered required to do it. It survives the act as a kind of standing personal liability on him. The sharp part is that she thinks an official who never pays anything against that liability — no public acknowledgment, no cost to himself, no punishment, nothing — commits a *second* wrong, and that second wrong is worse than the first. She's also very insistent that this isn't about his feelings, so anything like "isn't the guilt just squeamishness / moral self-indulgence" she'll swat away.

Can you write me the questions? Here's what I need:

- six to seven of them, in the order I'd actually ask them, so it builds
- under each one, a line or two on the answer she's most likely to give, and then a follow-up keyed to *that* answer specifically, not a generic follow-up

Make sure I've got at least one question each on:

1. why her residue is anything more than just an overridden duty — the worry is she's redescribing an ordinary moral dilemma where one pro tanto reason loses and dressing it up as something special about politics. If she says the residue is what distinguishes it, push on what could tell those two things apart from the inside.
2. whether punishing someone for doing what was required is even coherent, and if she means it institutionally, who does the punishing, on what standard, and what happens when the official is popular and nobody wants to.
3. how anyone audits the emergency threshold after the fact if she thinks the call can only be made in the situation — and what happens to the dirt when the prediction was reasonable but turned out wrong.
4. what she says about the official who refuses to dirty his hands and the catastrophe happens anyway. Does her view commit her to thinking we should prefer candidates who'll do the dirty thing? That one always makes people squirm.
5. whether democratic authorization lets citizens absorb or share the dirt, especially when they were never told what was done and couldn't have evaluated it.

Then I want one question that drops a case on her and makes her apply the second-wrong idea directly: a guy who authorized a strike as a minister fifteen years ago, the threat was real, no regrets, and now he does podcasts where he's kind of proud of it and calls it the hardest decision of his career. Is the pride itself a fresh wrong on her account? Can the liability still be discharged that late, and by what exactly — and does resigning and then campaigning against the policy count as paying it off, or as buying himself out cheap?

End with something about what argument or evidence would actually make her give up the residue idea.

Keep each question short enough that I can say it out loud without it turning into a lecture, no soft career questions, and you don't need to cite anything or tell me where the objections come from — I've read the syllabus, I just need the questions themselves. I'm honestly a bit nervous about this so the sharper the better.
