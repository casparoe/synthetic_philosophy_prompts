I spent thirty-five years as an applied entomologist and have come to philosophy late; next month I present a paper at a workshop on the ethics of invertebrate pest control. My thesis is a metric rather than a metaphysics: the moral cost of a control programme should be reckoned in expected life-days foregone, discounted by our uncertainty that the creature in question is sentient. On that reckoning a fast-acting contact spray, which kills adults near the end of a very short adult stage, is markedly cheaper than a slow-acting agent — an insect growth regulator or an entomopathogenic fungus — which destroys most of a lifespan in each member of an entire cohort of immatures. I also claim, more tentatively, that pheromone-based mating disruption is not automatically the benign option, since it turns on whether merely possible larvae count at all. The room is mostly animal ethicists of welfarist or utilitarian persuasion, several of whom work on the comparative evidence for insect sentience; there is one rights-oriented deontologist and one integrated-pest-management practitioner who regards the whole framing as unhinged.

Give me my Q&A preparation sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased in the vocabulary the objector would use, each with a reply I can deliver in under two minutes and a note on where that reply is thinnest; what I should concede in my opening remarks so the concession does not cost me later; the one reply that has to land for the metric to survive; and the question I am dreading — my own suspicion is that it concerns the non-target bycatch of broad-spectrum sprays, which my metric scores but my argument has so far ignored. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which objection is most dangerous and what I must do about it — and proceed from there, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
