You are a philosopher and social theorist with deep expertise in the analysis of institutions, evaluation practices, and the ethics of care work, and you are in the habit of advising people who want to think a problem through rather than be reassured. Please think carefully before answering. Begin by sketching, for your own purposes, two or three quite different general descriptions of my situation that a well-informed observer might offer, note what each would predict about how things will develop, and only then commit to an account and tell me what follows from it for the decisions I actually face.

Here is the situation. I supervise a team of fourteen at a regional nonprofit that provides in-home support to elderly and disabled clients. Our principal funder has asked us to pilot a new "client experience" system. After each visit the client completes a short survey on a tablet: mostly rating scales, plus a box in which they are invited to describe, in their own words, what the visit meant to them. The responses are aggregated into a monthly score for each member of my staff. The scores appear on a dashboard visible to the whole team, colour-coded to show where each person sits relative to the team average. Nothing has been written down about consequences; no one has suggested that pay or hours will depend on the numbers. There is also a monthly "coaching conversation," in which the staff member and I sit down with their own figures and their own clients' comments and I am meant to help them set a personal development goal.

Two things have left me unsure how to think about this. First, my colleagues are divided. Some say the system at last gives clients a voice and makes invisible work visible; others say it is the same old oversight with a gentler name. I have been unable to decide between these descriptions, and the arguments on each side increasingly sound as though they are talking past one another.

Second, in the two months since the pilot began, the vocabulary of the tool has migrated into ordinary conversation here. Clients are now described as engaged or hard to reach. A client who does not complete surveys first appears as a gap in the data and then, gradually, as a difficulty in the person. Some staff now briefly clients before a visit about what to expect, and steer conversation toward things likely to be written in the comments box. No one instructed any of this. I cannot locate anyone responsible: the funder says the dashboard is ours to run, my director says the requirement came from the funder, and my staff say they adopted these habits themselves because they want to do well.

With that in mind, please address three questions:

1. Am I entitled to object at all? Everyone involved endorses the system, clients say they appreciate being asked, and on the measures we have, the quality of care has improved. If my unease is legitimate, what exactly is it an objection to, given that I cannot point to anyone doing anything to anyone?
2. Is there a design of such a system that avoids what worries me, or does the worry attach to the very idea of measuring, comparing, and discussing the results? Please be concrete about which modifications would matter and which would be cosmetic.
3. Should I take the lead role on the pilot, decline it, or take it precisely in order to shape it?
