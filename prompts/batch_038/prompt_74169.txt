There is a view I have arrived at over the past year that I would like stress-tested rather than endorsed.

It has three parts. First, on sentience: I assign a credence somewhere between roughly one in ten thousand and one in a hundred — varying by taxon and by which capacities are in question — to the claim that at least some insects have morally significant subjective experience, including a capacity for suffering. Second, on aggregation: I think the correct response to that uncertainty is to weight welfare by credence, so that the expected moral significance of a population is its size times the probability its members matter, rather than to bracket invertebrates until the evidence is decisive or to apply a precautionary threshold that switches them on all at once. Third, on scale: there are plausibly between 10^17 and 10^19 insects alive at any moment and orders of magnitude more over a century, so even the bottom of my credence range makes the expected insect population enormously larger than the human one. Taken together, I think small invertebrates dominate the moral arithmetic of the terrestrial biosphere, and that this bears on land use, insecticide deployment, wild-animal suffering, and how research effort should be allocated. I am not asking for a defense of this. I am asking for a map of where it is exposed.

In particular:

- Which of my premises are load-bearing, and which could be surrendered at little cost to the conclusion?
- What findings in comparative cognition, neurobiology, or ethology should I treat as genuinely lowering my credences, as opposed to merely failing to raise them? I want the distinction between absence of evidence and evidence of absence drawn explicitly here, and an assessment of whether the indicators commonly relied on — nociception, opioid responsiveness, associative learning, self-directed grooming under injury, motivational trade-offs — track the property I actually care about or only correlate with it.
- Are there structural problems with credence-weighting itself? I am thinking of aggregation over vast numbers of very slight welfare changes; of whether per-individual welfare in a short-lived, high-mortality organism is additive across individuals in the way I have assumed; and of whether my view is committed to conclusions about insects that I would find unacceptable if they were stated plainly.
- What about the sign? My reasoning does not depend on insect lives going well. If they are on balance net-negative — heavy juvenile mortality, parasitism, starvation — does that strengthen the position or destabilize it, and is there a variant of the view considerably worse than the one I have stated?
- Does the prior survive scrutiny? I want to know whether a credence of one in ten thousand is even well-formed here, given how much of it is inherited from analogy and how little from independent evidence, and whether the whole exercise rests on a reference-class choice I have not justified.

Please separate what a committed defender of this view would themselves concede as counting against it from what only its critics say. And be willing to conclude that the position is in fact well insulated against evidence — in which case tell me whether that insulation is a defect for a claim of this kind, or an appropriate feature of one that is partly about how to act under deep uncertainty.

Format. Lead with a **Bottom line** of two or three sentences naming where you think the view is most vulnerable. Then:

1. **The position, restated** (~120 words): give my argument back to me as a small number of premises plus a conclusion, flagging any step where you suspect I have been careless.
2. **Load-bearing and not** (~150 words): which premises the conclusion actually depends on, and which I could abandon cheaply.
3. **Ranked defeaters** (~700 words): what would count against the view, sorted into three tiers — fatal, serious but survivable, merely awkward — with a sentence or two on why each belongs in its tier.
4. **What defenders concede** (~200 words): the objections a serious proponent would accept as biting, and how they would absorb them.
5. **Predictions and divergences** (~200 words): what my view implies about the world, about research priorities, or about adjacent moral questions that its rivals do not imply, so that I have something to check it against.
6. **Is it insulated?** (~250 words): whether the view risks anything at all, and whether that is a problem in this case.
7. **My verdict** (~150 words): your own assessment of how exposed the position is, and the single consideration you think I should weigh most heavily.
