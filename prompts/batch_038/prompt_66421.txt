You're a philosopher of probability who's taught the Dutch book arguments for years and has refereed a hundred versions of this fight. Think carefully, and please do the arithmetic before you judge anything.

Some background. Our friend Sam won't give a single number for the chance of rain tomorrow. He says his credence is the whole interval from 0.4 to 0.6, that naming a point would be false precision, because he trusts two weather models that disagree and has no basis for weighting one over the other. Two people in my reading group, Jules and Marcus, have been arguing about whether that's rationally permissible for about two weeks now and it's gotten heated. They've asked me to referee and I honestly cant tell who's winning, so here's the exchange as best I reconstructed it, with the moves numbered:

(M1) Jules: Sam is flatly Dutch-bookable. A bookie sells him a bet paying $1 if it rains for 55 cents, and a bet paying $1 if it doesnt rain for 55 cents. His own interval says rain might be 0.6, so the first looks good to him; it also says rain might be 0.4, so the second looks good. He takes both, pays $1.10, collects exactly $1, and loses 10 cents whatever the weather does. An interval isn't a refuge from incoherence, it's a bigger target.

(M2) Marcus: That quietly assumes Sam buys any bet that some member of his interval likes. He doesn't. He said explicitly he only takes a bet if every permissible value rates it favorable. At 55 cents he turns down both, because 0.4 kills the rain bet and 0.6 kills the no-rain bet. In fact he'd happily take both sides at 40 cents, which locks in 20 cents for him. There is no finite package of bets that's a sure loss for him, so the Dutch book argument never gets off the ground.

(M3) Jules: Then look at what you've bought him. He refuses a rain bet at 45 cents AND a no-rain bet at 45 cents, which means he walks away from a package costing 90 cents that pays $1 no matter what. That's not caution, it's paralysis, and it's a sure loss in every sense that matters except that the 10 cents lands in someone else's pocket as a gain instead of his as a payment.

(M4) Marcus: Failing to grab a free lunch isn't incoherence. Coherence is about not being guaranteed to lose money you actually have. Sam loses nothing. And if your rule is "take every bet with nonnegative expected value under some admissible sharpening," you've simply demanded that he commit to a sharpening he has no reason to make.

(M5) Jules: Also, none of this is about real bookies or 45-cent bets and you know it. The argument is about what your numbers commit you to, not about whether someone can find you at a racetrack. If you get to wave it away because the bets are imaginary, it was never an argument about anything.

(M6) Marcus: Fine, treat it as an argument about commitments. Then notice Sam's set is a set of individually coherent commitments — every member obeys the axioms. I don't see how a bundle of coherent states adds up to incoherence, since there's no single price at which he's committed to both sides of anything.

(M7) Marcus, in the same breath: And the imprecision isn't permanent anyway. Sam conditionalizes each model as evidence arrives and the interval narrows, so by the time the afternoon forecast drops he'll be sharp. It's a snapshot of a learning process, not a settled defect.
