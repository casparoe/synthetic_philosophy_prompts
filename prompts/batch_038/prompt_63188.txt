We're both non-philosophers — I do data work, he does music — but we've been reading around Parfit's teletransporter case together, the one where you're scanned, destroyed, and an exact copy is built on Mars, plus the fission variants. His move was to build an argument that he thinks forces you either to accept that death doesn't matter at all or to admit the whole what-matters-in-survival framing was a trap from the start. Then he added a twist of his own, a case I'm pretty sure isn't in the book, and that's the part I most want checked.

The twist: suppose a clinic offers an amplification procedure. Not copying you to Mars — instead it upgrades your own future brain, so that from now on you have lossless episodic memory, perfect retention of your current intentions and character, and continuous self-monitoring. The upshot is that the psychological connection between you-now and you-in-forty-years becomes denser than the connection between you-now and you-tomorrow is under ordinary conditions, where memory is already leaking overnight. Now his argument:

1. What matters in survival isn't identity but Relation R — psychological connectedness and continuity, with the right kind of cause.
2. R comes in degrees, and it can hold without identity; that's exactly what the teletransporter and the fission cases are supposed to show.
3. Identity can't come in degrees. So whatever comes in degrees isn't identity. Fine — that's just why (1) is true.
4. By (1) and (2), how much of what matters I get is fixed entirely by how much R there is, and by nothing else.
5. The amplified future me has strictly more R to me-now than the ordinary future me does. So amplification buys me strictly more of what matters than ordinary survival does.
6. But the same clinic can run the amplification on a stranger's brain after imprinting my current memories, intentions and character onto it, losslessly, so that R to me-now is exactly as high as in my own amplified case. By (4), that stranger's future matters to me just as much as my amplified future does.
7. So R is impersonal: nothing in it is mine. The amplified stranger is no further from me-now than the amplified me is.
8. Now apply this to ordinary life, where R is already weak and leaky. For any person and any future time, the R between them and a suitably imprinted stranger differs from the R between them and their own future self only in amount, never in kind.
9. Therefore nobody ever survives in any sense that gives them a reason to care. There's no fact about survival over and above R, R is impersonal, and impersonal R can't ground anyone's self-interested concern. Death isn't a harm to the person, because there's no person for whom R adds up to survival.

He says (9) is where a Parfit-style view has to end up if you don't cheat, and that it also means we should both start saving for the amplification, since it's the only reliable way to get a large amount of what matters. And he is completely unbothered by (6) — he says if you accept (4) you positively owe it to yourself to want the stranger's future.
