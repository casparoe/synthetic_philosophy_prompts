Was up till stupid o'clock last night arguing with my mate Rob about Thomas Schelling, and neither of us budged, so I'm after a third opinion. Rob's line is that if Schelling had lived another ten years he'd have looked at this program equilibrium stuff and declared the commitment problem basically solved. Mine is that he'd have called it a conjuring trick — real maths, wrong question. Schelling died in 2016, so all three of the things below landed after he'd gone, which is the whole point of asking.

Quick version of what I mean so we're starting from the same page. First: there's a setup in game theory where, instead of picking an action, each player hands a computer program to a referee. The programs get to read each other's source code and then choose the actions. So in a one-shot prisioner's dilemma, which normally ends in mutual defection, you can get mutual cooperation as a stable outcome — something along the lines of "I cooperate provided your program would cooperate with mine". I know there's a wrinkle, that the naive version of that rule is circular and you have to be cleverer to make it well-defined, but the punchline is that defection stops being forced. Second: pricing algorithms that were never told to collude and have no channel to each other, which nonetheless learn to hold prices above the competitive level, sustaining it with a short punishment phase when one undercuts and a slow drift back afterwards. Third — and I'm hazier on this one — experiments with LLM agents playing against each other where the main variable is whether they get to see each other's instructions, and visibility apparently changes how they behave.

So: what I want is Schelling's response, argued out of his own toolkit rather than from general vibe. The distinction between a threat and a commitment, the strategic move, the thought that a threat is more credible when it leaves something to chance, the rationality of irrationality, focal points and salience, his insistence that bargaining is a process rather than a one-off calculation. Take the three cases in turn and picture him as the discussant at a session on them. For each one: which of his concepts actually does the work, what verdict he gives, and — this is the bit I care about most — where he'd say the formal result is true but the strategic point has been missed entirely. Then tell me which of the three he'd have found genuinely interesting as opposed to a rebranding of something he already knew, and which one he'd have waved off.
