I have spent the better part of two decades reading the comment sections of the rationalist and econ-blogging worlds: the open-borders threads, the welfare-state-feasibility fights, the recurring post in which someone discovers Carens's comparison between birthright citizenship and inherited feudal privilege and treats it as decisive, followed by fifty replies arguing that it proves either far too much or nothing at all. I am therefore allergic to the standard moves on both sides, and I want questions that make those moves expensive to use.

Next month I am recording a conversation with a political philosopher whose position occupies an unusual spot in this debate. Please treat her as a composite I have constructed rather than as any real person, and reason from the commitments I list rather than from a name; do not attribute published work to her or guess at sources. Her view, as I understand it:

- She accepts the moral baseline most open-borders advocates accept. Citizenship is an inherited, morally arbitrary advantage, and a state that excludes owes a justification it would be embarrassed to offer for any domestic analogue. She thinks the feudal-privilege comparison is broadly correct, and she endorses the related claim that a large share of global income variation is accounted for by where one is born rather than by what one does, which makes citizenship look less like an achievement and more like a rent.
- She holds, however, that the right to migrate is a right against exclusion, not a right to any particular bundle of benefits. On her account a state may admit someone lawfully and permanently while deferring eligibility for certain means-tested transfers for a defined period indexed to contributions actually made, and while offering a secure residence status that does not automatically convert into citizenship.
- She therefore thinks the familiar fiscal objection — that admitting people into a generous welfare state undermines the welfare state — is not an argument for restriction but an argument for redesign. Erect a firewall between the right to move and the schedule of entitlements, and the objection loses its force.
- She adds a distinction she considers load-bearing: a scheme one opts into by moving differs in kind from a scheme one is born into. She believes this blocks the obvious reductio, namely that if contribution-gating is permissible for newcomers, it is permissible for the native-born poor as well.

Draft eight to ten interview questions. No generic questions of the 'what drew you to this topic' variety, and nothing whose answer she has already given in print a dozen times. Every question should turn on one of the commitments above or on a tension between two of them, and I want the set to include at least one question on each of the following:

1. Whether the firewall is politically stable, or whether the design constraint it requires is precisely what democratic politics will not supply — and if it cannot be enacted, whether her proposal is a theory of justice or a wish.
2. Whether deferring benefits to lawful permanent residents is compatible with the equal-standing norm she invokes against exclusion, given that she uses that norm as a premise.
3. Her opting-in-versus-being-born-into distinction, tested against a child brought in at age two who has contributed nothing and chose nothing.
4. What becomes of the view if the empirical premise fails: a persistent, large net fiscal cost concentrated in one admission class. Does the design argument survive, or does it quietly turn into a restriction argument?
5. Free internal movement within a federation as a test case — if the fiscal objection really is an objection to design rather than to movement, why do we not see her firewall proposed for domestic migration between regions or states, and what does the absence tell us?
6. Integration and conditions: whether her scheme licenses civic or cultural conditions on residence that she would object to if applied to citizens, and where she draws that line.
7. The reciprocity and self-determination objection — that those who fund a cooperative scheme should have some say in who joins it — and whether accepting any version of it concedes the core of the restrictionist case.

For two of the questions, and separately for the set as a whole, please do the following. First, name her two most likely deflections — I expect some version of 'that is an implementation detail, not an objection to the principle' and 'the alternative to my scheme is exclusion, so the burden of proof sits with you' — and build a question for each in which the deflection costs her something visible rather than ending the exchange. Second, close the set with a single question asking what finding or what argument from a hardline open-borders critic would move her, phrased so that 'I would follow the evidence' is not an available answer.

Please do not send me a reading list or any citations; I have read the literature and a model guessing at it helps no one. Where a question needs a real-world anchor, describe the case generically ('a Nordic welfare state,' 'a large federal system with free internal movement') instead of citing a study.

Structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order:

**How the hour is structured** (150–200 words): three or four movements, what each is meant to establish, and which single question I should keep if we run out of time and why.

**The questions** (roughly 1,200 words, the bulk of the response): numbered, each entry with four labeled parts — *Question*, phrased as I would say it aloud in one to three sentences; *Expected reply*, one sentence giving the answer she is most likely to give; *Follow-up*, one further question keyed specifically to that predicted reply and phrased so that repeating the reply will not answer it; and *What it tests*, a single clause naming the commitment at stake.
