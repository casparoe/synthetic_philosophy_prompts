I'm writing up a handout for our reading group on truthlikeness and I've argued myself into a corner I can't get out of. Maybe you can find the loose brick, because the conclusion is obviously too strong.

Setup: one countable first-order language, and theories are deductively closed sets of its sentences. For a theory T, Ct(T) is the set of what T entails; CtT(T) is the true part of that and CtF(T) the false part.

(1) What a theory says is exactly what it entails. There's no further ingredient in it.
(2) T2 is closer to the truth than T1 iff CtT(T1) ⊆ CtT(T2) and CtF(T2) ⊆ CtF(T1), with at least one inclusion proper. That's the old Popper clause, and it looks like a fair rendering of "more truth and less falsehood".
(3) Take any consistent false theory T with a false consequence p. For every sentence s of the language, p ∧ s is false and follows from T; there are countably many sentences; so |CtF(T)| = ℵ0.
(4) Every theory entails every validity of the language, and there are countably many of those, so |CtT(T)| = ℵ0 as well.
(5) So for any two false theories, the amount of truth they contain and the amount of falsehood they contain are exactly the same sizes: ℵ0 and ℵ0.
(6) But (2) is supposed to be how we compare amounts of truth and falsehood between theories. If the amounts never differ, (2) can't be tracking amount.
(7) So either (2) isn't about amount at all, and "more truth, less falsehood" is decoration on a bare inclusion test — or it is about amount, in which case every false theory is exactly as close to the truth as every other, and phlogiston does as well as modern chemistry.
(8) Take the first horn. Then this holds (I've checked it; the trick is that the falsity-content condition forces T2 to inherit every false consequence of T1): if T1 and T2 are both false and T2 beats T1 on (2), then Ct(T2) ⊆ Ct(T1).
(9) So on (2) a false theory only gets closer to the truth by saying less. Adding true content to a false theory can never move it nearer the truth, and no false theory can ever beat a true one, since a true theory's falsity content is empty and stays empty however much you add.
(10) Which means "our theories are getting closer to the truth although they're all false" isn't merely unproven on this account, it's excluded. And nothing in (3)–(9) specialises to the particular clause in (2) — it only uses the idea of spelling closeness out in terms of what theories say. So (2) is false, and (1) is false along with it: closeness to the truth isn't a matter of content at all.

I'd much rather keep (1) and (2) then accept (10), because if content is out I get shoved into the similarity-over-possible-worlds accounts, where you have to weight worlds and nobody can tell me how. But (3)–(5) look like plain counting and I cant see the gap.
