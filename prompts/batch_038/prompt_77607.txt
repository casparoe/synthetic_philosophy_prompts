Our department is throwing a retirement dinner for a colleague who spent thirty years arguing against what he calls "the felt-quality picture of pleasure" — his line was always that if you go looking for the one thing every pleasure feels like, you come back empty-handed, and that's a finding, not a failure. The room will be about a third philosophers (a couple of them hedonists who think he was wrong the whole time, and they'll be sitting near the front) and two thirds spouses, adult children, former students who went into other work, and emeriti who retired before any of this was a live debate. I'm giving the toast. Five or six minutes.

Here's my problem: I want the speech to actually make his argument, not just name-drop it. The pluralism has to do real work — it has to be why the thing is a celebration instead of an inside-baseball obituary for a dead position. Concretely I need it to do three things.

One: show, in language a non-philosopher gets on first hearing, why there's no single feel common to all pleasures — and it can't just assert that, it has to make somebody at the table nod along with it.

Two: use two of his own favorite examples, both of which he put in print decades ago and everyone in the department has heard him tell at parties: the pleasure of the first two minutes of a cold shower after a ninety-degree day, and the pleasure of getting the Thursday crossword entry at 3 in the morning. Those two have to carry the weight of the demonstration, because he'd hate a toast that talked about pleasure in general terms.

Three: turn the same move outward onto him. The reason he never wrote the big single-thesis book is that he thought a life, like a category, can be held together by an overlapping list of good things rather than by one thing everything shares — so the speech should end on that, without making it sound like a consolation prize or like I'm patting myself on the back for the analogy.

I also want it to handle the objection rather than step around it, because the hedonists in the front row will be thinking it: isn't "pleasure is whichever of these twenty things you happen to be enjoying" just a list dressed up as a theory? There needs to be a sentence or two in there that answers that at a level the whole room can follow, not a footnote for the specialists.

Title it "In Praise of a Plural Life." One joke is allowed and one joke only, and it has to land for both halves of the room. Warm, a little funny, absolutely no lecture voice — he'd get up and stop me if it turned into a paper.
