I'm in a philosophy of physics reading group and we burned most of a term on substantivalism versus relationism about spacetime, and I came out more confused than I went in, because the debate seems to have quietly evaporated without anyone noticing. Here's what I mean. Once you run the hole argument, the sensible substantivalist stops insisting that diffeomorphically related models describe genuinely different situations — they say those models represent one and the same physical possibility, differing only in how the manifold points are descriptively labelled. But then I can't see what's left that separates them from the relationist: both now agree about which models are empirically distinguishable, both treat the metric field as the whole story about spatiotemporal structure, and neither thinks there are any facts over and above the diffeomorphism-invariant ones. The relationist has a mirror-image problem, since they still have to admit there's a manifold, or at least some collection of points, for the metric field to be defined on, so it's not obvious their view commits them to anything less.

So what kind of disagreement is actually left? My working hypothesis is that it's not empirical, not formal, and maybe not about spacetime at all — it's about ontological ideology, about whether "there exist manifold points" is the sort of claim that can be true or false independently of how you carve up your quantifiers, so that the two sides are running an internal/external dispute in Carnap's sense without owning up to it. Is that right, or is there a substantive residue I'm missing? Haecceitism is the obvious candidate: if sophisticated substantivalism only avoids the hole argument by giving up haecceitistic distinctions, maybe the real question is whether that giving-up is a concession or just a notational convenience.
