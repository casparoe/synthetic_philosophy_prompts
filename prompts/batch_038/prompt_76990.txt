I want an essay that takes a side in a dispute about what George Berkeley's theory of money actually is. The text at issue is The Querist, which Berkeley published in the mid-1730s, after becoming Bishop of Cloyne, as a numbered sequence of questions addressed to the Irish public and organized around a proposal for an Irish national bank issuing paper money. Assume your reader knows the eighteenth-century context but has not read the work, and give them whatever they need.

Berkeley's starting point is anti-mercantilist: money is not itself wealth. Wealth consists in the necessaries and conveniences of life and in the industry that produces them, and money is worth what it is worth only insofar as it stirs that industry up. Consistently with this, he treats money as something like a ticket or counter, a token that records and transfers a claim on the labour of others rather than a thing valuable in itself, and he treats a nation's monetary problem as one of circulation and incentive rather than of the size of its hoard of gold and silver. Ireland, on this picture, is poor not because it lacks bullion but because its people lack a sufficient motive to exert themselves and its economy lacks enough circulating tokens to supply that motive.

Three readings of the resulting account are on the table.

- The institutionalist reading. Money is a creature of law and public authority: its status and value derive from its being the token designated and honoured by a body with standing to designate it. This is why Berkeley's remedy is a bank rather than a mint, and why he thinks paper, properly authorised and secured against the productive capacity of the country, can do the work of silver and do it better. The deep claim is about the social constitution of money.
- The motivational reading. The point of money is that it acts on the passions. Its value is nothing over and above its capacity to command industry, so the bank proposal is in substance an employment scheme: supply enough circulating tickets and people who now have no reason to work will have one. The deep claim is about human nature and the political economy of incentives, with the institutional apparatus merely instrumental.
- The expectation reading. A token commands industry only because people expect it to be accepted by others and honoured by its issuer; its value rests on that mutually sustained expectation, anchored ultimately in the goods and labour the notes can be turned into. On this reading Berkeley is a credit theorist avant la lettre, and the bank's real asset is credibility.

Your verdict should be driven by two tests rather than by which reading sounds more sophisticated.

First, the empty ticket. Suppose a token is legally designated, universally accepted in payment, and impeccably secured, yet commands no additional industry, because the population already works at capacity or because the token circulates only among people with nothing to sell and nothing to buy. On the institutionalist reading it is fully money; on the motivational reading it is money in name only, since it stirs up nothing; on the expectation reading it may retain value so long as the expectation holds, which is awkward, since the expectation is supposed to be of future command over goods and labour. Decide which reading survives the case and at what price. In particular, say whether Berkeley can avoid a circle here: the token has value because it commands industry, and it commands industry because it is taken to have value.

Second, the quantity constraint. If tokens are cheap to make and their job is to set industry in motion, what prevents more tokens from being better without limit? Each reading supplies a different brake: the issuer's discipline and the terms of the backing; motivational saturation, beyond which extra tickets elicit no extra effort; or confidence, which collapses when supply outruns the expectation that notes will be honoured and accepted. Say which brake Berkeley actually has and whether it is principled or ad hoc. If the brake is saturation, does that commit him to a claim about the relation between circulating tokens and the level of employment that he has no resources to state precisely?

Then confront the objection that the whole exercise is misdirected. The Querist is a polemical pamphlet in the form of leading questions, a genre built to move an audience rather than to state a doctrine; sorting it into later categories such as chartalism, credit theory, and incentive design may import a systematicity that is not there, and the honest verdict may be that Berkeley has a target, namely the mercantilist conflation of money with wealth, and a set of instruments, but no theory of money's value at all. Take this seriously. You may adopt it, but only by defending it and showing what it explains that the three readings cannot; you may not use it to escape choosing.

Finally, state what your verdict commits you to about a case Berkeley could not have had in mind: a token that is legal tender, that no authority can or will defend, and that is nonetheless accepted in trade because people expect others to accept it. Is it money on the view you attribute to him, and what does your answer cost you?

Aim for 1,500 to 2,500 words, organized as an argument rather than as a tour of the readings. Reach a clear verdict and defend it, engaging the strongest case for at least one reading you reject. No bibliography, no citations, no query numbers, and no invented attributions to other authors; if you want to refer to particular passages, paraphrase them rather than reproducing wording from memory.

Close with a short, clearly marked section saying what would change your mind. Name the specific consideration, argument, or feature of the text that would move you to a different reading, and identify the step in your own reasoning most likely to be wrong — for example, that you have read a coherent theory into a rhetorical pamphlet, or let a modern monetary vocabulary decide the question for you.
