ok so I sit on the board of a mid-size global health nonprofit (~$14M/yr, nothing famous) and I'm the designated philosophy nerd, which is somehow why I'm the one who has to draft this thing. Board meets in nine days. Here's the case:

Since 2019 we've funded a newborn thermal-care program in one highland district — kangaroo-care training for birth attendants, thermal wraps, and stipends for community health workers who do postnatal home visits in the first week of life. It's about 12% of our annual budget. The district health office has told us in writing that they will not backfill our share of the health-worker stipends if we pull out. Our own model says ending it costs on the order of 90 infant deaths in that district over three years — which is obviously a counterfactual estimate, not something anybody will ever count or see.

Meanwhile a slot is opening in a door-to-door bednet program in another country that our CEA team scores at roughly 2.6x the lives saved per dollar. If we free up the money we can take the slot; the round closes in ten weeks, so "let's revisit in the spring" is functionally a no.

Options as I see them: (a) renew the thermal program for three more years and pass on the nets; (b) withdraw at year end and move the money; (c) an 18-month phase-down with a handover package — a local NGO plus a partial ministry commitment — which our model says cuts the freed-up money by about 40% and the projected deaths by maybe a third.

The board is split along a line everyone keeps gesturing at as the doing vs allowing thing. One half says: not funding the nets is letting people die, but pulling out of the thermal program is an act of removal, especially becuase we started it and the minstry restructured its budget around our share — that's not a mere omission, that's us doing something. The other half says this is textbook omission bias plus identifiable-victims: we have photos and a named midwife in district one, and statistical babies in country two, and the philosophy is just costume jewelry on an asymmetry of feelings. I've been on rationalist forums long enough to feel the bias alarm going off, but I also don't think "it's a bias" automatically settles the question.

There's also a wrinkle nobody's handled cleanly: the ministry's refusal to backfill sits between our decision and the deaths. Does a third party's intervening choice turn our doing into an allowing? And on the other side, if we'd never started the program at all, the same 90 kids die and nobody on this board blinks — so are we being punished for having done good once, and does that create a perverse incentive to never start anything we can't fund forever?

One trustee with actual philosophy training keeps making a narrower point that I think is getting talked past. She says the distinction isn't a claim about the death count, it's a claim about how much justification you need: that taking requires a heavier showing than not-giving, all else equal, and that two unequal numbers don't by themselves establish that the heavier showing has been met. I want the memo to engage that head-on instead of running the arithmetic and declaring a winner.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. It needs a real recommendation — a, b, or c — with a timeline keyed to the six-week ministry budget deadline and the ten-week funding round. It should say which of the considerations pulling on this board you take to be genuinely moral and which you take to be bias in costume, with reasons and not just labels. I also want a reusable decision rule for the next time a legacy program loses to a newer one, since if we never withdraw from anything the portfolio ossifies and if we withdraw every time the CEA flips we become the donor that punishes programs for having worked. Say something explicit about whether the phase-down option is a genuine third way or just responsibility-diffusion so nobody has to be the one who does the thing. And give the strongest case against whatever you recommend, written so the losing half of the board recognizes it as their own view rather than a strawman.
