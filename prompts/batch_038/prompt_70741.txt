I am a fourth-year PhD student in computer science working on fault-tolerant distributed systems — Byzantine agreement, permissioned consensus, and the protocols that govern their own reconfiguration — and I have taken on a side project with a political theory group about the governance of protocol upgrades. My habits of thought are entirely technical: safety and liveness properties, adversary models (crash versus Byzantine), quorum intersection, trusted computing bases, discretionary versus mandatory access control, model checking over all admissible executions, and the difference between a property holding in the trace I actually observed and holding for every schedule. My political theory is thin by comparison: I have read Berlin's Two Concepts of Liberty closely, and I know the republican revival's account of freedom as non-domination only from summaries and one anthology excerpt.

Here is what I want from you. Take the republican claim that freedom is non-domination rather than non-interference and restate it in my vocabulary, as literally as you can manage, commitment by commitment. Do not summarize the political theory for me, and do not lecture me on my own field. Do the translation.

Begin with a fixed point: render Berlinian negative liberty — the absence of interference by others with what an agent might otherwise do — in my terms, so that I have a baseline against which to measure everything else. Then work through the following five republican commitments one at a time:

1. The indulgent-master case: a person whose choices are subject to another's arbitrary will is unfree even if that other never actually interferes, and even if the interference would never, in fact, occur.

2. The arbitrariness condition: what makes power dominating is not that it is exercised, but that it may be exercised without tracking the interests and ideas of the person affected. If it matters to the translation that some writers in this tradition analyze domination as a two-part relation — the affected party's dependency on the power-holder, conjoined with the arbitrariness of that power — fold that in here rather than treating it separately.

3. The contention that properly constituted law can enlarge freedom rather than merely purchase other goods at freedom's expense, provided it is controlled by those subject to it. This is, as I read Berlin, exactly where he would insist that law is a curtailment of negative liberty that something else must justify.

4. The robustness requirement: what one has reason to want is not the absence of interference in the actual course of events but its absence across variations in other people's inclinations and in circumstances nobody foresaw — which is why Pettit can say that non-domination is worth wanting resiliently, and why a guarantee that holds only as long as the powerful stay well disposed is worth nothing.

5. The status dimension: the thought that domination changes how people must present themselves to one another — Pettit's talk of being able to look others in the eye without fear or deference — so that freedom has a standing and a phenomenology that never shows up in any tally of interferences.

For each of the five, give me three things. First, the closest counterpart in my vocabulary, stated precisely enough that I could write it down as a property, an adversary assumption, or a protocol requirement. Second, what you had to bend, idealize, or quietly drop in order to get the fit. Third, whether anything remains with no counterpart at all.

Then I want an honest residue report, organized around three questions.

Does the substantive disagreement between Berlin and the republicans survive the translation, or does it collapse into a purely formal difference in quantification — no interference in this execution versus no interference in any admissible execution? I have a draft intuition that non-domination is just negative liberty evaluated under a Byzantine adversary model rather than a crash-only one, and I want to be told plainly whether that is a faithful compression or a cheat that hides the real content of the view.

My vocabulary is built to be motive-free: a Byzantine node's malice is beside the point, since only its capability set and the protocol's guarantees matter, and we design precisely so that goodwill is never load-bearing. Yet the arbitrariness condition seems to require intentions, reasons, and something like justification to come back in through the door. Tell me whether the translation can be completed without reintroducing motives into a framework that eliminates them by design, and if it cannot, whether that exposes a genuine poverty in the technical framework or shows that the arbitrariness condition is doing less work than its defenders claim.

Where something fails to translate, state explicitly whether you take that to be a deficiency of my vocabulary or a lack of clarity in the original claim. Be especially careful here not to let my framework's own presuppositions — that properties are checkable, that components have no interests of their own, that the model is the system — decide the verdict in advance. If a commitment only looks inexpressible because you have assumed everything of interest must be model-checkable, say so rather than recording it as a finding. Conversely, if some part of the republican position turns out to be nothing more than a demand for stronger quantification, do not dress that up as a deep insight about political freedom.

Roughly 1400 to 1800 words. A table is welcome for the five claims, but the residue report itself should be prose. Please do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I am asking how the translation goes, not what I should read next.
