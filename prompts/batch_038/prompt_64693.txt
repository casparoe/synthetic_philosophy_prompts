I farm about nine hundred acres of corn and beans, my formal education stopped at high school, and for two winters now I have been keeping a list. Some of the entries are decisions I actually faced; others are puzzles posed by my niece, who studies economics two hours away and comes home over the holidays with them, writes the odds on the back of an envelope, and insists I commit to an answer before she tells me what the averages are. She has now informed me that two of my answers cannot both be right if all I am doing is weighing outcomes by their chances, and that a third pair is inconsistent in a subtler way she does not want to explain until I have thought about it. I would rather find out for myself. What follows is the list as it stands, and I want to know whether the column of answers adds up to one principle or whether I am fooling myself.

When the elevator offers me either a flat $20,000 for my corn, or a contract paying $100,000 if the ethanol expansion is approved (they put that at one chance in ten), $20,000 if it is not, and nothing at all if the elevator itself folds before payment (one in a hundred), I take the flat $20,000 — even though my niece says the contract is worth $7,800 more on average. When the same people offer me either an eleven-in-a-hundred chance at that flat $20,000 or a one-in-ten chance at $100,000 with nothing otherwise, I take the one-in-ten at $100,000, and I do not experience that as changing my mind. I will not take a coin flip that costs me $100 if it lands wrong and pays $110 if it lands right, though it is five dollars in my favor on average and though I know perfectly well I would take the identical flip a thousand times running. I will take a one-in-a-hundred chance at $500,000 over a certain $6,000, which is a thousand dollars worse on average, and I would take it again tomorrow. I pay $3,000 to remove a one-in-ten chance of a $20,000 hail loss, which is a thousand dollars more than the odds warrant, and I have never regretted it. A five-in-a-hundred chance of losing the farm outright I will not accept for any sum on the other side, however large; but a five-in-a-hundred chance of losing half my savings I will accept if the upside is doubling them. And the entry that troubles me most is not about money: I refuse to haul a full sprayer tank through town on the shortcut, because of what a one-in-ten-thousand brake failure would do to a school bus, yet I drive to the next county three times a week on the highway and lose no sleep over the risk my own truck runs for strangers.

The decision I actually have to make in the next month is this: a guaranteed custom-farming contract worth $85,000 for the season, or working my own ground, which my agronomist puts at a 40 percent chance of $150,000, a 40 percent chance of $60,000, and a 20 percent chance of $15,000 — in that last case I would have to draw hard on the operating line, but I would not lose the farm. My own rough idea, for what it is worth, is that I am not tracking averages at all: I pay to erase possibilities that would finish me, I pay to keep alive possibilities that would change my life, and in the middle range I mostly just want the average to be respectable. Please tell me whether there is a single principle — one rule that takes a set of chances and payoffs and returns a ranking — that delivers every verdict above; if there is not, say which one verdict I should give up, and tell me plainly whether you recommend the simple principle that overturns a verdict or the elaborate one that saves all of them, and why. Test and repair my own rough idea against the list rather than simply replacing it. State each clause of the resulting principle in ordinary words, and if you need a technical name for anything, define it in one sentence; do not assume I have met the literature. Then give me the principle's verdict on the custom-farming decision, on two further cases of your own choosing that probe it, and on the sprayer-and-highway pair, saying whether that pair falls out of the same principle or needs a separate one. Finally, name the one additional choice, not on my list, that would best discriminate between the candidate principles you have been weighing. Begin your first sentence with your bottom line — the principle, or the verdict you cannot save — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
