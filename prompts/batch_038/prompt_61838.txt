I write literary fiction, no real philosophy training, and I'm three years into a book whose whole engine is one mind at two ages: the narrator at 26, the same narrator at 74, alternating chapters. The old one has opinions about what the young one should believe and the young one has opinions back. Before I let the 74-year-old argue my actual view out loud in the manuscript, I want a consistency check on the view itself, because I genuinely can't tell whether it holds together.

Here's what I hold.

1. Van Fraassen's Reflection principle is right and I want to live by it. To the degree I'm confident about what I'll believe later, I should believe that now. If I'm fairly sure that 74-year-old me will regard this book, and two of the people in it, as a mistake, that counts as a reason to think so today, not as a reason to sit and wait.

2. The only legitimate way for one of my degrees of belief to change is by conditionalizing on evidence I actually get. Everything else — mood, loyalty, fatigue, having simply come to see it differently — is a failure, not a method.

3. And yet I fully expect my future self to be worse then my current one in specific, predictable ways: more vain, more tired, more invested in the stories I've already told about myself and other people. My characters do this on every page. I'd be a fraud to claim an exemption.

4. On personal identity I'm a softie. No deep fact of the matter, just psychological continuity in degrees; whether the 74-year-old is really me is a vague question and I dont lose sleep over it.

5. Diachronic Dutch books scare me. If a package of bets I'd accept now and accept later guarantees I lose money, I take that to reveal something actually defective in my beliefs, not a curiosity about gambling.

6. Forgetting is not irrationality. I've forgotten enormous amounts and none of it was a failing of mine. A leaky memory doesn't make an incoherent mind.

7. And one more, which is more a conviction than a craft claim: I think some belief changes that aren't driven by evidence at all can be epistemically better than sitting still. Grief, love, a deathbed reversal, somebody finally seeing the light. Not just moving — better.

So, audit this. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and how bad is each one — rank them by severity, please don't flatten it into a list of equally sized worries. For every real tension, name the cheapest thing I could weaken or drop and tell me what that costs me. Then tell me which single revision buys the most coherence overall. And if it turns out the view can't be held whole, say whether the 74-year-old can still sound like a person who holds it — I'd rather find out now whether the character is incoherent or I am. Don't explain Reflection back to me and don't send me a reading list. I want the audit, not the seminar.
