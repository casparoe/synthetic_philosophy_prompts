I am preparing the closing reading for a graduate seminar session on bargaining, precommitment, and the strategic logic of binding oneself, and I would like a poem to read aloud in the last ten minutes of the hour. The subject is the commitment race: the situation in which two parties, each able to bind itself publicly to terms for some future interaction, discover that the advantage lies with whoever binds first, and therefore bind sooner and more rigidly than either would have judged wise; each now confronts a counterpart already pledged to incompatible terms, and the only remaining options are to break one's word — forfeiting credibility in every bargain still to come — or to escalate toward an outcome neither of them wanted.

The form is a pantoum of five stanzas, observing the customary closure in which the final stanza returns the third line of the opening quatrain as its second line and the first line of that quatrain as its last. Four stresses to the line; you may use near-rhyme wherever full rhyme would cost you sense. But the repeated lines must be repeated verbatim — not one word altered, not one mark of punctuation moved.

That verbatim repetition is where the argument is to be carried. A commitment differs from a mere threat precisely in that it takes away the committer's own option not to act, and a returning line in a pantoum does something structurally analogous: the identical sentence, said again, no longer describes a possibility but reports a fact. I want at least two of the returning line-pairs to work in that way — the first appearance reading as an offer, a hope, or a description of how matters stand, the later appearance of the same words reading as a threat already issued and no longer retractable. One of these pairs should carry a further distinction, the one I expect my students to have grasped by this point in the term: the difference between committing to an act and committing to a response to the other party's commitment, that second-order pledge which converts a contest of positions into a contest over who will flinch first. And somewhere in the middle stanzas the speaker should discover that the credibility he has been manufacturing — the cultivated impression of a man past the point of calculating consequences — has become true of him, or at any rate indistinguishable from true.

The poem is spoken by one of the two racers, addressing the other directly. It should be recognizable as being about commitment races to anyone who has read in the subject: the premium on moving first, the deliberate burning of retreats, the desirability of appearing irrational, and the manner in which two parties' individual prudence compounds into a jointly worse outcome than either's imprudence would have produced. It must not, however, name a theorist, invoke a named game, equilibrium, or decision-theoretic technical term, or resolve into a moral. Leave the speaker still committed at the end; the return of the opening lines should sound like a door closing rather than like a conclusion drawn.

When the poem is finished, add no more than three sentences identifying the two returning line-pairs on which the argument most depends, and saying what each accomplishes on its second appearance that its first could not.
