I cover behavioral science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I've been doing a run of pieces on nudge-style policy — defaults, opt-outs, form design, the whole apparatus. My editor now wants a first-person column where I say where I actually land, rather than another he-said-she-said roundup. Before I write it I want someone to check whether my position hangs together, because I suspect it doesn't quite, and I don't know where the crack is.

Here's the list, roughly as I'd state it to a friend:

1. There's no such thing as neutral choice architecture. Every form, every default, every ordering of options pushes somewhere, so "just don't nudge" isn't an available position — the only question is which way you're pushing.
2. Preferences aren't discovered, they get assembled on the spot: context, framing, order of presentation, mood, what was salient five minutes ago. I don't think there's a stable latent preference sitting underneath that good policy could simply read off.
3. Given that, the only standard for a nudge that seems defensible to me is endorsement by the person nudged — would they, thinking it over with reasonable attention, say yes, that's what I want done in my name?
4. I'm okay with nudges being invisible. If announcing one would kill its effect, I'd still rather have the effect. I don't accept that there's a general duty to tell people they're being choice-architected.
5. On self-regarding conduct I'm a fairly orthodox Millian: adults get to make bad decisions about their own bodies, money and diets, and paternalism needs a special justification each time it's invoked.
6. I'm for automatic enrollment in retirement saving with an opt-out, and for opt-out organ donation.
7. Boosts — teaching people the actual statistics so they can resist framing for themselves — are lovely in principle but won't scale, and leaning on them is mostly a way of doing nothing while feeling virtuous.
8. The manipulation objection is overrated. Advertisers, employers, supermarkets and app designers have been steering people far more aggressively for a century and nobody proposes a transparency rule for cereal placement.
9. I still describe myself as a libertarian about all of this and I roll my eyes when people say "libertarian paternalism" is a contradiction in terms.

What I want back is an audit, not a lecture. Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do — I expect at least one of my apparent inconsistencies is fine on inspection, and I'd rather you said so than flagged everything at equal volume. Then rank the real tensions by severity, say what would have to give in each case and what that costs me, and finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the most. Please don't just tell me to add caveats or hold them "in tension"; if an item has to be restated or dropped, say which one. I'm not attached to any particular item and I have no idea which one I'd sacrifice, so don't try to guess my favorite — argue it from the structure. And keep it in a form that would survive a smart reader on the internet taking one item and running with it, since that's exactly what will happen to the column.
