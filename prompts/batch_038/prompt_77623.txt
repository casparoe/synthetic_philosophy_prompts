I am a doctoral student in the philosophy of history, preparing both a thesis chapter and a graduate seminar session on the objectivity debate — the line that runs from Ranke's programmatic statement about what the historian is for, through Carr's objections in his Cambridge lectures, to Hayden White's claim that narrative form is imposed rather than found. My German is adequate for following an argument but not reliable when the syntax is archaic, and I have discovered that the sentence I keep citing is doing much more work in my chapter than I can presently justify. I would like help reading it closely.

The passage is from the Vorrede to Ranke's first book, on the Romanic and Germanic peoples from 1494 to 1535 (1824); the title is given in some sources with *Geschichte* and in others with *Geschichten*. The sentence I want to work on is this:

> "Man hat der Historie das Amt, die Vergangenheit zu richten, die Mitwelt zum Nutzen zukünftiger Jahre zu belehren, beigemessen: so hoher Ämter unterwindet sich gegenwärtiger Versuch nicht: er will bloß zeigen, wie es eigentlich gewesen."

I should note that I have encountered this sentence quoted with variants — most often with *Geschichte* in place of *Historie*, and with a different verb of attribution in the first clause. I am treating the version above as the 1824 printing. Whether the variants matter interpretively is one of my questions below.

My own rough rendering, offered so that you can see where I am already in difficulty rather than as anything I am attached to: "One has assigned to history the office of judging the past and instructing the contemporary world for the benefit of future years: this present attempt does not presume to such high offices; it wants merely to show how it actually was."

What I need from you is not a general account of Ranke's reputation — I have that — but answers to the following specific questions, in this order.

1. **The construction with *sich unterwinden*.** Please unpack "so hoher Ämter unterwindet sich gegenwärtiger Versuch nicht." I take the genitive plural to be the object of a reflexive verb meaning to dare or take upon oneself, and I take "gegenwärtiger Versuch" to be a subject standing in for the author. Is that right? What is the register of this construction in the early nineteenth century — is there a modesty convention at work, in which the work is named as an "attempt" so that the refusal of high office reads as deference rather than as a positive thesis? What is lost or gained by translating it as "does not presume" versus "does not take upon itself" versus "makes no claim to"?

2. **The number shift.** The first clause assigns a single *Amt* that comprises two functions — judging the past, and instructing the present for the benefit of the future — and then the refusal is of *Ämter* in the plural. Is the shift merely idiomatic, or does it matter? In particular: if the two functions are refused together and as two offices, is the sentence declining moral judgment and declining didactic utility as separable things, and does the conjunction of those two refusals carry the entire weight of what later readers called Ranke's objectivity?

3. ***Historie* rather than *Geschichte*.** In 1824, does *Historie* point to the discipline or written account rather than to the course of events, in a way that *Geschichte* would blur? If so, to whom or to what is the disclaimer addressed — to a practice, to an institution, to the reading public? And does the circulation of the sentence with *Geschichte* substituted change what the sentence can be used to support in an argument about objectivity?

4. ***eigentlich*, and the missing noun.** This is the crux for my chapter. Two readings are in competition. The first is adverbial and deflationary: *eigentlich* means really, actually, as a matter of fact — the claim is about empirical fidelity, and the sentence is a methodological program. The second treats *eigentlich* as marking what is proper or essential to the events: the claim is about grasping the true character of what happened, and it is compatible with, perhaps even inviting, a metaphysics in which events manifest something deeper. Which reading does the German of this sentence better support, and what in the sentence would decide it? A related difficulty: the clause "wie es eigentlich gewesen" has no noun, only the impersonal *es*. What is the antecedent supposed to be — the course of events, the state of affairs, whatever subject the narrative happens to supply? Does the absence of a subject make the claim more modest or more expansive?

5. **The scope of *bloß*.** Does *bloß* limit the ambition (merely show, and no more) or exclude the two offices just named (show rather than judge or instruct)? Is there an implicit contrast that the sentence leaves unstated, and does that unstated contrast matter for how strongly the sentence commits Ranke to anything?

6. **Disclaimers versus programs.** On your best reading, is this sentence primarily a modest disclaimer, a positive methodological program, or a disclaimer whose rhetorical form is doing the work of a program? If the question cannot be settled from the sentence alone, say so plainly and tell me what kind of surrounding material would settle it. I specifically do not want you to supply further text of the preface from memory or to reconstruct what Ranke says next; if you believe additional context is decisive, describe what it would have to establish rather than quoting it.

Once those questions are addressed, I want the conceptual payoff, stated carefully and without caricature.

First, does Carr's objection bite on this sentence? The objection, in its strongest form, is not that Ranke was careless but that the ideal of showing how it was conceals the historian's selective and interpretive labour and treats facts as though they spoke for themselves. Does that objection get purchase on the deflationary reading of *eigentlich*, on the essentialist reading, on both, or on neither? I suspect the two readings differ in their vulnerability — that one of them makes the sentence nearly immune to Carr and the other hands him exactly what he wants — and I would like to know whether that suspicion survives scrutiny.

Second, does a White-style analysis have a foothold inside the sentence's own wording? I mean specifically the verb *zeigen* combined with "wie es ... gewesen": whether a promise to *show how* something was already commits the historian to a shape, an ordering, and a figuration, such that the disclaimer of the two offices fails to disclaim the third office that the sentence is simultaneously claiming. Set out that argument as strongly as it can be set out, and then tell me honestly whether the sentence resists it.

Third, a question I cannot answer and have not seen handled: is "wie es eigentlich gewesen" an ideal that admits of degrees? Can an account show how it was more or less, so that the standard is regulative and approximable, or does the adverb make it all-or-nothing, so that any interpretive intrusion is not a shortcoming in degree but a failure of kind? Which of these the sentence implies will determine whether the ideal is reformable under pressure from Carr and White or whether it has to be abandoned.

For the deliverable, please give me: your own English translation of the sentence, with explicit notes on the three or four words where the translation is doing interpretive work and where a different choice would tilt the reading; a reasoned adjudication between the two readings of *eigentlich* that states what evidence within the sentence supports each and what remains undecided; and answers to the three conceptual questions above. Please write in prose rather than in fragments, and mark your confidence where the German admits of more than one construal.
