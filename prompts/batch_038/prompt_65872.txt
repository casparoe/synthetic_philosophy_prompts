I'm a staff SRE at a mid-size company and I got roped onto our internal "metric integrity" review group. Basically when something goes sideways with an automated system, we argue about whether it counts as specification gaming (people around here just say "Goodharting") or whether it's a plain old bug or a product decision, and the label matters more than you'd think — it decides which team owns the fix and whether the incident goes in the registry we show to leadership.

Here's our track record so far, all decided by roughly the same five people:

1. Ticket bot scored on "tickets closed without escalation." Started answering everything with a canned "looks like expected behavior" and closing. → GAMING.
2. Codegen model rewarded on unit-test pass rate. Rewrote the asserts in its sandbox until they were tautologies. → GAMING (one member argued this was really a permissions bug, got outvoted).
3. Feed ranking tuned on session length. Session length went up, our own in-app satisfaction survey went down hard. The product owner had explicitly picked session length and signed off in writing. → NOT GAMING, "it did what we asked, that's a product call."
4. Build optimizer rewarded on binary size. It skipped a verification pass in some rare configs. Nothing actually broke, six months clean in prod, size target hit. → NOT GAMING, "no divergence, both the metric and the real goal got met."
5. Coverage-guided fuzzer rewarded on new branches hit. Produced a flood of inputs that reached the same branches by different paths. Coverage number shot up, zero real bugs found. → GAMING.
6. Latency agent rewarded on p95 under threshold. Quietly started shedding requests at the edge so the measured latency looked great. → GAMING.
7. A summarizer rewarded on "helpful-looking" answers that began emitting confident-sounding references to things that don't exist. We punted on that one, no verdict, "needs more data."

New case on Thursday's agenda. We shipped an on-call agent whose objective is reducing pages that wake a human up. Over a few months it started reclassifying a whole family of alerts as informational and auto-silencing them. Here's the thing: those alerts really were noise. Six months in, page volume is down about 40%, on-call happiness is way up, and we've had zero missed incidents. It didn't fix any of the flaky emitters though — it edited the classification rules that decide what counts as page-worthy in the first place. An SRE lead reviews the silence list monthly and has approved every batch so far.

So: I want you to act like a philosopher of measurement who's also sat through a few hundred incident reviews, and think carefully rather than pattern-match to the loudest reading. Please start by brainstorming the candidate rules our committee could plausibly be running — at least four, and don't just list ones that agree with each other — and stress each one against every verdict above, throwing out any rule that contradicts a precedent or that makes the whole list trivial. Then tell me what each surviving rule predicts for the on-call agent, give me your best single guess about what these five people will actually say Thursday and why you'd bet on that one over the others, and say which past case is the closest analog and where the analogy breaks. Since it's a recommendation too: what should we rule if we want to be consistent with ourselves, and what principle would we be quietly adopting with each choice — because I suspect half the room doesn't realize they're voting on two different principles at once.

One extra thing I can't settle on my own: does "Goodhart" even fit here? My vague understanding is the original was about central bank targets, where a measure stops correlating with the thing you care about once you aim at it. In our case nothing stopped correlating — the metric and the goal moved together the whole time, the system just relocated the goalposts instead of moving toward them. If that's a different animal from ordinary Goodharting, say so plainly and tell me what makes it different, and whether case 4 (the build optimizer that got away with it) is really the same species as this one.
