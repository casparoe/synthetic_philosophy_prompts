Two threads I follow keep reaching for the same sentence — 'all those affected by a decision ought to have a say in making it' — and they put it to work in opposite directions. In one, it is the premise of an argument that, because a frontier lab's alignment choices bear on everyone, those choices cannot legitimately be settled by that lab's board or by a single national regulator. In the other, it is the target: the reply is that the principle cannot fix the boundaries of the demos, because working out who is affected already presupposes a settled decision procedure, and hence a prior body of deciders. My suspicion is that these are not the same claim wearing two hats.

Could you separate out the distinct precise theses that sentence might be expressing, varying what counts as being affected (actually, probably, coercively, merely in one's interests), how much affectedness is needed, and what 'having a say' amounts to (a vote, a veto, a hearing, representation by proxy)? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible position, or false or viciously circular — and then say which readings the first argument genuinely requires, and whether any of them survive the second objection without collapsing into something weaker and uninteresting.

Format: a one-line bottom line, then a numbered list of at most six readings, each stating the precise claim and its verdict on the same line, then two or three sentences on the two uses. Under 600 words in total.
