I lead a small team that builds compliance tooling for a payments company, and last year the same feature was rewritten twice in a month: once because a United States appellate court had held a statutory provision unconstitutional, and once because a Canadian counterpart told us a provincial statute was fully in force even though a court had said it infringed a protected right. Trying to understand how both of those could be true sent me into comparative constitutional design, and what I found is that three democracies have adopted three quite different rules for what happens when a court concludes that the legislature has violated a right. In the United States, a court can hold a statute unconstitutional and the statute loses its legal effect; federal judges hold office for life; and because amending the Constitution requires supermajorities nationally and in the states, the practical route to reversing a judicial reading of a right is another judicial reading. In the United Kingdom, courts have no comparable power over Acts of Parliament: they must first strain the statutory language to make it compatible with the Convention rights if that is at all possible, and if it is not, they issue a declaration of incompatibility — the statute stays in force, the court goes on applying it, the litigants gain nothing from the declaration, and whether to fix the law is left to Parliament, with a ministerial fast-track amendment available only if the government chooses to use it. Canada splits the difference in a way I find baffling: courts can invalidate legislation for inconsistency with the Charter, and they test infringements through a four-step justification analysis (sufficiently important objective, rational connection, least impairing means, overall balance of effects), but the same Charter lets a legislature, by ordinary majority vote and in express words, declare that a statute operates notwithstanding the core rights provisions for five years, renewable without limit — while withholding that override power from the rights securing elections, the existence of legislatures, movement between provinces, and official languages. Quebec used the override in 2019 to shield a statute banning religious symbols for certain public employees; a court subsequently stated that the statute infringed freedom of religion and expression, and was able to say so only because the override rendered the finding legally inert.

What I cannot get past is what these rules seem to assume about rights claims themselves. The Canadian constitution declares that any law inconsistent with it is of no force or effect, and then declares that a bare majority of one house can switch that consequence off for the rights it treats as most fundamental, while forbidding the same majority from switching it off for the rights that keep elections and legislatures running. That carve-out looks like the judgment that some rights are preconditions for there being a legitimate democratic decision at all, whereas others are claims that a legitimate democratic decision may override — but I do not see how one document can treat rights both as limits on majority power and as entitlements a majority may waive, unless it is holding two different views of what a right is at the same time. The five-year expiry adds a further wrinkle: it seems to assume that an override's legitimacy decays over time and is refreshed by an election, which sounds less like a claim about whether the right was actually violated than a claim about who must answer for the violation. And the British arrangement has a court rendering a considered judgment that the law violates a right while insisting the judgment changes nothing about the law's validity or anyone's obligations, which reads like a system asserting that rights questions have right answers and that those answers are advisory.

What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit theory in each design rather than a survey of the case law. For each of the three, what has to be true about rights, about legal validity, and about democracy for the rule to make sense: is the practice committed to there being determinate right answers to rights questions that trained reasoning can find, to legal validity coming apart from moral correctness, to disagreement about rights being the kind of thing better reasoning could in principle resolve; does it owe deference to legislatures because they are more likely to get rights right, or because they are the only bodies with standing to settle the question; and does the balancing step assume that one person's liberty and an aggregate public benefit can be weighed on a single scale? Please separate carefully what a practice strictly presupposes from what merely sits comfortably with it, because I suspect several quite different theories of rights would license the British declaration and the Canadian sunset clause alike — so tell me where each design is philosophically underdetermined and which observable feature of the practice, if any, would discriminate between the candidate theories. Then assess whether each design is coherent on its own terms, in particular whether Canada can hold the supremacy rule and the override together, and whether American courts can maintain that they are only applying law while producing precisely the outcome a policy-maker would have chosen. Finally, tell me what would have to change — institutionally, not just rhetorically — if the presupposed view were replaced by its main rival: if we decided that a rights claim is settled by nothing other than the polity's own decision, or the opposite, that it is settled independently of any vote and that getting it wrong is a defect in the law rather than a feature of reasonable disagreement.
