I've been going back and forth on Hofstadter's line about machine translation for a while — the argument in Le Ton beau de Marot, plus the later stuff he wrote about statistical translation, where he basically says a system that spits out a gorgeous rendering without grasping anything is doing a parlor trick. I keep half-agreeing and half-being annoyed, and I cant tell which half is right. So: write me a dialogue between two people who disagree about this, and make them both good.

Characters: a literary translator steeped in Hofstadter, who thinks comprehension is what a translation is *of*, and a computational linguist who thinks "understanding" is just a honorific we hand out to systems that produce the right outputs, so a good neural translator understands its source about as well as anybody does. Equally smart, equally informed. No strawmen, nobody getting flustered and dropping their argument. I want real back-and-forth — each of them responding to what the other actually just said — and at least one concession that costs the person making it something.

Here's what I want them arguing over, because it's a result I got poking at a translation model myself and I don't know how to read it. Setup: a four-line English poem addressed throughout to "you," with nothing in the text saying who "you" is. Target is French, so the translator has to commit to tu or vous on every line and can't hedge.

Three probes:

1. Bare poem, no context. The system outputs tu throughout, perfectly fluent, no hesitation, no footnote. But when I ask afterward whether it could have gone another way, it immediately and correctly says the whole thing could have been vous, lays out exactly which facts would settle it, and notes it had none of those facts. So it can articulate the ambiguity on demand — it just didn't seem to represent it while translating.

2. I prepend one line of context: the poet is writing to his estranged father. Now it outputs vous, as expected. But it also changes two things that have nothing to do with formality — it renders "the kitchen door" in a way that suggests a servant's entrance rather than the family one, and it shifts the tense of the final line. A native-speaker friend read both versions cold and said the second was clearly better, more specific, more human. My problem: I can't tell whether the system took my note as a premise about a situation it was modeling, or whether the note just worked like a style dial that pushed the output toward a cluster of registers it had seen co-occur in training. Both stories predict exactly what I saw.

3. Cross-examination. After it produces the father version, I ask it questions about the scene that anyone who'd understood the poem ought to answer coherently — would the speaker have been let in through the front door, roughly how old is the addressee, has the speaker been in this kitchen before. When my questions reuse words from my context note, it's consistent and sensible. When I paraphrase the same questions in fresh vocabulary, it starts contradicting the translation it just produced.

Have them fight over all three, but especially 2 and 3, since those are the ones that look to me like they could be read either way and I want to know if that's just me being wishy-washy. I'd like at least one moment where they step back and ask whether they're disagreeing about a fact at all, or just about what the word "understand" should be reserved for — and whether that move is a genuine insight or a cop-out. Set it somewhere mundane: they're sitting with printouts of my three runs between them, so they can point at specific lines.

Aim for maybe 1200–1500 words, and let it end without a clean resolution if that's the honest outcome.

Then, out of character, tell me which side you'd actually bet on, and — this is the part I care about most — what would change your mind. Not "more evidence" in the abstract: tell me which of my three results, read differently, would flip you, or what new result would, or which argument in the dialogue is the weakest load-bearing one and where your own reasoning is most likely to break down. Also flag anywhere the dialogue cheated — a line you handed a character that you don't really believe, or a spot where you leaned on a contested reading of Hofstadter to make the other side look stronger than he is.

After all that, give me a JSON object summarizing it, with exactly these fields:

- dispute_over_what: one sentence on what they're really disagreeing about
- probes: array of three objects, one per probe, each with probe_number, hofstadterian_reading, functionalist_reading, decisive ("yes" / "no" / "only_with_extra_assumptions"), and why
- concessions: array of objects with who and what_they_gave_up
- verbal_or_substantive: object with verdict ("mostly_verbal" / "mostly_substantive" / "mixed") and explanation
