I am preparing both a settlement objection and a short talk on the philosophy implicit in small-value class settlements, and I want the implicit theory reconstructed rather than the policy defended. The practice, described concretely:

A payment processor's rounding rule charged thirty-nine cents more at checkout than the price displayed, across roughly twelve million transactions over four years. No individual customer could have sued: the filing fee alone dwarfs the loss, and a court would treat a thirty-nine-cent claim as beneath its notice in any event. The class device exists precisely to bundle those fractions into a single injury of about $4.7 million, and the case settles for a $7 million fund — larger than the arithmetic overcharge, because part of it also buys a prospective change to how the fee is displayed, which nobody has priced. Of the fund, roughly $2.1 million goes to fees and notice administration, leaving about $4.9 million distributable; divided across the class, each member's pro rata share is about forty-one cents. Notice goes out by email because mailing it would cost more than the fund. Members who want their forty-one cents must submit a claim form, and claims rates in settlements of this shape historically run in the low single digits. Whatever remains after the claims deadline is split two ways: a portion to two nonprofit consumer-law clinics that no class member chose and that will compensate no one, and a portion reverting to the defendant.

What I cannot reconcile is that the same legal order is openly aggregative elsewhere. A regulatory agency treats a one-in-a-million reduction in mortality risk spread across ten million people as ten lives saved and will spend on the order of ten million dollars per statistical life to secure it. Regulation sums microrisks without hesitation; adjudication refuses to hear microharms. And within adjudication the practice seems to say two things at once — that the wrong was real and large enough to extract $7 million from the wrongdoer, and that most of that $7 million need never reach anyone who was wronged.

Underneath the procedure is the speck-and-torture structure, in money: can a harm too small to register in one person, multiplied enough times, amount to something that must be answered for? Please address the following.

1. Reconstruct the theory of small harms that this architecture presupposes, as precisely as you can. Does it hold that tiny harms do not count individually but do count collectively; that they count fully and the shortfall is a mere administrative tragedy; or that the operative unit is not the harm but the cost of vindicating a right? Which of these does the practice strictly require, and which could it equally live with?

2. Treat the residue clauses as the diagnostic. If the unclaimed remainder reverts to the defendant, what does that imply about who was wronged — each member severally, or the class as such? If it instead goes to clinics with no connection to the class, what does that imply? Can both commitments sit inside a single settlement document, or does one of them make the other unintelligible?

3. What does the prospective component do to the picture? Part of the remedy changes future conduct rather than compensating past episodes. Does that mean the practice locates the wrong in a pattern rather than in a sum of discrete injuries, and does that sit with the theory you reconstructed in (1)?

4. Are aggregative regulation and de minimis adjudication two inconsistent commitments, or can one view generate both? Test the candidate reconciliations: that the distinction tracks the kind of harm rather than its size; that it tracks who is doing the aggregating; that it tracks nothing moral at all, only the budget of the institution doing the counting. Say which, if any, survives scrutiny — and whether a practice whose stated rationale is explicitly non-moral can still be said to presuppose a moral view.

5. Which rival accounts does the practice rule out? In particular, a strict non-aggregation view on which a harm below some threshold counts for nothing no matter how many suffer it, and the view that aggregation is correct and therefore that every forty-one cents must actually be paid out, whatever the administrative cost. Is either more at home in the system as it already operates?

6. Finally, tell me what would change concretely under each replacement: a system that took the individual claim seriously but denied the aggregate, and one that took the aggregate seriously but denied the individual claim. I want rules — thresholds, standing, notice, residue — not a survey of positions.

Throughout, keep the commitments the practice cannot avoid cleanly separated from the ones it merely happens to fit well with, and say where the procedure is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory would license it.
