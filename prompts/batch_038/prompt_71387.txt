Bit of an odd request, so bear with me — I'm supposed to be delivering a draft in eleven weeks and instead I'm worrying about a prize.

I write novels. Four years ago I also got talked into chairing the jury for a small literary prize — second-book prize, £10,000, around 240 submissions a year, three of us, no administrator, no staff, no lawyers. It used to be a nice low-stakes hobby with a good lunch at the end. Then two years ago we had to make a ruling on a contested entry and we argued for four hours, split 2-1, wrote a paragraph explaining ourselves that made no sense, and the writer we ruled against posted the whole thing online. The board nearly sacked me. Since then I've been keeping a page of notes about what we got wrong, and what I actually want is a procedure.

Here's the thing: the cases we keep getting are not simple plagiarism. Nobody's lifting paragraphs off Wikipedia. What we get is entries where the words on the page are perfectly good and the question is whether the *achievement* belongs to the person who submitted it. In the last few cycles we've had: a book whose author's much more famous spouse had quietly rewritten the last third; a typescript dug out of a box by an academic, written by someone who died decades ago and never published, submitted by the academic who "prepared" it; a book where the entrant was completely upfront in the submission notes that most chapters were drafted by an AI tool and then revised by hand over two years; and a pseudonym that turned out to be a writer we'd already shortlisted six years earlier. We winged all of them.

What I want you to write is a decision procedure the three of us can run on a flagged entry in about twenty minutes. Constraints: one juror is a bookseller and one is a retired teacher, neither has read any philosophy and neither will read anything longer than three pages, so it has to be plain English and printable. We can't compel anything — no manuscript evidence, no metadata, no witnesses. All we can do is put written questions to the entrant and read their answers. The board's solicitor signs off at the end but she will not make the aesthetic call for us.

I want it grounded in whatever the people who think about this for a living have actually worked out. I know roughly the shape of the debate: there's the old puzzle about why a forgery so good that no expert can distinguish it from the original is supposed to be worth less than the original, even though looking at them side by side tells you nothing; and there's the counter-move that this puzzle doesn't apply the same way to novels and music, because there the work just *is* the words or the score, so a flawless copy of a novel isn't a forgery of anything and a forged signature on a print is a totally different category of problem. And there's the idea that a work is an achievement inside a history — that what you're valuing is someone doing something, at a particular time, against the difficulty that was actually available to them, which is why "who made this and how" can matter even when the object hasn't changed. Get whatever part of that is genuinely load-bearing into the procedure, and be explicit about which parts you're deliberately throwing away to make it usable, because I'd rather know that than have it smuggled in.

So, concretely:
- The questions to ask, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what counts as a non-answer.
- Which questions can be settled from the entrant's written reply and which can't be settled at all with the tools we have, and what you do in that second case — do we disqualify, do we lower confidence, do we punt to the board?
- The points where the procedure stops being about facts and becomes a vote about what the prize is *for*, clearly marked as such. We keep pretending those are factual and they never are.
- The failure modes. Where will this confidently give the wrong answer? Which kinds of fake does it catch easily and which does it wave through? Be honest about any place where a step is really a judgement call wearing a checklist's clothes.

Then run it on three cases, showing your working:

1. The spouse. The entrant's husband is a well-known novelist. A mutual acquaintance let slip at a festival that he did the last third "line by line." She says in writing that he read it and made suggestions, as any spouse would. There is no way for us to test this.

2. The box. The academic found an unpublished typescript among the papers of a writer who died in 1971, and says she did light editing — tidying, cutting a repetitive section, no new writing. The book is genuinely superb. Full disclosure: I love it, it's the best thing in this year's pile, and I've already told the bookseller it's going to win.

3. The AI one. Fully disclosed, no deception anywhere. The entrant cut a third of what the tool produced and rewrote essentially all the dialogue. Two jurors want it out on principle, one wants it in because the book is good and nobody was lied to.

At least one of those should come out against me — if your procedure lets all three in, it isn't a procedure, it's a rubber stamp, and I'd rather you told me now.
