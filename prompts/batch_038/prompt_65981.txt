I retired five years ago from a career as a marine sediment chemist, and I have spent much of the retirement reading philosophy of science — principally about underdetermination, and about the ways a favoured hypothesis can be insulated from refutation by adjustments to the assumptions surrounding it. For the past two years I have also volunteered with our county archaeological trust, where I sit on the four-person committee that approves the wording of the public interpretation panels for the sites we excavate or maintain.

The committee contains no professional archaeologists. We work from the written site report, with an occasional telephone call to the trust's archaeological adviser, and we meet for ninety minutes once a month. Panels are printed on weatherproof board, run to sixty or ninety words, and remain in place for a decade or more. We have twice been rebuked in the local press for presenting as settled what a visiting specialist later described as one conjecture among several. The error I most want the procedure to guard against is therefore the confident one; but I do not want panels so thoroughly hedged that they assert nothing worth reading.

What I should like is a procedure — a sequence of gates to be passed in order, terminating in a verdict — that a lay reviewer can run on a draft panel in under forty-five minutes using only the site report, with no recourse to specialist literature. Please satisfy the following requirements:

1. Define the available verdicts precisely (for example: state as fact; present as one reading alongside named rivals; return to the excavator with a specified further test; omit), and say what evidence would license each.

2. Require every claim to be tagged by its rung on Hawkes's ladder of inference — production technique, subsistence, economy, social or political institution, belief and ritual — and state which classes of material evidence can bear which tags. Say what to do when a single draft sentence climbs two rungs at once.

3. Build in a disconfirmation slot: before any claim may be stated as fact, the reviewer must be able to name a specific further find, sample, or analysis that would count against it. Say what follows when no such thing can be named.

4. Distinguish two kinds of underdetermination — rival explanations that further evidence could in principle discriminate, and rivals that are empirically equivalent in the sense that no conceivable find would separate them — and prescribe different panel wording for each.

5. Give the reviewer an actual test for the trap I see most often: a narrative that accommodates every feature of the record being treated as thereby supported.

6. Say when a comparison with a documented society may be admitted as evidence rather than as mere illustration.

7. Be candid about which of your gates are judgements of risk appetite or taste wearing procedural clothes, and what the committee should minutely record when it overrides one.

Then run the procedure on three draft sentences of ours. (a) Twenty-three bronze objects, deliberately bent and broken, recovered from a peat bog: the draft reads "offered to the gods of the wetland," the rivals being a founder's scrap cache concealed against raiders and wealth buried for later recovery. (b) A Neolithic chambered tomb whose axis lies within two degrees of winter solstice sunrise: the draft reads "built to mark the return of the sun," one rival being that the azimuth was selected after the fact from among hundreds of candidates and another that it follows the slope of the ground or the position of a visible landmark. (c) Fired-clay figurines from domestic floor contexts: the draft reads "a mother goddess worshipped in the home." This third case will go badly for us, since the figurine is the image on our leaflet and lends its name to our current fundraising appeal; I want to see exactly what the procedure obliges us to print instead, drafted to our word limit. At least one of the three must return a verdict the committee will resent.

Begin with the procedure itself rather than with an account of the debates in archaeological theory — I have read enough of those to be dangerous and no more. Define each technical term in a subordinate clause at its first appearance. And keep the whole response under one thousand words: I would rather have an instrument my colleagues will actually use than a complete one.
