I am a junior at a public high school, and over the past year I have become closely acquainted with a practice I cannot quite make sense of. Almost every college on our list keeps a record of what our counselors call demonstrated interest: whether an applicant opened the emails from the admissions office, whether she clicked the links inside them, whether she checked in at an information session, attended a virtual tour, requested an interview, or wrote to her regional representative first. Our guidance department feeds the practice by requiring all juniors to attend a set number of these sessions during study hall and sign a sheet, and our counselors coach us to send thank-you notes after interviews and to name two particular courses and one particular professor in our supplemental essays, because, they say, admissions readers can tell which applicants are genuinely enthusiastic. In the same conversations, and in the same guidance documents, we are also told to be entirely authentic, to write only what we actually think, and never to say what we believe the reader wants to hear.

I would like a careful reconstruction of what this practice presupposes about reasons for having an attitude. I have read a little about the distinction between considerations that bear on whether an attitude is fitting — whether this college is in fact admirable, or worth wanting to attend — and considerations that merely make it advantageous to hold the attitude, which seems to be the heart of what philosophers call the problem of the wrong kind of reasons. My questions are these:

1. What must be true of attitudes such as wanting, admiring, and preferring in order for a college to treat an opened email as evidence of one, and for a school to require students to produce that evidence on demand? Which rival pictures of motivation does the practice silently exclude — for instance, one on which desire is not responsive to reasons at all?

2. Does the practice strictly presuppose that such attitudes can be adopted at will in response to a reward, or could it get by with the weaker claim that they can be convincingly imitated, or that behavior is a fallible but useful indicator? Please separate what the practice cannot do without from what merely fits it comfortably, and say where it is underdetermined, so that several theories of reasons would license it.

3. Is there a genuine inconsistency between rewarding demonstrated interest and demanding authenticity, or can a single account, perhaps an evidential one, hold both together? And what would have to be true for the coached enthusiasm, produced in the first place for the sake of admission, to turn into the real thing?

4. Suppose the institution accepted the view that the only genuine reasons to want to attend a college are considerations about that college itself. Would the practice survive? What would have to change — the tracking, the counseling, or what an essay is supposed to be for?

I am writing this up for a proposed independent study, and I would rather have my framing of the problem corrected than confirmed.
