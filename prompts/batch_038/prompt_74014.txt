For a workshop session on the philosophy of death, I am replacing two short papers with a staged dialogue, and I need the script (1,400–1,800 words, two speakers, light stage directions only where the pacing needs them).

The shared setup, which both speakers accept and should not spend time re-litigating: Scheffler's doomsday scenario, in which you are told that you will live out your natural life and that the Earth will then be destroyed shortly after the last currently living person has died — so nobody dies sooner than they otherwise would, and no suffering is added by the catastrophe. Both speakers grant the prediction that most of us would find this devastating, and that our confidence that others will go on living is doing more work in our evaluative lives than our confidence in our own continued existence. Neither may explain the dismay by pointing to the deaths involved; that exit is closed by stipulation.

Speaker A defends the interpretation of this as a claim about value itself: that a great deal of what we care about is cared about under the presupposition that there will be people to carry on, and that losing that presupposition hollows out our reasons rather than merely distressing us.

Speaker B accepts the datum and denies the reading. B's central charge is a dilemma that must be stated precisely and pressed hard: either the claim is a psychological generalization about human beings — contingent, possibly a local quirk of creatures who think of their lives as having a shape, and in any case silent on what is actually bad — or it is a normative thesis about the structure of reasons, in which case the appeal to how we would feel is question-begging and the thesis needs support B has not seen. B should also offer a positive alternative: that our activities are intelligible only inside ongoing practices maintained by other people, so the dismay at doomsday is dismay at the disappearance of a social world that gives our doings their point — no hidden attachment to the species required.

To give them an instrument rather than an exchange of restatements, introduce two variants, both of which stipulate that nobody dies earlier than they otherwise would and no extra suffering occurs. In the Erasure, humanity continues indefinitely, but two centuries after your death every record, archive, language, and memory of your era is lost or becomes permanently unintelligible; later people live full lives, but nothing you did is retrievable by anyone and the practices you joined have been replaced by ones you would not recognize. In the Repudiation, everything about your era survives in meticulous detail and is widely known, but the future regards your values with contempt or clinical pity: what you took to be decent is classified as barbaric, and your work is preserved the way we preserve the artifacts of a cruel culture, as evidence against you.

Have the two speakers rank the Erasure and the Repudiation against doomsday and against each other, and use the ranking to attack one another's explanations. B's prediction should be explicit — that if the loss of an ongoing social world is what matters, the Repudiation ought to be markedly less bad than doomsday and the Erasure nearly as bad — and A must either accept the prediction, reject it with reasons, or split it, saying which half survives and what A then owes. A should land at least one genuinely damaging point on B, and B should extract at least one real concession from A; neither should win by wit or by the other's carelessness.

Please do not end in a courteous draw or a mutual appreciation of the other's subtlety. By the last exchange, one side should have gained real ground, and the script should make it possible for a reader to say why. Then append a note of about 200 words, in your own voice rather than either character's, stating which speaker ended up better placed, identifying the single exchange that decided it, and giving the losing side's strongest unexploited reply. The speakers should not cite anything, name-drop commentators, or refer to the secondary literature; they may refer to Scheffler's scenario as such, since both know it.
