I am in Year 13 at a comprehensive in West Yorkshire, and I am writing my EPQ on the question of whether an individual act can be wrong when it makes no difference to anyone. I have got to the point where the general arguments feel familiar to me — the claim that my driving, my chicken and my ballot are each causally impotent, the reply that this reasoning would exonerate everyone in any large-scale harm, and the suggestion that what I should really be tracking is the probability of making a difference rather than the certainty of it. What I have not been able to settle is whether my own judgement is consistent with any of these positions, so I have been writing down my actual verdicts on cases and I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Please reason from the cases themselves rather than from who has said what; I would rather not be sent a reading list, because I have no way of checking it and it would not help me decide anything.

Here are the verdicts. Not voting in our school council election last term felt to me like a genuine moral failure, not merely an imprudent one, even though the winner beat the runner-up by 214 votes out of roughly 900 cast and I cannot construct any story on which my ballot was decisive. Buying a supermarket chicken I think is wrong, though I accept the objection that the store orders in fixed batches weeks in advance, so my purchase causes no additional bird to be reared or killed. But when I buy a fish that is already dead on the market slab — where the boat has landed what it landed and will go back to sea regardless of what I do — I think that is at least as wrong as the chicken, and possibly worse, which is the first pair I cannot make sense of, since on the causal story I tell myself about chicken the fish should be clearly less objectionable. Buying one of the last two portions on a counter, when I know the shop restocks to match what has sold, feels straightforwardly wrong in a way that an ordinary purchase does not, and I recognise that the only difference is supposed to be one of probability. Donating fifty pounds to an appeal that pays out only if it reaches a target it almost certainly will not reach, I think is a waste of money but not wrong, and I would feel no guilt about it — the second pair I cannot reconcile, since the chance of my matter there is arguably no smaller than in the restocking case. Signing a petition with 900 names, which is even less likely to change anything than a vote, I felt better about doing than I did about voting, and would have felt worse about refusing. Leaving the lights on in an empty classroom seems barely wrong and leaving my charger plugged in overnight not wrong at all, yet I think each pupil who does either is doing something wrong, and I think the one pupil who does it in a school where every other pupil already does it is just as wrong as the rest — a claim that surprises me when I write it down. Relatedly, if a thousand people each raise the suffering of a victim by an imperceptible amount I think each is wrong, but if nine hundred and ninety-nine have already gone as far as they can and the thousandth adds an increment that is now plainly perceptible, I think he is much worse than the others, and not merely by a hair. Someone who flies to Spain twice a year but eats no meat strikes me as doing better than someone who never flies and eats meat every day, and I cannot justify this, since neither makes a difference to anything. And I feel good about using a reusable cup, do not believe it makes a difference, resent being told I do it only to feel good, and cannot say it is anything more than that.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small set of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not thought of, with each clause tied to the particular verdict above that motivates it. Then tell me what it says about the cases I have left out: the end-of-year trip, where the coach is hired whatever happens and there are two spare seats, so that my going costs nothing and adds no emissions, while the train option might see an extra carriage attached if enough of us book — I take the coach seat to be obviously permissible and the train seat to be not obviously permissible, which looks backwards if marginal emissions are what matters; whether "I am only one person" is ever a legitimate excuse, or only ever a true statement; and whether the same principle will require me to vote in the general election once I turn eighteen next year. Finally, say honestly which of my verdicts has to go: I would rather be told that a principle saving all of them would be so baroque as to be suspect, and shown which single judgement is the cheapest to abandon, than be handed a theory that quietly reinterprets everything until the tension disappears. Please also say whether recasting the problem in terms of expected harm resolves it or simply relocates it, and write throughout in British English.
