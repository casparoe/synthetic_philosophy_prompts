I'm TA-ing a first-year seminar this term called "Selves Without Bodies," where the prof pairs readings in medieval philosophy of mind with twentieth-century poetry on the theory that both are doing thought-experiment work — sounds gimmicky but it mostly pays off. This week is Avicenna's flying man. In my own words: suppose a person were created all at once, fully formed and in sound condition, suspended in the air with his sight veiled and his limbs spread so that nothing touches anything and no sensation reaches him at all. Such a person, Avicenna says, would still affirm that his own self exists, while not affirming the existence of his body or of any part of it — which is meant to show that the self he's aware of is not the body. Every student in the section is going to write the Descartes paper about this and the prof has allready banned the Meditations comparison, so I want something to bring in that goes sideways instead.

The poem I want read against it is Elizabeth Bishop's "In the Waiting Room." The nearly-seven-year-old waiting for her Aunt Consuelo at the dentist in Worcester, Massachusetts, reading the February 1918 National Geographic — the volcano spilling over in rivulets of fire, "Long Pig," the women with wire wound round their necks — and then her aunt's short oh! of pain from inside the door, and the shock that it was her own voice in her own mouth, "the sensation of falling off / the round, turning world." The line I keep coming back to is "you are an I, / you are an Elizabeth, / you are one of them." And the poem puts her back down at the end, the War on, night and slush and cold outside, still the fifth of February, 1918.

So: does the poem stage the flying man, or argue against him? I want a reading that takes both seriously — not "Bishop illustrates Avicenna's insight," and not the cop-out that Bishop is doing phenomenology while Avicenna is doing metaphysics so there's nothing to compare. Some things I'd like you to actually wrestle with, in whatever order works: Avicenna's man is supposed to be serene and certain, while the child is terrified and can't tell herself from her aunt — is that a difference in the phenomenon or a difference in what the two texts are for? Avicenna needs the self to be both immediately self-evident and numerically mine and nobody else's, and the poem's panic looks like it's about the second half ("Why should I be my aunt, / or me, or anyone?") — does that make the poem a counterexample, or is it pressing an individuation problem the flying man was never built to solve? And Avicenna gets to the self by deleting every sensation, whereas Bishop's child gets there by way of a sensation, a cry from outside that turns out to be coming out of her own mouth — so which of the two actually needs the body for self-awareness? One more thing: my roommate does cog sci and keeps saying that Sacks's case of the young woman who lost all her proprioception shows the flying man describes a real possibility and not just a good story. A sentence or two on whether that helps or is a category mistake. And then tell me where the reading has to stop working — what the honest critic concedes about the poem, and about Avicenna.

Please start with two or three sentences giving me the thesis of the reading, so I know which way it cuts, and then the argument; hard cap of 800 words. No bibliography and no quotations from scholars — I'll add whatever I need, and I have the poem.
