Been working through the fall-of-Rome stuff on and off — Gibbon, then the more recent revisionist and science-y books — and there's an argument pattern I keep running into that I can't find a name for anywhere.

It goes like this. Somebody proposes a candidate cause for the collapse: lead from the water pipes and from the grape syrup they boiled down in lead pots, or the army filling up with recruited Goths, or the silver in the coinage sliding downhill century after century, or just having too much frontier to hold. Then a critic says: but that was equally true at the height of the empire. Rome ran lead plumbing long before anything wobbled, non-Roman troops had been in Roman pay since the Republic, the denarius had already been cut once under Nero, and the empire had been defending roughly that much border since Hadrian. So the factor can't have caused the fall, since it was there when nothing fell. And the first person answers: fine, but it only started to bite once something else passed a threshhold — population thinned out, the tax base shrank, the buffer of client kingdoms went away — and that threshold is never dated or measured, it just gets set at whatever point makes the story survive.

What I want is the concept for the whole loop, not just the objection. I have a vague feeling there's a general point somewhere about a cause having to be absent when the effect is absent, and I know people distinguish the background situation from whatever set it off, so point me at whatever the closest established ideas are and then tell me how my thing differs from each of them: from just calling the reply an ad hoc rescue, from the ordinary demand that a cause make a difference, from the statistical complaint that a factor present in both outcomes tells you nothing, and from plain moving-the-goalposts.

Two things I especially want pinned down. First, does the loop always fail? The climate arguments get the identical treatment — the whole claim is that the earlier centuries were warmer and wetter and the later ones weren't, so the factor did change, and yet critics still call it a dodge, apparently because you can't run the counterfactual. So where's the line between a legitimate threshold reply and an empty one, and is it about the factor changing, about the threshold being independently located, or about something else? Second, is there anything special about how this works for a slow, multi-century, partly-semantic event like "the fall of Rome," where nobody agrees on when or whether it happened — does the vagueness of the explanandum make the objection weaker, or is that just another escape hatch?

If nothing in the literature actually names this, coin a term, define it, and give me the boundary cases, including an example of a threshold reply that's load-bearing rather than evasive. And I don't want just a label back — I want the carving: what the thing is, what it isn't, where its edges are. American English, please.
