This autumn I begin a two-year appointment on a municipal commission that reviews commemorative place names: the schools, parks, and bridges named for figures whose records we are now obliged to read closely. My own family's correspondence surfaced in one of the first dockets, which I mention only because it has made me acutely aware that the argument doing most of the work in our deliberations is some version of the "man of his time" defence, and that on our commission it is stated badly by both its proponents and its critics.

I also host a modest podcast on public philosophy. Next month's guest is a philosopher-historian who defends a disciplined version of that defence. As I understand her position, it comprises four commitments:

1. It is not a claim about rightness. Chattel slavery was wrong when practiced, and no appeal to context alters that; the defence concerns blameworthiness only.
2. Blameworthiness attaches to epistemic conduct: whether the agent formed and tested their moral beliefs as well as their situation permitted. Ignorance excuses only where the ignorance is not itself culpable.
3. Availability is a matter of knowledge, not of opinion. That contemporaries denounced a practice does not establish that this particular agent could have known better, unless the denunciation was accessible, credible, and cheap enough to take up in the way that moral knowledge requires.
4. The defence is asymmetric and inert. It can mitigate blame; it never blocks praise; and it has no bearing on what institutions should now do with names, monuments, apologies, or reparations.

Please draft ten to twelve interview questions for that conversation, each with one follow-up. The follow-up must be keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and you should state that anticipated answer in a clause before the follow-up — I want to see that you have predicted her move, not hedged with a generic "and what would you say to those who object." Label each question with the commitment it targets, or, where relevant, with the pair of commitments whose interaction it exposes.

At least one question each must cover the following, and I would rather you go deep on these than pad the list:

- Manufactured ignorance. Where the agent's own society discredited or suppressed the testimony of the people being harmed, their ignorance was produced by the injustice it is now invoked to excuse. Does commitment 2 survive contact with that fact, or does it invert?
- The regress, and the emptiness worry. If the failure to dissent is excused because everyone nearby believed as the agent did, what prevents the defence from being available to every agent in every era, and therefore from doing any work at all? Press her for the criterion that makes it selective.
- The asymmetry in commitment 4. Why does the same epistemic standard not convert the contemporary dissenter's correctness into luck rather than virtue — and if it does, what is left of the praise the defence refuses to block?
- A recorded private doubt. Jefferson, in the query on manners in his Notes on the State of Virginia, describes the relation of master and slave as a daily schooling in the most violent passions for the children who witness it, and says that he trembles for his country on the ground that divine justice cannot sleep forever. Ask her what her account says about an agent who forms a suspicion, writes it down, and then declines to pursue it.
- The framework case, of the Churchill type: abundant information, and what is missing is an interpretive framework rather than data. Does commitment 2 grade the agent on a framework nobody around them possessed?
- The structural silence case, of the Columbus type: the harmed parties were positioned so that they could not be heard at all. Commitment 3 then appears to excuse precisely the agents whose conduct created the silencing.
- The point of the exercise, given commitment 4. I sit on a body that must decide something. What would she say to a commissioner who has been persuaded that a given agent is not blameworthy, and is then told this settles nothing about the name on the building?

In addition, include two questions that are not attacks: one asking what finding about an agent would, on her view, remove the excuse entirely, and one asking her to state the strongest argument she knows against her own position.

Constraints on form. Each question must be answerable in roughly ninety seconds of speech. None may request a definition, a summary of her view, or anything about her career or how she came to the topic. None may be phrased so that she can dispose of it by observing that the objection misreads her — unless the question's purpose is precisely to test a commitment against something she has already committed to. Assume she knows every standard objection in the literature, so the questions should operate at the level of specific cases, thresholds, and trade-offs rather than restating familiar charges. Order them so that the conversation escalates from the commitment she can most easily defend to the one that costs her the most, and close on the commissioner's question.

Finally: if I have mis-stated any of her commitments — if, for instance, a careful defender of this view would not accept commitment 3 in the form I have given it — say so plainly at the top, and rewrite the affected questions to target the position she would actually hold. I would rather the questions bite than that they misfire.
