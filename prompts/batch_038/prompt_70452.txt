I'm one of the people who started a volunteer water-testing group in a rural county — thirteen of us, no paid staff, everybody gives maybe three to five hours a week. We take samples from lakes, river access points, and private wells, run them through a cheap field kit plus a mail-in lab, and then post public advisories on our website and a Facebook page that about six thousand locals follow: don't swim here, don't eat the fish from this stretch, boil or don't drink from this well. The county health department tolerates us, mostly because we hand them our raw data, but they've told us twice now that if we post something wrong and it costs a business or scares a town, they stop sharing anything with us. Two of our thirteen are grad students in environmental science, one is a retired state lab tech who is the only person who really understands the assay and its error bars, and the rest of us are a nurse, a couple of teachers, a guy who runs a marina, me (I do IT for a school district), etc. Decisions happen in a Slack channel, often at night, and sometimes they have to happen in a few hours because a bloom or a runoff event doesn't wait.

Here's the thing I keep turning over and can't get anywhere with on my own. Any one of us can post. We have no rule against it, just a norm of saying "I'm putting up the advisory for X" and waiting an hour to see if anyone objects. And I've noticed a pattern over three years: the advisories that actually go up are almost always the ones somebody was most worried about. Like, if six of us look at borderline results, four say "eh, probably fine, let's retest," one says "this could be real," and the one who's most worried is the one who's awake at 10pm and cares enough to write it up and hit post. So the group's public output ends up way more alarmist than any individual member's average judgement, and adding careful, sensible new people makes it *worse*, not better, because it's just more chances for somebody to be the outlier that week. We had two bad ones last summer: a swim advisory at a beach that turned out to be a false positive from a contaminated sample, and a well advisory that got picked up by a radio station and had a whole valley hauling water for ten days. I vaguely remember hearing someone describe this kind of thing — something about the winner's curse, or a "unilateralist's curse," I might be mangling it, and honestly it doesn't matter if I am, I just want to know whether my hunch is even right. Because our fix so far has been the obvious one — we tried requiring a second person to sign off before anything posts — and it made us both slower and, weirdly, more timid: the most cautious member effectively became a veto, and we sat on two advisories I'm now pretty sure we should have posted. So there's a bad direction on both sides and I don't know how to think about where the middle is.

What I want is an actual written procedure my group could adopt and run, not a discussion of the issues. Something like: the questions to ask in order before a public advisory goes out; what counts as a sufficient answer at each step and what makes the answer "not yet"; where the procedure just stops at no; where it hands the decision to a specific person rather than to a vote, and which person that should be given that only one of us really understands the measurements; what happens at 9pm on a Friday when the expert is unreachable and the thing is time-sensitive; and how to stop a unanimity rule from being captured by whoever is most anxious. I need it to be usable by a tired volunteer on their phone in fifteen minutes, with the judgment calls honestly labelled as judgment calls instead of dressed up as arithmetic. Then run it on three cases: a suspected algae bloom at the main swimming beach thirty-six hours before a holiday weekend, where the field test looks bad and the lab confirmation won't come back until Tuesday; a borderline well result that would mean telling about forty households to stop drinking their own water for a month; and a case where I'm the one who found something and I've been pushing the group for six months to post about a specific runoff site — I want the procedure to tell me what to do when the person most motivated to publish is also the person with the most invested in the answer, even if that means it tells me to stand down and I don't like it. Finish with a short list of what this procedure can't handle and will get wrong, so we aren't surprised.
