I help run a methods seminar shared between our philosophy department and a neuroimaging group that works with patients whose responsiveness is uncertain, and this term we spent three weeks on heterophenomenology: the third-person approach on which a subject's reports are recorded as descriptions of a world as the subject describes it, so that the subject is treated as authoritative about how things seem to them while the question whether the things described are really there is deliberately left open. The lab people like it for practical reasons that I think are legitimate. It lets them keep a sincere report on the table as evidence without canonizing it, which matters precisely in the cases they study: the subject who denies seeing anything at all and yet localizes a target above chance, the patient who insists her arm is moving while it lies still on the sheet, the person who reports a dot gliding smoothly across the gap between two positions that were in fact flashed a fraction of a second apart. Our job in the seminar was to ask what the method's neutrality is worth, and one of the graduate students came back with the following argument, which he says shows that it is worth nothing at all, and further that the entire enterprise is impossible.

(1) If a method is neutral about whether X exists, then everything that method delivers is compatible both with X's existing and with X's not existing.
(2) Heterophenomenology is neutral about the existence of what its subjects report: it records reports as descriptions of a world-as-described and takes no stand on whether what is described is really there.
(3) So everything heterophenomenology delivers is compatible both with its subjects being conscious and with their being non-conscious systems producing exactly the same reports.
(4) A method whose deliverances are compatible both with the existence of X and with the non-existence of X yields no evidence that X exists.
(5) So heterophenomenology yields no evidence that any of its subjects is conscious.
(6) Nothing here is peculiar to heterophenomenology; it follows from the method's being third-personal. Any method fit for a science of consciousness must produce data that independent investigators can check, correct, and pool, and all such data are of a public kind: behavior, verbal report, discrimination performance, physiology, functional organization.
(7) Every datum of that kind is compatible with the absence of consciousness in the system producing it.
(8) Nor can the first person fill the gap. What introspection hands a subject as evidence is a state that seems to be an experience, and a state that merely seems to be an experience cannot be told apart, from where the subject stands, from one that is not; whatever could tell them apart would have to be a datum of a different kind, which is by hypothesis unavailable to the subject as well.
(9) Therefore no method yields evidence that anyone is conscious, and not because consciousness is peculiar: the data a science is permitted to admit are simply silent about it.

He presents it as valid, and I have not managed to write down a diagnosis that does not beg the question against one of the steps. The conclusion I cannot live with is not the abstract one but the local one: our group would have to say that nothing anyone has done in that laboratory counts as evidence about consciousness, and that the patient in the next room is, evidentially speaking, on a par with a sufficiently well-built simulation of a patient, which strikes me as both false and corrosive of the reason the lab exists. What I want from you is four things. Identify the step you reject and say precisely what is wrong with it, since it still reads to me as unobjectionable when I take it on its own. Explain why it looked unobjectionable, and what has to be tacitly supplied to make it look that way. Price the alternatives: tell me what a defender pays who instead gives up (2), or (6), or (7), because each of those concessions sounds to me like surrendering something the method cannot do without, and I want to know which surrender is cheapest and why. And tell me whether an argument of this shape would run just as well against any science of entities its data do not entail, and what the fact that it would or would not so run tells us about where this one goes wrong. If I am forced to choose, I would rather keep (4), which reads to me like a truism about evidence, than keep (3); and I am most attached to (7), so please say bluntly if that attachment is the one that has to go. No references and no bibliography, I will add those myself; I want the diagnosis and the accounting.
