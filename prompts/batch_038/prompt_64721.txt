I am rewriting the section of a first-year PhD micro sequence that deals with trimming a normal-form game before solving it, and it has turned into a grab bag of procedures with no stated standard of legitimacy. Before I compare candidates, I want the specification.

Write the desiderata: what any rule for iterated elimination has to deliver in order to count as a legitimate way of deleting strategies from a finite normal-form game — legitimate in the sense that I can defend each deletion to a skeptical student and use the surviving game as a pre-solve device in applied work without distorting its equilibria. For each requirement, give the consideration or counterexample behind it, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and where a small explicit game (hand-checkable payoff matrix) makes the point better than prose, supply one.

Cover at least the following:

- The behavioral assumptions on the players that the rule presupposes. In particular, what is required for a deletion that leans on a mixed strategy dominating a pure one: is it enough that the player's preferences are linear in probabilities, or must she be understood as actually randomizing? Does the deletion remain sound for an agent whose preferences violate expected utility, and if not, what does the rule owe us in that case?
- Whether the reason for a deletion must be available to the player whose strategy is being removed, or whether it may be an outside observer's inference from what the others will do. These two kinds of deletion look identical in a payoff matrix, and I want them kept apart.
- The epistemic premises: how much knowledge of the game and of one another's rationality each successive round requires, and what becomes of a round-three deletion when the players have mutual rather than common knowledge of rationality.
- Order-independence of the surviving set, and what a rule should say if it lacks it.
- Preservation of the solution concept the rule is meant to serve: the scope conditions under which deleting strictly dominated strategies cannot destroy a Nash equilibrium; and where the equivalence between "never a best response to any conjecture" and "strictly dominated by some mixture" holds and where it fails — as a function of the number of players and of whether conjectures about the others are permitted to be correlated.
- The domain of games: what the rule owes us when strategy sets are infinite or payoffs discontinuous, including whether the iteration must be allowed to proceed beyond finitely many rounds.
- Deletion power against tractability, and how a rule that leaves nearly everything standing in any game of realistic size is supposed to justify itself.

Then tell me which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and identify any subset you hold to be jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect that demanding order-independence, demanding the removal of weakly dominated strategies, and demanding that no Nash equilibrium be lost cannot all be had together; I want the argument for that rather than the assertion, and I want to know if you think my suspicion is misplaced.

Close with a scorecard of one line each for five candidates: strict dominance by pure strategies only; strict dominance allowing mixed strategies; weak dominance; deletion of never-best-responses with correlated conjectures; and the same procedure restricted to independent conjectures. The weight should sit on the requirements, not the scorecard.
