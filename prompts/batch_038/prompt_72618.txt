**Against conversion: honour, ratification, and the end of the duel**

There are at least three ways in which a widely accepted practice can cease to be accepted. People may be persuaded that it is wrong; it may cease to serve the interests of those who uphold it; or it may cease to confer the standing it once conferred, so that continuing to do it becomes, if not wicked, then merely undignified. Kwame Anthony Appiah's account of moral revolutions assigns the decisive work in his principal cases — the British duel, Chinese footbinding, the Atlantic slave trade — to the third of these, and his argument is that honour is not a mere decoration laid over moral conviction but an independent engine of change. I accept this. What I want to argue is that "honour" is too coarse a category to do the explanatory work Appiah requires of it, and that once it is refined, the mechanism looks rather less like moral change than Appiah's framing implies.

Consider first why the reasons-based explanation fails for the duel. Arguments against it were available for more than a century before the practice died, from pulpits, from the bench, from essayists and satirists. They demonstrably failed: men who had written or preached against duelling fought duels, and men who thought it wrong went out at dawn anyway, because the alternative was worse. Nor did the practice end because it stopped paying. The duellist was buying a reputation, not an income, and duelling persisted longest precisely where it paid worst — among officers without means and younger sons without expectations.

What changed, I suggest, was not what anyone believed but who was administering the code. Honour of the kind that sustained the duel was never a property of an act. It was a status conferred by a small set of intermediaries: the second, who arranged the meeting and could also decline to arrange it; the regiment, which would or would not receive a man back afterwards; the editor, who reported an encounter as tragedy or as farce; the magistrate and the jury, who could treat a killing at ten paces as anything other than murder. As long as these intermediaries performed their offices, a refusal was ruinous and an acceptance cost nothing. Once they withdrew, the arithmetic inverted, and it inverted without anyone's moral beliefs changing at all.

I want to distinguish, then, between two operations that Appiah's category of honour runs together. In the first, which I shall call *re-pointing*, the code remains in force and is attached to new conduct: the gentleman is now honoured for declining, and the shame of duelling is the shame of a man who cannot govern himself. In the second, which I shall call *withdrawal of ratification*, the code simply ceases to be administered, so that the act falls out of the economy of standing altogether and becomes absurd rather than dishonourable. The distinction matters because the two have different implications for stability and for credit. Re-pointing preserves a moral outlook and extends it; withdrawal of ratification can occur with almost no moral argument and almost no moral conversion, and it is fully reversible, since a code sustained by ratification can be ratified again. I take this to explain something that otherwise looks puzzling: why the duel, unlike slavery, generated no body of testimony from its own victims and produced no converts. Nobody had to be persuaded, because nobody's mind was the site of the change.

This is where I want to bring in Bernard Williams, though only to a limited extent. His resistance to the progressivist reading of shame-cultures as simply less mature versions of guilt-cultures, and his treatment of the relativism of distance, both caution against reading our own outlook back into the past as its natural destination. If the intermediaries are what constitute the honour code, then the code is a social fact about how a particular group confers standing, not a set of propositions capable of being true or false; and a change in such a fact is not a discovery. On my reading this is Williams' general point against treating ethical life as a body of beliefs awaiting correction, and it supplies the philosophical frame for what I take to be the historical mechanism.

My specific requests are these. First, does the re-pointing/withdrawal distinction earn its keep, or is it a redescription of something already familiar — for instance, a claim about who bears the cost of enforcing a norm, or about the difference between a norm that is internalised and one that is merely policed? I should prefer to be told plainly that it collapses into an existing distinction than to be told that it is interesting. Second, is my mechanism claim too strong? I assert that nobody's moral beliefs need change for the practice to die; can you identify a case where the intermediaries went on ratifying and the practice nevertheless ended, or where they withdrew and it survived? Third, have I over-extended Williams, and if the passage in the final paragraph cannot be made to bear that weight, would you cut it or trim it? Fourth, please flag any historical claim in the draft that looks unsafe to you; I am working largely from one secondary source and cannot check the details, and I would rather delete a claim than assert it wrongly.
