A friend and I have just spent the better part of an evening on a question I would rather see settled by construction than by assertion: whether the mechanism Kierkegaard identifies in his 1846 literary review is really about anonymity, or about something else — the conversion of responsible individuals into an abstract "public" whose speech is chatter rather than action. Please build me a case to specification.

Requirements: a single realistic present-day scenario, no longer than one paragraph, set on a professional platform — a departmental forum, a clinical messaging system, a company-wide discussion board — on which every participant posts under their own name with role and affiliation attached, so that each is individually identifiable and answerable in principle. No pseudonyms, no anonymous accounts, no bots, and no absence of moderation. Design the scenario so that Kierkegaard's account predicts a specific pathology — the dissolution of decision into reflection, of a speaker into one more contributor to a collective noise — while the anonymity-based account predicts that pathology will not arise, precisely because anonymity does not.

Then run the checks. State what each account says about your case and why, and which feature of the scenario is doing the work. Report what happens to both verdicts if you reintroduce pseudonymity while holding everything else fixed, and what happens if you instead keep real names but make the audience algorithmically personalized, so that no two participants see the same discussion. Close with the strongest objection available to a defender of the anonymity reading that does not simply deny the mechanism you have built.
