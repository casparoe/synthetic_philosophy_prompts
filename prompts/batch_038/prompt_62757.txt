I'm drafting a chapter for a jurisprudence seminar on the way legal regimes encode contested moral theories, and New Zealand's decriminalization framework has become my test case — less because of the headline politics than because several of its provisions don't seem to sit together. Under the Prostitution Reform Act 2003, selling and buying sex between consenting adults is lawful, brothels may operate openly, and third parties may take a share of a worker's earnings, provided the business is run by someone holding an operator's certificate — a certificate that can be withheld from, or revoked for, people with certain criminal convictions. The Act states its purpose as decriminalizing prostitution "(while not endorsing or morally sanctioning prostitution or its use)" and directs that the resulting framework promote workers' welfare and occupational health and safety; to that end it applies health-and-safety employment legislation to sex work, so that a worker is treated as an employee for those purposes. Yet the same statute creates a free-standing criminal offence: a person must not provide or receive commercial sexual services unless they have taken all reasonable steps to ensure a barrier method is used where penetration or a comparable activity is involved, on pain of a fine. Separately, immigration law attaches a condition to every temporary visa barring the holder from providing — or investing in — commercial sexual services at all, with deportation as the consequence of breach. And the Act affirms that a worker may refuse a client and that entering a contract for services does not imply sexual consent, while leaving ordinary consumer law untouched, so that a client who has paid for services not rendered can still seek a refund.

Three of these are the ones I want pressed on. First, the condom offence: in hardly any other lawful occupation is a workplace-safety duty enforced by criminalizing the manner in which the individual worker performs the job, and here it is — enforced against worker and client alike, running in parallel with a health-and-safety regime that treats the worker as an employee with the ordinary right to refuse unsafe work. Second, the visa condition: the state declares the activity lawful work for its own citizens while treating it as a category of conduct that no non-citizen may perform regardless of what work rights their visa otherwise carries, which reads less like an immigration-control measure than a statement about who may sell sex. Third, the certification of operators, the device by which profiting from another person's sexual labour was made legitimate subject to a moral filter about who may do the profiting. So what theory of the transaction, and of the persons in it, does this regime presuppose? My working hypothesis — which I want tested, not adopted — is that there is no single theory but three in unresolved tension: a labour theory under which sex work is an occupation like any other, a public-health theory under which the state's interest lies in conduct risk rather than in the exchange, and a residual "taint" theory on which the activity remains morally exceptional and is therefore uniquely fenced off from migrants and uniquely policed at the level of the individual act. Say whether that reading survives contact with the provisions, or whether they can be unified under one coherent commitment; and address in particular what the scheme must assume about consent and exploitation. If taking a share of a worker's earnings is lawful and certifiable, the regime seems to assume that a consensual contract formed against a background of economic inequality cannot itself be exploitative — a substantive and highly contestable claim rather than a neutral default. Distinguish what the framework strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, marking the places where it is philosophically underdetermined because several rival views (radical feminist, libertarian, labour-protective, pragmatic harm-reduction) would license the same text, and then tell me what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced.

Be as explicit about confidence as about content: flag which parts of your reconstruction you would stand behind firmly and which are speculative readings of legislative purpose. And please don't accommodate me where I've gone wrong. If my description of the Act or the visa provisions is inaccurate, incomplete, or out of date, or if the inconsistencies I'm pointing at dissolve once the provisions are seen operating together — for instance, if the right-to-refuse provision and the consumer refund route are already reconciled by ordinary contract doctrine about personal-services agreements, so that nothing unusual is being asserted about sexual consent at all — say so plainly rather than building an elaborate diagnosis on top of my mistake. Likewise, if the framing itself is misguided, and a statute of this kind is better read as a political compromise with no theory to recover, I would rather have that argument than a reconstruction manufactured to satisfy the question.
