I want a theory of consciousness good enough to settle a first-personal question: whether my own reports about what it is like to see a saturated red, or to be in pain, amount to knowledge rather than lucky guesswork. The obstacle is the paradox of phenomenal judgment — if experience itself does no causal work, then whatever neural machinery produces my reports would have produced them just the same, experience or none, and their accuracy looks like a standing coincidence.

Write the desiderata such a theory has to meet. Which phenomena must it explain or explain away (the apparent reliability of phenomenal reports; the fact that we find an explanatory gap compelling at all; why we have no comparable reports about digestion or immune function)? Which distinctions must it keep apart (causal influence versus constitutive grounding; reliability versus knowledge; conceivability versus possibility; being disposed to make a report versus having evidence for its content)? Which settled results must it be consistent with (the causal closure of the physical; the documented failures of introspective access — change blindness, inattentional blindness, confabulated reasons for one's own choices)? For each item, give the consideration behind it and say which camp has a stake in demanding it.

Then tell me which of these are non-negotiable and which are one camp's prejudices dressed as data; whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so which, since that would be the interesting result rather than a defect in the list; and which single requirement eliminates the most candidates. Add at least one requirement you think nobody currently states, with the case that motivates it. Close with a brief scorecard for interactionist dualism, epiphenomenalism supplemented by a non-causal acquaintance route from experience to knowledge, reductive functionalism, and the view that there is no phenomenal character for reports to be about.
