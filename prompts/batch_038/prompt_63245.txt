Been going back and forth for like a year on moral worth — not the "was the act right" question, the "does this person get credit for it" question — and I want to know what my own verdicts add up to. Skip the intro material, I've read it: honest shopkeeper, Huck Finn, all that. Mine are mostly downstream of how obsessed my social circle is with self-modification and commitment devices.

1. Alice gives 10% of her income to global health charities. Her actual reason is that she read something about the simulation argument and wants to be the sort of agent a future civilization would bother simulating, and life-saving agents score well. Credit: yes, grudgingly, but real.
2. Bob gives the same 10% because his friend group would think less of him otherwise, and he's very aware of that. Credit: no. Same act, same effects, and I'd still take Bob over a non-donor.
3. Carol spent a month condtioning herself with an aversion app so lying now makes her physically queasy. She's truthful constantly, easily, without ever thinking about honesty. Credit: yes for the acts — but I want to say the real credit goes to earlier-Carol, who, awkwardly, installed the thing because she was sick of the cognitive load of tracking her own lies, not because honesty is good.
4. Dave desperately wants to lie (it would save his job), could lie easily, and doesn't, for no reason except that its wrong. Credit: yes, maximum, this is my paradigm case.
5. Erin looks identical to Carol from outside — always truthful — but only because she signed a contract with a friend who audits her and pays out if she's caught. She'd lie in a heartbeat if the auditing stopped. Credit: no.
6. Frank volunteers at a shelter under a device where failure sends $500 to an org he despises. Credit: no, and it feels like the device is the agent and Frank is the actuator. Though I run these devices myself and think they're great at producing behavior.
7. Grace started a med that made her noticeably more patient with her kids, chosen for exactly that reason. Credit: yes, derivative but real — she moved herself toward the right-making features on purpose.
8. Hal does all the right things under a completely bogus moral theory he made up, something about never taking more than a situation gives you. Credit: yes? I want to say yes, but it makes moral worth look like a coin flip on which nonsense you happened to inherit.
9. Jonas acts rightly out of aesthetic fit — that's simply not what one does — with no moral beliefs involved at all. Credit: yes.
10. A caretaker bot that behaves flawlessly toward its elderly charge and, let's stipulate, has no inner life. Credit: no. I don't know whether that verdict is about the bot or about me.

The one I keep flipping on: Kate, who does the right thing purely because she wants to be a person who does the right thing. Self-image maintenance, no appeal to the features that make the act right. Is Kate one rung above Bob's status-seeking, or is she the whole staircase?

My tentative principle: an act has moral worth iff the agent is moved by at least one of the features that actually make it right, and would still be moved by those features in nearby situations where the social and prudential payoffs had flipped; plus, if the motive was deliberately installed, credit transfers to whoever chose the installation, in virtue of what moved them. Test it. I already know it struggles with Carol vs. Erin — both would lie if they could, one gets credit and one doesn't, and the installation clause seems to rehabilitate Erin just as easily as Carol, which can't be right.

So: give me a small set of candidate principles, each clause tied to the case that's supposed to motivate it; tell me which of my verdicts each one is forced to overturn; and then say which you'd buy if the choice is between something baroque that saves all ten and something clean that dumps one. Also give me its verdicts on three cases I haven't thought of, one of which should be a third party installing a good motive in someone without their knowledge. And tell me whether the whole thing should be about acts or about characters — I suspect I've been sloppily mixing the two, and that some of my flip-flopping is just me switching levels mid-sentence.
