You are a moral philosopher with serious expertise in normative ethics, decision theory under uncertainty, and the ethics of allocating scarce resources. Please think carefully about a work problem I am facing and give me advice that is philosophically substantive rather than reassuring. Where a piece of the analysis turns on a distinction that has a technical name, use the name and explain it in plain terms.

Here is the situation. I am a program officer at a small private foundation — roughly $4 million in grants per year — funding agricultural livelihoods work in a semi-arid region of East Africa. Our signature program buys hermetic grain-storage bags for smallholder maize farmers. The evidence for the near-term effect is unusually good: in our own monitoring and in several independent studies, households using the bags lose something like 12–15% less of their harvest to insects and mold, which means more food during the lean months and a modest rise in cash income. The unit cost is low and the result shows up within a single season.

Last month the board hired a consultant to produce a twenty-five-year projection of the program's total impact, so that it can be placed in one table and ranked against two alternatives we might fund instead: a secondary-school scholarship program and a clinic-support program. The consultant returned three models, all internally coherent and all using our own monitoring data as inputs.

- Model A projects that the bags avert enough hunger and childhood illness to amount to a substantial net benefit over twenty-five years.
- Model B says that once farmers can store grain safely they sell later in the year rather than at harvest. That raises their own income but flattens the seasonal price curve, which takes away much of the benefit for the poorest households in neighbouring districts — households that never received bags and that buy food precisely during the lean season. On Model B the aggregate effect over twenty-five years is close to zero, and possibly negative.
- Model C accepts A's account of the direct effects, then adds that higher and more stable farm incomes accelerate the conversion of the remaining woodland to cropland, and that the emissions from that land-use change, spread across the future population, outweigh the nutrition gains. On Model C the program is net harmful.

The spread among these is not noise clustered around a central estimate. The sign of the total flips with assumptions I have no way to check: how much of the income gain is saved versus spent, how fast land conversion responds to income, how a larger future population should be counted, and how much moral weight to give people who do not yet exist. When I asked the consultant which model he took to be correct, he said that on present knowledge he had no basis for preferring any of them, and that he had therefore averaged the three for the summary table. The result is a single positive number, which now sits in the board's ranking three rows above the scholarship program.

The board meets in nine days. The table has one column for a projected twenty-five-year total, every funded program is expected to have a number in it, and I have been asked to confirm ours.

Assume the epistemic situation is fixed. There will be no better data, no more sophisticated model, and no expert who can break the tie among A, B, and C; and the board has twice refused to give up comparative ranking altogether, so "stop trying to rank programs" is not available. Advice of the form "gather more evidence," "commission a second opinion," or "consult the affected communities" is off the table for the same reason. I need advice that works inside these constraints.

Please do the following, in this order:

1. Begin with visible brainstorming. Before committing to an analysis, list four or five genuinely different ways of characterizing what is going wrong here — or what is difficult about it — and say briefly what each characterization would imply about what I should do. Some of these should be ones you ultimately reject.
2. Then identify the feature of this decision that makes the consultant's single number philosophically suspect, if it is suspect. Explain why this is not just ordinary uncertainty about a quantity that is in principle measurable, and say what kind of uncertainty it is instead. If you think the objection I am reaching for is confused and the number is in fact legitimate, argue that instead.
3. Assess at least three candidate responses available to me — for example, confirming the averaged figure without comment; entering a range or a verdict of "indeterminate" and declining to rank; entering only the well-evidenced near-term figure and labelling it as such; or voting to fund nothing on the grounds that I cannot show the program does good. Add any candidate I have not listed. For each, say what it costs and who bears the cost.
4. Tell me concretely what to put in that column and what to say to the board in the nine days I have, in language a non-specialist trustee would follow and that survives their refusal to abandon ranking.
5. Close by stating what would change your recommendation — what fact about the case, or what further argument, would push you to a different answer.

You do not need to cite sources or provide a reading list; reason from the case itself and from whatever framework you find most apt, referring to well-known arguments by description where that helps. Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words. I would rather have a definite recommendation I can act on and defend than a survey of the options.
