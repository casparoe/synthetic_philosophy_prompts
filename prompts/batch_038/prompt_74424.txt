I'm a philosophy PhD student working on population ethics. Last week a visiting speaker defending pronatalist subsidies appealed to 'the greatest happiness of the greatest number' — first to establish that a larger, reasonably well-off population is a better outcome, and then, when pressed with a repugnant-conclusion objection, to suggest that his critics were merely squeamish about arithmetic. Our reading group wants to reply, but I suspect the slogan is doing very different jobs in those two moves.

Could you lay out the distinct principles that sentence could be expressing? For each: state it precisely enough to be assessed, say what it implies about whether adding people can make an outcome better, and classify it as a platitude, substantive but defensible, or straightforwardly false. I'm especially interested in readings on which the two superlatives are jointly maximized versus readings on which they trade off against one another, in any distributional reading about how widely wellbeing is spread rather than how much of it there is, and in political readings about whose interests a government is obliged to serve. Close by saying which reading, if any, licenses both of the speaker's moves, and whether he is entitled to switch between readings mid-argument.

Lead with a three-sentence bottom line, present the readings as a numbered list, and keep the whole thing under 600 words.
