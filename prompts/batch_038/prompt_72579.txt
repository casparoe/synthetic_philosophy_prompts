I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, though I spent the five years before that on the manufacturing beat, which means I still think in the vocabulary of statistical process control: specification limits versus control limits, common-cause versus special-cause variation, capability indices, corrective-action loops, measurement-system analysis, Deming's funnel experiment, Crosby's four absolutes about conformance, prevention, zero defects, and the price of nonconformance. It is a habit I cannot shake, and a year of reporting on habit-tracking apps, streak counters, and corporate "operating discipline" has made it worse. So when I went back to the source — the scheme Franklin describes in his Autobiography for arriving at moral perfection — I found myself reading it as a quality program, and I now suspect I am either onto something or committing a category error I cannot see from the inside. I would like you to test that suspicion by doing the translation properly. Here is the material, so you are not working from my summary alone: Franklin fixed on thirteen virtues, each with a short precept defining exactly what he meant by it (Temperance: eat not to dullness, drink not to elevation; Order: let all your things have their places and each part of your business have its time; Humility: "Imitate Jesus and Socrates"); he ordered them so that earlier acquisitions would ease later ones, starting with Temperance because it procured the coolness and clearness of head the rest required; he decided to give one virtue a week of strict attention rather than attempt all at once, leaving the others to their ordinary chance; he made a little book with a page per virtue, each page ruled in red ink into seven day-columns crossed by thirteen lines, on which he marked with a black spot "every fault I found upon examination to have been committed respecting that virtue upon that day"; he was, he says, surprised to discover how much fuller of faults he was than he had imagined; and he found that while guarding one fault he was surprised by another, because, in his phrase, "habit took the advantage of inattention." He cycled the thirteen-week course several times a year, was defeated above all by Order, told the anecdote of the man who grinds and grinds at his new axe to get the whole head as bright as the edge and ends up concluding that a speckled axe is best, conceded that he never arrived at the perfection he had been so ambitious of obtaining, and yet judged himself a better and happier man for having attempted it than he would otherwise have been — comparing himself to those who copy engraved models of handwriting, never reach the excellence of the copy, and improve their hands anyway. Elsewhere he describes his own misdeeds with the printer's word for typographical slips that can be corrected in a later edition, and he left the book about the method — the "Art of Virtue" he kept promising — unwritten.

Restate all of that in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can and without borrowing the conclusion you want. Be explicit about the mapping: what plays the role of the specification, and who wrote it; what the critical-to-quality characteristics are and whether thirteen separate characteristics can be tracked on one chart without confounding; what the sampling plan and inspection frequency are (nightly self-examination as a measurement system — and what you would say about its gauge repeatability, given that the inspector is the process and the record is reconstructed from memory each evening); what counts as a nonconformance, what the defect rate is being compared against; what the weekly attention rotation amounts to as a control strategy; what "zero faults" means as a performance standard when the process is a person; what the corrective action is, given that the only lever Franklin identifies is attention and the breaking and acquiring of habits; and what the speckled-axe episode corresponds to when a team discovers its specification was never achievable — a revised spec, a scrapped project, or something else. Translate the closing claim too, the one about being happier for a failed attempt: what, if anything, in cost-of-poor-quality accounting or capability improvement can carry the idea that a program which never reaches spec was still worth running?

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually need. Three buckets, kept separate: what translates cleanly; what translates only with distortion, naming the distortion in each case; and what has no expression at all in the language of control charts and capability. Run the report in both directions — I want to know not only what Franklin's scheme contains that my vocabulary drops, but what my vocabulary contains that his scheme conspicuously lacks (I have in mind measurement-system analysis, the control-versus-specification distinction, and the whole apparatus for deciding whether a deviation is signal or noise). Here is my hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a finding: that from inside quality engineering Franklin's ledger looks like a textbook case of tampering — reacting to every observed deviation as though it were special cause when most of it is the ordinary noise of one temperamental man, which Deming argued makes the process worse, not better — and that his weekly rotation is a self-administered exhortation program with extra steps. Tell me whether that is right, wrong, or right but incomplete, and in particular whether there is something about a first-person, self-authored end with no customer and no tolerance band that specification-and-control language simply cannot hold, versus something about Franklin's project that was confused from the start. Please keep the translation honest rather than importing my framework's assumptions and reporting them as findings, and do not give me a reading list or citations — I want your reasoning, not pointers. Write it as continuous prose in three clearly signposted movements rather than as a bulleted listicle, and end with a single line in exactly this form, choosing one label and adding no more than ten words of justification: Verdict: ILL-POSED | VOCABULARY-BOUND | BOTH | CLEAN — <justification>.
