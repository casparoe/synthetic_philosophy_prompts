I'm a staff engineer at a mid-size infrastructure company, and twice a week I chair a forty-five-minute release review where teams ask to move forward on assumptions nobody has verified yet: that a migration is reversible, that a vendor's published latency numbers hold in our region, that a newly reorganized team can absorb the on-call rotation. The outcome currently depends on who happens to be in the room — one reviewer blocks anything without data, another approves whatever the presenting team sounds confident about. I want a procedure we can run inside those meetings, built on the old Clifford/James dispute over whether it is ever permissible to act on a belief you lack sufficient evidence for, but written for people who have never read either of them.

Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the conditions under which proceeding without evidence is legitimate and the conditions under which it never is, and the exact step where the meeting stops and hands the call to one named person because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. Tell me which distinctions from the two positions have to survive into the checklist and which I'm allowed to drop because a forty-five-minute room of engineers can't use them — including whether James's criteria for a choice that licenses believing without proof can be checked in real time by a non-philosopher, or only asserted after the fact. Note also how the two positions treat a belief held by one team that ends up poisoning everyone else's reasoning, since that's the failure we actually see.

Then run the procedure on three cases: shipping to five percent of traffic before the load-test results land; telling a team we believe their rewrite ships by Q3 because saying otherwise would demoralize them; and my own — committing the org to a storage architecture that three of us favor and nobody has benchmarked. I expect the third to fail, and I'd rather be told so plainly than talked around. Finish with how the procedure gets gamed (teams relabeling decisions as experiments to skip the evidence gate) and what it cannot settle at all.
