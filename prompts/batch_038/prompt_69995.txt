Background, sorry, this is a bit long: I did a philosophy degree, drifted into data work, and six months ago took a job in a city planning department modelling housing need and travel times for the people who actually decide where stuff gets built. The department runs a standing reading group, someone assigned the Vienna Circle, and I volunteered to present on Carnap's politics — which I'd always assumed didnt exist, and which turns out to be a thing, or at least a thing people argue about. Then last week a public meeting on a proposed infill development went sideways: our own analysis showed the plan would displace a few hundred households, one councillor said "well then the data tells us not to do it," somebody from the developer's side said "no, the data tells you nothing about what you ought to do, that's just your values wearing a spreadsheet," and the room split along exactly that line for the better part of an hour. I've been carrying that exchange into the Carnap material all week and I've now built an argument that that I think demolishes the position I wanted to defend, and I can't find the exit.

Here's the reconstruction of Carnap's case for a scientifically planned society, as I'd put it to the group. I know I'm compressing, and I know "planning" meant something different in his context than it does in my job title, so correct me if I've mashed him into something he wasn't — that's part of what I want from you.

(1) For Carnap, value judgements aren't truth-apt. They express the speaker's attitude to life and are meant to move other people's attitudes around; the only truth-apt claims are the empirical ones and the analytic ones. So "society ought to be organised to reduce suffering" is not a claim about the world — it's an expression plus a nudge.
(2) What his philosophy offers is a method, not a doctrine: keep your claims testable in principle, and keep the whole system — beliefs and aims alike — free of contradiction. The method is supposed to be neutral about what you point it at.
(3) So rationality can settle means, and it can catch you wanting mutually incompatible things, but it can never settle ends. Ends are given by attitudes, not established by the method.
(4) Framework choice is the model here. Between two internally coherent frameworks the choice is pragmatic — which one is handier for your purposes — and there's no fact of the matter about which is really right. Nobody's forbidden from picking the other; the whole tolerance point is that logic doesn't legislate.
(5) A planned society is a choice of social framework: a set of institutions plus the collective ends they're supposed to serve.
(6) Therefore, between a plan run on liberal-democratic lines and a plan run on authoritarian lines, if each is internally coherent and efficient at its own ends, the choice between them is again merely pragmatic. Carnapian rationality has no resources to prefer one.
(7) Which means his own conviction — that scientific clarification plus the right institutions yields a freer, more cooperative, less cruel society — has to be just another expression of attitude, with a methodology draped over it for respectability.
(8) So Carnap's rationalism about planning is decisionism with a slide rule: formally it excludes nothing, and the humanism he actually held has no better standing than its opposite.

(8) is the conclusion I can't swallow — I want to defend the position, and I don't believe Carnap was that stupid. My gut says the shakiest link is (5), since a society isn't a linguistic framework and the analogy is doing a lot of unearned work, but a friend who does philosophy of science for a living told me that's just me objecting to a metaphor rather than to an argument, and that the real damage happens somewhere else. He wouldn't say where.
