(b) Explain what the common prior assumption actually commits you to, and why it is not simply the claim that two agents happen to hold the same beliefs.

(d) It has been suggested that models in which agents are unaware of some possible states of the world can deliver cases where two agents commonly know that they disagree, with no failure of rationality anywhere. The student's answer:

(a) Aumann's agreement theorem says that two Bayesian agents who share a common prior, and whose posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, must have the same posterior. The assumptions are: a finite set of states with a probability distribution over it; each agent's information represented by a partition of that set, so that learning the state is drawn tells the agent only which cell it falls in; both agents update by Bayes' rule; the prior is common to both; and the event consisting of "agent 1's posterior is x and agent 2's posterior is y" is common knowledge at some state. Then x = y. The proof idea is that a common-knowledge event has to be a union of cells of the finest common coarsening of the two agents' partitions, and the prior of such a union can be computed by summing over either agent's cells, which forces the two posteriors to come out equal. An immediate corollary is that if two fully rational forecasters state their probability estimates to each other once and still differ afterwards, at least one of them must be less than fully rational, or must be sitting on private information they haven't shared.

(b) The common prior assumption gets misread a lot. It isn't the claim that the two agents currently believe the same things — of course they don't, they've seen different signals. It's the claim that their differing beliefs can be represented as coming out of a single probability distribution over states, conditionalized on each agent's private information. So the CPA is a structural claim about where beliefs come from, not a report about what anyone happens to believe at a moment.

(c) Harsanyi's argument, which Aumann accepts, is that if beliefs are entirely determined by information, then two agents who have processed the same information must arrive at the same beliefs; and since any difference in their beliefs traces back to a difference in their information, you can always imagine a body of prior information that both of them have, at which point their priors have to coincide. Aumann proved this, so the common prior is a requirement of rationality and a model in which two rational agents have genuinely different priors is implicitly modelling one of them as irrational. The objection, from Gul and Morris and others, is that this treats the CPA as a conceptual truth when it's really a substantive assumption that can fail: agents might carve up the state space differently, their belief formation might not be pure information processing, or their experience might not be fully encodable as information about one common set of states. On that view a heterogeneous-prior model isn't incoherent, it's just describing agents whose beliefs aren't fully determined by their information.

(d) In an unawareness model an agent's beliefs are defined only over a subspace of the full state space; he has no opinion at all about the states he can't conceive of. Two such agents can each be perfectly rational, know that the other is, and yet commonly know that they assign different probabilities to some event, because there is no single state space over which a common prior could be defined. What the model gives up is the assumption that there's one objective space of states that both agents' priors are priors over. Common knowledge of rationality is untouched, and so is the requirement that the posteriors be commonly known; it's the common prior that stops being well defined rather than turning out false.
