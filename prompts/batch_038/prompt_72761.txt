A guest at our department's talk last week argued for replacing some city council votes with prediction markets: pick a measurable outcome for the city, open two contracts — what that outcome will be in five years if the council approves the project, and what it'll be if they reject — and take whichever action's contract trades higher. Two grad students I know started arguing about it in the hallway afterwards and I mostly just watched.

(1) Mara said the point is that prices aggregate beliefs about consequences, while votes aggregate preferences and group identities, and since real money is on the line nobody can cheap-talk. (2) Dev said you still have to choose the outcome measure, and that choice is pure politics — futarchy doesn't get rid of the value question, its just relocates it somewhere with even less scrutiny than a council vote gets. (3) Mara said sure, but that's a one-off decision made openly, whereas the markets handle the thousands of downstream choices where things actually go wrong. (4) Dev said the real killer is manipulation: a developer who stands to make fifty million can afford to lose two million nudging the price. (5) Mara said manipulation is self-defeating — push a contract up and you're just donating to whoever takes the other side, so it only pays when the manipulator genuinely has private information. (6) Dev said that assumes a thick, liquid market full of honest traders who think a one-off zoning case is worth researching, which is exactly the situation where none of that holds.

Neither budged and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through it move by move, say who's right at each of the six steps, and flag whatever both of them missed?
