I direct the research function at a mid-sized family foundation, and three years ago we adopted a protocol under which every program officer attaches explicit probabilities to the outcomes they forecast, on the theory that soft words like "likely" conceal disagreements rather than expose them. The protocol has mostly paid for itself, but last week it produced an embarrassment I had not anticipated. Our senior analyst, Ines, submitted her third-year forecast for a pilot we are considering scaling up, listing three outcomes she described as mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive — the pilot is scaled up as proposed; the pilot is renewed but redesigned; the pilot is discontinued — and assigning them 0.50, 0.40, and 0.35. Our decision scientist, Owen, noticed the sum, and pointed out that anyone who knew those numbers could sell her each of the three one-dollar bets a cent below what she had said she would pay, collect $1.22, hand back exactly $1.00 whichever outcome obtained, and leave her 22 cents poorer come what may. What followed was not a discussion of Ines's forecast but an argument between Owen and Marcus, an external evaluator who trained as a philosopher, about whether that book shows anything about her beliefs at all. I chaired the meeting and said very little; I have to run the follow-up session on Thursday, and I would like to walk in knowing who was right about what.

Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Owen: the book is a reductio. Any set of degrees of belief that permits a sure loss of that kind is defective, and the general theorem says that only credences obeying the probability axioms are invulnerable, so Ines's numbers are irrational on their face.

(2) Marcus: the theorem gets its grip only by identifying her degrees of belief with the prices at which she would accept bets, and by assuming those prices simply add up across a package. Ines said explicitly, in the same meeting, that she would take any one of those bets at that price and would refuse all three together. If she evaluates bets against her portfolio rather than one at a time, there is no book to make, and you have shown something about how she buys bets, not about what she believes.

(3) Owen: restate the whole thing in utilities rather than dollars and the objection evaporates. Compare the package with not betting at all: in every state of the world the package leaves her strictly worse off, and since more money is better for her than less, the loss survives any concavity you please. She is left, on her own valuation, holding a strictly dominated option — and a set of individually attractive bets that jointly guarantee a loss is exactly what an incoherent set of valuations looks like.

(4) Marcus: notice what that required. The book is built by someone who already knows her prices and who can put all three bets to her at once. Absent such an adversary she loses nothing, so the defect you have identified belongs to Ines-plus-her-epistemic-environment, not to Ines. If rationality is supposed to depend on the agent alone, your argument has quietly stopped being about rationality and started being about who happens to be standing across the table from her.

(5) Owen: the bookie is only a device for making an inconsistency visible, the way an imagined interlocutor works in a reductio. And in any case we can dispense with him. There is a parallel result: any non-probabilistic set of credences is accuracy-dominated, meaning some alternative set of numbers is closer to the truth in every possible state of the world by any reasonable measure of closeness. That is a defect in her estimates themselves, no adversary required, so the money was never load-bearing.

(6) Marcus: then say so and drop the Dutch book, because the two arguments are not interchangeable. Yours needs a measure of closeness and a rule for comparing credence functions across states, and the theorem only delivers your conclusion for measures chosen so that probabilistic credences come out on top; that is a design decision dressed up as a discovery. The book argument, whatever its problems, needed nothing beyond her own prices. Either the bookie matters, and rationality goes environmental, or he does not, and you have switched to a different argument carrying a fresh set of contestable premises. You cannot collect the benefits of both.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the six, tell me whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and why; where a move depends on a premise the other side never challenged, name that premise and say whether it holds. I would also like to know whether any move answers an objection the other person did not actually make. Then tell me who came out ahead overall, and whether their dispute is substantive or a case of the two of them using "irrational" in different senses — and if the latter, say which sense our protocol should care about only if it falls out of your adjudication, not as a separate piece of advice. Finally, tell me what neither of them put on the table that a careful referee would have wanted raised. I am not after references or a reading list; I want the reasoning, and I want it specific enough that I could have intervened usefully on Thursday.
