For the past three years I have served as one of six lay members of my city's Public Commemoration Advisory Board. I write novels for a living, which I assume is why the council thought it wanted me; what the board actually needs is a method. We meet once a month, we allow forty-five minutes per proposal, and we issue a two-page recommendation that the council may accept or ignore and that the local paper will print either way. The six of us are a retired history teacher, a solicitor, two councillors, a nurse, and me. None of us has any training in ethics, and in two years we have decided far more by taste than by argument: the same facts produce removal at one meeting and retention at the next, and we have begun to be accused of it in public.

Our chair has asked for a volunteer to draft a procedure we can actually run. I have volunteered, and I want your help writing it. Please take the part of a philosopher who works on public memory and civic honour and who has, ideally, sat through hearings of this kind. Think the problem through before you start drafting — I would rather read five paragraphs of you working out which distinctions genuinely matter and which are merely elegant than receive a checklist I cannot defend in front of a hostile room. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want the reasoning, in your own terms.

The framework must handle three classes of proposal: removing or relocating an existing monument; renaming a street, square, or public building; and commissioning a new monument. It should give me questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure returns a no, and the points at which it must stop and hand the matter back to the council because what remains is a question of values or of politics rather than of fact.

Certain distinctions I have picked up in my reading and in arguments with colleagues need to be either built in or explicitly discarded, with your reason. Among them: the difference between honouring someone, commemorating them, and merely recording that they existed; the difference between what the builders of a monument asserted when they erected it and what the city asserts by keeping it standing, which need not be the same claim; whether honour attaches to a whole life or to a particular deed, so that a monument could in principle honour a deed done by someone we now regard as contemptible; whether a street name still refers to the person it was named for after a few generations, or has passed over into being a mere address, in which case the objection to it may evaporate; and whether the content of a monument is fixed by the intentions of those who raised it or by what present-day viewers reasonably take it to be saying. Tell me which of these a volunteer board can apply in forty-five minutes and which must be dropped or collapsed into something cruder, and be honest about what the simplification costs.

I also want the procedure to confront the two objections we hear most: that removal amounts to erasing history, and that a name is not an endorsement of everything the namesake did. Neither should be allowed to settle a case merely by being repeated at us; show me where in the procedure each is answered, or where each has genuine force.

Then run the procedure on three cases. Treat my descriptions as the whole of the facts, and do not supplement them with anything you believe you know about real people or real cities.

One. A bronze statue on the courthouse lawn, erected by public subscription, of a physician who served as city health commissioner, is credited with containing a typhoid outbreak, administered the city's segregated wards, wrote in favour of restricting the franchise, and chaired a committee that recommended compulsory sterilisation. A petition for removal carries four thousand signatures; the medical society opposes removal.

Two. A short residential street named not for a person but for the riverside estate that occupied the land until the 1930s, an estate worked by enslaved people and, before them, by indentured labourers. Residents, most of whom had no idea of the estate's history, oppose renaming on grounds of cost, confusion, and attachment.

Three — and I warn you that I have a stake in this one, which is precisely why I want it run. A proposal I have championed myself: naming a new square after a bookseller and pamphleteer who ran a lending library for factory workers for forty years and was much loved here. Since the proposal was filed, letters have surfaced showing that she informed on two of her own employees to the police during a period of political repression. If the procedure you build condemns her, I want it to say so plainly rather than find a way around it.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do — the kinds of case it will get wrong, or settle only by smuggling in a judgment it does not declare. Then state how confident you are in the framework's main claims, separating the steps you consider close to settled from those you consider genuinely contestable, and single out the one point you are least sure of: the step where you suspect a better procedure would do something other than what yours does. I would much rather have that flagged than have the whole thing presented as airtight, since six volunteers are going to be relying on it in public.
