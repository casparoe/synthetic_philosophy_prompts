I have forty-five minutes with the eleven members of a county grant-review panel — public-health people, two accountants, no philosophy — and the chair wants them to understand why the panel's new written decision procedure matters. Here is the history I will be working from. Two years ago the panel voted separately on whether a proposal met each of three funding criteria and then separately on whether to recommend it: every criterion passed by majority, the conjunction failed, and the recommendation was rejected, though a straight vote on the recommendation alone would have carried it. Over the following year the same proposal came back three times; no member revised a single position on any criterion, yet the panel's decision flipped twice, depending on the order in which questions were put and on which of them were treated as premises at all.

Plan the explanation rather than giving it to me. Which pieces must be in place before those reversals stop looking like mere sloppiness, in what order, and what depends on what; the one worked example to carry the whole thing — mine, or a better one — and what it must contain and must contain no trace of; the readings the members will reach for ('that's just a voting cycle,' 'so always decide the criteria first,' 'the ballot was simply mis-designed'), and the precise point in the sequence at which to head each off; what I should announce as out of scope at this level and why; and one question I can put at the end whose answer would show they have grasped that choosing a procedure is choosing what the group is responsive to, not repairing a bookkeeping error.
