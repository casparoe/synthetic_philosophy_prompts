I spent thirty-five years in a calibration lab — pressure and temperature transducers, mostly national-standards work — and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy of mind the way other people fall into birdwatching. For the last year I've been going to a Tuesday group that meets in the back room of our town library, about a dozen of us, and in six weeks they've handed me forty minutes plus questions to present something I've been chewing on. What I want from you is a prep sheet for the question period. Not a defense of the view, and please no reading list or bibliography — I've read what I've read, half that room hasn't read anything at all, and I want to know what they'll say, not what I should have cited.

The view, roughly. I think the "is introspection reliable?" debate is malformed until you say what quantity is being measured, at what resolution, and what the instrument does when it has no reading. Introspective reports mostly aren't observations, they're reconstructions: the mind generates a plausible account of itself using the same causal theories it would use to explain somebody else, and fills gaps with whatever fits. Three things push me there. Nisbett and Wilson's old review, where people give confident reasons for judgments whose actual determinants were shown to be irrelevant. The choice-blindness work in Science, Johansson and colleagues, where people explain at length a preference for a photograph they never picked. And Schwitzgebel's point that even careful, reflective people disagree wildly about basic features of their own ongoing experience — peripheral vision, the hum of the refrigerator — so introspective care doesn't converge the way looking again does with a voltmeter. My own bit, the part I actually want defended, is that introspection has never had an independent calibration target: any second measure of a mental state is either behavioral, which is a different quantity, or itself introspective, which is the same instrument again. So calling a report "unreliable" often smuggles in the assumption there was a definite value at the time of the report to be wrong about. I've been half-jokingly calling it a metrology of first-person report, and I'd like to propose that the tractable question is repeatability: which reports survive re-measurement, a distractor task, and perturbation, and which ones dissolve.

Now the room. Margaret, retired clinical psychologist, thirty years in practice, will say that you can absolutely tell in the session when a client is avoiding something and that I'm explaining away her whole career's competence. Tom meditates an hour a day and has for a decade, and will insist that trained attention is a real instrument and that I've never bothered to use it. Two of them — a former literature teacher and a guy who's read Heidegger twice — have been pushing phenomenology at me all year and will say first-person report isn't a measurement at all, that I'm turning lived experience into data, which they consider a category crime. There's a young woman doing a part-time masters in cognitive science who will want effect sizes, and who will happily point out that demand characteristics explain half of what I'm citing. And Dave, who is worried on principle that anything like my view is one step from saying nobody's actually conscious. Order the objections by how much damage they'd do to the talk, phrase each one the way that person would actually phrase it — including the ones where they're being unfair, because that's the point — and give me a two-minute reply for each with an honest note about where the reply is thin. Tell me what I should concede in the first five minutes so it doesn't come back at me at minute thirty-eight. And I already know the question I'm dreading: if introspective reports are reconstructions, then my sense of what I was doing when I reasoned my way to this conclusion is a reconstruction too, and the whole thing saws off the branch its sitting on. I want that one prepped properly, plus whatever second-dreaded question you think I haven't noticed yet.
