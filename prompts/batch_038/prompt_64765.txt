I am a graduate student working in philosophy of mind, and in a reading group last week I suggested that pain asymbolia puts pressure on a claim most of us treat as settled — namely, that what makes pain bad is how it feels. My description of the condition was the standard one: patients with lesions in and around the insular cortex keep the sensory-discriminative side of nociception, so they can localize a pinprick, characterize its quality, and grade its intensity, yet they do not withdraw, show little of the expected autonomic distress, sometimes react with indifference or even amusement, and typically say that the pain is present but does not bother them; a number are reported as never acquiring the usual avoidance responses to stimuli that go on injuring them. Comparable reports come from patients after cingulotomy or leucotomy, who say that the pain is still there but that they no longer mind it. My suggestion was that if a subject can have the felt sensory character of pain while nothing thereby goes badly for her, then the badness of pain cannot be exhausted by, or grounded in, that sensory character.

The leader of the group disposed of this in a single sentence: pain is intrinsically bad, so the asymbolia case does not touch it. Pressed, he elaborated only that asymbolics still feel pain, that pain is bad in itself, and that the phenomenon therefore shows something at most about motivation rather than about value. I was left unpersuaded but unable to say precisely why, and I now suspect the slogan was doing at least three distinct jobs in that exchange.

Could you lay out the separate claims that this slogan can be used to make, as a menu I can take back to the group, distinguishing the platitudes from the substantive and defensible theses and from those that are probably false? I would like the readings separated at least along these axes, plus any others you think matter:

- whether the word *intrinsically* is doing the work of *not merely as a means* — badness as an end, as opposed to instrumental badness — or the stronger claim that the badness is a function of the pain's own intrinsic nature, such that any exact phenomenal duplicate of a pain is exactly as bad;
- whether the claim is that every pain is bad, that every pain is bad *for its subject*, or that pain as such is bad impersonally, and whether these come apart;
- whether the badness asserted is pro tanto or all-things-considered, and how that interacts with the familiar observation that a particular pain can be worth having;
- whether the badness is meant to attach to the sensory-discriminative dimension of pain, to the affective dimension, or to pain as an undifferentiated state.

For each reading, say whether it is a platitude nobody in the room would deny, a contested thesis with live arguments on both sides, or one that the asymbolia evidence counts against — bearing in mind that the literature includes both the position that these patients do not feel genuine pain at all and the position that they feel pain that is unpleasant but very much less so. Then tell me which reading, if any, actually licenses the dismissal I received, and whether it would survive being stated explicitly in those terms. Finally, indicate which reading people who repeat the slogan in ordinary philosophical conversation most often have in mind, and whether it is the unmarked switching between readings that makes the sentence feel like a conclusive reply.
