Here's the draft in condensed form. §1–2 I'll just summarize, §3–5 are close to final.

**§1–2 (summary).** Standard setup. An agent with a strict cycle A ≻ B ≻ C ≻ A starts out holding C. A dealer offers a sequence of swaps, each costing her one cent, and the cents are stipulated small enough not to disturb her ranking of the items. She takes B for C (B ≻ C), then A for B (A ≻ B), then C for A (C ≻ A), and is back where she started, three cents poorer; the dealer can run it again indefinitely. Since nothing but a requirement of rationality could rule this out, the pump is meant to show that intransitive preferences are irrational. §2 walks through the two familiar escapes — the resolute agent who forms a plan and sticks to it, and the sophisticated agent who looks ahead by backward induction and refuses the first swap because she can see where it leads — plus the standard cost of each: resolution looks like willpower bolted on to dodge a logical consequence, and sophistication can, in some trees, walk the agent into a terminal outcome she'd have paid to avoid.

**§3.** My complaint is that this dialectic is set up in the wrong terms. Both escapes get described as repairs to a defective agent: her ordering is bad, so she needs a supplementary disposition to compensate. But look at what they have in common. Resolution supplies an object — the plan — that spans the whole sequence, and the agent's choices are assessed by reference to it. Sophistication supplies a procedure, backward induction, that evaluates the tree as a whole from the terminal nodes up. In both cases the agent acquires a standpoint from which the entire sequence is one thing she can have a view about. That's exactly what the pump's punchline requires in order to be a punchline: "back where she started, three cents poorer" is only a damning description of her situation if her situation is the sort of thing that can be assessed in the aggregate. Call this hidden requirement the "aggregate standpoint premise."

So the resolute and sophisticated agents are not two rival fixes to a bad ordering. They are two different ways of *granting* the pump's premise. And the agent who grants neither — who has preferences at nodes and simply no view about the sequence taken as a whole — is not thereby caught out. The pump shows that she lacks an aggregate standpoint. That's a real cost, but it isn't a demonstration of irrationality unless rationality already requires such a standpoint, and that requirement is precisely what's in dispute. Pumpability, on this reading, is a three-place relation: an ordering, a disposition, and a way of carving the episode into nodes at which the ordering gets applied.

To make that concrete rather than hand-wavy: same cycle, same dealer, but now suppose the agent's standing disposition is to accept a swap unless it would be her third accepted swap within the present episode. She takes B for C, takes A for B, declines C for A, and goes home holding A with two cents gone. No pump. Nothing heroic, no plan, no lookahead — just a budget rule keyed to a feature of the path.

**§4.** The objection I anticipate: she does in fact have a view about the whole sequence, because she'd rather not be poorer; if she cares about her money at all, the aggregate description engages her. The draft's reply is that this concedes the point it's meant to block. Caring about (final item, cents remaining) is to care about a fine-grained description of outcomes, and once you've granted that, her real ordering is over the fine-grained descriptions and the live question becomes whether *that* ordering is transitive. So the debate relocates rather than resolves: it turns into a dispute about which description of the outcomes is the right one for rational assessment, and there is no description-neutral way to settle it. Hence the pump can't be a knockdown argument. It's pressure in a negotiation about how finely to describe outcomes, not a proof.

**§5 (where I'm stuck).** §4 makes the view look dangerously permissive: any agent escapes any pump by redescribing. I tried to block this with an admissibility constraint — a feature of an outcome may figure in rational assessment only if the agent can detect it at the node where she chooses. Path features pass, since she remembers her own trades. But then everything passes, because memory makes every path feature detectable, and the constraint does no work at all. Tightening it to features detectable other than by memory excludes path, but that just legislates the node-by-node view I was trying to argue against rather than assume. So I currently have either an empty constraint or a question-begging one, and I don't see the third option.
