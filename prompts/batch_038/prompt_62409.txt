Everything below concerns finite two-player games; "dominated" always means strictly dominated, possibly by a mixed strategy of the same player, and "optimal" means maximin-optimal.

For a reading group on solution concepts I have written up an argument to the effect that dominance reasoning is the whole of the apparatus. The conclusion is far stronger than anything I actually believe, but I cannot locate the bad step.

(1) In a two-player game, a strategy is dominated if and only if it fails to be a best reply to every mixed strategy of the opponent.

(2) So iterated deletion of dominated strategies removes exactly the strategies a rational player could never play, whatever they believe about the opponent; the order of deletion makes no difference; and what survives is the set of rationalizable strategies.

(3) The deletion is invisible to equilibrium analysis: no dominated strategy carries positive weight in any mixed equilibrium, and a profile that is an equilibrium of the reduced game is an equilibrium of the original.

(4) Specialize to zero-sum. By the minimax theorem the equilibrium pairs form a rectangle — one player's optimal strategies crossed with the other's — and every equilibrium pays the same value.

(5) Each player's optimal strategies are among their rationalizable ones, since a dominated strategy cannot be optimal: whatever dominates it guarantees strictly more against every reply.

(6) The converse inclusion holds too. Suppose one of your strategies is not optimal. Then it fails to hold me to the value, so I have some reply that does better against it than the value allows; so it can be beaten; and a strategy that can be beaten is dominated, which means (2) has already deleted it. Once (2) has run to exhaustion, then, everything still standing is optimal for both players.

(7) Therefore, in any two-player zero-sum game, the rationalizable strategies are the optimal strategies are the equilibrium strategies, and iterating dominance hands you the entire equilibrium set with no fixed point anywhere in sight.

My test case, row player maximizing, entries the row player's payoffs:

          L     R
    U     3     0
    M     0     3
    D     2     2

Neither player has a dominated strategy here, so (2) deletes nothing and (7) makes U, M and D all equilibrium strategies for the row player — yet I cannot construct any equilibrium that gives U positive weight. I would much rather surrender (7) than (1) or (4). Tell me which step fails, why it survived my scrutiny, what would have to be true for it to be true, and what the account loses without it. Then: if I retreat to the claim that iterating dominance recovers the equilibrium set exactly in those zero-sum games where it collapses the game to a single profile, do I have a theorem, and how much of zero-sum game theory does it leave outside?
