Our philosophy-of-biology reading group has gotten weird — it's about half phil-bio PhDs and half people from a comp-neuro/ML lab down the hall, and every meeting somebody says "the function of X is Y" and the room splits four ways without anyone noticing they were using the word differently. I want to build a sorting instrument for them. Not an essay, not a lit review: a handout people spend twenty minutes on and at the end find out which account of biological function they're actually committed to, based on what they say about cases rather than on which label they like.

What I need:

1. Nine or ten cases, three or four sentences of setup each, three or four answer options per case. The options have to be verdicts about the case — is the ascription true, false, true only relative to some interest, not the kind of thing that's true or false, that sort of move — and never labels. Nobody should be able to short-circuit it by thinking "oh, that's the selected-effects answer." Where two options would look identical to someone who hasn't thought about it, put a parenthetical saying so, so I can tell which cases are actually doing work.

2. A scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. Leaves I want in there: selected-effects / etiological; causal-role (the Cummins-style one where a function is just what the part currently contributes to the containing system); a Boorse-ish dispositional or goal-contribution leaf; an organizational / systemic-teleology leaf where a self-maintaining system generates genuine norms with no selection history at all; proper function with the derived version for artifacts included; a pluralist or pragmatist leaf where "what's the function" is ill-posed until you fix an explanatory interest; and an error-theory / deflationist leaf for whoever says none of these sentences are literally true.

3. The bit I care about most: do the logical space first, before you place any names. Give me the branch questions and the combinations of answers they admit, then locate the positions in that space, then tell me which cells come out empty and why each one is empty — incoherent, just unattractive, or nobody's bothered yet. The axes that matter for my group specifically are (a) history versus current causal contribution, (b) whether the shaping process has to be lineage-level selection or whether something like gradient descent against a reward signal counts as the right kind of history, (c) monism versus interest-relativity, and (d) whether malfunction is definable at all. Build the tree off those, not off a list of authors.

Cases to use, reword or extend as you like:

- the lens protein one. a protein that in most tissues works as a metabolic enzyme but in the eye lens is stockpiled at enormous concentration purely for its optical properties, and it still catalyzes the reaction there. which ascription is right, and is that one function or two?
- a gene that duplicated, like, four generations ago; the copy is transcribed and its product does something genuinely useful in the current organism, but there's been no selection on it and it's way too recent for any.
- a molecule-for-molecule duplicate wolf that condenses out of swamp muck with no ancestry whatsoever. its heart circulates blood. does it have the function of circulating blood?
- a moth population that lost its bats two hundred generations ago. the evasive dive to ultrasound still fires reliably, did its job for millions of years, and now contributes nothing to survival or reproduction.
- the broken heart: an individual whose heart can't pump at all, in a species where hearts are obviously pumping organs.
- a self-replicating strand in a warm prebiotic pool, where there is no larger system whose capacities it contributes to and no lineage yet.
- a bridge bearing designed to carry load which also, as it happens, channels rainwater away from the abutment; the engineer never gave that a thought.
- the vision module in a robot. nobody designed the behaviour, there's no lineage, but it was shaped by a training process over a few million episodes and it now reliably does one thing and would be retrained if it stopped.

4. Flag honestly which pair of leaves the instrument can't separate. My suspicion is that etiological and organizational collapse as soon as the containing system itself has a maintenance history, and maybe the dispositional and causal-role leaves do too — but tell me if I've got it wrong, and tell me whether some case could pry them apart or whether it's a difference with no possible test.

And for each leaf, one sentence on the most embarassing thing it has to say about one of the cases above. No padding, no bibliography, no page numbers or "see also" pointers — I'll put the names in myself and I'd much rather you not guess at citations.
