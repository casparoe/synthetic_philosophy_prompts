just got out of a three hour argument with my roommate about the brain-computer analogy and I need a tiebreaker who isn't in the room with us. His whole move was: von Neumann wrote the book on this — literally, The Computer and the Brain, the unfinished Yale Silliman lectures that came out in '58 after he died — and in that book he sets out the differences between brains and computing machines and treats every one of them as an engineering detail rather than a difference in kind. From that my roommate thinks it follows that the analogy is licensed by the guy who built the machines, and that substrate independence and whole-brain emulation isnt a live philosophical question anymore, just a funding question.

My counter, which he wouldnt buy, is that von Neumann was way more guarded than that and that the disanalogy material in the book does the opposite work from the one hes using it for.

Can you audit the analogy as von Neumann himself set it up? Specifically:

- the mapping. what he takes to correspond to what — the arithmetical organ, memory, the code, the input/output channels, whatever else he lines up. a table is fine.
- the disanalogies he flags himself. I remeber some of them and I'm probably mangling at least one: something about nervous system precision being only two or three digits deep where a machine of that era went ten or twelve; about memory not having an identifiable organ in the brain the way a machine has separate primary and secondary stores; about the language of the nervous system being combinatorial or logical rather than arithmetical; about digital and analog being mixed together rather than one or the other; about size and energy use. tell me straight where I've misremembered instead of quietly going along with me.
- then the part that matters for the argument: which of those gaps have actually closed since 1958 — nets quantized down to 8 or 4 bit weights, weights doing the memory job with no separate store, neuromorphic and analog hardware, models whose internal "code" looks nothing like arithmetic — and which ones are still wide open. don't let either of us win by default.
- what the analogy smuggles in that the target lacks. I suspect this is where the real fight is: does "the brain is a computer" quietly assume substrate independence, or a well-defined representation language, or that precision is a dial you can turn rather than a kind difference? and did von Neumann think the choice of the computing machine as the comparison class was a discovery or an artifact of the historical moment — I have a memory of him worrying about exactly that and I want to know if that's real or something I invented.
- and finally whether my roommate's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the whole argument and von Neumann never signed up for the load.
