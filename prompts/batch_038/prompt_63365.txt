Never taken a philosophy class, so bear with me, but I rewatched The Godfather this weekend and the bit that stuck with me wasn't the violence — it was the very first scene, Bonasera going to the Don after the courts handed the guys who beat up his daughter basically nothing.

Can you read the first film through the honor / dignity / victimhood cultures framework from Campbell and Manning? The version I have in my head: honor cultures do self-help justice and are super sensitive to insult, dignity cultures have internalized worth and shrug off slights, and victimhood cultures keep the honor-style sensitivity to slight but channel it into third-party appeals, like going to HR or an admin body instead of handling it yourself.

Two things I'd like you to actually work through rather than just summarize the framework:

1. The Don is a third party. People escalate to him to adjudicate, and they pay in loyalty and favors. So is the Corleone operation an honor culture at all, or does the framework just misclassify it? I suspect it's missing a category for a private substitute-state, but tell me if I'm wrong.

2. Michael starts out as the assimilated dignity-culture guy (college, Marines, telling Kay that's his family and not him) and ends up the don. Is that a fall out of dignity into honor, or is the family's structure — consiglieri, capos, carved-up territories — actually something colder and more bureaucratic than honor ever was, and the movie knows it? And what about Kay getting the door shut in her face at the end: is the film taking a side, or just filming it?

Wrap up with what the movie adds to this debate that the sociology doesn't get, plus anywhere you think a reading like mine would distort the film by treating it as an illustration.
