So a couple months ago I got an email I wasn't expecting. The county historical society back home — small town in central Illinois, the kind of place where the elevator and the Lutheran church are the two tallest things — is doing a big sesquicentennial banquet in three weeks, and the director asked me to give the keynote. She knows I'm finishing a PhD in anthropology and that my dissertation is about health in early farming communities, so she phrased the request as "tell us how farming changed everything, keep it around ten minutes, make people feel good about the weekend." The program booklet is already at the printer and the whole thing is billed as a celebration of turning prairie into breadbasket. There's going to be a living-history plowing demonstration on Saturday morning with a replica walking plow, and the society's most prized artifact is a seed-corn basket from the 1870s that will be on a table right next to where I'm standing.

The problem is that my actual field is basically the study of why that celebration is more complicated than it looks. I've spent four years reading about what happens to human bodies once people start depending on a handful of domesticated crops — stature dropping in some of the early villages, teeth going to hell once maize takes over in the Americas, infectious disease getting a foothold once you're living in dense settled groups with your animals, and the work week getting longer instead of shorter compared to the foragers who came before. And I came up on the other side of it: the whole original-affluent-society argument, the claim that the foragers had more leisure and a more varied diet than the farmers who replaced them, plus the pop-science article from the eighties that made its way around every dining table in my family, the one arguing agriculture was the biggest blunder in the species' history. I'm not going to stand up in a gymnasium full of fourth- and fifth-generation farm families and tell them their ancestors walked into a catastrophe. But I also can't give them the Hallmark version about progress and ingenuity, because I know what the bones say, and my dad would know I was lying, and honestly I'd know. And I don't want to do the coward thing either, which is just not mentioning any of it and talking about the seed-corn basket for ten minutes, because she asked me specifically to talk about how farming changed everything.

Here's the idea I want the speech to actually be built on, not just mention: the mistake question doesn't really get off the ground, because nobody made the decision. Farming accumulated over generations — a few weeds tolerated near a camp, a stand of grain protected, more mouths to feed, no way back down — and every individual step was locally sensible while the exit quietly narrowed. Each generation was better off than the one before by their own lights and worse off by some longer-run standard, and there was no moment at which anyone could have chosen otherwise. That framing is what lets me say the hard stuff out loud without insulting the room, and it's also the thing that makes the speech about them rather than about the past, because the same lock-in structure is sitting on their kitchen tables right now: my cousin farms three thousand acres and is in an ugly fight with half the township over whether to sign a solar lease on ground his great-grandfather broke, and there's debt and consolidation and a new seed trait every few years that everyone adopts because not adopting it costs you the year. Please write the actual speech, not an outline — deliverable out loud in about ten minutes, so twelve to fourteen hundred words, with one genuinely funny moment that works for farmers and not for academics, no jargon, no slides, no quotations from famous people (if you gesture at that eighties article, gesture without quoting it). It should name the health and inequality evidence honestly but without a data dump, it should not slide into a defense of industrial agriculture — my cousin would hear that as a free pass — and it should end somewhere that isn't sentimental. Three hundred people: FFA kids in their jackets at the front tables, the county board, a few folks from the state university who know exactly what I do, and my dad in the third row. Don't make it a lecture, and don't make me look like I've come home to convert anybody.
