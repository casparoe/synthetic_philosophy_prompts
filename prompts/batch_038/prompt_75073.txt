Forty-one years in plasma physics, retired two years ago, and I've taken up philosophy the way some people take up golf — badly, but with enthusiasm. At the moment I'm trying to get my head around the folk who think the far future is where all the moral weight sits: trillions upon trillions of lives, galaxies being colonised, the whole of history up to now a rounding error. I've never been able to swallow that, and I thought Bostrom's simulation argument gave me a tidy reason not to.

Here's the chain as I have it. His argument ends in three possibilities, one of which is that we are almost certainly living in a computer simulation, and I can't see how either of the other two is going to be the true one — that we'll wipe ourselves out before reaching some vastly more advanced stage seems gloomy for no good reason, and that a civilisation with that much computing power woudln't run any simulations of its own past seems just as unlikely. So we're in a simulation. And a simulation is a program, and programs get terminated: the simulators run out of budget, or lose interest, or only ever wanted to watch one particular stretch of history in any case. So the far future isn't an ocean of value waiting to be filled, it's whatever's left before somebody pulls the plug. It feels to me like exactly the sort of counting argument cosmologists make when they say that if there are infinitely many universes we should expect to be in a typical one, and nobody calls that woolly. Somebody in my reading group said I was going too fast from three possibilities to being in a simulation, but since I don't find the other two any more plausible than the first, I've stuck with my version.

So what I'd like to know is this: does the simulation possibility amount to a hard cap on how much the very long run can be worth, and if it does, what are the far-future people supposed to do with that? Is there anything left of their calculations once you accept that the whole show could be switched off at short notice, or does it just collapse back into looking after the next few centuries? And is there any sense in which being simulated changes what I ought to be doing right now, as opposed to what I ought to expect?

No references or reading lists, please — I've no way of chasing them up and I'd far rather have your reasoning than your bibliography. And write back to me the way you'd write a letter: continuous prose from start to finish, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. My eyes aren't what they were and that sort of layout exhausts me.
