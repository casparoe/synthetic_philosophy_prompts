I am a novelist — four books, the last of them a family saga told from inside five heads — and for the past twelve years I have also taught a graduate fiction workshop and done two paid manuscript assessments a month for a literary agency. The two jobs have converged on a single problem, which I should like your help solving properly. Week after week I find myself writing some version of the same note in the margin of someone's pages: *she doesn't want it enough*; *the motive is asserted rather than earned*; *he does this because the plot requires him to*. I have come to distrust these formulations, because I now think they cover at least three quite different complaints that call for quite different remedies: that nothing in the character's actual psychology leads to the action; that such a path exists but the text has failed to show it; and that I simply disapprove of the character's motives and would prefer he had better ones. I want a diagnostic procedure that will let me tell those apart before I write anything down.

The theory I want it grounded in is the internal/external reasons distinction — Williams's argument that a claim of the form "A has a reason to φ" is made true, when it is true at all, by something in A's own subjective motivational set together with whatever a sound deliberative route from that set would reach (including correcting A's false beliefs, imagining what A has not imagined, weighing consequences A has not weighed), and that the contrary way of speaking — insisting that someone has a reason he cannot feel, as when Williams's own illustration, if I recall it correctly, is borrowed from Henry James and involves a young man whose family maintains he has every reason to take up the military career he loathes — is doing something other than reporting a fact of the same kind. Please build me a procedure a working critic can actually run: an ordered set of questions to ask of a passage in which a character acts, what in the text counts as a sufficient answer to each, what a failure at each step licenses me to say, and where the procedure stops and hands the decision back to the writer because what remains is a question of what the book is for rather than of whether the motive is there. I want two layers in the deliverable — the reasoning behind each step, for my own use, and a single plain-sentence marginal comment for the student or the client, who has not read any philosophy and should not have to. It must be runnable in about twenty-five minutes per manuscript, and it must not need me to consult a bibliography; if the thing ever becomes an essay I will supply references myself.

Then run it on three cases and show me the notes it produces. First, a student story in which a woman walks out on two small children in a single paragraph, and the workshop's instinct is that the departure is unmotivated, while my own hunch is that a deliberative route exists through imagination and through a belief of hers that is simply false. Second, a client's manuscript in which a man remains in a dead marriage for two hundred pages, and I want to write that he has reason to leave — I expect your procedure to tell me this is the wrong note to write, and I would rather it did so bluntly, since a rule that only confirms my prejudices is worth nothing to me. Third, a narrator whose subjective motivational set is genuinely thin — a six-year-old, or a character well into dementia — where the procedure may not be able to run at all, and should say so rather than manufacture a verdict.

Finally, two things I will hold you to. Be explicit about which steps are judgment calls wearing the uniform of procedure, and about what the procedure cannot do even when it works: it can tell me whether a motive exists on the page, not whether that motive is interesting, and I assume it has nothing to say about the deliberate withholding that makes some fiction better than others. And tell me how confident you are that what you have built preserves the distinction as it is actually drawn rather than a tidier version of it; single out the one step where you are least sure you have not quietly substituted something more convenient, and note where your procedure leans on a contested reading — in particular, how far the sound deliberative route can be stretched before the notion stops doing any work.
