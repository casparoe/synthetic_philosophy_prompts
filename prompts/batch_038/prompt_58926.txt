I have spent eighteen years in product data management for aerospace suppliers: bills of material, part numbering, revision and effectivity control, and the recurring argument about when a welded assembly gets one part number and when it gets a breakdown. I have no philosophy training, though I read around the subject, and I recently ran into the question philosophers call the special composition question — when do some things compose a further thing — along with the standard menu of answers: nothing ever composes anything; anything whatsoever always composes something with anything else; composition occurs when things are in contact or fastened together; when they are fused; and van Inwagen's answer, that composition occurs exactly when the activity of the parts adds up to a life.

Restate that whole debate in the vocabulary I actually work in, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report.

Begin with the question itself. What is the counterpart in my framework of the claim that some things compose something? Is it that these rows get rolled up under a single parent part number, that this shows up as one line item on the next level of the structure, or something else? Say which rendering you are choosing and what the choice costs you before you build anything on it.

Then take each candidate answer one at a time. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression in my framework at all. Please use the awkward features of my world rather than the tidy ones, and say what each of them does to the translation: a single physical article legitimately carries three different structures — a design bill, a manufacturing bill, a service bill; phantom assemblies are parents with no physical referent; a part number can be assigned to a bag of loose fasteners that nobody would call one thing; effectivity dating means the same serial unit has different decompositions at different dates; and the part-of relation in my world is a stored, owned, revocable assignment made by a person, not a discovery about the metal.

Then give me the verdict on the residue. Where a philosophical answer cannot be stated in my terms, is that because my framework is missing something — no way to represent part-whole structure that is not somebody's decision — or because the philosophers are in fact arguing about how to stipulate a data model and presenting it as a claim about the world? I want a reasoned position, not a shrug in both directions.

Finally, one special case. There is a view on which artifacts such as tables do not exist at all, even though the simples arranged tablewise do. Does that position correspond to anything expressible in a system where a part number can exist with no single physical item answering to it, or is it inexpressible in principle?

Two constraints. Do not let my framework's conventionalism do the argumentative work for you: if at any point you find yourself saying that the answer depends on how we choose to structure the data, flag that moment explicitly as a place where the translation is smuggling an assumption across the border rather than carrying one. And do not grade the philosophical answers by whether they would survive a design review; I want to know what the translation preserves, not whether it approves. Structure the response with a short section per answer and a closing residue section, roughly 800 to 1,000 words.
