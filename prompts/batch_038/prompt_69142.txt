I chair the employee appeals panel of a regional health system, and roughly a dozen times a quarter we are asked to settle one question on which everything else turns: did this worker resign, or not? The determination decides whether the person is recorded as having left voluntarily — forfeiting reinstatement rights and internal appeal standing, and shaping the system's exposure in unemployment-insurance proceedings — or as having been separated by the employer. Our written standard is thin to the point of useless. It says only that a resignation must be clear, and that it is to be judged by what a reasonable person in the position of the manager who received it would have taken the words to do; the worker's later account of what they meant is not by itself decisive.

The panel is three laypeople — a nurse, a facilities manager, a community appointee — none of them legally or philosophically trained. We have forty-five minutes per case and must produce a one-paragraph written rationale that counsel could read without embarrassment if the matter is litigated. Our current unwritten habit is to ask whether the worker really meant it. That produces outcomes that track sympathy rather than facts, and two panels hearing the same case would disagree.

I want to replace that habit with a procedure grounded in what the theory of speech acts actually says about utterances that do not merely describe but perform: what has to be in place for an act of that kind to come off at all; the several ways it can fail, where some failures leave no act behind and others leave an act that is defective yet real; the part the hearer plays in completing it; the difference between the act succeeding and the act merely having consequences; and the fact that such acts are liable to be void or voidable rather than true or false. Translate that into a sequence of questions a layperson can answer in order.

What I need from you:

1. An ordered set of tests. The ordering must do work — state which failures terminate the inquiry with a finding that no resignation took effect, and which allow the panel to proceed to the next question.
2. For each test, an evidential standard: what in the record before us would satisfy it, and what would not. Where a test turns on the reasonable hearer, say whose situation counts as the reference point and why.
3. Explicit markers at every point where the procedure must hand the decision back to human judgment because what remains is a question of institutional values or fairness rather than a question of what the words did. Do not disguise those steps as further tests.
4. An account of the translation itself: which distinctions from the theory you are keeping, which you are collapsing or dropping for lay use, and why the dropped ones can be spared. I would rather have a short procedure candid about its simplifications than a long one that pretends to precision it does not have.
5. The procedure run on the cases below, showing the answer at each test rather than only the verdict. I expect at least one of them to come out against the worker in a way the panel will find distasteful; if so, say so plainly and note whether that is a defect of the procedure or of our written standard.
6. A closing section on what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of case it will get wrong, the direction in which it errs, and whether the error is systematic.

The cases:

(a) During a shouting match with her supervisor over scheduling, a medical assistant says, "Fine, I'm done, I'm not doing this anymore," and walks off the unit. She returns twenty minutes later and says she was upset and is not leaving. The supervisor had already emailed human resources that she had quit.

(b) An employee sends his manager a message reading, "I can't keep working like this. If nothing changes I don't see the point of staying." Nothing changes. He reports for work for six further weeks, then stops coming in.

(c) A respiratory therapist tells two colleagues in the break room — not her manager — that she is putting in her papers. One colleague mentions it to the manager the following day. She never submits anything and never tells the manager herself, but the manager, on hearing the report, completes a separation form.

Finally, tell me what your procedure does with a fourth case, and use it to expose the limits: an employee simply stops appearing and stops answering calls and messages, so that there is no utterance to test at all. Our handbook calls this job abandonment and treats it as equivalent to a resignation. If your procedure has nothing to say here, that is an acceptable answer — but explain what is missing and where the decision must go instead.

Please do not include a reading list, citations, or references; I need something the panel can hold in two pages and use next month.
