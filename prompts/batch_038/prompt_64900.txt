I'm building a session on escalation of commitment for a law-and-econ reading group and I want to use Apple v. Samsung as the running case — but through the dollar auction specifically, not the vague sunk-cost-fallacy talk everyone defaults to. Here are the facts as I have them so you don't have to trust your memory of the numbers: Apple sued in spring 2011, and by late that year the two were litigating something like twenty cases in ten countries. The first San Jose jury in 2012 handed Apple $1.049 billion; that got trimmed and partly retried over the next couple of years. The chunk that ended up mattering was about $399 million in design-patent damages, which Samsung took all the way to the Supreme Court and won a remand on in December 2016 — and then at the 2018 retrial the jury gave Apple $539 million, more than the amount that had just been thrown out. They settled that summer, roughly seven years in, with each side's legal bills reported in the hundreds of millions.

So first, map it. Who exactly is the bidder at each stage — the company, the general counsel, outside counsel, the board, and are those even the same decision maker? What is the pot, what counts as one bid increment, and where were the points at which a player could have folded and what would folding have cost them in precedent and in standing thier own executives had built up internally? I keep coming back to the fact that under the American rule each side eats its own fees win or lose, which is the one structural feature that makes the analogy look right at all — bids are sunk whether or not you take the prize. Push on that: is it the dollar auction, or is it a war of attrition with a negotiable pot, and what turns on which?

Then tell me what the model genuinely explains. My hunch is the Supreme Court trip over an issue worth less than the cost of litigating it, and the 2018 retrial handing Apple a bigger number than the one just vacated, are the best fits — spending more to avoid losing a smaller pot is the $1.05 bid for the dollar almost exactly. And then be honest about where it doesn't reach: settlement is available at every stage in a way it never is in Shubik's game, the fees bought discovery and bargaining information rather than being pure waste, and both companies got enormously more valuable over those seven years, which makes it hard to score who "lost." Which of those are fatal to the analogy and which are just awkward?

Here's the wrinkle I want you to engage with directly rather than politely wave at. Call it the expressive dollar auction: the pot isn't fixed, its value to a bidder rises with what you've already sunk into it, because past some point what's being bought isn't $399 million anymore, it's the verdict that you were right all along. There's the well-known report that Jobs told his biographer he was prepared to burn through Apple's entire cash reserve to destroy Android because he believed it was built on stolen ideas — which sounds like the pot was never money to begin with. Does adding that variable rescue the model or empty it out? If you can always say the prize grew to match the spending, is there any episode the theory could fail to fit, and what would a real defeater look like?

I'd rather have the places the dollar auction stays silent than a forced fit. And end by telling me which direction the case actually runs: does Apple v. Samsung confirm the model, or expose something it never anticipated — two players for whom the pot is trivially small relative to their balance sheets, which may be exactly why the classic paradox never got a chance to bite.
