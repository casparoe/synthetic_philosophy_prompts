I am a novelist, and I am writing to you in a personal rather than a professional capacity about something that has unsettled me far more than I expected. Please answer as a philosopher who works on minds, language, and what we can know about other people — someone in the habit of treating ordinary feeling as a datum to be accounted for rather than a reflex to be explained away. Think carefully before answering; I would rather have a considered argument than a prompt reassurance.

The situation. For nearly four years I have corresponded with a man I met once, briefly, at a small-press reading, and with whom I have since exchanged nothing but letters and emails. We have never spoken on the telephone. This correspondence has been the most important intellectual friendship of my working life; I have quoted his sentences aloud to friends and copied them into my journal dozens of times. Last month a mutual acquaintance let slip that this man suffered a stroke six years ago — before our correspondence began — which left him able to read normally and to speak in content words and short phrases, but not to produce fluent sentences of his own. He has since composed his longer writing by dictating fragments, notes, and corrections to a piece of software that expands them into finished prose. He reads everything I send, and every letter is sent with his approval. The thoughts, the references, the opinions, the jokes — all genuinely his, I am assured. But the sentences themselves, the particular ones that undid me, were assembled by a machine out of material he supplied.

There is a second fact, which I put on the table because I suspect it bears on the case. During the year my own health failed, I dictated much of my correspondence — to my sister, to a paid secretary, and twice to a similar drafting tool. Rereading those letters now, I cannot always tell from the inside which of the sentences in them were mine.

What I feel is not anger. He concealed nothing out of malice and gained nothing by it; the letters say exactly what they always said; nothing in them has been shown to be false. What I feel is a compound of grief and humiliation, as though I had lost a person I have not in fact lost, together with an embarrassment about my own trade: for twenty years I have worked on the assumption that sentences are evidence of the mind that produced them, and it turns out I could not tell.

So: is my feeling tracking something real, or is it a prejudice I ought to argue myself out of? I do not know the technical name for the problem I have stumbled into, and I would rather you told me than that I guessed at it. Specifically, I would like you to

(i) begin by laying out two or three candidate diagnoses of my reaction, including ones you will go on to reject, before arguing for the one you think is right;

(ii) say whether the fact that my own letters were partly machine-mediated should change the verdict — and if it does, how; if it does not, why the asymmetry I feel is nonetheless legitimate;

(iii) identify which features of the case are doing the work: that he did not choose the words, that he paid no cost in producing them, that I can no longer reread the past four years in the way I had been rereading them, that the letters have ceased to be evidence of anything, or something else — and be willing to conclude that my feeling is misplaced, or exaggerated, or right for the wrong reasons;

(iv) tell me what to do next: whether to write again, whether to raise the matter with him, and whether the answer differs depending on whether I had known all this from the start.

Please do not build me a reading list or cite sources; name a thinker only where doing so sharpens a point. Nor do I want general counsel about honesty and communication in friendship.

Once you have argued the case, set out your analysis as a table titled "What, exactly, have I lost?" with exactly these columns:

- candidate_loss — a short label for one distinct thing my grief or humiliation might be tracking; aim for five to seven rows, drawn from your own argument rather than from stock categories
- what_it_consists_in — one or two sentences, in plain language, saying what this loss would amount to
- actually_lost — Yes, No, or Partly, given the facts precisely as I have described them
- proper_object — whether, if lost, it is a proper object of grief or of humiliation: Yes, No, or Partly, with a clause saying why
- flip_condition — what would have to be different about the story for your entry in the third column to change

Close with three or four sentences of bottom line: what my feeling is, whether it is justified, and what I should do.
