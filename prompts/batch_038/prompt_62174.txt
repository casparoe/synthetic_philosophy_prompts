I sat in on a reading group last Thursday evening — a friend of mine belongs to it and owed me a ride home, so I tagged along and spent two hours in a department common room listening to people argue about Daniel Dennett. They were working through his 1991 paper on real patterns alongside the material on the physical, design, and intentional stances. Two of the regulars, whom I'll call Nadia and Owen, got into a sustained argument about whether the compression criterion can carry the realist weight Dennett places on it. Neither was posturing and neither was talking past the other, which is what made it interesting to watch; I took notes because I couldn't decide who was winning. Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it.

Nadia (1): The criterion says a pattern is real if some description of the data is more efficient than the bit map. But efficiency is always relative to a description language, and languages can be cooked. Invent a single primitive predicate whose extension is exactly the collection of states I want to dignify as beliefs, and I have covered an arbitrarily large stretch of the data in a handful of symbols. Nothing in the criterion excludes that, so it cannot separate genuine patterns from mere relabeling — and if it cannot do that, it cannot deliver the realism Dennett wants from it. Owen (2): That maneuver does not compress anything. A name whose extension is simply stipulated entails no bits of the map; the information has been relocated into the definition of the term, not eliminated. What makes a description a compression is that it, together with the rest of the data, entails particular bits the map alone does not hand you. Stipulate all you like — you still have to pay for the extension somewhere, and if you pay for it you have saved nothing. Nadia (3): You pay once and amortize forever. If my gerrymandered predicate picks out a very large but sparsely specifiable set of states, buying the extension is a one-time cost that repays itself across thousands of predictions. And look at what that implies: whether the purchase is worthwhile depends on how the rest of the corpus happens to be arranged, so change the sample and a different family of predicates comes out ahead. The criterion makes what exists hostage to accidents of the data set. Worse, when two comparably short descriptions disagree about which pattern is present, it supplies no reason to prefer the intentional one — which is the only one Dennett actually cares about. Owen (4): The corpus-relativity charge runs backwards. As the corpus expands toward all the data there is, ad hoc predicates stop paying and robust ones keep paying; the relativity contracts rather than explodes. And the underdetermination complaint assumes realism requires a unique winner at every scale. Dennett's picture is that real patterns overlap and compete, and that which one you adopt is settled by what the stance buys you for the purpose at hand — which is precisely why there are three stances rather than one privileged level. The bit map remains the single arbiter: a description either earns its keep against it or it does not. Nadia (5): Then notice what you have just given up. All the criterion delivers is that describing a creature in intentional terms is instrumentally economical. That is a claim about the economy of our descriptions. To move from "this saves bits" to "beliefs are real" you need a bridging premise connecting compression to ontology, and no argument for that premise has been offered anywhere — it is simply assumed. So the view is either a stipulation dressed in realist clothing or an instrumentalism that has quietly renamed itself. Owen (6): The length of the shortest adequate description of a data set is not a fact about anyone's interests; it is a feature of the data. If the actual microphysical history of a system is compressible at the level of beliefs and desires, that is a structural discovery about the world, and withholding ontological status from it commits you to holding that only the bit map is real — a view on which levels of organization become inexplicable. And by your standard quarks are instrumental conveniences as well, since particle physics is likewise nothing but a compression of detector output. Nadia (7): One more turn of the screw. Complexity measured as shortest description length is invariant across description languages only up to an additive constant, and for a finite corpus that constant is not negligible: it can be comparable to the entire saving you are claiming. So even the objectivity of "shortest description" presupposes a designated language, and physics does not hand you one. Two physically respectable encodings can disagree about whether the intentional description wins, and the criterion has nothing to say about which encoding gets to decide. Owen (8): The savings in question are not marginal. Replacing a neuron-by-neuron account with an intentional one compresses by many orders of magnitude, and language-relative constants of that order do not touch it. Where the ambiguity would genuinely bite is where the savings are small — and there the honest answer is that the question is vague, which is exactly what a graded, corpus-relative notion of reality should predict.

They ran out of time and neither budged. Could you go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right at each step? For each of the eight turns I would like a verdict — lands, partly lands, or misses — with a sentence or two of justification, rather than a single overall winner. I am particularly interested in whether Nadia's stipulation objection in (1) and its restatement in (3) really is neutralized by Owen's appeal to entailment in (2), since that strike seemed to me to be the hinge the rest of the argument turns on; if (2) fails, I suspect (6) and (8) go down with it, and I would like that dependency made explicit. Then, separately: what is neither of them addressing? I left with the impression that at least two substantive issues about the compression criterion and about the relationship between the three stances went entirely untouched, and that the debate might have taken a different shape had they come up. Please identify those and say briefly how each would bear on the moves above.
