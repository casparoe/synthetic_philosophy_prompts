Weird ask, but bear with me.

Two years ago I helped start a mutual credit scheme at our community centre — about 90 households, our own printed scrip, one note = one hour of somebody's labour, spent at the monthly swap night. It's dying and I'm the one who has to say so out loud. Over the last six months people stopped spending their notes and started stuffing them in drawers for a rainy day, swap night went from forty-odd people to nine, and now nobody wants to do work for notes that nobody else will take. In three weeks I stand up in front of the whole membership and tell them either we change the rules or we fold, and I don't want to be guessing when I do it.

Trying to work out what went wrong, I fell down a hole and ended up reading Berkeley on money — not the idealism, the economics. Mostly the Querist (first published 1735 and reworked after that; the query numbering is a mess between editions so don't trust mine) and his 1721 essay from after the South Sea crash. And the guy is describing my town. Wealth isn't the tokens, it's the four elements plus human labour. Query 5: "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?" Query 23 asks whether the true idea of money, as such, "be not altogether that of a ticket or counter." He also asks whether less money circulating fast isn't effectively the same as more money circulating slow. And from the 1721 essay: "Money is so far useful to the public as it promotes industry, and credit having the same effect is of the same value with money; but money or credit circulating through a nation from hand to hand, without producing labour and industry in the inhabitants, is direct gaming." Plus his stuff about fashion and manufactured wants being what actually gets a population moving, and the sketch at the back of the Querist for a national bank issuing paper notes on good security rather than gold (land, I think, but don't quote me on that).

Separately — and this is why I'm asking you instead of just moping — I teach a Thursday-evening adult philosophy class at the community college. 18 enrolled, 13 or 14 show up, ages 19 to 71, two of them are actual accountants who will definately notice if my numbers don't add up. Two hours, minus about 15 minutes of admin and announcements.

Next session is money. I want a game, not a talk. Design an in-class game where they discover Berkeley's picture of money by living it, and where they don't hear his name or get told that money is just a counter until the debrief.

What I need:

- rules, roles and a timeline, plus a payoff/scoring scheme that doesn't use real money. I can bring a bag of poker chips, about 60 index cards, markers, and maybe $10 of coffee and candy if you want something for them to spend on.
- participants should have to actually produce something, some cheap little task, so there's a genuine industry for the tokens to circulate against. Otherwise its just chips changing hands and nobody learns anything.
- at least one round that reproduces what happened to us: hoarding, the market quietly suffocating, everyone acting sensibly on their own while the whole thing dies anyway. I want them to feel the dead loss, not be told about it afterwards.
- something that also stages the velocity point and the wants-have-to-be-created point, not just the hoarding point. Those are the two bits my members never got and I think they're the ones that matter.
- a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto specific claims of his, including the moments where they were already reasoning like him without knowing it.
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it. I've never facilitated anything with real stakes in it, and one of my regulars is a committed rules lawyer.
- an honest line on which part of Berkeley's view a classroom token game flat out cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to supply myself.

Last thing, and it's maybe the real reason I'm writing. End the session with the class having to decide whether a local scrip like mine can be rescued by rule changes or is doomed — but not as an opinion poll. Set it up so they have to argue from what just happened to them in the game, and tell me how to turn that into something I can carry into the membership meeting without it being "my philosophy class said so."
