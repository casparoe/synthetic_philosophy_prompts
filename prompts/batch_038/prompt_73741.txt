I am the junior author on a synthetic-biology grant proposal, and I have been asked to check its framing section before it goes out. That framing rests on an analogy I do not trust, and two of my co-investigators disagree about how much of it survives scrutiny.

The analogy is Hofstadter's typogenetics. In the toy game he sets up in *Gödel, Escher, Bach*, a strand is just a string over a small alphabet of letters; the string is read off in pairs, each pair standing for one entry in a fixed list of amino acids, and the resulting sequence folds into an enzyme whose shape determines which strands it recognizes and what it does to them. A strand that specifies an enzyme which recognizes that same strand, and copies it, is a self-replicator. Hofstadter's point is that the strand gets at itself only by way of the coding that maps strands onto enzymes, just as a Gödel sentence gets at itself only by way of the numbering that maps sentences onto numbers.

My co-investigators take this to establish two substantive claims, and the proposal asserts both:

(i) Self-replication is a genuine, non-metaphorical instance of Gödelian self-reference; therefore any formal model rich enough to describe a self-replicating system falls under the incompleteness theorem — it is either incomplete or inconsistent, and there is some truth about the replicator it cannot establish.

(ii) Consequently no self-replicating machine can simulate itself completely, and the artificial-life programme of writing down a closed self-description of a replicator is chasing something the diagonal argument forbids.

Set out the correspondence explicitly: what in typogenetics plays the role of the base string, of the coding function, of the fixed point, of the proof predicate, and of the sentence that is true yet unprovable. Then give me the disanalogies, sorted into those that actually threaten (i) and (ii) and those that are merely cosmetic. In particular, say what typogenetics would have to acquire — semantically and structurally — before anything inside it could be true but unprovable; and for (ii), identify which sense of complete self-simulation the argument needs, and whether diagonalization delivers that sense or some other constraint entirely. Then say whether (i) and (ii) survive, whether a weaker claim does survive and is worth keeping in the framing section, and what the better analogue or formalism would be if one exists.

Finish with a single line of the form "Verdict: X", where X is exactly one of: neither, (i) only, (ii) only, both.
