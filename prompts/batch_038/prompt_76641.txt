I work on inference infrastructure at a company that ships a chatbot, and a lot of my day is spent staring at sampling code. Something in our stack has been bugging me in a way that turned out to be a philosophy problem, and I dont have a philosophy background, so please be patient with my framing and correct me where I've got the terminology wrong.

Here's the setup. When we generate a reply, at each step the model produces a vector of logits, we divide by a temperature, run a softmax, and sample a token out of that distribution. That number is a real propensity of the machine: hold the context fixed and run it a million times, and the frequency with which some given token comes out is just the softmax value, to as many decimal places as we care to measure. Now imagine an agent, call it A, that can introspect: before sampling, A can read its own logit vector, and A also has beliefs. So A can have a credence about "what will I emit next," and A can also look up the number its own hardware is reporting. That seems to me exactly like the situation Lewis's Principal Principle is about — there's an objective chance, and your credence is supposed to defer to it, given admissible evidence. But then there's a second principle that sounds almost identical and defers to a totally different thing: van Fraassen's Reflection Principle, where your present credence lines up with what you expect your own future credence to be. In our stack those two come apart, and I want to understand how.

Concretely: tonight I redeploy A with temperature 0.4 instead of 1.0 and I strip out the logit introspection. A at time t knows all of this in advance. So when A-at-t forms beliefs about what A-at-t+1 will emit, there's no accessible chance number to defer to anymore, but there is an expected future credence. And the part that really messes with me: our generation is autoregressive, so whatever token gets sampled at step 1 becomes part of the context that sets the distribution at step 2. The chance function isn't fixed in advance, it moves depending on what the process has already done. So the chance A defers to at step 2 is partly downstream of A's own deference at step 1, and I can't tell whether that breaks the Principal Principle's admissibility condition, makes Reflection the more basic principle, or something else.

So I want the two compared head to head, not just described. Specifically: what is the source of the authority in each case — a feature of the world versus a feature of the agent; what each one says when the agent expects to acquire new evidence; what each says when the agent expects to be reconfigured or to become less rational; and whether violating each is best described as irrational or as merely getting the world wrong. Then tell me which one you think is more fundamental and which is derivative, and actually adjudicate my sampler case: for A-at-t reasoning about A-at-t+1 under the new temperature with no introspection, which principle gives the right answer, and does the autoregressive feedback loop change your verdict? If you think my framing smuggles in a mistake — for instance if you think a softmax output isn't really a chance at all, so neither principle applies — say that plainly and tell me what it would have to be instead. Please answer in American English.
