I am assembling a small reading group on the measurement of violence in history, and one member has circulated a long essay from a general-interest magazine that sets out, for non-specialists, the thesis made famous by a prominent cognitive scientist's 2011 book: that violence has been in decline for centuries and that we are living in the most peaceful era our species has known. The essay's central passage reads:

"The numbers are not close. Homicide rates in Western Europe are now a small fraction — by most reconstructions, an order of magnitude or more lower — of what they were in the late Middle Ages. Battle deaths, measured against the size of the populations that produced them, have fallen over most of the last five centuries, and since 1945 the great powers have not gone to war with one another at all. The explanation is not that we have been born again: our ancestors' appetites for cruelty, dominance, and revenge were much the same as ours, and what changed is everything around them — a state that monopolizes force, commerce that makes other people worth more alive than dead, literacy and print that let a person take up perspectives other than his own, and a moral circle that has kept widening. So we have become gentler people, in the only sense that matters: at any given moment, fewer of us kill and maim. Those who deny the decline are mainly victims of the news cycle, which reports the crash and never the flights that landed safely. And because the trend is long, steady, and multi-century, we can expect it to keep going: the institutions that produced the decline will go on producing it. Which is why those who demand ever larger defense budgets and permanent war preparedness are arguing against the evidence."

I came away thinking that something like a decline is real but that the essay is leaning on it harder than it can bear, and I don't trust my own instincts about where exactly the line is. Could you audit the passage claim by claim? In particular:

1. Which statements would the author of the book being summarized endorse roughly as written, which are fair simplifications for a general audience, and which are claims he explicitly disowns or that the underlying evidence does not support?

2. Three moves especially worry me. First, the essay slides from per-capita rates to talk about fewer people dying — do those come apart here, and if so, in which periods and in which direction? Second, it says our ancestors' appetites were the same as ours and then concludes that we have become gentler people; is that a contradiction, a sloppy formulation of a real distinction, or a legitimate restatement? Third, the last sentence reads a historical trend as a forecast about institutions. Does the book itself license that inference?

3. The essay implies the two world wars are consistent with the pattern once deaths are divided by population. Is that a defensible reply, and what would a historian object to about it?

4. Critics answer that the whole question is unanswerable, because what counts as violence keeps shifting — physical harm only, or also coercive institutions, preventable deprivation, and psychological damage — and because the records for the distant past are too sparse to fix a denominator. Is this dispute empirical, verbal, or some of each, and is there a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, or only facts relative to a stipulated definition? Please say which criticisms bite and which the essay's author could absorb without giving up the thesis.

5. Finally, sort the essay's conclusions into those that survive correction, those that survive in weakened form, and those that do not survive at all — and, where one fails, tell me what a fair-minded version of it would say instead.
