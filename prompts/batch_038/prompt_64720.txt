So I'm a grad student who also flies, and I've gotten myself stuck on a training practice that everyone in aviation treats as completely obvious.

Here's the setup as I understand it. The whole cockpit discipline is built on not trusting memory: for normal and non-normal procedures you read the item off the card or the screen, you do it as challenge-and-response between the two pilots, and running a flow from memory when a checklist exists is treated as the cardinal sin. But sitting inside that system there's a small privileged list — the so-called memory items or immediate action items, things like an engine fire or failure during the takeoff roll, a rejected takeoff, a windshear escape, a terrain-warning pull-up, a cabin altitude drill — that you are expected to run from memory, instantly, before anybody's hand goes near the book. And the justification you hear is roughly that these are the cases where the aircraft will be lost in the seconds it takes to find the page. Fine, except that plenty of non-memory items are also time-critical, so the boundary looks arbitrary from where I sit.

Now the part that actually puzzles me, and it's local. My flying club rents out little single-engine pistons and it's debating whether to approve a tablet-based emergency-flow app for use in the pattern — engine failure after takeoff, engine fire on the ground, off-field landing — on the argument that a laminated card is slower to dig out than a screen that's already mounted and already lit, and that the hands are busy anyway. Half the instructors are viscerally against it and can't articulate why beyond "you need to know it cold." Meanwhile the same instructors are totally relaxed about students offloading everything else to the tablet: weight and balance, fuel numbers, frequencies, even a lot of the nav planning. So the club tolerates externalized cognition at the planning desk and forbids it in the air, and nobody can say what the dividing principle is.

My own attempt at a dividing principle, which I'm not sure is coherent, is this: if a step is allowed to be looked up, then the pilot is allowed to have already lost the airplane by the time they look it up — the permission to consult is only granted in states where the state is still recoverable by a slower process. I've been calling it the lookup tax. It might be garbage.

So what I want from you: reconstruct the theory of mind that this two-track system actually presupposes. Not which philosophers it reminds you of — what has to be true about where knowing and skillful coping live for the memory-item/no-memory-item line to make sense at all, and for the planning-desk/cockpit asymmetry to make sense. Does it require something internalist, or something more like skill-is-in-the-body, or is it really a claim about latency and about which parts of a procedure are still available when the situation has degraded past some point? Say explicitly which rival pictures the practice rules out — I'm thinking of the Otto-and-his-notebook parity line, where the tablet ought to count as part of the pilot's cognitive system on exactly the same terms as the memory item does, and of the distributed-cognition picture where the crew plus the book plus the aircraft is the unit that knows things and the individual pilot's memory is just one component. Under either of those, is the memory-item list incoherent, or is it something those views can license just fine? And be careful to separate what the practice strictly needs from what merely fits it comfortably — I suspect there's a big gap there, and I'd rather you point at the gap than fill it.

Then tell me what would have to change, institutionally and in the cockpit, if we dropped the presupposition and treated the tablet as genuinely part of the extended system — what breaks, what has to be redesigned, and whether the club should approve the app on that basis.

Two requests about how you handle this. First, commit. If you think one reading of the practice is right and the others are rationalizations, say so and argue for it rather than laying out a balanced menu; I'd rather have a strong claim I can attack than a survey. Second, please separate the places where you're reasoning from how flight training works in general from the places where you're actually confident about the specifics — I may well have described the memory-item business wrong, and if so I want to know which of my premises you're relying on and which ones you'd have to check. And if you think the whole framing is a category error — if the memory-item list isn't a claim about cognition at all but just an artifact of certification and liability, with no implicit theory of mind to extract — then say that plainly up front instead of humoring me, and then tell me what the practice does presuppose instead.
