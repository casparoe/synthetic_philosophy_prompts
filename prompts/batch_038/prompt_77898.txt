I TA a first-year algebra course and our lecturer has a line he uses every year when the axiom of choice comes up: Zorn's lemma is just induction grown up. His version is that you do the same three checks -- there's something to start from, you can always take one more step, and endless runs of steps have somewhere to land -- and then you're guaranteed to reach the top, so there's nothing spooky about choice, it's just bookkeeping. Students eat it up and it does diffuse the panic, but two things happened this term that made me suspicious. First, a couple of them walked away thinking the element Zorn hands you is the biggest thing in the poset rather than merely a thing nothing beats. Second, someone asked why we need the axiom of choice at all if the whole thing is really induction, and the answer was basically that choice isn't doing much work, it's just formalising the idea of keeping going.

So: audit that analogy for me. Lay out the correspondence explicitly -- what in Zorn's lemma plays the role of the base case, the successor step, the limit step, and the thing being climbed -- and then where the mapping breaks. I want the breaks sorted into the ones that matter for the job he's using the analogy for and the ones that are cosmetic, not just a long list of complaints. Specifically: does the "you can always take one more step" check really correspond to an induction step, given the exact way it's phrased in the hypothesis? Is there a base case at all, or is it hiding inside one of the other conditions? And above all, where does choice get in, since the story never mentions it -- if Zorn really is induction with the bookkeeping hidden, then the bookkeeping has to be where the choice lives, and I want to be able to point at the exact line.

Then tell me whether his two conclusions survive the disanalogies: that the axiom of choice is nothing to worry about, and that checking the chain condition is a mechanical step you can do on autopilot without thinking about what you're choosing between. If the analogy cant carry them, say what it can carry, and what a better one would be if there is one -- plus the three sentences I should say to the student who asks me this again next year.

Bottom line first, please: open with your one-sentence verdict on whether the analogy holds, holds with caveats, or actively misleads, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
