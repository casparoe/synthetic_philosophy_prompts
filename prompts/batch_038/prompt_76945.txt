I am writing a dissertation chapter on Carnap's separation of the political sphere of life from the sphere of scientific cognition, and I built the following argument to sharpen what I take the separation to cost. I expected to wield it as a criticism of Carnap. It now looks to me as though it proves far too much, and I cannot find the exit.

Background, as I read him. Carnap holds that a sentence is cognitively meaningful only if it is analytic within some adopted framework or empirically testable; otherwise it is the expression of an attitude wearing indicative clothing. Value commitments belong, for him, to the sphere of life and political practice rather than to the sphere of theoretical cognition. Science can serve that sphere richly — supplying causal knowledge, evaluating means, checking whether a program is internally consistent and empirically realizable — but it cannot establish the ultimate ends from which planning starts. Those are adopted, not inferred. The same voluntarism governs the choice among frameworks, which he treats as a practical matter to be settled by fruitfulness for a purpose rather than by truth.

The argument.

(1) By the criterion, end-assigning sentences — suffering ought to be reduced; planning should displace market coordination; the socialist program is to be preferred — are neither analytic nor testable, since every possible observation is compatible both with retaining and with abandoning the end.

(2) Therefore end-assigning sentences are not truth-apt; they express volition.

(3) Rational assessment applies only to truth-apt content, because to assess a claim is to assess its fit with evidence and with other claims, and an utterance without truth conditions has no such fit to assess.

(4) Therefore end-assignments are not rationally assessable, and disagreement about them can be resolved only by persuasion, conditioning, or force.

(5) Carnap's own positive program — scientifically advised social planning, the unity-of-science enterprise, and the recommendation of empiricism and tolerance — is advanced in recommending and end-assigning sentences.

(6) A recommendation is an end-assignment in the imperative mood, and mood does not alter its status under (1)–(2).

(7) Therefore, by (4), Carnap's own program is not rationally assessable, and his adoption of it is not a rational choice but the expression of an attitude.

(8) Therefore nothing in his philosophy gives him a rational ground for preferring the socialist planner's commitment to the romantic irrationalist's or the decisionist's: all three sit in the same non-cognitive sphere, and science is by hypothesis silent among them.

(9) The criterion is reflexively exposed. It is not analytic — it is not derived from the rules of any framework but is a constraint on which frameworks count — and it is not testable. So it, too, is an expression of attitude. If it merely expresses an attitude, it excludes nothing, including the metaphysics it was designed to exclude; if it does exclude, it excludes itself.

(10) Consequently Carnap's philosophy has no rational advantage over the metaphysics and the political irrationalism it was built to oppose, and scientifically informed planning has no more rational standing than any rival program.

What I want to keep is (1)–(2) and the claim that science cannot settle ultimate ends; that is the whole point of the position, and I am sympathetic to it. What I cannot accept is (8)–(10). Notice also that (8)–(10) are not specifically about Carnap: on the same reasoning, any non-cognitivist, any pragmatist, and arguably any philosophy whatsoever that recommends something is refuted, since every such view issues recommendations. An argument that destroys every position with a normative upshot makes me suspect the argument.
