So a bit of context: I write about science and data for a general-interest magazine, mostly stories where the fight is about what counts as evidence. Back in the spring I did a piece on a batch of electric buses — 412 of them, retrofitted in 2019 — where a brake-sensor fault kept showing up. The claim our reporting supported was, roughly, every retrofitted bus has the fault. The transport authority pushed back with a line my editor wanted to run: of some twelve thousand *other* vehicles inspected across the fleet, none had the fault, and in their words that "confirms" their maintenance regime is fine. Our data editor, who I'll call Priya because she'd murder me, built a little spreadsheet to keep track of the back-and-forth and it ended up being a weirdly perfect illustration of Hempel's ravens, right down to the fact that a coffee cup on her desk was technically evidence about buses. My old tutor now runs a second-year module called Evidence, Error and Public Reasoning, and she's asked me to contribute one question to the final paper because she wants something from the wild instead of another textbook recitation. Every draft I've written so far can be answered by a student who has just memorized the words "equivalence condition" and "Nicod's criterion" and can spell them correctly, which is exactly what I don't want.

Here's the artifact I want the question built around. Priya's ledger has four rules: (1) anything observed that is both retrofitted-2019 and fault-positive adds a point to the hypothesis; (2) anything observed that is neither retrofitted-2019 nor fault-positive also adds a point, because it's an instance of the contrapositive; (3) anything that is retrofitted and fault-free kills the hypothesis outright; (4) the desk keeps two separate running totals, one for "all retrofitted buses have the fault" and one for "all fault-free vehicles were not retrofitted in 2019," and both get reported side by side in the weekly memo. Under those rules the memo's headline number goes up every time anyone looks at anything at all, and it goes up twice over for most observations, because the same pigeon outside the window scores a point in both ledgers. The size of the classes never enters anywhere, so one look at one bus out of 412 counts identically to one look at one non-bus out of basically everything, and there's no slot at all for what we already knew going in — which matters, because the depot's own records told us in advance which vehicles had been retrofitted, so the "was it a 2019 retrofit?" question was never really open in the way the ledger pretends.

What I need from you: one essay question, plus a model answer of about 700 words, plus a marking scheme. The question has to force students to actually fix or dismantle the ledger and to say whether anything like rule 2 survives the repair — and it should end on the newsroom bit, i.e. whether that sentence from the transport authority should be allowed to stand in print and in what form. It's a closed-book, no-citations paper, so the model answer can't lean on named solutions or a reading list; I want the reasoning self-contained, and I'd rather students who've read further than the module got an easy ride than have the answer be a bibliography in disguise. For the marking scheme, make it out of 25 with five or six must-hit moves, say what each is worth, and give me one line per move on how students typically botch it — then a short separate list of wrong turns that should cost marks even in an otherwise fluent answer. I have my own list of suspects and I want to see if yours overlaps: saying the paradox is merely psychological, denying the equivalence condition without spelling out what else you lose, "a white shoe isn't a raven so obviously it isn't evidence" as if that were an argument rather than a restatement, wheeling out falsificationism as though it made the confirmation question disappear, and pasting Bayesian vocabulary — priors, likelihood ratios — over the problem without ever saying what the reference classes or the likelihoods actually are. Tell me if I'm missing the big one.
