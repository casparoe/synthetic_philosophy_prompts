The argument over whether common knowledge of rationality is an admissible assumption in game theory has been running for decades, and in the seminar I help convene nobody moves. The positions are entirely familiar. One side maintains that the assumption is a modeling device rather than a psychological claim: it restricts the state space, its consequences are conditional, and demanding empirical warrant for it confuses a stipulation with a hypothesis. A second side maintains that the assumption is not merely false but self-undermining: if I am credited with knowing that you are rational, and you then play something rationality rules out, the evidence I would have to use to interpret your move destroys the very knowledge I was credited with, so in any game where off-path play is reachable the assumption cannot survive contact with the game it is applied to. A third side points to laboratory results and to bounded-rationality equilibrium concepts that drop the iteration altogether while fitting observed play better, and concludes that the matter has been settled empirically against the idealization. A fourth, more technical voice observes that the epistemic-conditions literature has derived some of what is commonly taken to require the full common-knowledge assumption from much weaker mutual-knowledge hypotheses — in the two-player case together with a common prior, as I understand it, though I would want that checked rather than assumed — and concludes that the dispute was never about a load-bearing premise at all.

My working hypothesis, which I want assessed rather than adopted, is that these camps are not disagreeing about how strong an assumption is. They are disagreeing because one phrase is doing duty for at least three different claims: a constraint on which states a model admits; a counterfactual robustness claim about actual reasoners, to the effect that they would continue reasoning correctly however the reasoning went; and a normative closure requirement on rational agency itself. On this picture, each standard objection bites on exactly one of the three while being directed at all of them, which is why no exchange in the debate has ever landed.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a survey of the positions. Specifically:

1. Say which camps are talking past which, and whether any substantive disagreement survives the disambiguation. If one does, state it in a form that could in principle be adjudicated — a claim someone could be shown to be wrong about.

2. Test the three-way partition against the standard flashpoints: agreeing to disagree and the common prior assumption; the epistemic conditions for equilibrium; reasoning about deviations in centipede-type games; and the bounded-rationality alternatives. In each case, say which reading of common knowledge of rationality the objection actually targets, and whether the response it usually gets addresses that reading or a different one.

3. Tell me whether my partition is right, too coarse, or too fine. If it is wrong, give the correct one and explain why the version I proposed fails.

4. State what would count as progress here — a definition, a theorem, an experiment, a change of subject — and give an honest answer about whether the dispute can be ended. If it cannot, argue why, and say what the continuing argument is nevertheless for.

5. Address one point of fairness: whether the empirical camp's evidence is genuinely responsive to the formalists' claims, or whether it re-runs the same ambiguity in new vocabulary.

Refer to results and arguments by name where you need to, but I am not asking for a reference list or a literature review; I will supply the citations myself. What I need from you is the structure of the disagreement and a defensible judgment about what kind of disagreement it is.
