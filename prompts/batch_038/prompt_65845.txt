I built an argument for a borders seminar that was supposed to show that states cant turn away asylum seekers, and it worked too well — as far as I can tell it now shows that no state may ever turn away anyone, and also that a state isn't the kind of thing I wanted it to be. I can't tell whether the second part is a real flaw or just me flinching. Chain below.

Stipulated setup so the numbers are concrete: R is a coastal country of about 6 million with a working legal order. Its neighbour collapses and 900,000 people reach R's coast over four years. Most are fleeing violence; a substantial minority are fleeing starvation and statelessness; essentially none of them have anywhere else to go.

(1) Aggregation constraint: a state's right over territory is derived from the rights of the people who make it up, so it is no stronger than what those people could have conferred on it. Nothing extra appears when individual holdings are pooled.
(2) In the law of property, an owner's right to exclude gives way to private necessity — the skipper who ties his boat to your dock in a storm is no trespasser, you are not permitted to cut him loose, and the afterwards-accounting runs in your favour only for what he actually used or broke. That's not charity on the owner's part; it's built into what owning amounts to.
(3) If (1) is right, whatever limits bind an individual owner bind the pooled owner too. So the necessity limit in (2) transfers to R's border unchanged.
(4) R's arrivals are in the relevant condition: for each of them, being turned back means violence or death.
(5) So R may not turn them back. Admission isn't a favour R can decline to grant; it's what R's derived right already permits, and R has no residual discretion to withhold.
(6) Note what the necessity limit is keyed to. It is defined entirely by the intruder's need. It never asks how many intruders there are, how long the need lasts, how much the property can absorb, or what the owner had planned for it. It asks only whether this person, at this door, would otherwise come to harm.
(7) So neither numbers nor duration cut against (5). 900,000 privileged entries are just 900,000 instances of a privileged entry, and if the peril lasts a generation, the privilege lasts a generation.
(8) And nothing in the reasoning actually used persecution. It used would-otherwise-come-to-harm. So it runs identically for people whose state merely can't feed or protect them, and for people whose harm is chronic rather than imminent. R's border is open to everyone in need.
(9) But then R is not a self-determining political community at all. What R does with its territory is set entirely by who arrives and how badly off they are; R's members get no say in the size or composition of the arrangements they live inside — which is precisely what made (1) appealing, the idea that the state's right is *their* right, pooled.
(10) Therefore (1) is false. States hold a territorial right that is not the sum of their members' property rights, and the whole derivation was a category mistake.

I don't want (10) and I'm not convinced the argument has earned it, but I also don't want to just declare victory for the conclusion I started out liking. So: point at the step where it actually breaks, if there is one, and tell me why that step looked perfectly innocent when I wrote it down — I want the diagnosis, not just the verdict. Then price the repairs. What do I lose if I keep (6) and (7) but restrict (8)? What do I lose if I deny (3) and say the necessity limit doesn't survive pooling at all? Is (9) doing something illegitimate, or is it just stating the cost honestly? And is there any version of the necessity analogy that gets me a genuine duty to admit without collapsing into (8)?

For what it's worth I'd rather keep (1) and (2) than anything else in there, they're the two doing the interesting work, and I suspect im being sloppy somewhere between (6) and (8) — but I might be wrong about the location and I'd rather you tell me where the load is actually bearing.
