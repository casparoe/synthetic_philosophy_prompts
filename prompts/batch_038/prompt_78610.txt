I'm on the exhibitions board of a county museum and we vote Tuesday on a wall label that's split us three ways.

The setup: a rescue dig in the late 70s off a ring road turned up a single crouched inhumation, roughly 3,000 years old, that our lead curator wants to headline the new gallery as "The Priestess of the Marsh." Her case is that the body lies apart from the main cemetery cluster, oriented on the winter solstice sunset, with a small bronze mirror, a headdress strung with perforated red-deer teeth, and a bowl of charred plant material. No other burial on the site has anything like that combination.

Three things I've dug up that complicate it: the seed report, re-read last month, says the charred remains are mostly a common weed that grows in threshing waste, so it could just be intrusive fill; the deer teeth where never individually dated and their style looks a couple of centuries older than the grave, meaning heirlooms or a disturbed earlier burial; and the solstice alignment is one grave out of forty-something, with no control sample of which way the others face. Meanwhile our local history society has been calling her the priestess in its newsletter for two years and will be annoyed by any walk-back.

So: evocative title with a "may have been," a dry descriptive label that just names the objects, or two or three competing readings laid out side by side?

Draft the memo I'll circulate to the board. Sections in this order: **Bottom line** — what we should decide, in 3–4 sentences; **What the objects can and can't carry** — the strongest honest version of the priestess inference, and exactly where it gives out, ~350 words; **The case for going evocative anyway** — argue it properly, ~200 words; **The text I'd put on the wall** — actually write the label, ~120 words; **A test for next time** — a short criterion curators can apply to any speculative title, ~150 words.
