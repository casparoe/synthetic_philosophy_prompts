So I've been going in circles about the ethics of infinite futures and I want to find out whether there's an actual principle underneath my gut reactions, or whether some of them just have to go. Here they are, roughly in the order they came to me. Levels are happiness on a 0-10 scale, where 0 is a life exactly not worth living and below 0 is worse than never existing.

1. Two futures with exactly the same people, and everybody's a bit better off in the second one. Second is better. No hesitation, even if the bump is microscopic.

2. Future A: endless generations, every life at level 1, forever. Future B: same shape, every life at level 2, forever. B is better. Also no hesitation — and note I can't justify that by pointing at extra happiness, because both contain infinitely much of it.

3. Future C: a trillion years of level-9 lives, then the universe goes dead and there are never any sentient beings again. Future D: an eternity of level-0.1 lives, barely worth it, no end. I think C beats D. Pretty confident, which suprises me.

4. But take D and raise every life in it by a hundredth of a notch. Better. Do it again. Better again. I can't find any step where making lives better stops making the future better. So there's some exact level between 0.1 and 9 where "eternity at that level" flips from worse-than-C to better-than-C, or else I'm contradicting myself, and I can't see what would make that flip point anything other than arbitrary.

5. Guy offers me a deal: hand over $5 and there's a one-in-a-googleplex chance he'll bring about an eternity of level-10 bliss. I refuse, and I think refusing is right, not just cowardly. Meanwhile I also think a certain eternity of level-10 bliss beats any finite good whatsoever. Both of those feel like bedrock and I know they can't both survive.

6. This is the one I keep flip-flopping on. Eternity of level-5 people. Now one extra level-5 person gets added, nothing else changes. I think that's a genuine improvement, a new good life is a good thing. But now instead add a whole second non-overlapping eternity of level-5 people — infinitely many extra good lives — and I want to say that's no improvement at all, just the same infinity with more rooms, hotel style. If one more is an improvement, infinitely many more has to be too. If infinitely many more isn't, one more can't be. I can't make it come out consistent.

7. A child saved from something awful in the year ten million counts exactly as much as a child saved next Tuesday. Distance in time isn't a reason to care less. (I'll admit I feel some pull from "we don't know what they'll want," but that's uncertainty, not discounting.)

8. Related: if the future is infinite no matter what I do, then the total value of the world is infinite whether I grind myself down trying to shape it or whether I stay home and play video games. Yet I'm completely sure those two choices aren't equally good. So whatever I'm comparing, it isn't totals.

9. The doubling coin-flip lottery — heads $2, two heads $4, three heads $8, on forever. I'd pay about $25 for a ticket. I'm happy to call that a limitation in me rather than a mistake in the math.

10. Option 1: the next million years are level 9, every year after that is level 3, forever. Option 2: the next million years are level 3, every year after that is level 9, forever. I take option 2, and I take it precisely because of 7.

11. Eternity where half of everyone is at 9 and half at 1, versus eternity where everyone is at 5. I lean toward the even one. But change it to half at 9.5 and half at 0.5 versus everyone at 5 and I lean the other way, like the highs are worth the lows at that point. I don't have a stable view here and I know that's a problem.

12. Future X: infinitely many people, all at 5. Future Y: infinitely many people, all at 6 — but they're completely different people, no pairing, no counterpart for anyone. I still say Y is better, and I can't explain that with "each person is better off," since nobody's in both.

My best shot at a principle so far: compare two futures by looking only at the first N years, add up the happiness in that window, see who's ahead, then let N grow. Whoever's ahead for all big enough N is better; if the lead keeps swapping, call it a tie. I like it because it never has to subtract one infinity from another. But it seems to get 3 exactly backwards, since D out-accumulates C in every long window. It gives ties for both halves of 6, which is right for one half and wrong for the other. And it's blind to 11 entirely.

What I want: one principle, or two or three competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it myself to a case I've never seen before. For each clause, tell me which of my numbered verdicts it's in there to capture. Then tell me what it says about 6, about 4's flip point, and about the tension in 5 — and also about a few cases I haven't listed that you invent to stress-test it. Finally be straight with me about which of my verdicts it can't save, and which one you'd tell me to give up first and why. I don't want a list of who's argued for what, just the principle and the reasoning.
