I've been going around in circles on the could-I-have-come-from-a-different-sperm-and-egg question, and what I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. Someone in my reading group ran a Kripke-style argument at me — his own version is about a table and the particular hunk of wood it's made from, but he transplanted it to people, and I'm pretty sure it now proves far more than he wants. Here it is, as best I can reconstruct it.

Let g be a gamete pair (one sperm, one egg), and say a person *originates from* g if their development begins from the zygote that g forms.

1. Necessarily, at most one person originates from any given g. [He conceded the twins worry and patched it: in a splitting case, take the origin to be the post-split half, or read "originates from" as "is the one whose life begins from g without g's product having divided." Grant the patch for now — I don't think that's where the trouble is, but tell me if you disagree.]
2. If x and y are distinct persons, then necessarily x ≠ y. (Leibniz's law inside a necessity operator, he said.)
3. Let z be the pair I actually came from and let g be any other pair. Let b = the person who originates from g.
4. I am distinct from b.
5. So by (2), necessarily I ≠ b.
6. In any world in which I originate from g, I am the person who originates from g; by (1), that is b. So in any such world, I = b.
7. Therefore there is no world in which I originate from g. g was arbitrary: I could only ever have come from z.
8. Nothing in (1)–(7) turned on anything special about me, z, or g. So for any person p and any pair g: p originates from g only if p actually originates from g.
9. Now take a pair g\* that no actual person came from — say, the pair that would have formed had a different sperm got there first. Suppose for reductio that some possible person c originates from g\*. By (8), c originates from g\* only if c actually originates from g\*. Nobody actually does. So no possible person originates from g\*.
10. Every person originates from some pair or other. So every possible person is an actual person.
11. So there couldn't have been anybody who doesn't exist. My parents could not have had a child other than the three of us, and the ordinary thought "if a different sperm had won, there'd have been a different kid and no me" isn't merely false but describes an impossible situation — there's no such kid on offer.

He thinks (7) is a theorem and (11) is a bullet worth biting. I think (11) is a reductio, but I can't locate where the argument breaks, and every time I try, I end up denying something I wanted to keep. I especially want to keep (2) — it feels like it's just Leibniz's law — and I'd keep (1) too if the twin patch holds. What I can't live with is (11). I'd happily settle for (7) if I could get it without (11).

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Tell me whether the problem is that some step doesn't actually follow — an equivocation, say, or a quantifier that shifts somewhere between (3) and (10) — or whether the thing is valid and I simply have to pick the least expensive premise to give up. And is there a principled stopping point: can I get the modest conclusion I started out wanting without the runaway one, or does whatever reasoning delivers the first also deliver the second? If different diagnoses require giving up different premises, lay out the options and say which you'd take and why.

Also, please seperate out what's settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this. I can't tell whether (2) is common ground and its application to individuals who don't exist is exactly the disputed move, or whether the whole principle is up for grabs; and I can't tell whether the twins case is treated as a fatal counterexample to origin essentialism or whether its just a technicality that everyone knows how to absorb. Don't dress up a contested position as the consensus, and if the honest answer is that competent people disagree about where this argument fails, say so and say what the disagreement turns on.
