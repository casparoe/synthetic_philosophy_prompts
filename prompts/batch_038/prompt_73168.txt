ok, sorry in advance, this is a long one and I don't really have a neat way to ask it.

I work nights doing inventory at a distribution warehouse — counting pallets, reconciling what the system says is on the rack against what's actually there. That job gave me a fixation on the difference between a thing being missing and a thing being missing on paper, and a couple years ago I started reading about famines with that in my head. I've read Sen's Poverty and Famines, slowly, plus stuff on Bengal in '43, the Irish one, the Great Leap Forward, and some people arguing famines should be treated as political crimes rather than disasters (de Waal's book mainly). No degree, nobody to talk to about it except a little meetup I run at the library, six of us, once a month.

What I actually want: to spend the next year writing maybe ten long essays, roughly one a month, built around the questions that are still genuinely open — not the ones where everyone already agrees and I'd just be restating things in my own voice. So which open questions in this area matter most, and in what order would you put them?

I care way more about the ordering and the reasoning than the list. Three criteria I'd want you to use and weigh against each other: would settling it change what anyone should do; can somebody like me move it at all, given I can't do fieldwork or new datasets or archives and can only think hard about cases that are already documented; and how much other stuff hangs off it. Tell me where those three pull in different directions.

Two obsessions of my own — tell me whether they deserve a slot or whether they're just Sen with different words:

1. I keep thinking the real variable isn't democracy, it's the lag: the time between the first person starving and an official number existing that says so. There are actual numeric thresholds for declaring a famine, something like two deaths per ten thousand people per day, and it strikes me that the definition of the thing requires a body count that by its nature only exists after the fact. Is "nobody had written it down yet" a separate variable from Sen's free press and elections argument, or the same one wearing a hat?

2. On the crime framing: in Bengal there isn't really one perpetrator. There's like thirty people each doing something locally defensible — war priorities, the boats, not wanting to declare a famine and admit failure — and the deaths come out of the pileup. Does "political crime" still describe that, or does it need something like structural negligence, and does the legal idea of a crime even fit a thing where there's no single act to point at?

Also please say plainly which questions I should cut as settled or rhetorical. "Are famines natural or man-made" feels to me like a false binary, since there's always some exogenous trigger and then a political response that decides how many people die — but maybe the binary is doing real work and I'm missing it. Same for the Irish case, which keeps getting brought up as the one that doesn't fit Sen because the food the poor actually ate really did disappear.

And one more thing: don't give me a reading list or citations. I'm not asking for those. If you think my whole framing is wrong, say so and tell me what the series should be built around instead.
