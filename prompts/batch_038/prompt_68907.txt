A conference volume on the origins of the First World War is to open with a jointly signed statement from two historians who have spent decades on opposite sides of the controversy set off by Fritz Fischer's Griff nach der Weltmacht (1961, published in English as Germany's Aims in the First World War). The first maintains that German expansionist aims explain why the July Crisis of 1914 ended in a general war, and that Germany consequently bears a share of responsibility decisively larger than any other power's. The second maintains that the crisis is explained by an interaction system — alliance credibility, mobilization timetables, the operational requirement to strike west before turning east, and each capital's fear of falling behind the others — in which intentions were largely downstream of constraints, and that ranking the powers at all is a category mistake rather than a finding. Their editor has commissioned the document an adversarial collaboration ought to produce. Draft it.

Before the history, the statement must fix the vocabulary, and I want three specific things settled there. First, both parties should agree to distinguish causal contribution (what a power's decisions changed about the course of events), moral culpability (what attaches to agents in virtue of what they knew, intended, and could have done otherwise), and liability to bear costs (the function the peacemakers in fact needed discharged). Second, they should record, side by side, which of those three the opening article of the reparations section of the Treaty of Versailles was asserting when it provided that "The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies." Third, they should say explicitly which of the three they have each been arguing about all these years, and whether any of their disagreement survives once the decomposition is made.

The body of the document should then contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both can sign, each labelled historical, methodological, or conceptual. These should be substantive, not platitudes — for instance, that the Septemberprogramme of September 1914 postdates the outbreak of war by several weeks, and that this fact is compatible with both camps' readings; that the December 1912 meeting between the Kaiser and his senior military advisers is genuine but its evidential weight is exactly what is in dispute; that a state's responsibility presupposes decision procedures, informational access, and a real alternative, so that the two camps need an account of when a government can be treated as an agent before they can disagree about this one.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the disagreement stated neutrally; the wording the aims-first historian would accept as a description of her own position; the wording the systems historian would accept for his; and what would have to be shown for each side to revise, as distinct from merely to be embarrassed. Where a row records a dispute about which element of a jointly sufficient set is salient given an explanatory interest, say so, and say whether salience of that kind can support a determinate ranking at all.

3. A cruxes section. Two cruxes should be treated as fixed by the editor, and you may add others. The first is the direction of inference between aims and decisions: were expansionist aims causally upstream of the July risk-taking, or articulated afterwards as wartime improvisation and coalition management? State the evidential standard each camp would accept for settling the direction — what a pre-July-1914 document would have to say, and what pattern of post-hoc rationalization would count against the aims-first reading. The second is whether intentional states bear on the verdict at all: suppose the record contained an explicit German policy memorandum from before July 1914 ranking a localized Austro-Serbian war against a general war and stating a preference. Each camp must record in advance what such a document would imply for its position, and the systems historian must say whether it would move him, given that on his view the constraint set made German preferences largely irrelevant to the outcome. Relatedly, both should give pre-registered predictions for the counterfactual in which Russia stands down and the crisis remains localized.

4. A short annex identifying the terms that have been doing illegitimate work in the exchange — guilt, responsibility, aggression, preventive war, inevitability, continuity, and above all the referent of the war itself, since the July crisis, the four-year conflict, and the separate decision at each capital are three different objects of assessment — with replacement vocabulary each side can live with.

Do not adjudicate between the two historians, do not dramatize their exchange, and do not append a bibliography or notes. Where a party appeals to documentary evidence, describe the document or class of document rather than quoting it. The test of the exercise is that a reader who shares neither position could read the result and say precisely what is still in dispute and what would end it.
