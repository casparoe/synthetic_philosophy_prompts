I teach a third-year seminar on the philosophy of history — 14 students, mostly philosophy majors with three or four history majors mixed in, meets twice a week for 100 minutes. We're in week four and next week they read Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation, the one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life. For six years I've handled this by lecturing the three modes straight at them — monumental, antiquarian, critical — and it never lands. They write the three words in their notes like a taxonomy of butterflies and reproduce them on the midterm, and the thing that's actually interesting about the piece, namely that each way of using the past has a characteristic disease of its own and that you can't choose between them just by getting more of the evidence right, goes completely over thier heads. So this year I want to hit them with an activity before they've read a word of it, and I don't want to lecture beforehand.

Design me a 100-minute in-class activity that gets them writing history in those three modes themselves and then running into the problem. Rough shape I have in mind, and feel free to improve it or tell me it's wrong: small groups all get the same dossier on one episode, plus a sealed instruction about who they're writing for and what that audience needs from the past, which differs from group to group; each produces something short; we read them out; and then the class has to decide which version actually gets published, with no neutral way to settle it. The moment I want them to feel is the one where they notice that pointing back at the sources doesn't break the tie, and that the standard they're groping for is something about what an audience can metabolize and go on living by. Don't name the modes until after that point — the reveal is the last ten minutes and I'd like you to write out exactly what I say when I put the three words on the board.

Two practical things. First, write the handouts out in full — the dossier documents and the sealed instructions — so I can just print them; everything should be self-contained, no prep beyond a photocopier and a whiteboard. I'd rather the episode be non-American and at least a century old, and if you use a real one, keep the documents clearly flagged as pedagogical paraphrases instead of dressing them up as primary sources — or else just invent a plausible composite (a company town, a botched colony, a disputed inheritance, whatever you like). Invented is fine with me as long as its rich enough that the very same facts genuinely feed all three modes, and you should say which route you took and why. Second, give me the debrief in detail, mapping particular moments of the activity onto the three modes and onto the uses-versus-disadvantages structure; tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time it's run — my guess is one group writes something so obviously propagandistic that everybody laughs, another just lists dates, and somebody assigned the critical mode refuses on principle to put their own people on trial — and how I steer it; and be frank about which parts of Nietzsche's argument the activity can't reproduce, because I suspect it can't get at the claim that a healthy culture needs all three in some balance, nor at what he means by life and by plastic power, and I'd rather know that going in than discover it in the room. Also tell me whether 100 minutes is too tight for 14 people and what to cut. American English, please.
