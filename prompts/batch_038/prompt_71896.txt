I'm the token grad student on our university's style-guide advisory committee — it's "advisory," which I already suspect is part of the problem — and Thursday we vote on three changes to the official guide: singular *they* as the default for a person of unspecified gender, *chair* instead of *chairman*, and *first-year* instead of *freshman*. Half the meeting got derailed by the dean of the college, who said some version of "it's just a convention" twice, and not the same thing both times. The first time it meant the old usages have no authority beyond the fact that people happen to use them, so if we prefer something else we can just have something else. The second time it meant there's no fact of the matter about correctness here at all, so the vote is really about values rather than about usage. A student rep shot back that the committee can't decree anything because people will keep writing *chairman* no matter what we publish, and a linguist colleague answered him with "conventions change all the time, look how fast singular they took off" — which somehow conceded and denied the same point. Nobody ever said what they thought they were disagreeing about, and I'm the one who got volunteered to write up the conceptual terrain before Thursday.

I half-read Lewis's *Convention* for a seminar last year and remember the gist: a convention is a regularity in some recurrent situation, nearly everyone conforms, nearly everyone prefers that others conform on condition that others do, and — the part I think matters here — some other regularity would have served just as well. What I can't do is turn that into a reply to the dean. So can you lay out, as a menu I can literally carry into the meeting, the distinct precise claims that "it's just a convention" and "conventions are arbitrary" could be making? Start with whatever is a platitude nobody in that room would deny, work through the substantive and defensible ones, and end with whatever is flatly false or at least never actually argued for. For each reading say whether it's true and, separately, whether it would license the job it was being asked to do, which were four different jobs: (a) the committee's changing the guide, (b) the claim that nobody has any reason to keep using *chairman*, (c) the claim that there's no fact of the matter which usage is correct, and (d) the prediction that publishing a change will produce the change.

Two more things. Someone in that seminar said Lewis himself distinguishes more than one sense in which a convention is arbitrary, and that people routinely slide between them without noticing; if that's right I'd like those senses pulled apart and mapped onto the menu, and if you think my seminar-mate was confabulating, just say so. And I want to know which side Lewis's account actually lands on for the student rep's objection — if a convention is self-perpetuating in the way he describes, does an advisory committee with a style guide have any standing to change one, or does change only become possible when there's some focal authority capable of making the new expectation common knowledge? Which would also tell me whether our mandate is even the right instrument. Feel free to tell me the whole thing is a bad analogy, since *chairman* isn't obviously a coordination problem the way driving on the right is — but if so, tell me exactly where the analogy breaks, because that's the spot the dean will press on.
