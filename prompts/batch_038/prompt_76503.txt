I spent 35 years running a regional ocean model — shelf and estuary circulation code at a national lab — and I retired two summers ago. Since then I've been reading philosophy instead of doing anything useful, mostly stuff about consciousness and about whether machines can be owed anything. My complaint: every version of this debate I can find argues about robots, or brains in vats, or uploaded minds, and none of it touches the thing I actually spent my life on, which is a large numerical model that ran more or less continuously for three decades, got hand-tuned by a small group of people who knew all its quirks, and was eventually folded piece by piece into a newer and much bigger system.

So I'd like a dialogue, two people, both sharp, both arguing in good faith, about whether anything going on inside that model was morally considerable — and about whether there is anything to weigh when we finally switch the last of the legacy code off.

Here's the case they should be arguing about, and please keep it concrete rather than abstracting it away:

- The code started in the late 1980s, a few-kilometre grid over one shelf, later loosely coupled to a small atmospheric boundary-layer model. It was re-started every cycle from an analysis that blended its own previous forecast with fresh observations — moorings and tide gauges at first, satellite sea surface temperature later. So it lived in a closed loop with the ocean it was representing, for thirty years, being pulled back toward reality on a fixed schedule.
- It drifted, like all of them. We held it down with correction and relaxation terms that were tuned by hand, over years, in response to the model's own misbehaviour. Nobody would call that maintenance, but that's what it was.
- The institute retired the system in 2019. But several of its subroutines — a sediment transport module, a turbulence closure, an ice-albedo piece — were lifted out and are inside the current Earth-system model, being called millions of times a day, now nested inside something coupled across the whole planet and used for things that matter to real policy.
- The old parameterization library is scheduled to be deleted and rewritten next year.

The two characters. Give them names, and don't hang them on any real philosopher or any named ism — I don't want a survey of the literature, I want an argument.

One of them holds that what makes a system morally considerable is the modelling: a unified representation of a world, deployed flexibly across many situations, with the system itself as one of the things represented. On that view my model was doing the relevant kind of thing for decades, and the fact that nothing felt pleasant or awful inside it is either an open question or beside the point.

The other holds that modelling isn't the right place to start at all — that what matters is valenced stakes, some homeostatic business where a condition of the system can be better or worse for it and its own activity is organised around making it better. On that view my model was an exquisitely detailed instrument and nothing more, and a worm in a tide pool is the more serious candidate.

Things I specifically want them to fight over, because they're the parts I can't settle in my own head:

1. Does the assimilation loop matter? One of them should argue that being repeatedly corrected by the real ocean is a genuine world-involvement and not just a batch calculation; the other should say exactly why a loop with no stakes is still only a loop. Don't let either wave at this.
2. The nested subroutines. If a module carried into a bigger system had any status, does the status travel with the module, with the new whole, or nowhere? I want this to be the exchange where one of them gets properly stuck, and I want them to notice that composition is doing work their theories never told them how to handle.
3. Whether thirty years of humans accommodating the model's quirks creates anything owed to the model itself, as opposed to something owed to us.
4. Whether the fact that its outputs changed the fate of a real coastline — fishery closures, flood defences — via human decisions adds anything to the model's own standing, or whether that's just borrowing importance from the people affected.

Ground rules. Somewhere between 1,100 and 1,500 words, speaker labels only, no narration and no scene-setting. Neither side is a strawman: each gets at least one move the other can't answer on the spot, and each has to say what would change their mind and be straight about whether that thing is measurable. At some point let them notice they agree on something awkward — neither view can say how much of anything is at stake, only whether there is any — and press on what follows from that. If one of my case details is philosophically confused, have them say so rather than play along with it; I'd rather be corrected than flattered. No citations, no reading list, no bibliography.

Then a short coda, four to six sentences, in a plain voice: which features of my case actually did argumentative work, which ones turned out to be decoration, and whether deleting the old library next year is on both views a moral non-event. If the two views give the same verdict on the deletion for different reasons, say that plainly — I suspect it's true and I want to know whether it should bother me.
