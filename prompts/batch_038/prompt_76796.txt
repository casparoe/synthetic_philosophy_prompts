I have been reading about interpretations of probability on my own for a couple of years now — no academic training, just enough persistence to get myself properly stuck on one case.

The case is the one usually referred to as the Blue Bus case: an old American decision, I believe from the first half of the last century. As I understand it — and please work from my description rather than from your recollection of the record, and tell me if you think I have the shape of it wrong — a pedestrian was struck and injured by a bus after dark. There were no eyewitnesses able to identify the vehicle, no paint transfer, no maintenance records, nothing connecting any particular bus to the accident. What the plaintiff could establish was that the defendant company operated roughly four out of every five of the buses running in that city. The court held this was insufficient and the plaintiff lost. The hypothetical version that gets discussed asks whether a juror who inferred, from that evidence alone, that it was the defendant's bus would have been reasoning well.

My difficulty is not the usual one. I am not asking whether statistics make good evidence in general, or about knowledge and sensitivity, or about how the standards of proof work; I have read enough to know that is a large literature with its own vocabulary. What I cannot get straight is what the 0.8 in that case is supposed to *be*, and whether the answer to that changes the verdict.

Take the three interpretations I know. On a frequentist reading, 0.8 is a long-run relative frequency within some reference class — but which class? All buses in the city, all buses on that route, all buses on that route at that hour, all buses that have ever been involved in a collision on that street? The numbers come out differently, and nothing in the case seems to select the right one. On a subjectivist reading, 0.8 is the degree of belief a coherent juror ought to have, and since that is comfortably above a preponderance, a civil plaintiff should simply have won — which makes the actual outcome look like an error about the law rather than about probability. On a propensity reading, 0.8 would have to be a single-case physical disposition belonging to that situation, and I cannot see what disposition it would be: the accident happened once, and there is no physical setup out there that is loaded eighty percent toward the defendant's bus.

My own suspicion, which is what I most want pressure applied to, is that all three are aimed at the wrong target, and that the case turns on something I have not found a name for. Roughly: the frequency the plaintiff offered is a fact about how often buses of that kind pass along, while what the court needs is a fact about what caused this one injury, and no quantity of the first kind seems to turn into the second kind, however precisely the first kind is measured. I do not know whether that is a genuine fourth option, or propensity talk with the metaphysics quietly removed, or just the reference-class problem wearing a different coat.

Working through the case, then:

1. For each of the three interpretations, say what the 0.8 is, whether the inference from it to the defendant's bus is licensed, and what verdict follows. Where an interpretation has nothing to say — and I expect there are places — say so flatly rather than stretching it to cover the case.

2. Take my variant. Same accident, same darkness, same total absence of identifying evidence, but instead of a market share the plaintiff produces a dispatch log showing that of the buses sent out on that route that evening, four in five belonged to the defendant. The number is identical, yet the epistemic situation feels different to me. Explain, on your preferred interpretation, why it feels different — or argue that it is not different and that my instinct is simply a mistake.

3. Tell me which interpretation best explains why the court was right to throw the case out, or, if you think the court was wrong, which best explains that. I want a choice with reasons attached, not a weighing of pros and cons.

4. Give me a verdict on my fourth-option idea: keep it as something distinct, reduce it to one of the three, or reject it outright. Say which, and say what would have to be true for you to change your mind about it.

Please commit to a position. I would much rather have a definite answer I can argue with than a balanced survey that leaves the decision to me. At the same time, flag any point where you are relying on a contested reading of one of these interpretations, and any point where you suspect I have the legal facts wrong.
