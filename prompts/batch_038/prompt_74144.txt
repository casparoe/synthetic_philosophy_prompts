ok so I've been arguing about Malthus with a friend who does agricultural economics and I've realised I can't actually state my own position in a way that risks anything, which is why I want you to take a swing at it. Not the "Malthus was wrong because yields went up" version — I can get that anywhere — I want the specific claim below treated as the target.

My view isn't the prophecy version. I don't think Malthus dated any famines, and I don't think the doubling-every-25-years business is load bearing, it's just an illustrative rate. What I hold is narrower: the principle of population is a constraint claim about energy and land. Per-capita food in any society is bounded by the ratio of external energy inputs per hectare to what a person needs, and whenever a society finds a big new energy subsidy — fresh land, imported nitrates, cheap irrigation diesel, whatever — it spends that subsidy on more people rather than on permanently richer people, with a lag of a couple of generations. On this reading the last two centuries are not a refutation at all, they're one enormous instance of the same mechanism operating with the boundary temporarily pushed out, and "right but early" is the honest description: the law didn't fail, the constraint moved. It also means I don't get to treat the demographic transition as a counterexample — the preventive check just arrived by way of contraception and girls' schooling instead of misery and vice. The check still fired, it got cheaper and quieter. So Malthus was right, he was just early by roughly two hundred years, and the interesting question is when the boundary stops moving.

What would count against this? Rank the defeaters, fatal at the top and merely awkward at the bottom, and sort them by which clause of the above they actually bite on, because I now suspect the energy-constraint half and the "spends the subsidy on people not on wealth" half are much less tightly welded together than I've been assuming and only the second one is doing the Malthusian work. Concretely: what would the data have to look like, over how long, in how many places, controlling for what, before I'd have to say the mechanism is false rather than delayed? How much does the demographic transition really hurt me, given that fertility fell hardest exactly where incomes rose hardest, which looks like the opposite of the wage-to-children response the model needs? Is there any case of a sustained multi-generation rise in per-capita consumption with no net new land or energy subsidy that I'd have to count as a genuine counterexample instead of an accounting trick — and does a place that imports its food count as having the subsidy or not, because I can see that question swallowing my whole position. Also whether the constraint half is secretly a truism: every society eats what it can grow, and all the content sits in the second clause, which may be doing no work whatsoever.

Then the part I actually care about. Have I insulated this into unfalsifiability? "The boundary moved" is available as a reply to every possible outcome — good harvests, famines that failed to happen, population collapse in the richest countries — and I can't tell whether that makes the view degenerate or just slow, the way a law in geology or epidemiology is slow, where a claim that has never been tested on the relevant timescale isn't thereby untestable. Pick one and say so without hedging, and if it's the bad one, tell me whether a claim of this shape (long run, aggregate, mechanism based, no dated predictions, unfalsifiable in any one human lifetime) is worth holding at all or whether I should drop it. Two things would move me most: a historical case where my mechanism was plainly running and should have produced a Malthusian outcome and didn't, with no auxiliary assumption about inputs that rescues it; and anything showing my position collapses into a rival's. On that second point I'm already worried — if what I've described turns out to be indistinguishable from the Boserupian line that population pressure is what drives agricultural invention, just with a gloomy tone laid over it, I'd rather be told that straight.
