Trying to figure out what I actually think about when a flat-out assertion is OK, just going off my gut on cases. Doctor tells a patient "this works nine times out of ten, you'll get better" — fine. Me saying "your ticket lost" when it's forty million to one against — not fine, even though the odds there are way better than the doctor's. Telling a friend "I'll be there at eight" — fine, and I don't know that, I could get hit by a bus; but "it won't rain tomorrow" on the strength of a glance out the window — not fine, and both are just claims about the future. Guy in a county full of convincing fake barns looks at the one real barn and says "that's a barn" — I want to call that improper even though he's right. Someone who knows something on a reliable friend's word but has nothing to offer when challenged, just "I know" — I think that's still fine. "I think the meeting's at three" is fine where "the meeting's at three" isn't, same evidence both times. And a publicist who knows the thing is defective saying "it's completely safe" is obviously bad, but I suspect bad for a different reason entirely and I don't want the principle to be about that one.

The case I keep flip-flopping on: you say the bridge is safe on the strength of a proper inspection report, and it goes down a week later. Was the assertion bad all along, or good when you made it with something still owed now? Is there one principle that gets most of these, which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and what does it say about a case I havn't listed — me confidently asserting a conclusion I just derived from two things I do know, without checking the arithmetic?
