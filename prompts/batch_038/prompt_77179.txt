I draw letterforms for a living, and I want to identify a pattern I keep running into but have no vocabulary for. Let me set out two instances before stating it.

First. A lowercase 'a' from a particular book hand — a two-storey form, but with the bowl broken open at the lower left and the shoulder reduced to a flick — is genuinely ambiguous in isolation. I have shown it alone on a card to fluent readers and received 'a', 'o', 'd', and 'e'. Show the same glyph on a specimen sheet, mid-alphabet in that same hand, and the hesitation vanishes: everyone reads 'a' at once and reports that it could hardly have been anything else. No linguistic context is doing this work, since a specimen sheet has none. What seems to settle it is the rest of the set. That hand has no 'd' with a straight ascender, so the flick cannot be a 'd'; its 'e' carries a horizontal bar, so the open bowl cannot be an 'e'; its 'o' closes fully and is rounder throughout. I cannot name any positive feature of the glyph that is on its own both necessary and sufficient for its being an 'a', yet inside the alphabet the identification is not merely probable. It feels non-negotiable.

Second. When I alter one letter, other letters I have not touched begin to look wrong. Closing the lower bowl of a 'g' will make an unrelated 'y' read as slack, and the repair is almost never local: either the 'g' is reverted, or several neighbours are re-adjusted until the alphabet reads as a different but coherent system. Evaluation appears to attach primarily to the set and only derivatively to its members, and a change to one member retroactively changes what the others are.

What I want is a name for this, or failing that, a name I ought to give it. I know the neighbouring ideas and am dissatisfied with all of them. Family resemblance describes overlapping similarities without a shared core, but what I am describing looks closer to the opposite: identity fixed by what an item excludes rather than by what it resembles, to the point that its positive features can be partly missing and the identification still holds. I have half-absorbed the structuralist line that the elements of a system have no positive terms, only differences; a line like that is not a concept, and I cannot tell whether my first instance is an example of it or merely sounds like one. Ordinary perceptual context effects — a shape read differently because of its neighbours, priming, the advantage conferred by embedding a letter in a word — look like a near-miss, and I suspect they are one, but I would rather be told why than assume it.

So: is there an established concept, in philosophy, linguistics, aesthetics, or the theory of type design, for identity conferred by position within a closed set, together with the corollary that altering one member alters the rest? If several candidates fit partially, rank them and say precisely what each one misses. If none fits, coin a term, define it with explicit boundaries, and give me at least two things that look like instances of it and are not — including whether my second instance genuinely belongs under the same heading as the first, or whether I have conflated two phenomena and should be asking about them separately.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — the identification or its absence, plus the single strongest candidate — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of the question back to me. I am not ultimately after the label; I am after the carving, so spend your words on what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
