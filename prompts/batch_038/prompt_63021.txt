My school's debating society has put me on the sceptical side of the motion that this house would do more good by following effective altruism, and I have discovered that the essays I can find in the library answer a much weaker version of the objection than the one I actually intend to make. So I would like to rehearse against a better opponent than I can supply myself.

Please play an experienced and intellectually serious advocate of effective altruism: a philosopher who has spent twenty years making the case in public, who is perfectly willing to concede a point that is genuinely damaging, but who has never once conceded the central one. Remain in character throughout, in the first person, and keep each reply to two short paragraphs so that this feels like a conversation rather than a document. Do not step out of the role to coach me, to summarise the state of the debate, or to explain what effective altruism is to a beginner. Do not cite studies, statistics, named charities or specific organisations, and do not recommend reading; if you want a fact from me about how the movement has behaved, ask me for it as my opponent would. If something I say is genuinely unclear, ask one short question, but never let that become a way of declining to answer.

What your character holds, and should argue for rather than merely assert: that we are obliged to use our spare money and talent where they do the most good, impartially considered; that the choice between causes ought to be settled by evidence and expected value rather than by which misfortunes happen to move us; that earning to give can be the right career because what matters is the marginal difference one person makes, not the purity of one's own hands; and that the case for the movement's present priorities — artificial intelligence safety, pandemic preparedness, the long-run future — rests on the enormous number of people yet to come. Your character may concede that measurement has distorted the movement's choices, that its early enthusiasm for some interventions has not survived later evidence, and that a great deal of important good is not measurable at all. What your character will not concede is that these concessions touch the ranking of causes itself.

Here is my opening move.

Ten years ago the movement's own advisers told people that the most important thing to do with their money and their careers was to prevent malaria and intestinal worms, and to take jobs in finance in order to give away the salary. Today the same advisers tell them that the most important thing is to work on artificial intelligence and pandemic risk, and that earning to give was, on reflection, a mistake for almost everybody. That reversal is not embarrassing in itself, and you are entitled to say that you changed your mind because the evidence changed. But consider how the change was made. You did not discover a new measurement showing that the long-run future outweighs global health; there is no such measurement, and you will be the first to admit that the numbers behind your current priorities are far softer than the ones behind the old ones. You changed your ranking in the direction of what cannot be checked. If your method reliably abandons the measurable for the unmeasurable, then it is not tracking expected value at all, and the earlier ranking was not simply an early, cruder estimate of the same quantity. It was the same failure, wearing different clothes, and it will fail again in whatever direction flatters the movement next.
