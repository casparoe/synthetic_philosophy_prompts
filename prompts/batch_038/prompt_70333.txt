I work on backend infrastructure at a mid-size company that was recently approached by a defense prime about hosting and fleet-scheduling software, and the argument that broke out in our internal channel left me with one sentence I can't parse. A colleague — the sort who reads a great deal of military history but has no more formal philosophy training than I do — opposed taking the contract on the grounds that the line we were drawing (we will do logistics, never targeting) is self-serving nonsense, because in a modern industrial economy there are no innocent civilians. Everyone is inside the war machine now, he said: taxpayers, voters, plant workers, the farmers supplying the garrison, the engineers writing the code. The protected category has simply evaporated, and pretending otherwise is a way for people like us to feel clean. I found the argument disturbing and also, I think, wrong, but each time I tried to say what was wrong with it I ended up conceding some part of it, which is why I am writing this up carefully instead of arguing further.

My suspicion is that the sentence is doing several different jobs at once, that at least one of them is trivial, and that the one he actually needs is not. I would like the candidate readings laid out as a menu rather than an essay. For each claim the sentence could be making — for instance that civilians are causally entangled in the conduct of war, that they are morally culpable for it, that they have forfeited some right, that they are legitimate targets, that the word innocent in this tradition never meant not-guilty but something closer to not-engaged-in-harming (someone once told me that and I may have garbled it), and any others you think are genuinely in play — please state it precisely enough that I could see what would count for or against it; say whether it is a platitude, a live contested thesis, or simply false; and say whether it is compatible with any combatant/noncombatant distinction surviving at all. Then tell me which reading or readings his argument requires in order to reach his conclusion, and what he would have to add to get from each of those to the logistics-versus-targeting point, since I suspect the gap is where my objection lives.

One further thing I cannot resolve. I have seen the same sentence deployed by people arguing the opposite way about the same war: as a complaint that our side's strikes are indiscriminate, and separately as a reason to think that a conscripted nineteen-year-old on the attacking side and a worker at the shell-casing plant that employs the whole town are in morally the same boat, so that the older view that soldiers on both sides of an unjust war fight blamelessly is either vindicated by this or rendered pointless. Say whether these uses are one reading doing double duty or a coincidence of phrasing, and if they turn out to be different readings, which of them the plant-worker-and-conscript comparison actually supports. I would rather have the distinctions and the verdicts than a summary of the debate, and please do not soften the readings you think are flatly false.
