I am a junior, and for the past year I have been sitting in on a Saturday-morning reading group at my town's public library that is mostly adults. Last month the group took up Malthus, and two members — Ruth, a retired high school history teacher, and Dev, a college sophomore majoring in economics — got into a sustained argument that the rest of the group eventually gave up on and that I could not resolve. I wrote the main exchanges down as carefully as I could, in order.

(1) Ruth opened by saying Malthus is the textbook case of a confident theory that was simply refuted. He argued that population increases geometrically — he took the American colonies as his evidence and concluded that population there had been doubling roughly every twenty-five years — while food production increases only arithmetically, so any improvement in living standards gets competed away by additional mouths until wages fall back to subsistence. Instead, world population has gone from about a billion in 1800 to about eight billion today, and average real income per person is many times what it was in his lifetime. A prediction that has been failing continuously for more than two centuries is not "right but early," she said; it is wrong.

(2) Dev replied that Ruth was confusing a mechanism with a forecast. The mechanism is this: whenever resources per person rise, people rear more surviving children, and that pushes resources per person back down. That mechanism, he said, held essentially everywhere until the nineteenth century — for most of history living standards barely moved while populations grew, which is what the theory predicts. It stopped operating only because of two things Malthus had no way to anticipate: sustained yield-increasing technical change in agriculture, and a voluntary reduction in family size as incomes rose. So on Dev's account Malthus was right about the trap, right that the trap existed and that humanity had lived inside it, and mistaken only about the boundary conditions.

(3) Ruth said that formulation was not a defense but an unfalsifiability machine. On Dev's account, there is no possible history of the world that could count against Malthusianism: if living standards collapse toward subsistence, the mechanism operated; if living standards rise for two hundred years, the mechanism was suspended by an auxiliary assumption about technology and fertility. A hypothesis compatible with every outcome, she said, has been confirmed by none of them, and "right but early" is just the label we attach to that.

(4) Dev said Ruth's standard would outlaw all of science, because a hypothesis is never tested in isolation. Testing Malthus's mechanism necessarily involves auxiliary assumptions about agricultural technique, institutions, and how family size responds to income; when a prediction fails you can always locate the failure in the auxiliaries, and doing so is not automatically cheating. He cited the discovery of Neptune, where astronomers preserved a failing prediction by postulating an unobserved planet and were vindicated.

(5) Ruth said the analogy worked against him. The astronomers said where to point the telescope, in advance, and the planet was there. Dev's auxiliary, she said, was manufactured only after the fact and licenses no prediction anyone could have stated in 1800.

(6) Dev said the auxiliary is independently documented rather than manufactured: cereal yields per acre have risen several-fold, and falling fertility followed rising income and falling child mortality in country after country in the same sequence. Those, he said, are separate bodies of evidence that would have to be invented wholesale if the patch were ad hoc.

(7) Ruth then asked him to state the discipline: if the theory is still live, which observation would show him it is refuted, and by when. Dev answered that if richer people everywhere turned out to have systematically more children, and kept having them, then the suspension he described would be false. Ruth said that would confirm the mechanism rather than refute it, so he had handed her a confirmation condition and called it a refutation condition. Dev then offered a second answer: the theory would be refuted if the pre-industrial trap turned out never to have existed at all — for instance, if evidence showed living standards rising substantially and sustainedly long before any of the agricultural or fertility changes he had cited. Ruth said he had by then quietly exchanged the theory for a different one.

I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general essay about Malthus. Please rate each of the seven numbered moves — treating a move as whatever was said at that step, by either party — on a scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify each rating in a few sentences, identifying the specific premise or inference that is doing the work. Where a move is partly right, say which part is right and which is not. Then tell me who was more right overall and on what the verdict mainly turns. Finally, tell me what both of them were treating as settled when it is actually the crux; in particular, say whether "Malthus's theory" is one claim or several distinguishable claims that could have different verdicts, and say whether the income-and-fertility relationship Dev is relying on in move (2) is the same relationship Malthus himself assumed or the reverse of it, and what that does to his boundary-condition reply.

Please begin your response with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the overall verdict, stated in one sentence — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restating of the argument back to me.
