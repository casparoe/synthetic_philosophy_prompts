So this actually happened at my job and I want to turn it into something for our end-of-year team thing, but also I'm auditing a philosophy-of-social-science seminar and my tutor said if I could get the material to *do* something it'd count toward participation, so I'm trying to kill two birds. I work in ops at a mid-size logistics firm, about 90 people over three floors, and for years there's been an unwritten arrangement about the two microwaves in the second-floor kitchen: the left one is for reheating your own lunch, the right one is the "fish one." Nobody decided this, nobody can say when it started, nobody ever mentioned it out loud, and it just held. Then a new starter emailed all-staff to ask whether it was official policy, and the thread ran for nine days and forty-one replies before facilities physically unplugged one of them.

Can you write that thread as a piece of satire? I mean an actual email thread, headers and all (From / To / Date / Subject), around 850 words, maybe nine to twelve messages, with Subject lines that get progressively more unhinged with Re: and FWD:. The voice should be ordinary corporate internal comms — nobody in it is a philosopher, nobody cites anything or name-checks any real book, and the one person who took a course once should deploy the idea slightly wrong, in a specific way, without being the butt of the joke. The humour has to come from the shape of the situation, not from winking at the reader. What I want a specialist to recognise is that the piece gets the difference between "we all do X" and "we all should do X" exactly right, and shows how fast a group slides from one to the other and back again.

A few things it needs to hit, none of them stated as such:

- The first email should be a genuinely innocent question that destroys the thing it's asking about. The arrangement was working precisely because nobody had to know it was an arrangement.
- Every attempt to pin it down needs an announcement about the announcement. Someone asks for confirmation of receipt, then someone asks for confirmation that everyone confirmed, and the regress should just be visible in the thread rather than commented on.
- Someone from HR wants to make it a rule with a sanction attached. The ops manager, dryly, points out that the moment there's a penalty people are doing it for the penalty and not because they expect each other to — so there's no arrangement left to enforce. Let that land as a workplace observation, not a thesis.
- Somebody drags fairness into what was purely a "which one do we use" question, and the thread should make it dawningly obvious that this is now a different *kind* of problem, with winners and losers, because nobody can agree on the answer anymore. Nobody is allowed to articulate why.
- The ending: facilities plug the microwave back in, the whole thing quietly re-forms around whichever one is nearer the door, nobody ever revisits the thread, and a postscript from the office manager notes it had been eleven years since anyone last thought about it.
- And one last thing — the final email should show a case the tidy analysis can't handle: by then nobody knows what anyone else knows, half the thread has stopped reading, the chain of "everyone knows that everyone knows" is visibly broken, and the pattern still holds. A reader who knows the literature should feel the floor drop out a bit there. Nobody says so.

Keep it deadpan throughout. I don't want a punchline at the end and I don't want any authorial nudging — the target is the reflex to formalise spontaneous regularities (and the HR-ification of everyday life that comes with it), not any individual, so keep everyone reasonable and everyone a little bit right.
