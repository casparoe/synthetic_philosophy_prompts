I am the elected Secretary of a housing association of some eighty households. I own a unit here; I took the office in the spring because the Charter's procedures needed someone who would actually follow them, and until a fortnight ago I had enjoyed the work. Under Article IX §2 the Secretary certifies adopted amendments and that certification is not subject to review by the Assembly, which means there is no one above me to refer this to.

The Charter provisions that matter, in full:

- Article I §1: The Association consists of the persons entered on the Roll of Members, which the Secretary maintains.
- Article VII §3: No unit may be let for a term shorter than thirty days.
- Article IX §1: Any provision of this Charter may be amended, and new provisions may be added, by the affirmative vote of two-thirds of the members present at a meeting of which ten days' notice has been given, provided that the full text of the proposal was circulated with the notice.
- Article IX §2: An amendment takes effect when the Secretary certifies that it has been duly adopted. The Secretary's certification is not subject to review by the Assembly.
- Article X §1: Neither Article IX nor Article X may be amended or repealed.

The Charter says nothing anywhere about how a conflict between two of its own provisions is to be resolved.

A member who has been collecting proxies, and who is understood to act for parties interested in converting units to short lets, has circulated a proposal adding a new Article XI:

"XI §1. From the moment of its certification, this Charter shall be construed and given effect as though Article X had never been adopted.
XI §2. Article IX §1 shall be read as follows: any provision of this Charter may be amended or repealed, and new provisions may be added, by a simple majority of the members present.
XI §3. This Article may not be amended or repealed."

The covering memorandum defends it in these steps:

(1) Article IX §1 confers two distinct powers: to amend existing provisions, and to add new ones.
(2) Article X §1 forbids only the amendment or repeal of Articles IX and X. The proposal does neither. It adds an Article, and it leaves the text of Articles IX and X standing word for word as they are.
(3) So the proposal is within the power conferred by IX §1 and is not caught by the prohibition in X §1.
(4) XI §1 does what it says: once certified, Article X is treated as never adopted, and the entrenchment is gone.
(5) With X gone, XI §2 supplies a simple-majority power of repeal, and nothing then prevents the Assembly from repealing Article IX itself, or Article VII §3.
(6) XI §3 entrenches the new order as firmly as the old order was entrenched, so this is not a temporary seizure but a change of constitution.
(7) Therefore the proposal is in order: a two-thirds majority may replace the entrenched order at a single meeting without touching any entrenched text.

I have not been able to stop at (7). Continuing in the same vein:

(8) If any entrenched order can be displaced in this way, then no provision of this Charter has ever limited the Assembly, and the limits are advisory at most.
(9) The same reasoning reaches Article I §1, which is not entrenched at all. The Assembly could add an Article redefining the Roll — seating new households, striking old ones, and declaring itself the continuing successor of the Association it displaced.
(10) So the Charter does not determine who the Association is, and every certification I have signed, including one from March in which I ruled a member's motion out of order and she accepted my ruling as final, was a formality with nothing behind it.
(11) I cannot accept (10). If it is true, I owe that member a public retraction and I have been exercising an authority I never had.

Each step strikes me as something I could be made to defend in my own name, and I cannot identify which one is false. If it comes to choosing, I should most like to keep (1), (2), and (11).

Three questions. First: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable — what must one already assume for it to look that way? Second: if you think the chain is sound and the real question is which premise to abandon, tell me which abandonment is cheapest and price each of the candidates for me. Third, and most urgently: on Tuesday I must rule on a point of order as to whether Article XI is in order. Tell me what the ruling should be and what reasoning it should give, in terms I can put to eighty households, a good number of whom would like me to say yes. If you think Article X can be redrafted so that this cannot recur, give me the wording — but I want to know whether any wording that actually succeeds does so by making the Secretary the judge of substance rather than form, which is a power I should prefer not to hold.
