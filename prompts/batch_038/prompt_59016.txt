Last month I took up a post as head of long-range planning for a regional water utility serving a basin now in its eleventh consecutive year of drought. The role came with an assignment I did not anticipate: our board's strategy papers have begun to lean heavily on the archaeological record — the Classic Maya, Rapa Nui, the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, occasionally Angkor and Cahokia — as warrant for particular infrastructure and rationing decisions. I read history as an undergraduate and remember enough to be uneasy about these invocations, but I have not been able to say what my uneasiness consists in. I have now been asked to write an internal note setting out when a historical collapse claim may legitimately be used as evidence about us.

Before I write a word of that note, I want the specification. What must an account of societal collapse deliver in order to count as adequate — adequate, specifically, for the use my board is making of it: as evidence bearing on the trajectory of a presently existing, technologically complex, market-integrated society that can read the past and act on what it reads?

Give me the requirements list: the phenomena any such account has to explain, the historical cases it has to get right, the distinctions it must not run together, the methodological constraints it has to respect, and the findings from archaeology, palaeoclimatology, and demography that it cannot contradict. Attach to each requirement the concrete case or consideration that motivates it, so that I can judge whether I actually accept the requirement rather than merely nodding at it.

Then address three things explicitly.

First, separate genuine desiderata from the preferences of particular schools that have taken on the appearance of requirements. I suspect a good many of the standard demands are of the second kind, and I want to be told which ones I am likely to accept only because they happen to suit the conclusion I already lean toward.

Second, identify the requirement that does the most exclusionary work — the one that, applied honestly, would rule out the largest share of the collapse talk currently circulating in our board papers.

Third, tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. My inarticulate suspicion is that an account cannot at once be precise about which quantity is being measured, neutral as between this society failed and this society became something else, and serviceable as a warning to a present-day reader. If you agree, say which of the three has to go and defend the choice. If you disagree, tell me where I am conflating things. This is the part I most need.

Finally, and briefly, say how the main contenders fare against the list: the account that treats collapse as a reduction in sociopolitical complexity driven by diminishing returns on investment in that complexity; the account that treats it as a society's failed choices in the face of largely self-inflicted environmental damage; and the sceptical school for which most purported collapses are better described as transformations, the real losses being of elites and of state apparatus rather than of populations or of ordinary ways of life. Three or four sentences each will do. I care about the list, not the scorecard.

Two house rules. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I cannot chase them and would not trust them in any case; argue from the cases themselves. And where the evidence for a case is genuinely disputed, say so in the body of your answer rather than in passing, because the dispute is precisely what my note has to convey to people who currently do not know it exists.
