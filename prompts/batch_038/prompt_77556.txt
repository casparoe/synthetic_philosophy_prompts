Subsidiarity, as the European Union actually institutionalises it, strikes me as a philosophically peculiar object, and I want to know what it has to assume in order to work. Let me set out the arrangements concretely so that we are reasoning about the same thing rather than about the slogan.

**The EU mechanism.** Subsidiarity operates only where Union competence is not exclusive. The test asks whether the objectives of a proposed act cannot be sufficiently attained by the member states acting at central, regional or local level, and can therefore, by reason of the scale or effects of the proposed action, be better attained at Union level. Proportionality sits beside it and asks only whether the act goes beyond what is necessary to reach the treaty's objectives.

Compliance is policed in two distinct ways. Politically: within eight weeks of a draft's transmission, any national parliament or chamber may send a reasoned opinion objecting on subsidiarity grounds; each of the twenty-seven parliaments holds two votes, one per chamber where there are two; if reasoned opinions reach one third of the votes — one quarter for drafts in the area of justice, freedom and security — the Commission must review the draft and may maintain, amend or withdraw it, stating its reasons (the yellow card). A majority of the votes triggers the orange card, under which, if the Commission maintains the draft, a simple majority of the members of the European Parliament or fifty-five per cent of Council members can stop it. Judicially: the Court of Justice has jurisdiction, reviews with marked deference, and in practice has essentially never annulled an act for breach of subsidiarity alone.

The yellow card has been triggered three times: in 2012 on a proposal concerning the exercise of the right to take collective action, in 2013 on a proposal to establish a European Public Prosecutor's Office, and in 2016 on a proposal to revise the directive on posted workers. In the second and third cases the Commission reviewed and maintained the draft, concluding that it complied with subsidiarity. In the first it likewise found no breach of subsidiarity, and withdrew the draft on the stated ground that it was unlikely to gather the political support needed for adoption. The orange card has never been used.

**Three comparators.** Germany has its own necessity clause: for a large block of concurrent legislative subjects the federation may legislate only if and to the extent that establishing equivalent living conditions across the federal territory, or maintaining legal or economic unity in the interest of the state as a whole, makes federal regulation necessary — a condition the constitutional court polices seriously, while a separate provision permits the Länder to enact deviating rules in specified fields (nature conservation and landscape management, land distribution, spatial planning, water resources, university admission and degrees, property tax). Switzerland makes subsidiarity a general principle governing the allocation and performance of state tasks, commits the federation to assuming only what the cantons cannot discharge, treats the cantons as residual sovereigns, and gives citizens the power to demand a referendum on federal statutes. The United States has no subsidiarity test whatever: enumerated powers, a Tenth Amendment, a presumption against preemption, and a Supreme Court that since the 1980s has held that state sovereignty is protected chiefly by the structure of the federal government rather than by judicial review of the substance of federal statutes — while still policing one narrow point, that Congress may not commandeer state legislatures or executives.

With that in view:

1. Reconstruct the theory of legitimacy the EU mechanism presupposes. In particular, is "better attained" one notion or three — productive efficiency, the epistemic quality of the decision taken at the relevant scale, and the proximity of the decision-maker to those bound by it? Which of these could the yellow-card procedure possibly track, given what it asks national parliaments to do and what it rewards them for doing?

2. Take up the objective-setting problem: the test compares attainment of an objective, but the objective is characterised by the institution proposing to act, and framing an objective as the removal of divergences between national rules makes central action look necessary almost by construction. What does a review procedure that takes the objective as given presuppose about who is entitled to define the question? Is that a flaw in the principle or an unavoidable feature of any competence-allocation rule?

3. Explain the mismatch of addressee. The mechanism empowers national parliaments, not subnational ones, although in federal member states it is the subnational tier whose room for action Union legislation narrows. What conception of the citizen, and of which polity the citizen belongs to, does that allocation of the objection right presuppose?

4. Diagnose the 2012 withdrawal. If the Commission can deny the alleged breach and withdraw anyway for want of political support, is the mechanism a truth-tracking test of a normative principle or a signalling device for measuring salience? Can a practice be what it claims to be while operating as something else?

5. Set the three comparators against the EU. What does the German criterion of equivalent living conditions presuppose, and does it sit in tension with an experimentalist reading of subsidiarity on which diversity of rules across units is itself a good? What does the Swiss reliance on the referendum presuppose that the EU cannot borrow? And does the American refusal to have any necessity test presuppose a rival theory of legitimacy, or is it a refusal to have one — bearing in mind that the anti-commandeering doctrine protects the *form* of state authority without asking whether federal action was necessary?

6. Distinguish carefully what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely fit comfortably with, and identify where the design is philosophically underdetermined — where two incompatible theories of federal arrangement would license exactly the same institutional text.

7. Finally, say what would have to change in practice, not only in doctrine, if the presupposed view were replaced by (a) a purely epistemic subsidiarity concerned with which level produces better decisions, (b) a subsidiarity grounded in the claim of a self-governing demos to decide its own affairs, or (c) an equality-based version of the German kind.

Argue from the material described above; I know these mechanisms and do not need them recounted, and I am not looking for citations or a reading list. Where you think a step of my framing is mistaken, say so rather than accommodating it.
