I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size e-commerce company and about six months ago I accidentally ended up owning our internal metrics platform — the thing everyone builds dashboards and experiment readouts on top of. Which means I'm now the person who has to referee arguments about numbers, and I have no stats background beyond one undergrad probability class I mostly slept through. The argument I keep having, maybe two or three times a month, is Simpson's-paradox-shaped: a PM posts a screenshot showing conversion up 4% overall, an analyst replies that it's actually *down* in every one of the four user segments we track, and the room splits into whoever trusts which screenshot. Last week it was a checkout redesign. Overall conversion up, down for mobile, down for desktop, down for tablet, down for in-app — apparently because a marketing campaign sent us a big wave of high-intent users who happened to skew mobile, so the mix shifted under us. Our data scientist said something like "the reversal is a fact about the weighting, not about the world," which sounded deep and didn't help me decide anything, and I ended up punting to a VP, which I hated. Anything that moves a launch or a pricing decision needs sign-off from me, the PM, and the analytics lead, so I can't just shrug — I have to write down *why* we're believing one number and not the other.

So: can you write me a procedure I can actually run on these? Something a competent engineer on rotation can do in about half an hour with SQL and a spreadsheet, not a stats PhD, ideally as a numbered list of questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, the points where the procedure stops at a definite no-go, and the points where it hands the thing to a human because the question turned out to be about what we're trying to decide rather than about the data. Be blunt about which steps are really just me making a judgment call with extra paperwork attached. I need it to cover: how you even know to start looking for a reversal, since you can't disaggregate by everything and there are thousands of plausible cuts; what to do when the reversal shows up in a cut nobody specified before looking at the data; how to decide whether the aggregate or the disaggregate is the number that should drive the decision, since I suspect that depends on what the decision is and not on any arithmetic; and what happens when the variable that explains the reversal is itself changed by the thing you're evaluating — I have a feeling that's the case that breaks everything, and I want it handled explicitly rather than footnoted.

Then run it end to end on three cases so I can see it work and where it bites: the checkout redesign above; an A/B test where the treatment wins overall but loses inside every cohort defined by *whether users ever hit a certain feature* (a teammate insists this is just Simpson's paradox again and we should report the disaggregate, and I think he's wrong but I can't say why); and a support dashboard where average handle time dropped overall but rose in every agent skill tier. For each one I want the answer the procedure actually produces, plus what I'd have to tell the person who's going to be annoyed by it. And finish with a short list of what this procedure flat out cannot do — the failure modes where it'll hand me a confident wrong number, so I know when to stop trusting it.
