I am preparing a seminar handout on Camus's Reflections on the Guillotine (1957), and to give my students something testable I reconstructed what I take to be the essay's central argument in explicit form. The trouble is that the reconstruction ends somewhere I cannot follow it, and a colleague insists the fault lies in the essay rather than in my reconstruction. I cannot tell whether a step is invalid or whether a premise simply does not belong to Camus. The steps:

(1) Camus's case does not rest on sympathy for the condemned man — he says as much — and he even allows that within a religious framework in which punishment opened onto judgment and atonement, the death penalty could have had a logic of its own. So the objection is not that the state kills a man who may not deserve it. It concerns the manner of the killing.

(2) What separates the state's act from the criminal's is that it is premeditated in a way no private crime can be. Camus's own comparison is to a murderer who warned his victim of the date of his death months ahead and kept him in his power until it arrived, and he says no such monster is to be encountered in private life.

(3) The general principle at work is therefore: an inflicted harm is aggravated by the inflictor's advance knowledge of it and by the length of the interval across which the victim is made to await it.

(4) Both features belong to every sentence a state imposes, not to capital sentences alone. A term of years is designed, dated, counted out, and administered; the prisoner is told its length and lives it day after day under institutional management.

(5) Camus dwells on the interval between sentence and execution as the heart of the horror — the months during which a man knows the date of his own death — and treats the physical event as almost secondary. If the awaiting is what does the harm, then the harm accumulates across it: a life of penal labor contains thousands of such mornings, and the scaffold only one.

(6) So a life of penal labor is a graver wrong than an execution, by Camus's own measure.

(7) Camus nevertheless recommends penal labor for life in place of the guillotine, and proposes that France try the substitution for a decade.

(8) The principle generalizes further still. If foreseen duration is what makes an inflicted harm monstrous, then a state obliged to punish at all should compress the interval as far as the offense permits, so that prompt killing becomes the merciful option and long confinement the most atrocious punishment available. That inverts Camus's presentation of his own recommendation as a humane half-measure.

(9) Therefore either (3) is not the principle Camus is using, or the essay argues from premises that condemn its recommendation more severely than they condemn the institution it sets out to abolish.

I should like to keep (1) and (2), which I believe are faithful to the text, and I should rather not concede (9). So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable to me when I wrote it down? Then price the alternatives. If (3) goes, say what grounds remain for condemning the guillotine once premeditation is no longer doing the work, and whether those grounds can carry the weight the essay's rhetoric puts on them. If (5) or (6) goes instead, tell me precisely which sense of worse is in play at each — worse for the man who undergoes the punishment, or worse as an act of the authority that imposes it — and whether Camus can hold the two apart on principle rather than by stipulation. Note that he appears to think a life of labor is in fact the harsher fate and to prefer it to the scaffold anyway, which suggests he does hold them apart; if you can identify the ground on which he does so, say whether it defeats (8) outright or merely blunts it, and what a defender of (8) could reply.
