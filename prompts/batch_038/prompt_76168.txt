I am designing a constructed script for a novel and have run into a problem I cannot settle myself. Beginning from one seed glyph, I generated forty variants of what is to be the script's equivalent of 'a', each warped a little further than the last. Shown the series, readers sort the glyphs into that-is-the-letter and that-is-not with startling agreement — up to a single variant near the middle, where they split almost exactly in half, each half convinced the other is not looking properly.

Write a dialogue between two theorists who come to my studio to argue over that one glyph. The first holds that a letter is an abstract structural type, of which particular marks are more or less faithful instances, and that style is nothing but the noise of instantiation. The second holds that letters have no essence beyond their differential role in a writing system: what makes a mark the letter is the work it does among its neighbours and in combination, not any shape it shares with the rest of the set. Both should be formidable, and both should answer the other's actual point rather than a convenient version of it — the essentialist explaining why the boundary turns out vague and reader-relative if the type is fixed, the role-theorist explaining why my readers converge so tightly, and why the disputed glyph still reads as the letter to people who have never seen the rest of my script on its own. Neither should walk away with a clean victory, but neither should be humoured either.

After the dialogue, add a brief note distinguishing which turns in the exchange reflect disagreement that is genuinely open among people who work on these questions, and which rest on a contested reading that one of my characters is leaning on as though it were settled.
