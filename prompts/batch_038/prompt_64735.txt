You are a philosopher working on practical rationality and decision theory, with a specialist's command of the literature on transformative experience. Treat what follows as a design problem with pass/fail specifications rather than an essay question: think carefully, and check each specification against your case before you present it.

A brief gloss, so that we are working from the same framework. On L.A. Paul's account, a transformative experience is one that you cannot fully grasp in advance of undergoing it, and transformation comes in two dimensions. An experience is epistemically transformative when you cannot know what it will be like beforehand; it is personally transformative when undergoing it changes your core preferences — who you are and what you care about, not merely your tastes or your circumstances. Her argument, in rough form, is that when an option's outcome is transformative you cannot choose rationally in the standard way: you cannot assign subjective values to the outcomes, because you lack epistemic access to what they are like, and the values you would assign belong to a self that will not survive the experience, so it is unclear whose values should govern the choice. Readers differ about whether that second pillar is on its own sufficient to block rational choice. Make your own interpretive assumption about this explicit at the top of your answer rather than letting it work silently.

Your task: construct a single decision case, concerning a named ordinary adult, that satisfies all of the following.

(a) It is personally transformative in the sense above: taking the option reliably alters the agent's core preferences.
(b) It is epistemically transparent: the agent has first-personal acquaintance with what the experience is like, and that acquaintance is earned by the structure of the case rather than stipulated. Tell me precisely where it comes from.
(c) Despite (a), the choice is rationally decidable: the agent can run an ordinary expected-value comparison over subjective values and reach a defensible decision.
(d) Realism: an ordinary professional, medical, or domestic situation. No neurotechnology, no science fiction, no stipulations about unusual psychology, no deception or misinformation as a plot device, and none of the stock cases — parenthood, vampirism, going to war, sensory-restoration surgery.
(e) The scenario proper runs to a single paragraph of under two hundred words.

The purpose of the case is to probe the second pillar in isolation: whether personal transformation alone can block rational choice once the agent has full epistemic access. That fixes what counts as cheating. A case that merely announces that the agent decides rationally fails; the decidability must be visible in what the agent actually has access to, and the transformation must be genuine rather than a change in circumstances dressed up as a change of self.

After the scenario, deliver the demonstration:

1. Epistemic audit. Show that (b) is met, and argue that the acquaintance you have built in is not merely descriptive knowledge relabelled.
2. Transformation audit. Show that (a) is met: specify what changes in the agent, what stays fixed, and why the change is core rather than peripheral.
3. The calculation. Write out the decision table — at least two acts, at least two outcomes — assign subjective values, and mark each cell as one the agent can fill from her own acquaintance or history versus one she must fill by projection. If every cell turns out to be fillable, say so plainly and explain why that does not beg the question.
4. Verdict. Does the argument still block the choice here? Which pillar gives way, and what exactly about this case makes it give way?
5. Load-bearing feature. Name the single feature of your case that does the work, and say what the case collapses into if that feature is removed.
6. Best counterattack. Give the strongest reply available to a defender of the view that transformative decisions cannot be made rationally, including the version of the reply you find hardest to answer, and state whether you can answer it.
