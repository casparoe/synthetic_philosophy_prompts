I recently read an afterword by a journalist-historian in an otherwise sober collection of essays on counterfactual history, and one paragraph has been annoying me for a week. I have transcribed it as faithfully as I can:

"For most of the twentieth century, historians divided between those who took large structural forces to be everything and those who gave the credit to decisive individuals. Chaos theory has made that quarrel academic. Weather, ecosystems, markets and wars are all systems in which a difference too small to register when the process begins grows until it is larger than any of the causes we ordinarily bother to name; the old rhyme running from a missing nail to a lost kingdom was groping toward this long before anyone could state it precisely. Two things follow. First, the future was never fixed at the level at which historians describe it: macro-states underdetermine what comes next, so a structural account is at best a convenient fiction about averages, and whether a convenient fiction counts as an explanation is the entire question. Second, counterfactual history, so long dismissed as a parlor game, acquires standards at last; if we represent the amplification honestly we can say not only that things might have turned out otherwise but how they would have, and with what probability. What the rhyme gets wrong is the suggestion that the kingdom was hostage to that particular nail. In a system of this kind a change of the same magnitude could have been produced by perturbations almost anywhere in the chain, which is why the search for the decisive turning point is a mistake even though the turning points are real."

I have spent enough years reading rationalist blogs and forums to be reflexively suspicious of arguments of the shape "mathematics has now shown that reality is thus-and-so," but I would rather not simply distrust the paragraph on reflex. My inclinations, which I cannot yet defend: the closing sentence is the strongest thing in it, the second consequence is backwards, and the first is either a confusion or a respectable thesis in a bad hat.

Could you audit the paragraph for me? Specifically:

1. Which claims would a working historian of counterfactuals accept, which would they reject as a misdescription of their own discipline's objection to counterfactuals, and which would they not recognize as live issues at all?
2. Which claims would someone who actually works on dynamical systems wince at? I am especially unsure about the slide from "some systems exhibit sensitive dependence" to "weather, ecosystems, markets and wars all do," and about whether the paragraph is entitled to move from unpredictability to openness.
3. Does the second consequence survive? It looks to me as though the very property invoked to license it is the property that makes it unavailable, but I cannot state that precisely — and I suspect there is a further difficulty about which counterfactual is even being asked.
4. Is anything in the paragraph genuine support for the closing claim, or does that claim rest on quite different grounds the author never mentions?
5. Is there a defensible version of the nail proverb left over once the paragraph is corrected — some sense in which small causes really do have large historical effects — or does the whole family of intuitions dissolve?

Please say how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the point you are least sure of. Where a sentence is wrong only on one contested reading and right on another, say so rather than filing it under error.
