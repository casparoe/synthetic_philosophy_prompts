So I'm not a scientist or anything, I just went down a rabbit hole on this a while back and one part of it won't let go of me.

Apparently there's an actual committee of geologists whose job is to settle the divisions of deep time — the eras and periods and epochs on that colour-coded chart. And the rule is you can't just declare a new epoch because something enormous happened. You have to point at one specific place on the planet, a lake bed or a cliff face or a cave deposit, where there's a physical change in the layers, and argue that this particular layer is the line — and it's supposed to be matchable everywhere else on Earth too. Then a bunch of people vote on it. A few years ago they voted down the Anthropocene, the idea that humans have started a new epoch, largely on the grounds that the proposed start date (around 1950, using radioactive fallout from bomb tests settling into lake mud) was far too recent and the layer of sediment far too thin.

Here's the thing I can't get past. We know to the day when the first atomic bomb went off. There are documents, photographs, and the people who did it wrote down exactly why. None of that counted for anything, because it isn't in the rock. The fallout counted — but only as a chemical stain in a lake, not as an event anybody understood. So it looks like this procedure assumes a real division of time has to be something a total stranger could read off a cliff a million years from now with no records to hand: knowing what happened doesn't matter, only that it left a mark.

But then the complaint was also that it's too recent, that the change is still going on, and that feels like a completely different rule — like your not allowed to name a period you're standing in the middle of. Those don't seem like the same requirement to me, and I don't know which one is doing the real work. It also strikes me as odd that they'll happily define other boundaries by when some tiny shelled creature first turns up in one region, which as far as I can tell was never simultaneous anywhere, while a fallout layer that genuinely was laid down across the whole planet within a couple of years still wasn't good enough.

So what I want is: what picture of time, or of what the timescale is even for, does this whole procedure presuppose? Is that committee finding out a fact about Earth's history, or making a decision about labels — and does the setup present itself as the first while actually doing the second? Please pull apart which of these rules the practice strictly can't do without and which ones it just happens to sit nicely with, and say where a few different underlying views could all license what they actually do. I'd also like to know where the reason they gave and the reason that seems to be operating come apart. And if they'd adopted the opposite rule — let something count as a new period because of what it means to the creatures living through it, even while it's still unfolding — what would have to change about how boundaries get picked, and would the chart stop being the kind of thing it is at all?
