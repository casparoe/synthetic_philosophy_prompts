been going back and forth on Portugal's drug law for years (the 2001 decriminalization thing) and I keep landing on a piece of it that doesn't fit the story everyone tells. The story is "they realized drug use isn't a crime, it's a health problem." But that's not quite what they did. Possession and use for personal amounts — defined as up to a ten-day supply — stayed illegal. They just moved it out of criminal court and into these administrative panels, the dissuasion commisions, staffed by a legal person plus health/social workers, which can fine you and apply other sanctions. And most of the people who end up in front of one aren't dependent users at all — the referrals skew heavily toward non-dependent people, mostly cannabis. So the state still shows up and says "we disapprove and here's the penalty schedule," it just stopped calling it a crime and started calling it treatment outreach.

Meanwhile a bunch of other places went a completely different route on cannabis: full legalization, licensed storefronts, taxed, age-gated, regulated roughly like alcohol. Nobody summons you to a panel to discuss your choices.

So: what view of persons and of state authority does the Portuguese setup actually presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not just labeled. Specifically —

- does it presuppose that using drugs isn't a free choice, or only that it isn't a *criminal* choice? those feel like different claims and I can't tell which one the machinery actually needs
- the whole thing is framed as a disease/health problem. does treating something as an illness require giving up on treating it as chosen? and what does it mean that the sanction menu survives the reframe — if it's really a disease, what are the fines *for*?
- the panel is supposed to get you to consent to treatment, but it can also penalize you, and showing up isn't optional. what does that structure assume about what consent is worth when the alternative is a penalty? is that just ordinary medicine with extra steps, or something else?
- and what theory licenses the legal-storefront route — is it compatible with the Portuguese one or a rival? I keep seeing them lumped together as "the reform side" and I suspect they rest on opposite premises about who owns the decision

Then tell me which parts of the Portuguese practice strictly need the presupposed theory and which are philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where a libertarian or a plain public-health utilitarian could staff the same panels and tell a totally different story about why. Please don't give me "it's complicated, many factors" — commit to a reconstruction and flag where you're extrapolating past what the practice shows.

Last: if you swapped in the legalization theory, what concretely changes — who gets summoned, who decides, what counts as a bad outcome? And is there a coherent version of the Portuguese system that keeps the panels but drops the sanctions entirely?
