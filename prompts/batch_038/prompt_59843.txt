I retired four years ago after a career in physical chemistry — mostly reaction kinetics and nonequilibrium thermodynamics — and have spent the retirement working my way through the philosophy of mind. Last month I attended a public lecture in which the speaker defended the causal efficacy of the mental against Jaegwon Kim's exclusion argument. His central device was an analogy I have since heard repeated by several of his admirers: the mind is to the brain as software is to hardware. The transistors do all of the work, of course, but nobody says that the word processor caused the text on the screen only in a loose or honorific sense, and nobody thinks that citing the program double-counts the causal story already told by the electronics. Anyone who believes the program is excluded by the transistors, he suggested, has simply misunderstood what levels of description are for.

When I raised my hand and proposed that the analogy he ought to have used was one I know rather better — thermodynamics to statistical mechanics — he disposed of it in a single line, to the effect that thermodynamics causes nothing and merely describes, which is exactly why it is the wrong model for a mind that is supposed to make a difference. I left the hall not at all sure he had earned that dismissal, and I would like the question answered properly.

Please audit both analogies against the job they are being asked to do, which is to block the exclusion argument. For each, give me the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — identifying what plays the role of the mental property, the physical property, the principle of causal closure, the effect, and the threat of systematic overdetermination. Then say where the mapping breaks, and, more importantly, which features of the source domain are being quietly imported into the target that the target may not possess. In the software case I suspect at least one: that a program is individuated by its functional organization independently of any particular substrate, so that "the same program" can be recognized across implementations, which is a substantial assumption about how mental properties are individuated and not a neutral fact about computers.

Separate the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the intended use from those that are merely decorative. Then answer the question the lecturer refused. Are the two cases — software/hardware and thermodynamics/statistical mechanics — on the same footing with respect to exclusion, or does one of them do something the other cannot? I could not articulate the relevant differences on the spot, but I take them to include whether the higher-level property is definable from the lower level by bridge principles, whether one and the same higher-level property admits of wildly heterogeneous realizations, whether there is any rival candidate at the higher level competing for causal credit, and whether the higher-level description is used to predict or only to summarize. Tell me which of these differences does real work here and which are red herrings.

If neither analogy survives the audit, propose one that does and state plainly what it licenses. I am aware that the best available analogy in this area may license something modest — for instance, that mental causation is real but not additional to physical causation, along the lines of the proposal, associated with Stephen Yablo, that a cause ought to be proportional to its effect — and I would rather have a modest conclusion honestly earned than a grand one smuggled in.

I want a committed answer rather than a survey of the options. Take a definite position on three questions — whether the software analogy carries the anti-exclusion conclusion, whether the thermodynamic one does, and which, if either, you would use in the lecturer's place — and give me your reasons. Where you think he was right to wave off my analogy, say so directly; where you think he was not, tell me what he owed me and did not pay.
