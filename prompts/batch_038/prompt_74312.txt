This term I have been sitting in on a reading group that meets on Thursdays in the philosophy department's common room. It is mostly graduate students; I am neither, and go for the arguments rather than the reading. Two weeks ago the topic was a puzzle the person running the session stated as follows. An island has a hundred inhabitants, every one of whom has blue eyes. Nobody knows the colour of their own eyes; everyone sees everyone else's eyes plainly; there are no mirrors, and eye colour is never spoken of. Any inhabitant who comes to know that their own eyes are blue leaves the island at midnight that day, and who has left is plain for all to see each morning. All of this — the rules, the inhabitants' perfect rationality, their unlimited deductive powers, and the fact that each can observe each — is common knowledge among them. One morning a visitor announces, in the hearing of all of them at once: "At least one of you has blue eyes." Nothing happens for ninety-nine nights. On the hundredth night, all hundred inhabitants leave.

Two regulars, Elena and Callum, both of whom had met the puzzle before, fell out over what the announcement actually does, and the exchange ran roughly like this. (1) Elena: the announcement is literally empty. Each inhabitant could already see ninety-nine pairs of blue eyes, so each already knew precisely what the visitor said; nobody's knowledge of any fact about the world changed by a jot. If no one learned anything, nothing they later deduce can be owed to the visitor — so either the puzzle is a conjuring trick, or the departures are really driven by the accumulating nights on which nobody leaves, which is genuine new evidence about what the others have and have not worked out. The visitor merely started a clock. (2) Callum: Elena is confusing content with status. What changed is that the fact became common knowledge — not merely known by each, but known by each to be known by each, and so on without end — and that iterated structure is exactly what the induction over the nights consumes. Only an utterance made in the hearing of everyone at once can create it; without it, no quantity of staring and deducing would ever have got anybody off the island, given a thousand years. (3) Elena: then be precise, because I do not think you can be. Take only two blue-eyed inhabitants, A and B. Before the announcement, A knows the fact and B knows it — that is one level, and the announcement adds a second: A knows that B knows, and B knows that A knows. Now scale up. With a hundred inhabitants the state before the announcement is not one level deep but ninety-nine: each sees ninety-nine blue-eyed neighbours, each sees that each of those sees ninety-eight, and so on down the chain. So the islanders already had ninety-nine levels of nesting, and the visitor supplied the hundredth, the hundred-and-first, and the rest. But any particular deduction is finite; nobody runs an argument a hundred levels deep, and the chain that actually fires on a given night uses a handful of steps. The announcement adds levels that are never touched. (4) Callum: that is exactly where you go wrong. To leave on the hundredth night, A must rule out the hypothesis that there are only ninety-nine blue-eyed people — that she is not among them. Ruling it out requires her to know what those ninety-nine would have done had she not been blue, and knowing that requires her to know what they know about what the others know, along a chain whose length tracks the number of people in the very hypothesis she is eliminating. Since she must eliminate the ninety-nine case, the ninety-eight case, and so on down to the case of a single blue-eyed inhabitant, no depth below the hundredth carries her through. The levels you call idle are the ones the argument spends. (5) Elena: then your notion does no work that could ever be checked, and I say it is empty. If common knowledge is an infinite conjunction that no finite creature can hold, and the puzzle needs it, the puzzle is about beings that do not exist. Real people coordinate on conventions, bank runs and junctions without anything of the sort, and in groups vastly larger than a hundred. So either common knowledge is a fiction the puzzle lends respectability to, or — what I suspect — some finite depth always suffices, in which case we are back where I started: the visitor added nothing beyond a quantity of nesting the islanders could in principle have assembled for themselves. (6) Callum: they could not have assembled it, and that is not a small difference. Nothing any one of them does — looking, deducing, waiting — raises the depth by a single level, because the depth is fixed by what the others can be relied upon to know, and no private reasoning can repair that. Only a shared event can. Your everyday cases argue for me rather than against me: a run on a bank begins when something becomes publicly visible, not when each depositor privately concludes the bank is shaky. And as for fiction — an idealisation is not a falsehood. What the puzzle establishes is that the depth of what a group shares constrains what its members can deduce, and that survives the idealisation intact.

They appealed to the rest of the room, which split down the middle and so settled nothing. Could you take the six moves in turn and give a verdict on each — which are right, which are wrong, and which are right in part but overstated — saying in each case what exactly is true and what exactly is not? Two questions need to be worked out rather than asserted. First: how deep does the nesting of knowledge actually run before the announcement, and how deep after it, for two, three, and a hundred blue-eyed inhabitants? Please establish the small cases from scratch, by tracking what each inhabitant can and cannot rule out, rather than quoting a formula, since moves (3) and (4) stand or fall on the answer. Second: is Callum right in (4) that the deduction requires depth equal to the number of inhabitants, or is Elena right that a smaller finite depth would have done the work — and if a smaller depth suffices, does that vindicate her conclusion in (5) that the visitor supplied nothing the islanders could not have had? Note explicitly where the answer is delicate or where the idealisations make a difference to it. Then say who is more right overall, and identify anything of substance that both of them are missing: assumptions neither questions, a distinction neither draws, or a way of restating the disagreement on which they might have agreed from the beginning. Please write your answer in British English.
