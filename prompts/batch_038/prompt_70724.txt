My friend and I went at it for about three hours last night over Athens and got exactly nowhere, and I've decided the honest move is to write the argument down properly instead of shouting some more. So I want you to draft the document the two of us should have produced — an adversarial collaboration, not a summary of the debate.

Position A (mine): Mytilene, Sicily, and the trial of Socrates aren't three unlucky weeks, they're one defect showing up three times. An imperial democracy that takes irreversible decisions in a single open mass vote, where the people casting the vote aren't the people who pay for it, and where the only credential for being listened to is being good at talking — that machine produces these outcomes reliably, and calling them accidents is a way of not looking at the machine.

Position B (his): each of the three is a broken auxiliary part, not democracy as such. The pre-deliberative filter had no teeth, there was no independent review of an assembly decree, they had no actual intelligence about Sicily, and there was no workable way to remove a general who was losing. Give Athens those parts and the same demos doesn't do the same things. He thinks I'm indicting a whole regime for a missing QA step.

The specifics we kept throwing at each other, which you should actually use rather than going generic:

On Mytilene — the assembly first decreed killing every adult male and enslaving the women and children, changed its mind the next day, and the show of hands at that second meeting was nearly even. The counter-order got there only because the second trireme had the wind with it and the Mytilenean envoys kept the rowers going with wine and barley cakes and promised rewards; it came in after the general had already read the decree out and was set to carry it out. He says that's the system correcting itself. I say a correction that depends on the weather isn't a correction, it's a coin flip that happened to land well.

On Sicily — Nicias, who opposed the expedition, tried to kill it by demanding an absurdly large force, and the crowd wanted it more because of that; and the people who thought it was a disaster kept their hands down, because voting no looked like being disloyal to the city. Then the thing that actually finished the expedition was Nicias refusing to let the fleet sail for twenty-seven days after an eclipse of the moon, on the soothsayers' advice, with the ordinary soldiers pushing for the delay. And when he finally wrote home saying they were beaten and asking to be relieved of command, the assembly didn't relieve him — it voted him colleagues and a second army. So which part of that is the democracy doing the damage? The vote, the silence of the opposition, the general's piety, or the inability to act on bad news from a man they'd elected?

On Socrates — it wasn't an assembly decision at all, it was a jury of a few hundred drawn by lot, and there was a second stage where he could have proposed exile and instead proposed the city should feed him at public expense. My friend uses that to say I'm pattern-matching: different body, different procedure, different failure. I use it to say the same thing turns up wherever you hand final authority to a big random sample of the demos with no appeal. One of us is wrong about this and I'm not certain it's him.

What I want in the document:

Some of these will be thinner than either of us likes, that's fine, but don't pad the list with stuff that's true of literally any regime ever.

2. A disagreements table: the question, my wording, his wording, and a verdict on whether each one is substantive or partly just us drawing the boundary of the "institution" in different places. If you think a chunk of last night was verbal, say which chunk and why — that's the part I most suspect I'm guilty of.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where an answer would actually move one of us rather than re-describe the standoff. I'd guess one is whether the Mytilene reversal counts as error-correction or as a lucky save, and another is who you attribute Sicily to at all, given that the delay that killed them was ordered in the field by a general with the troops behind him and never went to a vote. But don't just take my word for which ones those are; if there's a bigger crux I'm missing, put it in.

4. For each crux, what would count as bearing on it — and be honest that we can't run this, so say which comparisons are actually available in the record we have and which ones we'd be making up (counterfactual Sparta, counterfactual Athens-with-a-real-council, other Athenian decisions that went fine). Write down each side's prediction in advance, in a form that could come out wrong, and say what each of us would have to concede if it did.

Don't split the difference. I don't want both-sides mush about Athens having some good days and some bad ones; I want the residual disagreement as sharp as it can be while we can both still put our names to the thing. And if there's a claim in my position that my own three examples can't actually deliver, flag it in the document instead of quietly dropping it — I have a feeling there is one and I want to know which.
