I'm giving a twenty-minute talk next week to an interdisciplinary reading group at our institute — the audience is roughly half philosophers of mind, two condensed-matter physicists, and a few people from clinical psychology — and I'd like a critical read of the draft before I commit to its structure. The talk is built around Douglas Hofstadter's careenium: the sealed box filled with countless tiny particles he calls simmballs, which bounce off one another strictly according to ordinary physical law, and in which certain stable clumps and drifts of simmballs correspond to what we would call symbols — a belief, a memory, a desire, a self. He introduced the device in a dialogue about free will (later published in Synthese in the mid-1980s) and returned to it in *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007). My own interest isn't exegetical; I want to use the careenium as a clean toy model for asking whether high-level symbolic talk describes genuine causes or merely convenient patterns. A colleague who read an earlier version said the piece "begs the question in the third move," but wouldn't say which move he meant or why, and I've stared at it too long to tell. I'm at a field station with poor connectivity until Thursday, so please argue from the structure of the claims rather than sending me off to the literature; what I need is diagnosis, not a reading list.

Here is the draft.

---

**Who Does the Shoving? The Careenium and the Reality of Mental Causes**

*§1. The device.* Inside the careenium, nothing happens but collisions. Every change in the configuration of the box is fully accounted for by the positions and momenta of its simmballs at the previous instant; there is no room in the story for anything else to intervene. And yet, when we try to predict what the careenium will do ten seconds from now, the particle vocabulary is useless. No one can track 10^23 trajectories. What we actually use — what Hofstadter himself uses, and what anyone watching the box would spontaneously use — is a vocabulary of patterns: that clump attracts that clump, that configuration avoids that one, this symbol has just activated that memory, and so the box will now lean left. This vocabulary predicts reliably and compresses enormously.

*§2. From indispensability to efficacy.* There are two ways to take this. On the modest reading, the pattern talk is a lossy but indispensable shorthand for shoves that are, at bottom, done entirely by simmballs; symbols are real the way weather systems are real, but they exert no force of their own. On the stronger reading, the patterns are doing causal work that the particle description simply does not capture. I want to argue for the stronger reading, on three grounds. First, the pattern descriptions have predictive content that no finite particle description can match, and a description that licenses successful intervention cannot be mere talk about something else's work — the map that gets you to the water is doing something the territory-by-itself is not. Second, this costs physics nothing: a symbol cannot inject energy into the box, and I am not claiming it does. What I claim is that it redistributes motion already present, the way a switchman redirects a train without supplying its fuel. Conservation laws are therefore untouched, which is the only constraint physics actually imposes on a causal theory. Third, the standard objection — that if the simmballs' collisions are already sufficient for every subsequent configuration, the symbol-level cause is excluded — assumes that a cause must be identical to some particular physical configuration. But the same symbol can be realized by many different clumps of simmballs; it is not any one of them. Since it cannot be reduced to a physical configuration, the physical configuration cannot have squeezed it out of the causal picture.

*§3. The payoff.* If symbols are causes in their own right, then the self — which on Hofstadter's account is just an unusually rich, self-referential symbol inside the careenium — is a cause in its own right too. And since the self is not any particular collection of simmballs, what I decide is not settled in advance by the particle physics of my brain. The strange loop is not an escape hatch from determinism, but it is a genuine exemption: the level at which I exist is a level at which the collision-by-collision story has nothing left to say. That, I suggest, is what is right about the intuition that I author my own actions, and it is why the careenium dialogue is usually taken to vindicate free will rather than merely to redescribe it.

*§4. A residual worry.* After the last draft I circulated, someone objected that the pattern description and the particle description are descriptions of the very same shoves, so that asserting the efficacy of the first adds nothing to the second — that I have talked about causation at length without saying anything causal. My reply was that two descriptions of the same event cannot both be causally complete, so if the symbolic description is complete at its level, the particulate one must be incomplete at ours. I am not entirely satisfied with this, but I don't know how to sharpen it.

Five things I'd like comments on, in rough order of importance:

1. Does the inference in §2 from predictive and interventionist indispensability to distinct causal power actually go through? If it doesn't, what is the strongest claim about high-level causation that the careenium setup will bear without collapsing into the modest reading I dismiss?
2. Is my statement of causal closure in §2 a fair statement of the principle, or have I quietly swapped it for something weaker — and if the latter, what does the real principle forbid that my switchman analogy is trying to permit?
3. Does the appeal to multiple realizability do any work against the exclusion argument, or have I confused two different debates? Please be explicit about which premise of the exclusion argument, if any, my §2 third ground attacks.
4. Is §3 consistent with what Hofstadter is actually doing in the careenium material? My sense is that he is more deflationary about free will than my §3 allows, but I can't articulate the mismatch. If I've over-read him, how should the section be reframed so that the interesting conclusion survives?
5. §4 is the part I most want help with. Is my reply to the "same shoves" objection any good, or is it a restatement of the thing being objected to? And given that I have twenty minutes and no slides, which of §1–§4 should be cut entirely rather than compressed?
