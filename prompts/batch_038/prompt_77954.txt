So I've just crawled out of a nearly three-hour argument with my mate Dev — data scientist, not a philosopher, but he reads a bit and he is extremely stubborn — and I've decided the whole thing should become an exam question for my final-year epistemology students. Mainly because if he can talk himself into this position over two pints, I gaurantee a decent chunk of the cohort can too, and I'd rather they met it in a controlled environment.

The topic was bootstrapping. You know the shape of it: someone drives a car with a reliable fuel gauge that she has no independent reason to trust, reads the gauge each morning, checks it against the dipstick, notes the agreement, and after a few hundred days concludes the gauge is reliable. Standard menu of diagnoses — question-begging, or Wright's transmission-failure line, Cohen's easy knowledge, or biting the bullet and denying closure. All familiar, and frankly all a bit regurgitable, which is the problem I want you to solve.

Here's where Dev went off piste. He reckons the badness of bootstrapping is entirely a second-order affair rather than a first-order one. His claim: the reasoning goes wrong only when the subject already has a settled view about whether the source is trustworthy — she suspects it's dodgy, or she's taken it on faith that it's fine. If she's genuinely agnostic, has no opinion either way, then the bootstrapping run is epistemically in order and she comes out the other end actually knowing the gauge is reliable. He calls it the agnosticism loophole. When I said that makes it absurdly cheap to manufacture knowledge — just cultivate a studied blankness about the question and then run the procedure — he said that's a feature, and that the case only feels bad to me because I keep imagining someone who secretly suspects the gauge of lying to her.

I couldn't shift him and I'm not entirely sure he's wrong, so now I want to weaponise it. Please write me:

1. The exam question itself. Single essay question, 45 minutes, 25 marks, final-year honours level. It has to be fully self-contained: state every fact the student needs inside the question, because it's closed-book and they'll have nothing in the room but a pen. The case you set up should combine two things — a subject who is genuinely agnostic about the indicator's reliability in Dev's sense, so his loophole says the reasoning is kosher, *and* who knows before she starts that the procedure is going to hand her a favourable verdict come what may, because she's already decided a mismatch would be blamed on the dipstick rather than the gauge. That combination is the crux I want them sweating over: agnosticism is present, and yet the procedure still looks like it can't produce information. Don't let the question be answerable by recounting who said what in the literature — no name should be enough to score marks on its own.

2. A model answer of about 700 words. It needs to state what the three main diagnoses actually commit you to, then say what each one predicts about this combined case, and then judge whether Dev's loophole survives the contact. I specifically want it to take up the worry I raised with him — that the loophole looks like an internalist constraint smuggled into an otherwise externalist account of knowledge, since on his view what she knows depends on her prior attitude to a question that (ex hypothesi) she has no evidence about. And it should end somewhere definite, not with a shrug.

3. A marking scheme built out of five must-hit points, with the marks for each adding up to 25, and a one-line note under each saying how students typically mangle it. Then a last line telling me what separates a first from a solid 2:1 answer, in terms of the moves rather than the polish.

One more thing: keep the model answer free of citation-style name-dropping. It's an exam, and I want it to read like someone reasoning, not someone performing familiarity.
