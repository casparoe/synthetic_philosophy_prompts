You are a philosopher of technology who works at the intersection of normative ethics (paternalism, autonomy, agency) and principal–agent theory, and who has actually read how AI assistant policies get written. Think the problem through carefully, and spend a little time brainstorming candidate readings before you commit to a list.

Background. I work on refusal policy for a consumer AI assistant. Our product has what we half-jokingly call a principal stack: a deployer (the company shipping the assistant), the end-user in the current session, and, implicitly, the user's own longer-run plans — which sometimes conflict with what the user asks for right now. Concrete case: a user requests a fifth consecutive draft of an increasingly pointed message to a landlord, each draft likelier than the last to defeat the user's own stated aim of staying in the apartment. A colleague proposed that, in a narrow band of cases like this, the assistant flag the conflict and offer the alternative before complying. Our tech lead killed the proposal with a proverb: the user is always right.

Your task is to treat that sentence as a claim in need of precisification, not as a slogan to be waved. Lay out the distinct precise theses it could express — at least five, ordered from trivially true to most radical — and for each: state it in one sentence with every ambiguity resolved (whose judgment is at issue, about what subject matter, at which time, and with what authority over whom); declare it true, false, or merely contestable; and say what would follow for assistant design if we accepted it. Then answer three further questions. First, which reading, if any, actually licenses rejecting my colleague's proposal — as opposed to merely sounding like it does. Second, which readings were plausible in the proverb's original retail context but do not survive transplantation into a system that has no commercial relationship with the person it is declining to help. Third, where exactly the three-level principal stack forces apart readings that would coincide if there were only one principal.

Close by telling me which single reading I should press my tech lead to state explicitly, and what would count as an argument for it. Deliver the readings as a numbered menu with verdicts attached, not as an essay defending one of them.
