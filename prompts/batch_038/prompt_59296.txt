I'm auditing a cogsci seminar on analogy and categorization and I've been sitting on this half-written thing for a couple of weeks. It's supposed to turn into maybe 2500 words for a small conference, but right now it's somewhere between a blog post and a rant. Here's the draft, roughly complete except I never wrote the intro and the temperature paragraph is a stub:

---

Copycat answers problems of the form abc → abd, so ijk → ? by a parallel terraced scan. There's a workspace holding the letter string, a coderack full of little agents waiting to fire (some just scout a possible action, some actually build or knock down structure), and a slipnet of maybe sixty concepts, each with a conceptual depth that ranks it as a best bet, joined by links whose effective length shrinks when the concept labeling that kind of link is highly activated. Pressure from the source side of the analogy makes a description slip: rightmost letter becomes rightmost group, and ijk becomes ijl. How freely things slip is governed by temperature, which is high when the workspace is a mess of unbonded objects and weak links and drops as structure accumulates; a hot system explores and slips, a cold one locks in an answer. The upshot is the thing the whole project was after — a concept with no fixed boundary, where the boundary moves under local pressure and how far it can move is a function of conceptual distance in the network.

Here's my complaint. Slippage in this picture is state-dependent but not history-dependent. Sure, the slipnet changes over the course of a run — activations move, link lengths breathe — but those changes just track the current configuration, the topology never changes, and everything resets to the same initial state on the next run. The pressures a codelet feels at a given moment are a function of what's in the workspace and what's activated right now. So a run that arrives at some configuration by route A should, from that point on, behave like a run that arrived at the same configuration by route B. Slippage is frictionless and reversible. There's no ratchet.

But in ordinary conceptual life, slipping seems to leave a residue. My running microdomain: is a band that has replaced every original member, one at a time over thirty years, still the band? When people argue about this the criterion slides constantly — from who's standing on stage, to who owns the name, to who writes the songs, to whether it sounds like the records — and the direction of the slide depends on which verdict you started out defending. More interesting: once a group of friends has conceded the name criterion mid-argument, they don't retract it when the last founder leaves; the slip gets absorbed and they move on from the new position. And order matters. Narrate the same final lineup via a different sequence of departures and you get different verdicts about a configuration that is, by the end, identical.

I want to call this slip debt: every slippage taken during an episode deposits temporary weight in the direction it went, lowering the cost of slipping that way again. Patch Copycat with a ledger that does this and you get genuine path dependence, plus a prediction — order effects in human microdomains with matched endpoints. If the ledger is real, the effect should scale with how much slippage the episode forced, not with how memorable the story was.

Same structure shows up smaller: whether a chili is still your grandmother's chili after substitutions. The person who made the change slips toward what-the-ingredients-do; the person being served slips toward what-it-tastes-like. Slip debt would say those aren't two different concepts, they're one concept plus a ledger pointing different directions.

---
