I constructed the following argument for a philosophy-of-action seminar, and it now proves far more than I can defend. The setup is the familiar one: a chairman is told that a new program will raise profits but will also affect the environment; he says the environmental effect is nothing to him and starts the program. When the effect is harm, a large majority of respondents say he harmed the environment intentionally; when it is help, only a small minority says he helped it intentionally.

(1) The two vignettes are matched for what the chairman foresaw, for how his decision produced the side effect, and for any skill he exercised; they differ only in the moral valence of the outcome.
(2) Grant, for argument's sake, that the asymmetry is not a performance error: it survives reflection and rewording, appears in children as young as four and in unrelated languages, and the speakers who produce it can say what they take themselves to be applying.
(3) If competent speakers apply a term differently when only the moral valence of the outcome changes, and this is not performance error, then the term's content is sensitive to moral valence.
(4) "Intentionally" is a folk-psychological term of a piece with "believed," "wanted," and "decided": it reports an agent's mental relation to an action and nothing more.
(5) So the content of some folk-psychological terms is sensitive to moral valence.
(6) A term whose content is sensitive to moral valence fails to pick out an evaluator-independent property, since which property it tracks depends on the moral attitudes of whoever applies it.
(7) Therefore folk-psychological terms fail to pick out evaluator-independent properties, and folk psychology is not describing psychological reality at all.
(8) Hence the project of grounding criminal liability in the mens rea distinctions that folk psychology supplies is built on a mistake, and so is the project of naturalizing the mental.

I am attached to (2), because I have read the cross-cultural and developmental work, and to the first half of (8), because that is the part my audience found interesting. I cannot live with (7), and I am uneasy about (8)'s second half. Which step should I give up? Explain why that step looked unobjectionable when I wrote it, what position I am left holding once it goes, and what the rival diagnoses would each cost me instead: the conversational-implicature account, the account that treats the asymmetry as a competence-level moralization of the concept, and the account on which "intentionally" is a hybrid concept that was never purely descriptive to begin with.
