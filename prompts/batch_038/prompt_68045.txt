A retail sportsbook's price list has a peculiar structure. For a given event it posts one price per possible outcome, and those prices, converted into implied probabilities and summed, come to substantially more than one hundred percent — enough that a punter who stakes proportionately on every outcome loses a fixed fraction of his total stake whichever result occurs. It posts these prices in one direction only: it will sell you a bet at the quoted price, but it will not generally buy one back at the same price, and for most markets there is no price at which it will take the other side. It moves prices during the day, not chiefly in response to news about the runners but in response to where the money has come in. It also sells compound bets — accumulators priced by multiplying the prices of the individual legs, and bets on the order of finish — where the implied probability is the product of the legs' implied probabilities; traditionally it has declined to sell such combinations when the legs are connected, as where one leg makes another much likelier (that a team will score three goals, and that the same team will win the match). It caps stakes, reserves the right to refuse any bet, voids bets on withdrawn runners, and when a customer wins consistently it reduces his maximum stake or closes his account. Meanwhile the same firms market compound bets hard, since those carry a much heavier margin than singles. Alongside this sits a different institution: the betting exchange, which posts no margin at all, where members quote two-sided prices at which they will take either side, and where the operator takes a commission on net winnings.

What account of belief and of rationality does the sportsbook's practice presuppose? I would like an answer that separates what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits well with, and that flags where it is philosophically underdetermined.

In particular:

1. The coherence argument says that an agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability axioms can be led into a package of bets that loses money in every possible state, and that this shows the violation to be irrational. The bookmaker's margin structure is an attempt to be on the winning side of exactly that test. But it wins by declining the conditions the argument assumes: a single two-sided price, unlimited stakes, acceptance of every gamble offered at one's stated price, and a fixed menu of gambles over a set of propositions closed under conjunction and negation. Which of those assumptions does the practice deny, and what survives of the claim that incoherence is ipso facto irrational once they are denied? Is the practice committed to coherence as a norm of belief, or only as a constraint on pricing under idealized access conditions?

2. Does the practice presuppose that the bookmaker has degrees of belief at all? At least three stories fit the same posted prices: it has genuine credences and adds a margin to them; it has no credences, only a liability-balancing rule that tracks the aggregate beliefs of its customers; or its prices are estimates of what the crowd believes rather than of how the world is likely to go. Which of these does the operation require, and which does it merely permit? What evidence within the practice would settle the question?

3. The bookmaker's aim is to render the outcome irrelevant to its payoff. That is not choosing the act that maximizes expected utility relative to its own credences; it is choosing a pricing policy that flattens expected utility across states. Can the coherence argument for expected-utility maximization accommodate such an agent, or does the practice presuppose a different norm — immunity to sure loss taking precedence over the mirror-image liability of passing up favourable gambles? If the latter, is that a claim about rationality as such, or about the shape of the bookmaker's utility function under repeated play and the threat of ruin?

4. Diachronically, prices move with money. Does a moving book expose the firm to being Dutch-booked across time by someone who takes the early price and the later price on opposite sides? Its response is to limit or expel whoever does this. Does the practice presuppose that diachronic coherence is not required of a rational agent, that it is unattainable, or that it is attainable only by refusing to trade?

5. Marketing accumulators presupposes that the customer who knowingly buys a package with negative expected value is not thereby marked irrational. Is the firm applying two different standards — the sure-loss test for its own book, something else entirely for its customers? Can it hold both consistently, for instance by appealing to the non-monetary value of the bet, to probability weighting, or to the claim that the test governs prices rather than acts?

Finally: suppose the whole industry were replaced by the exchange — two-sided prices, no margin, commission on winnings. What would that change about the commitments in question? Does the exchange satisfy the conditions the bookmaker violates, and would that vindicate the coherence argument as a workable norm, or expose it as a norm addressed only to agents who face no better-informed counterparties?
