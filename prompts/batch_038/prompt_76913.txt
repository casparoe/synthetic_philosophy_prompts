A journal has invited me to contribute a statement of my position to a symposium on the metaphysics of psychiatric classification. One of my co-symposiasts has read my earlier work closely and intends to argue, in her response, that the position is unstable — that I cannot hold the metaphysics I hold and make the policy arguments I make. Before I draft a reply, I want an internal audit of the commitments I plan to state in public. I have written them out as plainly as I can below. I am deliberately not telling you which of them I would be most willing to abandon, because I do not know; please treat them as equally mine and equally weighted.

1. Psychiatric categories are interactive kinds, in roughly the sense Hacking developed: classifying a person alters the person classified — through self-understanding, through the expectations of others, through the treatment the label licenses — and the altered people in turn alter the category, its criteria, and its epidemiology. There is no fixed target for the classifier to converge upon.

2. Notwithstanding (1), at least some psychiatric categories are projectible in a robust sense: they support inductions about course, comorbidity, and treatment response that arbitrary groupings of symptoms do not. This is why I think classification is a substantive enterprise and not merely an administrative one.

3. The engine of the loop is the availability of an idiom. A label becomes a resource for describing oneself, and once it circulates widely, people come to experience and report their distress in its terms. Crucially, the diagnostic criteria are themselves the idiom: any instrument constructed out of those criteria measures idiom-shaped distress, not distress antecedent to the idiom.

4. Therefore, prevalence estimates taken before an idiom was widely available and estimates taken after are not measurements of the same quantity, and are not strictly comparable.

5. It follows that when recorded prevalence rises sharply after a label enters public circulation, my default interpretation is artefactual. I do not treat such rises as evidence of a real increase in underlying distress, and I am sceptical of what I call epidemic narratives.

6. At the same time, I hold that community survey data — structured interviews administered to the general population rather than to clinic attenders — give us our best estimate of the true prevalence of a disorder, and that the large gap between survey prevalence and treated prevalence establishes substantial undertreatment.

7. On the strength of (6), I have argued in print for broadening the criteria of at least one existing category, on the grounds that undertreatment is the greater harm and a wider net reaches people who are suffering without recourse.

8. I have also argued against introducing a risk category for young people presenting with mild, sub-threshold symptoms thought sometimes to precede full psychotic illness. My reason was looping: labelling people who are not yet ill supplies a script, and scripts get followed.

9. Yet I do not think looping is simply a hazard. For many of the people I have interviewed, receiving a diagnosis was the first time their experience became describable, and being classified was experienced as validating rather than diminishing. The mechanism I treat as harmful in (8) is what made the diagnosis worth having for them.

10. Categories resembling melancholia, mania, and panic recur across cultures and historical periods that had no contact with our classificatory system. I take that recurrence to show stable attractors in the space of possible human suffering, and to show that the space is not indefinitely plastic.

11. Methodologically I am a pluralist and something of a fictionalist about syndromes: the symptom, and the causal relations among symptoms, are the real units of analysis, and the syndrome label is a serviceable fiction. Nevertheless, I think the field's most valuable investment is in biomarker research, on the grounds that if any revision of our categories is coming, it will come from that direction.

Please audit this set rather than expound it. I do not need the looping-effects literature rehearsed back to me; I know it. What I want is: which pairs genuinely pull against one another and how badly, ranked by severity; which pairs only look like a tension and dissolve once the right distinction is drawn, with an account of why they dissolve; for each genuine tension, what has to give and what the cheapest revision is, together with what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list; and finally, which single revision would resolve the most. End with a verdict on my co-symposiast: is the position unstable, and if so, is the instability fatal or a matter of qualifications I have simply left unstated?
