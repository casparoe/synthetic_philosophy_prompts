I'm a materials physicist — I work on ferromagnets and phase transformations, so hysteresis loops, state functions, exact vs inexact differentials, path independence, dissipation per cycle, and "perpetual motion of the first kind" are basically my native language. Decision theory is not: I've read a couple of popular treatments and one paper a colleague handed me about money pumps, and I half-follow it.

Here's the setup as I understand it. An agent's preferences over three options cycle: she'll take a small payment to swap A for B, another to swap B for C, another to swap C for A, and at the end she's holding what she started with but is poorer by three payments. The argument is that this is a reductio — preferences like that can't be rational, because a rational agent isn't a device you can run around a loop and extract work from. Then come the replies: that a sophisticated agent who foresees the loop should refuse the first swap even though it looks locally attractive; that a resolute one can commit to a plan and hold it; and that maybe the constraint only binds within a single moment, so there's no one agent being pumped, just a series of short-lived ones each of whom is fine on its own terms.

Translate all of that into my vocabulary as faithfully as you can. Cycle = what exactly? The net extraction = area enclosed by a loop, and if so in what space? Is "preferences must be transitive" secretly an integrability condition on a differential, i.e. the requirement that the thing you're integrating be a state function rather than a path function? What are the foresight and resolute-choice replies — a boundary condition, a different ensemble, a system that has been prepared rather than merely described? And is "you would pay to have your preferences" literally a dissipation-per-cycle claim or just a flattering coincidence of wording?

Then the residue report, which is what I actually want. Which claims map cleanly, which you had to bend out of shape to fit, and which have no expression at all in thermodynamic language — and for each of those last ones, tell me whether my framework is impoverished or whether the philosophers are being less precise than they sound. Two things I'd like flagged specifically: whether the no-foresight case and the foresight case are the same physical situation described differently or genuinely different situations, and whether the money pump against intransitive preferences and the Dutch book against incoherent degrees of belief are one argument or two, because in cycle language I can't tell them apart.

Be strict with me. Don't quietly import conservation laws or entropy-increase premises and hand them back as translation, and don't tell me the correspondence is deep if all it amounts to is that both fields noticed a loop with net gain looks suspicious.
