I've been running a grad seminar on moral worth for six weeks and we've gotten nowhere, which is weird because everyone in the room is smart and nobody's being obstinate. The lay of the land as I see it: one camp (the Arpaly line, Huck Finn and all that) says an action has moral worth when the agent is responsive to the features that actually make it right, whether or not she could say what they are or even has the relevant moral beliefs; a second camp (Markovits and the people who follow her) says thats too cheap, because responsiveness can be lucky or mechanical, and what's needed is that the agent acts *for* the reason that makes the act right; a third camp says both of those miss the point and that what's really being tracked is moral understanding or a species of know-how — being able to explain why the act was right, draw out its consequences, and so on; and hanging around the edges there's the de re/de dicto move people trace back to Michael Smith, which everyone claims to have settled and which keeps coming back. Meanwhile the case method just generates more vignettes — sympathetic do-gooders who can't articulate anything, unsympathetic ones doing the right thing out of a sense of duty, people who get it right because they defer to someone better — and each camp absorbs the new cases and adjusts without giving up an inch.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to attack rather than flatter: this isn't one debate. "Moral worth" is doing duty for at least three questions — what makes an agent praiseworthy, what it takes to act for a reason in the morally relevant sense, and what counts as evidence about either — and each camp is answering a different one, so the counterexamples never actually land. And underneath that there may be a shared, undefended assumption doing real work: that worth comes all-or-nothing, so any account that lets it come in degrees is disqualified before the argument even starts.

Is that diagnosis right, or is this a genuinely substantive disagreement with a determinate answer that's just hard? If the latter, tell me what the crux proposition is — the thing one side should be forced to deny. I'd especially like to know whether the dispute is partly verbal (a term of art with no pre-theoretic anchor to argue about), partly about which cases count as data at all (is Huck Finn evidence about moral worth, or evidence about literary characters?), or whether there's a hidden question in the metaphysics of acting for a reason that nobody has flagged as such.

To keep you from merely surveying positions, run the diagnosis through a case I made up for the seminar and we could not resolve. A hospital resident has an ethics-support app that tells him what to do in the hard cases. The app was built by people who are much better moral reasoners than he is and it's right about 95% of the time. He follows it reliably, including when his own judgment points the other way and when he feels nothing either way about the outcome. He can't explain why its recommendations are right and couldn't if you deleted the app tomorrow; but the features that make his actions right are exactly the features the app is keyed to, so the causal history of the right thing does run through him, in some sense. Variant: same resident, but he only consults the app when he's already inclined to do what it's going to recommend.

What I want out of you: (1) a diagnosis of the stall, argued for from what the camps actually say — no survey; (2) an explicit verdict on whether the app resident's actions have moral worth under each of the three readings I sketched, and whether the case discriminates between them — if all three come out the same way, say so plainly, since that would make the case useless as evidence, which is itself informative; (3) what would count as progress: a distinction someone could draw, a kind of case that forces a choice, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. A page or two is plenty. And no reading list, I've got the papers — I want the analysis.
