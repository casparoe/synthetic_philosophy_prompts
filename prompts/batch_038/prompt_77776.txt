Six weeks ago I took a post as the independent living-donor advocate at a regional transplant center. My job is to meet every prospective living kidney donor separately from the surgical team and from the recipient's family, and to satisfy myself that the donation is informed, voluntary, and untouched by money. In that first month I have found myself applying rules I never saw written down in one place, and they do not obviously hang together. Federal law forbids transferring a kidney for anything of value, yet my office routinely signs off on reimbursements covering the donor's travel, lodging, lost wages, and childcare, and nobody here regards that as a sale. Meanwhile the four-way paired-exchange chain I spent two weeks coordinating — a pure barter arrangement, matched by an algorithm, in which each donor receives a kidney for their intended recipient conditional on their own kidney going to someone else's — is treated not merely as permissible but as the system's proudest achievement. Two hundred yards away, the fertility clinic advertises for egg donors at four- and five-figure sums; three blocks away, a commercial center pays plasma donors per visit, in a country that supplies most of the world's plasma on the back of those payments; and a donor who offers half a liver, an organ that regenerates, receives nothing but a parking voucher.

The detail that started me digging is a labeling rule I came across in the blood regulations. Containers of blood and blood components intended for transfusion must carry a donor-classification statement — either "paid donor" or "volunteer donor" — printed no less prominently than the proper name of the product itself. The test for which side of the line a donor falls on is cash-convertibility: time off from work, membership in a blood-assurance program, and the cancellation of a nonreplacement fee are expressly not payment, whereas anything readily convertible into cash is. The requirement also attaches only to products intended for transfusion, so paid plasma destined for fractionation into medicines need not be stamped at all. So this one regulatory scheme insists on inscribing the transactional provenance of a unit of blood in letters as large as its product name, while nothing of the kind is required of a vial of sperm, a harvested egg, or a transplanted kidney.

I would like a reconstruction of the theory of value that this cluster of practices presupposes, rather than an evaluation of whether paying donors would be good policy. Specifically: what is the operative distinction — cash versus in-kind, price-for-a-thing versus reimbursement-of-a-loss, regenerable versus non-regenerable tissue, the donor's motive, or the social meaning of the transaction — and which of these does the pattern actually track once all the cases are laid side by side? What does the blood-bag label presuppose about the relationship between a supplier's incentives and the reliability of what the supplier says: is the provenance marking a safety device, a moral record kept for its own sake, or a disclosure owed to the patient who will receive the unit? Please distinguish what these practices strictly require from what they merely happen to be consistent with, and say where the pattern is philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible accounts — a Kantian line on dignity and price, an exploitation-based line, a corruption-of-social-meaning line, and a plain welfarist line about harm and consent — would each license the same rules. Then identify the genuine tensions: whether celebrating barter chains while banning cash is stable on any of those accounts, and whether the label's cash-convertibility test does any moral work or only administrative work. Finally, tell me what would have to change in my own practice — the forms donors sign, the questions I am obliged to ask, the arrangements I would have to refuse — if the presupposed theory were replaced by one under which kidneys may be bought and sold at a regulated fixed price, and which of my current duties would survive that substitution unchanged.
