Everybody reaches for the panopticon when they want to sound smart about platforms, and I think the reflex is wrong in a way that matters. Foucault used Bentham's design as the diagram of disciplinary power because of a specific feature: the inmate is permanently visible and never knows whether he is being looked at right now, so he has to behave as if he always were. The analogy to social media is too easy and it inverts the mechanism. On a platform there is no uncertainty about whether you are watched; you are watched continuously, and you know it, and a large fraction of the time you want to be. The engine is not the anxiety of an unverifiable gaze. So the panopticon is the wrong instrument, and I want to argue the examination is the right one — but only after it gets modified, because it breaks in an interesting place.

In Discipline and Punish the disciplinary apparatus runs on three instruments: hierarchical observation, normalizing judgement, and the examination. The examination is the one that matters here, because it is the one that produces the individual as a case. It combines the gaze with the judgement and, crucially, it generates documentation: the report card, the medical file, the case number, the running record that follows a person from institution to institution. On my reading discipline is fundamentally about paperwork. What makes a person governable is not that their body has been drilled but that they have been made legible — converted into comparable entries in a register, so they can be ranked, sorted and distributed along a curve. The docile body is a downstream effect of the record, not the other way around. Read this way, a platform looks like the examination finally perfected: continuous, automatic, and self-reporting, producing a file on each person that no headmaster could have dreamed of.

But here is where it stops fitting, and this is the part of the essay I actually care about. The examination in the barracks or the classroom measures you against a norm the institution fixed in advance — the ideal soldier, the curriculum, the class average. The reference class is the population and deviance is a distance from a mean. A recommendation system doesn't do that. It does not compare you to the population average; it compares you to a prediction of you, generated from your own history. Your behaviour is scored by how well it matches what the model expected you to do. Deviance stops being distance-from-the-mean and becomes surprise-relative-to-baseline. Call it self-referential normalization. The consequence is that the sanction changes shape too. Disciplinary correction pulls you back toward the norm — the bad mark, the extra drill, the intervention. What happens on a feed is that you are not corrected, you are re-priced: you get a different set of options, a different neighbourhood of content and offers, and nobody ever tells you that you failed at anything. There is no verdict, which is exactly why there is no appeal.

This difference only bites because of a limitation in the disciplinary analysis, and I want to be honest that it is a limitation. That analysis is built around a bounded institution with walls and an identifiable authority standing behind the norm: the prison, the school, the workshop, the regiment. Once the norm is produced by a system that has no inside and no outside and no one standing behind it, the apparatus runs out of parts. What takes over, I want to suggest, is something Foucault described at length but for a different historical domain: pastoral power. In the lectures where he works this out he analyses the Christian pastorate as a form of power that is exercised over a flock and simultaneously over every single sheep; that aims at the salvation of the flock, so its goal is defined by the welfare of the governed rather than the glory of the ruler; that treats obedience as a virtue in itself rather than a means; and that requires the sheep to know and disclose its own interior — the examination of conscience, the direction of souls. That fourth feature is what a platform runs on. It does not observe you from a tower, it elicits you. Everything it knows, it knows because you told it: by clicking, by lingering, by posting what you ate and what you're afraid of at two in the morning. Pastoral power is individualizing and totalizing at the same time, and so is the feed — it addresses you personally and it addresses everyone, and it never stops asking.

So the thesis is that platforms are pastoral rather than disciplinary, and that this is why the standard Foucauldian complaint about surveillance rings false: it is looking for a guard who doesnt exist.
