I am setting the final paper for a graduate seminar on the philosophy of emotion, and I need your help drafting the third of three essay questions, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The paper is two hours long, and this question is worth 30 marks.

What I want is a question that turns on a piece of stipulated evidence rather than on any published case, study, or argument in the literature, so that a student cannot answer it by reproducing a summary of the debate. The question stem must therefore present the following set-up in full and in plain terms, and must be genuinely self-contained.

Group A (neurologically intact subjects). After acquiring a firm, reflectively endorsed belief that a spider in plain view poses no threat whatsoever, subjects continue to feel fear of it. The spider still looks threatening to them; they report that their fear presents it as dangerous even while they deny that it is; their autonomic arousal and avoidance persist. Their responses to standard visual illusions are entirely ordinary: the two shafts of the Müller-Lyer figure still look unequal after they have learned that the lines are equal.

Group B (subjects with a rare focal lesion, otherwise neurologically and emotionally normal). Their fear responds normally to genuine danger: when the spider really is dangerous they feel fear, with normal arousal, expression, and avoidance, and their susceptibility to the Müller-Lyer illusion is fully intact and unaffected by knowledge. But once they acquire the same firm belief that the visible spider is harmless, their fear is extinguished immediately and completely. There is no residual sense of the spider as dangerous, no arousal, no avoidance. The extinguishing follows the change of belief itself and is not the product of training, habituation, or distraction.

The stem should then characterize, in a sentence or two each and without naming anyone, the three views the student is to work with: the view that emotions are evaluative judgments or beliefs; the view that emotions are perceptual states whose evaluative content derives from perception of the subject's own bodily changes; and the view that emotions are non-doxastic construals, a kind of seeing-as that falls short of belief.

The question itself should have three parts:

(a) Say what each of the three views predicts about Group A and about Group B, and identify which of the two data sets, if either, constitutes a problem for each view.

(b) The defender of the perceptual view typically responds to recalcitrance by assimilating it to persisting visual illusion. Explain whether Group B undercuts that response, and say whether there is any further asymmetry between recalcitrant emotions and persisting illusions that bears on the analogy — and, if so, whether Group B sharpens or blunts it.

(c) Conclude by stating which of the three views the evidence best supports, and what that view has to give up in order to accommodate the other group.

Please supply a model answer of roughly nine hundred words. It should not survey the options and stop; it should reach and defend a determinate verdict on part (c). It is a model answer, not a timed student script, so it may be more tightly argued than a candidate could manage under exam conditions.

The marking scheme should divide the 30 marks across the three parts, and for each part list the specific moves that earn credit rather than the topics that must be mentioned. It should then say explicitly, in three or four named moves, what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one. Finally, it should list at least five characteristic errors with the deduction each attracts — for instance, treating Group B as straightforwardly refuting the perceptual view without noticing that perceptual systems differ among themselves in their sensitivity to belief; conflating cognitive impenetrability with recalcitrance; assuming that because a state is non-doxastic it cannot be rationally criticizable, or that rational criticizability entails doxastic content; explaining away Group A as a pathology when the stem says the subjects are intact; reading lesion data as evidence about the nature of a state rather than about its causal dependencies without comment on the difference; and citing a philosopher's name in place of an argument.

Students should not be expected to produce references, and the marking scheme should say so: positions are to be identified by description, and no marks should depend on recall of who held what.
