I am one of two teaching assistants marking the second essay for a third-year cross-listed seminar on the social history and social psychology of mass violence, and I have not yet read this one. Our course reader sorts explanations of perpetrator behaviour into four families: (A) dispositional, locating the cause in stable features of an individual's character; (B) situational, locating it in the immediate social setting, the chain of command, and the presence of peers; (C) cultural-ideological, locating it in the beliefs and categories a person has absorbed from a wider community; and (D) structural-bureaucratic, locating it in the organisation of the task itself, meaning the division of labour, routinisation, physical and psychological distance from the victim, and the diffusion of responsibility across many hands. The essay question I set was: using Browning's account of Reserve Police Battalion 101 and Goldhagen's reply to it, (i) place each account within this framework, (ii) say whether category (D) does any independent explanatory work in Browning, or whether it is only category (B) described at a larger scale, and (iii) assess whether the framework can accommodate Goldhagen's insistence that a perpetrator's culture and his situation cannot be prised apart. Students were told they may draw on the two books and the reader, that this exercise carries no citation requirement, and that a page reference is worth nothing if it is wrong. We mark out of twelve: four for accuracy about the two accounts, four for the use of the framework, and four for whether the argument actually holds together. Please do not penalise the student for the absence of references, and please do not supply page numbers, chapter titles, or quoted wording of your own to decorate the assessment; where you are less than fully confident about a point of fact or interpretation, say so plainly and mark it as a place where I should check the books myself rather than guessing.

The answer, reproduced in full, runs as follows.

Browning's Ordinary Men is a situational explanation in the sense the reader gives to category (B). Reserve Police Battalion 101 was a Hamburg order-police unit of about five hundred men, mostly middle-aged and working-class, not recruited for any special commitment, and from 1942 it was used for round-ups and mass shootings in occupied Poland. At Józefów the commanding officer offered his men the chance to step out of the firing line, and a small minority, on the order of a dozen, took it; Browning's later estimate of those who avoided the role of shooter altogether comes to something like a tenth or a fifth of the battalion. His conclusion is that the men killed under the pressure of the immediate setting: the wish not to appear weak in front of comrades, deference to the men giving orders, careerism, and the gradual routinisation of the work. The account is explicitly informed by the obedience-research tradition, and its final chapter generalises from the battalion to a claim about human beings under group pressure rather than about Germans in particular.

Goldhagen's book is the cultural-ideological alternative, category (C). He argues that an eliminationist antisemitism specific to Germany, a widely shared cognitive model in which Jews figured as a menace that had to be removed from the world, made the killing intelligible and even attractive to the perpetrators. He accepts most of Browning's factual findings, including that the men had the option to stand aside, and adds the point that Browning underplays, that no German was ever seriously punished for refusing to kill Jews. But he denies that this shows the situation doing the work: on his account a single explanatory factor suffices for the overwhelming majority of the perpetrators, and that factor is culture.

The thing to notice is how much closer the two historians are than the public controversy suggested. Both accept that the men of the battalion could not realistically have declined to take part, and neither explains the killings by reference to sadism or individual pathology. What they disagree about is only which pressure was decisive, the squad at Józefów or the centuries of German culture standing behind it. On the reader's taxonomy that makes the whole exchange an argument inside the situational family, between category (B) and a longer-range version of category (B).

Category (D) does independent work in Browning, and this is where he is strongest. The killing was broken into sub-tasks, cordons, searches, guarding, loading, escorting, so that a great many men contributed to it without ever firing a weapon, and the work recurred often enough to become unremarkable. That is not peer pressure described at a larger scale; it is a property of how the operation was organised, and it is what allows Browning to say that the men became killers without ever having to decide to become killers.

The framework handles Goldhagen badly, and Goldhagen is right that it does. A taxonomy that files culture under (C) and situation under (B) presupposes that the two can be separated, which is precisely what he denies, since for him the men's perception of the situation was itself cultural and there is no situation-independent fact about what the peer group was demanding of them. There is also a standard objection to his position: a factor already present in German society for generations cannot explain why the killing started when it did, because a constant cannot account for a variation. The same objection disposes of Browning, however, because conformity to one's peers is constant across human societies generally, as Browning's own conclusion concedes. The episode illustrates a further lesson about perpetrator evidence. Browning worked mainly from the statements collected for the West German proceedings against members of the battalion, whereas Goldhagen built his case largely on the perpetrators' letters and memoirs and on contemporary German public discourse, so the two rest on different evidential bases; yet the same men's postwar statements ended up being read by two able historians in opposite directions, which shows how little weight what perpetrators later say about their own motives can bear.
