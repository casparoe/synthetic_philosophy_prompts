I step down this month as director of a neighborhood canvassing program, and I owe five minutes of remarks at the farewell luncheon. The room will be foundation board members who funded us, three city council staffers, and about a dozen of my own field organizers — the last group the only people who will catch what I am actually saying.

For six years our flagship method was exposure: bringing outside speakers into the neighborhood so residents would hear directly from people whose views they had dismissed, on the assumption that their problem was simply not having heard the other side. It didn't work, and sometimes it backfired. Residents came away surer that the outside world was hostile and dishonest, and two of the local voices we most needed stopped returning our calls. What did move things was slower and much harder to fund: getting a handful of people the neighborhood already trusted to vouch for information it would otherwise have rejected. And the uncomfortable wrinkle is that the distrust was not manufactured from nothing — two of the outside experts we brought in got the facts wrong.

Write the remarks. They must concede, to a room of donors who loved the exposure program because it was legible and photographable, that the thinking behind it was wrong, without implying either that the residents were gullible or that the donors were fools. The distinction the speech should turn on is the one between a group that has merely not heard the other side and a group that has been taught to distrust it, since only the first is repaired by more information — but the terms "echo chamber" and "epistemic bubble" must not appear anywhere, and no other jargon may substitute for them. It must still be a farewell, and land as gratitude rather than as a post-mortem.

Under 450 words, plain enough to be read aloud, continuous prose with no headings or lists.
