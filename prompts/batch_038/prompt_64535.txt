Forty-one years I spent taking other people's numbers on faith. Calibrations done by a technician I never met, a melting point out of a handbook, a referee who insisted we redo a measurement, a co-author who swore up and down the sample was pure. Nobody ever taught me to notice that almost nothing I believe about the world I found out for myself. Now that I'm retired I've been reading the philosophers on testimony — Hume and Reid, and the moderns arguing about reductionism and anti-reductionism — and my head is spinning, mostly because everyone seems to be arguing past everyone else and I can't tell which disagreements are real.

So here's what I want: build me a decision tree that sorts a person into a position in this debate, the way you'd identify an unknown compound from its reactions.

Audience is my reading group — six retired scientists, none of us has read the literature properly, and not one of us will admit to a label in advance. So every branch node has to be a question you could answer honestly without knowing where it leads. Nothing like "do you think testimony reduces to perception plus induction?" That's the answer wearing a question's clothes.

Draw the cases out of scientific life, not out of "a stranger tells you the way to the station." I mean things like: your grad student says the run hit temperature but the log page is missing; a preprint reports an effect you can't reproduce and the authors won't release the data; a textbook lists a constant with no source given; a footnote credits a personal communication with a man who died in 1998; an anonymous referee asserts your method is unsound without saying why; someone at a conference mentions in passing that a famous result is an artifact of one particular setup. Ten to twelve questions, seven or eight leaves.

For the leaves, give each its usual name and one sentence on what it costs the person standing there. I want at minimum: the Hume-ish global reductionist; the local reductionist who wants positive reasons case by case; the Reid/Coady anti-reductionist; the Burge-style entitlement view; Lackey's dualism; and the assurance or trust view — Faulkner, Moran — where believing someone is closer to trusting a person than to weighing evidence. If there's a deflationary option that says testimony isn't really a source at all, put it in too.

One thing I want the tree to either accommodate or throw out: in my experience the thing you trust is usually not a mouth and a pair of ears but a chain — a sample, an instrument, a lab notebook, a calibration traceable to some standard in another country. So include at least one branch point that turns on whether the testifier is a person or an institutional chain, and tell me at the end whether that axis is orthogonal to the reductionism debate or genuinely cuts across it.

Then, after the tree, lay out the logical space itself: list your questions as coordinates and mark which combinations come out empty, and for each empty cell say why — incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody's got round to it yet. I have a suspicion there's a cell for "positive reasons required, but required of the institution rather than the individual hearer," and I'd like to know if anyone is standing in it.

Flag every pair of leaves your tree can't actually seperate, and say what you'd have to ask to pull them apart. And if any branch question is one where a working scientist would find both answers absurd, say so — that's probably where the philosophy has gone wrong and I'd rather know.

No bibliography please, no reading list. I can't chase papers down and neither can you, as far as I know. Just the structure and the reasoning. Write it so I can print it and hand it around: plain questions, plain branches, jargon only in the leaf names.
