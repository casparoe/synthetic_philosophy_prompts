So I'm about 200 pages into a novel and I've hit a structural problem that turns out to be a philosophy problem rather than a craft problem, and I need help settling it. What I want is an actual essay — an argument with a verdict at the end, not a tour of the positions and not a "it depends what you mean by dilemma" shrug. I need to know whether the situation I've built for my main character is a genuine moral dilemma or a fake one that any reader who took an ethics course is going to catch me on.

Here's the setup, and the details matter because I built them deliberately to dodge the obvious objections.

Nadia is a physician. Eight years ago, as a junior resident, she was told something in confidence by a patient who has since died, and she was legally required to report it and didn't. She also made him a promise not to repeat it. So that obligation is now impossible to discharge — it's already been violated, permanently, and there's no present action that satisfies it. Cut to now: she has good reason to believe a third party is at serious risk from something connected to that same information, and telling him looks like what she ought to do, all the way down.

The thing I'm interested in is that these two "oughts" don't collide at the same time. You can't get an obligation to do both, because one of the two is no longer doable by anyone. So I want the essay to take seriously whether the standard logical attack on dilemmas — ought implies can, plus the principle that lets you conjoin obligations, so a real dilemma would have you obligated to do something impossible — even gets a grip here, or whether it slides right off because the conflict is between a violated past obligation and a live present one. If it slides off, is what's left still a moral dilemma, or is it just guilt sitting next to a duty? That's the crux for me.

Second wrinkle: she could physically do the present thing. Nothing stops her except that it would cost her license and, realistically, the care arrangements for her disabled daughter. So on a plausible view she isn't *required* to eat that cost, and the "ought" softens into a should-that-loses-out-to-other-considerations. Does making the impossibility normative rather than logical rescue the dilemma, or does it just collapse it into ordinary weighing, which is not tragedy at all?

Third: she walked into this. Eight years ago she had a clean choice and made the wrong one, so the whole situation is self-inflicted. I want to know whether that changes the verdict or is a red herring.

So: weigh the arguments on both sides — the logical-impossibility case, and the moral-residue / agent-regret case that people like Williams pressed against it — and then tell me where you land on Nadia specifically. Conditional verdicts are fine ("genuine on reading X of 'can', not on reading Y"), but I want a real commitment, and I want you to say which of my three wrinkles is actually doing the work and which is decoration. Also tell me what would change your mind.

And then, at the very end, one paragraph of practical advice: in the last chapter she says, out loud, that there was no right thing for her to do. On your conclusion, is that line literally true, true-but-loose, or false? Because if it's false I need to rewrite the scene and I'd rather know now.
