Every year, the second-year cognitive science students in my lab section arrive convinced that learning a concept from examples is simply a matter of finding the rule that fits the data, and that any two people who fit the data equally well have got it equally right. I have one 100-minute session, 18 of them at six tables of three, a printer, index cards, and three whiteboards — no screens — and I want to break that conviction without lecturing them out of it. They will have read nothing beforehand and I do not intend to assign anything.

The vehicle I want is the family of puzzles that first appeared in an appendix to Mikhail Bongard's 1967 book on pattern recognition: a card showing twelve simple line drawings, six to the left of a dividing line and six to the right, with the solver's task being to state the rule that puts them on their respective sides. Some of my students will have met one or two of these. I do not want them working on authentic cards, though — I want you to invent new ones.

Structure the session in three phases, so that the difficulty arrives as a surprise rather than as a warning. In phase one, tables work several cards under what looks like naive scoring: a rule that fits all twelve boxes earns a point, speed earns a bonus, everyone is confident and competitive. In phase two, reveal that each card had a thirteenth, held-out box and recompute their scores under a harder rule, which I have only sketched and which I want you to build out properly. A candidate rule earns credit only if it classifies the held-out box as the designer intended, and only if an adversary at another table — handed the same twelve boxes and the candidate rule — cannot construct a rival rule that also fits all twelve but sorts the held-out box the other way. Work out what I have left open: when the held-out box is shown and who is allowed to see it; how the adversary is compensated, so that the role is neither sabotage nor a formality everyone performs badly; how tables are paired so no one sees its own problem twice; and what happens when the adversary fails. That last case matters most. I want the game to leave it genuinely unsettled whether an unrefuted rule shows the concept was determinate or only that the adversary was unimaginative — do not resolve this in the scoring, and do not resolve it in the debrief either.

Phase three is a vocabulary freeze: each table is handed a short closed list of predicates and must state its rule using nothing but those. It should turn out that for some cards the freeze makes the problem trivial and for others it makes it impossible, and the students should be the ones to notice that the openness of the descriptive vocabulary was doing real work all along.

Please deliver:

1. A run sheet with timings that sum to 100 minutes, setup included.
2. A compact text notation for specifying a card's twelve boxes and its held-out box, legible enough that a student can parse a card in under a minute and that I can typeset without drawing anything, with one fully worked example.
3. Five original problems in that notation. For each: the intended rule; the held-out box; at least two rival rules that fit all twelve boxes but disagree with the intended rule on the held-out box; and a sentence identifying what an actual solver leans on to land on the intended rule rather than a rival — relative simplicity, which features the drawings make visually salient, or an assumption about what the designer meant to convey. Treat that last item as the intellectual center of the deliverable, not as decoration.
4. The phase-three predicate lists: for each of the five problems, one list that makes it trivial and one that makes it unsolvable, each with a sentence explaining why.
5. A debrief consisting of questions I put to the room, in the order I should ask them, each tied to a specific moment in play: that the space of candidate rules was never given to them; that finitely many examples underdetermine; that solving these puzzles is closer to guessing another person's intention than to reading a fact off the data; and that no fixed feature vocabulary survives contact with all five cards.
6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it — including the table that guesses its rule in ninety seconds and then idles, and the student who objects that the whole exercise is unfair because the intended rule was never uniquely specified. That objection is correct, and I want a plan for using it rather than answering it.
7. A frank closing paragraph on what a paper-and-notation version cannot reproduce about the real puzzles, and what I will have to supply by hand to cover the gap.

Do not include references, a bibliography, or a suggested reading list. Everything the students need must be on the cards or in what I say out loud.
