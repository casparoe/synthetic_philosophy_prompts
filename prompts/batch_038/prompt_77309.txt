So I've been on our university museum's collections committee for about three months — I'm faculty, not a curator, I do nineteenth-century European stuff — and the first real thing that landed on me is the cast gallery in the basement. I have to write the memo the committee votes on in March and I keep flip-flopping, so I want to see what an actual draft looks like before I try.

Personal stake, briefly: I grew up two blocks off campus and I was in Saturday art classes in that building as a kid, and I remember those plaster reliefs standing in the lobby. I had completely forgotten them until I opened a crate in November and found out half of them are cracked and everything else is gypsum dust.

The situation. In 1911 the university bought a set of plaster casts — some Parthenon reliefs, a couple of Nineveh slabs, a row of Roman portrait heads — from one of the big European mold workshops, as teaching objects. They hung in a hall until the late sixties, got crated when the department switched to slide projectors, and nobody has opened most of the crates since. They're cracked, several were badly overpainted at some point, and there are iron staples and old fill everywhere.

Meanwhile a donor has offered to fund a full-scale milled replica of one of the metope panels, produced from photogrammetric data captured directly off the original marble in London at sub-millimeter resolution, in a resin that takes pigment well. On any measure of surface information it's a better record than our plaster is, because ours is a third-generation object: marble to mold to cast to mold-taken-off-that-cast to our cast. Every generation loses something.

Three camps on the committee, all reasonable people:

A. Dispose of the plaster. Degraded, derivative, and eating climate-controlled storage we don't have, while the replica does the teaching job better. Photograph and document the crates thoroughly, then let them go.

B. Conserve the plaster and politely decline the replica money. The plaster has a causal chain running back to the stone and back through 1911 hands, and it's an artifact of how this university taught — the overpaint, the staples, the invoice are all part of the object. The replica is information without an ancestry.

C. Do both: restore one bay of plaster and hang the milled panel beside it as an exhibit about copying itself.

Here's the wrinkle I don't think any of the three camps has absorbed. Our conservator has been going through the set, and she's now fairly convinced at least four pieces — including one of the two best — were not made by the workshop named on the 1911 invoice at all. They look 1930s or 40s, and she thinks they were molded off our own casts, in this building. So the paperwork is wrong, the chain to the marble doesn't reach the marble for those four, and what we have is a fourth-generation object whose entire descent happened indoors. Worse, one of the four doesn't appear to be a cast of an ancient relief at all, but a cast of some nineteenth-century sculptor's invented reconstruction of a fragment — the endpoint of that chain isn't an antiquity, it's a fake that was sold to us as a cast.

So the memo has to handle the fact that provenance-chain and physical-information-fidelity can come apart in both directions here. Camp B's argument looks like it proves too much (a chain four generations long and mostly indoor isn't obviously more valuable than no chain) and too little (if the chain is what carries the value, the milled panel has a shorter and cleaner epistemic route to the actual stone — the data went straight off the marble — so it might win on B's own terms). And camp A's argument, applied honestly, disposes of the four suspicious pieces too, which is the one outcome the conservator says would destroy the only surviving evidence about them.

Can you draft the memo, in a form I could actually circulate? It needs to come down on a recommendation for the March vote: a specific disposition for the set as a whole, a specific disposition for the four disputed pieces, and a yes or no on the donor's money — with a timeframe, who does what, and what happens if the conservation findings turn out to be wrong in either direction. It also has to state the strongest version of whichever camp it rejects, seriously enough that a member of that camp would say fine, you understood me, I just disagree. And please don't let further study become the whole answer; if you want more study, name the piece, name the test, and say in advance which result would flip the recommendation.
