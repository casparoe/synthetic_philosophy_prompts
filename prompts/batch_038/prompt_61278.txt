I am a novelist at work on a third book, and what I need from you is a piece of philosophical engineering, not advice about writing.

The premise of the novel: my narrator, a woman in her late sixties who has spent forty years restoring damaged silent film for a regional archive, has arrived — without ever reading a page of philosophy — at something close to Berkeley's idealism. She cannot shake the conviction that the canister sitting in the cold vault across town, in the dark, on a night when nobody has looked at it, is not there in the way she is there. Late in the book she devises a test on herself: an attempt to catch herself in the very act of thinking of something that is not being thought of. I want the reader to see from inside her head both that the test is serious and that it fails — or that it succeeds, if you can build one that does.

Build me that thought experiment, to specification, such that I can set it in a scene.

1. One scenario, no more than roughly 250 words, in a workaday setting — a house, an archive, a road, a shop. No supernatural machinery, no science fiction, no equipment she could not plausibly own. No dreams, no anaesthesia, no memory loss.
2. It must be a test she performs on herself, in real time, and it must be something she could say aloud in a kitchen to a friend without either of them losing the thread. Prose, not notation.
3. It must genuinely engage what commentators call Berkeley's master argument: the challenge to conceive of a sensible thing existing unconceived, together with the claim that the attempt defeats itself, since whatever one frames in the very act of framing it is thereby conceived. State that argument yourself in the analysis; I do not want the scene to contain a summary of it.
4. God is not available. The case must retain its force for a reader who refuses to admit an omnipresent perceiver into the furniture of the world. Neither the scenario nor the analysis may lean on divine observation.
5. The ordinary escapes must be closed. The object in question may not be one she remembers, one she expects to encounter again, one that another person is presently attending to, or one whose traces — a dent, a log entry, a surviving frame — are open to her. No tree in the quadrangle while she walks away from it.
6. A realist and a Berkeleyan must give divergent verdicts on one and the same question: has she, in that moment, conceived of a thing existing unconceived? The realist says yes and means it. The Berkeleyan says no — and that no must rest on something other than the remark that she is conceiving it right now, since that remark disposes of every case anyone has ever proposed. If you conclude that no scenario can force the idealist's negative to rest on anything else, tell me so plainly rather than supplying a case that the retort dissolves.
7. It must be possible for her to be sincere and mistaken, and possible for the reader to see how.

The deliverable: the scenario; then a check against each of the seven specifications, stating which position delivers which verdict and why; then an account of which single feature of the case does the work — I suspect it will concern what she has actually managed to frame, and whether that is a determinate object or only a general description wearing the clothes of one, but do not take my word for it; then what becomes of the case if that feature is removed, and whether it collapses back into the stock examples; then the sharpest attack a careful Berkeleyan would mount on it, with the strongest reply available to me; and finally your honest judgement about whether a case meeting all seven constraints exists, and if not, which constraint must go and what the nearest achievable version looks like.

I would much rather be told that the design fails than be handed a scene that cheats — one that stipulates the verdicts instead of making the two positions produce them.
