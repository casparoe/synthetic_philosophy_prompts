Context. I am auditing the handout from a graduate seminar on possible-worlds semantics. To introduce counterpart theory, the instructor offered the analogy of a fictional character across screen adaptations: Holmes is played by a different actor in each adaptation; each portrayal is a distinct particular, yet we say without hesitation that Holmes lives in London, that Holmes might have been more sympathetic, that in one adaptation Holmes solves the case and in another he fails. The instructor used the analogy to license two conclusions. First, that the counterpart apparatus is not an exotic revision of our modal thought but a regimentation of something we already do comfortably. Second, that the familiar complaint against counterpart theory — that what comes out true is not about Humphrey himself but about a numerically distinct individual who merely resembles him, so Humphrey gets no comfort from his counterpart's victory — misses its mark, since the parallel complaint in the Holmes case (that was not Holmes, that was an actor) is cheerfully granted by everyone, who then goes on talking about Holmes without embarrassment. A classmate objected that the analogy is worse than useless: if fictional characters are abstract artifacts created by an author and realized in each work that features them, then Holmes is literally the same character across adaptations, so the analogy presupposes transworld identity and begs the question against the very view it is meant to defeat.
