I write software for a living and have no formal philosophy training, so I have set this out the way I would set out a design review — numbered steps, no rhetorical connective tissue — because that is the only way I can keep track of it. For several months I have been arguing with a friend who holds the Molinist position: that before God decides anything about creating, he already knows, for every possible person and every complete set of circumstances that person could be placed in, what that person would freely choose in those circumstances. My friend thinks this buys a strong doctrine of providence and genuine libertarian freedom at the same time, with no determinism involved. I tried to write down what I took to be the decisive objection to that combination, and I now think the argument proves considerably more than I intended.

1. For every possible person S and every complete circumstance C, God knows before creating what S would freely do in C.
2. What God knows is true and could not have been otherwise; if it could have been false, God's knowledge could have been mistaken.
3. God freely decides which circumstances each created person is in fact placed in.
4. So once God makes that decision, only one outcome for each person's choice is consistent with (1) through (3): God's decision, together with what God already knew, settles what each person chooses.
5. If something outside a person settles which choice that person makes, that person does not make the choice freely.
6. Therefore, on Molinism, no created person ever chooses freely.
7. But the entire purpose of (1) was to preserve libertarian freedom alongside providential control.
8. So Molinism cannot deliver both; it delivers the control and loses the freedom.

Two things trouble me. First, the argument does not appear to need anything specifically Molinist. If I delete the talk of circumstances and counterfactuals and say only that God knows from eternity what each person will in fact choose, (4) through (6) seem to follow unchanged. What I have written is then not an objection to middle knowledge but an objection to infallible foreknowledge as such — a much older and much better defended commitment than the one I set out to attack, and one I happen to share. Second, (5) does not seem to care who the knower is. Applied to God's knowledge of his own future decisions, it appears to show that God is not free either, and I do not think anyone on either side of this dispute wants that result.

I cannot accept (6). Ranking my preferences: I would rather keep (5) — I am reluctant to give up the intuition that freedom requires that nothing external has already fixed the outcome — than keep (1), and I would rather keep (1) than accept (6).

So: is this argument valid, leaving me a premise I am obliged to abandon, or does one of the steps fail to follow from the ones before it? Tell me which step you would reject, and then — this is the part I most want — explain what made that step look unobjectionable when I wrote it, since each of them seemed obviously true to me at the time and I cannot reconstruct how I got from there to (6). Then price the exits, which as far as I can see are four: deny that there are any truths of the kind (1) asserts; deny (5) and accept that foreknowledge is compatible with freedom; accept (6) and adopt a deterministic account of providence; or give up infallible foreknowledge altogether. For each, say what it costs and how much of the original motivation survives. Finally, tell me whether the argument's survival after the removal of middle knowledge weakens it as an objection to Molinism, or whether it reveals something about the argument itself.
