The lecturer has let me help mark one set of take-homes, partly as a learning exercise for me, and I could do with a second pair of eyes because I can't yet tell a good essay from a merely confident one.

Here is the question the students were set:

"Mises argued in 1920 that rational economic calculation is impossible under socialism; Lange responded in 1936–37 with a model in which a Central Planning Board sets producer-goods prices by trial and error. Explain how Lange's proposal is meant to answer Mises, and explain Hayek's objection to that proposal. Then say whether the collapse of Soviet-style planning in 1989–91 settled the theoretical dispute. Pure chronology will not pass." (900 words max.)

And here is one student's answer, more or less in full:

---

Mises' argument, first set out in his 1920 paper, is an argument about knowledge. His claim is that the information a planner would need — which machines are wearing out, which local supplier can be relied on, what a particular foreman knows about his own shop floor — is spread across millions of people, is often tacit, and can never be gathered into one head or one building. Prices, on this view, are a communication device: they compress all that scattered local knowledge into a single number. Mises concludes that without them the planner flies blind.

Hayek's version, developed later in the 1930s, is more radical and more fundamental. He argues that the problem is not that the planner cannot collect the data but that under socialism there is nothing for the data to be about: with no private ownership of the means of production, capital goods are never actually exchanged, so no genuine exchange ratios for them can ever form. The planner can keep accounts, but accounting entries are not prices. Without prices for capital goods there is no way to compare alternative production methods at all, so rational allocation is not merely difficult but meaningless. On this reading Hayek, not Mises, is the one who holds that socialism is conceptually impossible, while Mises thinks it is merely informationally hopeless.

Lange's answer accepts the formal challenge. In his model he keeps consumer sovereignty and free choice of occupation, and lets a Central Planning Board do what Walras' auctioneer does: it announces shadow prices for producer goods, managers are told to combine inputs so as to minimise average cost of any given output and to produce the output at which price equals marginal cost, and the board raises prices where there is excess demand and lowers them where there is a surplus. Iterated, this converges on the allocation a competitive market would reach. Lange later added that a board with a big enough computer could solve the whole system of simultaneous equations at once, so the trial and error could in principle be speeded up enormously.

So the dispute was settled at the level of theory in Lange's favour — socialism is logically possible — and what was left over was an engineering question. That is why 1989–91 does not settle anything theoretically. The Soviet system failed becuase of the arms race, the self-interest of the nomenklatura, and the falsification of plan reports by managers who had no reason to tell the truth; these are political and incentive failures of a kind any regime can suffer. If the information problem is in principle solvable, then the collapse of actually existing socialism shows only that the Soviet Union never implemented the Lange solution, not that the calculation argument was right.

---
