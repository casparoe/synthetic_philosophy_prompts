Two months ago my department put me on the university's animal research oversight commitee — I'm the humanities seat, so I'm the one who isn't a scientist, and I've kept my mouth shut through two full meetings because I didn't want to be the person who shows up and relitigates everything. The next agenda has one item I can't stay quiet about, and I need help writing it up.

A neuroethology group has submitted a protocol on juvenile cuttlefish. The design: an arena with a food reward on one side and a zone that delivers a mild noxious electrical stimulus to the arm on the other, and they want to see whether the animals will make the trade-off — basically how hungry they have to get before they'll cross. Its a reasonable question and I understand why they're asking it. Three things are snagging me:

1. The PI plans to sedate them with magnesium chloride and says that puts them properly under. Our vet raised an eyebrow at that during pre-review and then didn't say anything further, and I can't tell whether the eyebrow meant something or was just the vet being the vet.
2. The protocol includes a control arm running the identical apparatus on fruit fly larvae, described in the methods as the "assumed-insentient comparator." That phrase is doing an enormous amount of work and nobody has checked it. I've read enough to think fly larvae have nociceptive neurons and do a characteristic whole-body thrash when you prod them with something noxious, so tell me if I'm way off base.
3. Our charter's coverage clause says we oversee vertebrates plus "any invertebrate taxon for which the committee finds credible evidence of a capacity to experience pain." So we're supposed to make a status determination before we can even vote on the protocol, and we have never actually had to do that, because nobody has ever brought us invertebrates. (My understanding is that in Europe cephalopods are simply covered by the research rules and here they aren't, which is part of why this landed on our agenda as a judgement call.)

And here's the part I keep circling. The usual shortcut isn't available. With a rat you can argue from shared ancestry: we're close relatives, the machinery is homologous, so the inference transfers. With a cuttlefish the cleverness and the big brain are convergent rather than inherited — the last common ancestor we shared was a worm with no nervous system worth mentioning. So if we grant status to cuttlefish, what exactly are we granting it on the grounds of, and does that same ground cover the fly larvae in arm 2? If it does, the protocol's control group is a mess. If it doesn't, I want to know how the committee is supposed to explain why not without the explanation coming out as "closer to us equals more real," which is exactly the kind of reasoning I thought this body was supposed to be better than.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting? What I need in it:

- A clear recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, defer, or reject — with a timeframe attached, because the PI has a renewal deadline in six weeks and the animals are already on order and everyone in the room knows that I know that.
- The status determination handled explicitly rather than smuggled in under the protocol question, including what standard of evidence the committee should apply when the homology argument is off the table, and what it should do for a taxon where the evidence genuinely looks evenly balanced. "We don't know" is going to be the honest answer somewhere in here and I want the memo to say what follows from that instead of treating it as a license to punt.
- The PI's side stated as strongly as the PI would state it. There's a real argument that precaution applied to every invertebrate taxon would freeze the very research that produces the evidence we're demanding, and another one that this committee spends twenty minutes a year on rodent protocols causing considerably more harm, and I don't want either of them dodged.
- Something we can actually vote on: the conditions, or criteria a reviewer could apply to the next invertebrate protocol without needing to be me.

Two pages max, written for scientists who are impatient with philosophy but not immune to it. No references and no reading list — I can't chase citations and neither can they, so if a study matters, describe it. Please don't let it turn into a lecture about pain being private and other minds being unknowable; that's true and it's useless here. And above all it has to read like a committee document rather than a manifesto. I gave up eating animals about a year ago, the chair knows it, and if this comes across as campaigning I've burned whatever credibility I built out of two months of shutting up.
