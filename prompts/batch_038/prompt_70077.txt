I am preparing a session of my graduate seminar on meaning, information, and levels of description, and I want to place before my students a strong, self-contained case for a thesis I myself hold but which the assigned reading resists. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind and of information, thoroughly at home with Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach, with the frame-theoretic idiom he borrows from work on knowledge representation, and with his general claim that a message carries meaning in virtue of a recoverable isomorphism between it and something else. Think the problem through carefully rather than reaching for the obvious objections.

So that we start from the same place, and so that you need not reconstruct anything from memory, here is the distinction as I take it to be. In the chapter of Gödel, Escher, Bach on the location of meaning, Hofstadter suggests that the meaning of a message is not all in one place but distributed over three: the frame message, which specifies how the message is to be decoded (the conventions of playback, the cellular machinery that reads a gene); the outer message, which is information about the message rather than information conveyed by means of it (the jacket and label of a record, which tell you what sort of object you are holding); and the inner message, the content that emerges once decoding succeeds (the music, the phenotype). His two running illustrations are the phonograph record and DNA: a disc without a turntable is a spiral of plastic, and a gene outside a cell is a long polymer.

The thesis I want argued is that this trichotomy does not survive Hofstadter's own method. The three categories are not three kinds of part of a message; they are three roles that one and the same item can occupy, depending on which decoder you hold fixed, and the book's own insistence on moving freely between levels of description forbids holding any decoder fixed. What is invariant is a two-place notion — what an artifact means relative to a decoder's standing capacities — together with a single quantitative question: how much of the decoding routine the artifact carries with it, as against how much the decoder must already supply. The trichotomy is that one question wearing three hats.

Please argue for this thesis, not merely expound it. Your argument should accomplish three things.

First, demonstrate the instability by explicit reassignment. Show, in the record case and in the DNA case, that an item Hofstadter places in one category can be moved into another without straining the facts, and identify what feature of the situation is doing the assigning. Then supply a third case of your own devising, drawn from somewhere the book does not go, and show the same thing there.

Second, put the replacement account on the table with enough precision that it could turn out to be false. Say what counts as a standing capacity of a decoder as against something carried by the artifact, and say what would count as a case the replacement cannot handle.

Third, take up the two strongest objections and answer them. The first is that the trichotomy earns its keep by explaining phenomena your relational account leaves untouched: why a disc played at the wrong speed is noise even though its grooves are unchanged, and why a gene in a test tube means nothing at all. The second is that the replacement is vacuous, since any account indexed to a decoder is trivially level-relative and can therefore never be embarrassed by a reassignment. Do not close with a paragraph of balance that concedes the trichotomy its place; I want my students to have something to push against.

Begin with no more than six lines of working notes, listing and ranking your candidate arguments and objections by strength; then deliver the argument itself. The argument must open with a single sentence stating the conclusion you will defend, must be written in continuous prose with no headings and no bulleted lists, and must not exceed 900 words. Offer no citations, no page references, and no bibliography: make the case from the ideas themselves, and write for a reader who has read the book but has not thought about this question.
