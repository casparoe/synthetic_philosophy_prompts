I teach a twelve-student graduate seminar in philosophy of science on objective chance, and I am now writing the closed-book final: three hours, two essay questions, no texts in the room. One question is already set — a fairly conventional one comparing Humean and non-Humean accounts of what chance is. I want the second question to be the discriminating one, the item that separates students who can reason with the Principal Principle from students who can recite it. Last year's version of that question failed badly in this respect: a study guide circulated through the cohort, and I ended up awarding top marks for performances that were really just a well-rehearsed list of slogans. So the question I need here must be one that a student cannot answer well from notes alone, must not be answerable by rehearsing the standard labels for the positions in the debate, and must force a decision about a concrete case rather than an exposition of a controversy.

Here is the case material I want the question built around; please formalise and polish it, but keep its structure. A device — call it the Nightlight — flashes either red or green once each night at midnight. Let H be the chance hypothesis saying that for every night n, the chance of red on night n is one half, with nights independent; let H* be the hypothesis that the device is stuck and will flash red on every night forever with chance one. At night zero, before the first flash, the agent assigns credence one half to H and one half to H*. The exam should then ask students to determine whether each of the following is admissible relative to H at night zero, under two competing characterisations of admissibility that the question itself must state: the past-and-chance characterisation, on which admissible evidence is information about times earlier than the chance in question together with information about chances themselves, and the PP-preserving characterisation, on which admissible evidence is whatever evidence the Principal Principle may be applied to without inconsistency. The three items are these. E1: a factory ledger recording that of one thousand units shipped from the plant exactly one was of the stuck kind, and that this unit was selected at random from that shipment. E2: a sealed diagnostic printout, dated night one thousand and one and delivered by a mechanism the agent trusts completely, reporting that the Nightlight flashed red on each of the first thousand nights. E3: a report that on night one thousand and one the agent will be shown that printout and will, on seeing it, have credence one hundredth in H. The question should require students to state the Principal Principle precisely as a constraint on conditional credence, to give admissibility verdicts on E1 through E3 under both characterisations, to say whether either characterisation can be defended given those verdicts, to explain what their verdicts imply about whether the Principal Principle is a substantive thesis or a trivial one, and finally to state and then either defend or reject the claim that in the presence of E2 the Principal Principle and Bayesian conditionalisation cannot both be honoured.

Please deliver three things: the question exactly as it will appear on the paper, including the formal statement of the principle and the complete description of the case, since nothing may be left to the students' memory; a model answer of roughly 750 to 850 words that argues its way through the case instead of surveying positions; and a marking scheme out of twenty built on five must-hit points, each with its mark value and a one-line note on the error that most often costs students that particular mark — for example, treating the principle as a constraint on unconditional credence, or pronouncing E2 inadmissible without stating the general rule that ruling presupposes. Add a brief note on what an answer must do to reach the top band rather than merely clear the five points. Students will be told to write no more than twelve hundred words in forty-five minutes, so please calibrate the demands accordingly. Two further constraints. First, no citations, article titles, or philosophers' names other than Lewis's should appear anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme, and the model answer should paraphrase throughout rather than reproduce any wording from the literature. Second, if you judge that the future-dated printout in E2 is doing dialectical work that a more mundane piece of evidence could not do — that is, if you think students will dismiss it as science fiction and thereby escape the real problem — say so explicitly and supply a realistic substitute item that generates the same tension, which I can slot in place of E2.
