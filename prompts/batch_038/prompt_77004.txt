Just got out of a four-hour argument with my friend Dana about whether the world is getting less violent, and I've realised we never once disagreed about a number. She's a rates person: homicide per hundred thousand, battle deaths per capita, all trending down for centuries, and by her lights that settles it. I kept coming back with the fact that the absolute body count rose through most of that stretch simply because there are far more of us now; that "violence" as she's using it excludes most of what determines whether a given person gets harmed; that the record we're reading off isn't neutral, because categories like assault get redefined and destigmatised over time so a rising count can mean a less violent society that finally bothers to write things down, and because the violence that deterrence prevented leaves no trace at all and is exactly the thing you'd most want to measure; and that a couple of quiet decades is weak evidence about anything if the process is heavy-tailed, where a handful of enormous wars do most of the killing with long lulls in between. She said I was moving the goalposts. I said her goalposts were doing the arguing for her. Neither of us budged and it ended with both of us mildly annoyed and nothing learned, which is what makes me think the actual disagreement was about what would count as settling the question rather than about the question.

So before I read one more thing on either side, write me the spec. What does a claim of the form "the world is becoming less violent" have to deliver before it's even assessable — the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the facts about evidence and measurement it can't contradict — with the case or consideration behind each item, not just the item. The purpose I'm fixing is adjudication: I want a list strict enough that two people who accept the same data and still disagree can be told which of them is making a substantive error and which is making a legitimate choice of framing. So I especially want you to be explicit about the places where the demand is doing normative work dressed up as measurement — the denominator question (whose wellbeing is the rate indexed to, and why per capita rather than per person-exposed-to-interaction, or per society, or absolute), the category question (does an economic order that predictably kills count as violence, and if you say yes, does the concept still pick anything out, or does it stop discriminating at all), the aggregation question (when homicide, interstate war, genocide, torture, incarceration, and the treatment of farmed animals are all moving in different directions, what licenses saying which trend is "the" trend), the weighting question (are deaths fungible across causes, or is a death in a bombing different from a death by policy-induced famine, and does duration of suffering matter or only realized harm), and the baseline question (over what window, against what counterfactual, and what's the difference between "less violent than it used to be" and "less violent than it would otherwise have been"). If it helps: I keep coming back to whether this is more like a natural kind question or more like asking whether a garden is getting weedier, where "weed" only means something relative to what the gardener wants — tell me whether that analogy holds or where it breaks, because that seems to decide whether there's a fact of the matter at all.

Then the parts I actually care about most. For each requirement, say whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or whether it's the prejudice of one camp wearing a lab coat — my suspicion is that "must exclude structural or systemic harm" and "must include it" are both camp commitments rather than desiderata, but I can't tell. Flag any subset of the list you think is jointly unsatisfiable, and if there is one, say which item has to go and what giving it up does to whether the question has a determinate answer; if the unsatisfiable cluster is something like {stable category across centuries, moral relevance to present concerns, determinate truth value}, that's the result I want spelled out rather than smoothed over. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most positions. And only after all that, briefly score the camps — the rate-based optimist, the structural-violence critic, the heavy-tailed skeptic — against the list, one or two sentences each, because the thinking is in the criteria and I'll do the scorecard myself. No bibliography, no article titles, no author names, and please no specific statistics or numbers, I don't want anything I'd have to go verify and I don't want you inventing figures. A table is fine for the list itself, prose for the unsatisfiability argument.
