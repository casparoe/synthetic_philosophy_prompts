I am a professor of philosophy at a large research university, and at the end of this term I have been asked to deliver the five-minute toast at the retirement dinner of a colleague who spent thirty-five years building systems that try to infer what users actually want rather than serve them what they say they want — work that would now be described as intent alignment rather than value alignment. She has always maintained that the gap between the two is not a technical matter but a moral one, since a system that faithfully optimizes a person's stated preferences can ruin that person while never once disobeying them. The room will contain roughly forty people: former students and collaborators who know the literature and the failure cases intimately, her two adult children, and a number of colleagues from unrelated departments who will have heard the phrase artificial intelligence and nothing more. Write the toast for me. The intent-value distinction must do structural work rather than serve as ornament: it should determine what the toast can claim about her career and what it must refrain from claiming. It has to survive the scrutiny of the experts in the room, including the objection that her systems were, at bottom, only ever approximating click-through rates; it has to be fully intelligible to her children without condescension; and it must land as affection rather than as a lecture. Please write in American English.
