I am a novelist, and for the epigraph of my fourth book I have undertaken to render into English a short verse letter by Clément Marot: the one addressed to a young lady who is ill, which Douglas Hofstadter made the fixed point of his 1997 book on translation and referred to by its first words, "Ma mignonne." As I understand the commission, the constraints are severe and not open to negotiation — twenty-eight lines of three syllables each, in rhyming couplets, with the closing couplet rhyming on the lady's given name. I have some thirty drafts. What interests me is not the drafts but a list I have been keeping of which of my own solutions I am willing to live with and which I am not. The list does not appear to have been written by a single coherent theory, and I should like to know whether it was.

My verdicts are these.

1. A draft that keeps all twenty-eight lines, keeps every line at three syllables, and keeps every rhyme, but which in order to manage this replaces the substance of Marot's plea — recover, come back, the court is nothing without you, your illness has taken the colour from your face — with a complaint about a headache and a cancelled supper: not acceptable. It reads as a different poem in fancy dress.

2. A draft that abandons the three-syllable line for a longer, looser English line but retains twenty-eight lines, retains the couplet rhymes, and retains the final rhyme on her name: acceptable — and it reads more like the original than the first draft does.

3. The same as 2, but twenty-seven lines, because one couplet's content was absorbed into its neighbour: not acceptable. I cannot explain this even to myself. Altering the length of every line in the poem is survivable; the loss of a single line is fatal.

4. A draft in which I failed to rhyme the sixth couplet and made the loss good by adding an internal rhyme in the twelfth: acceptable — the poem's total quantity of rhyme feels undiminished. A draft in which I lost a detail of sense, namely that she is confined to her bed, and made it good by adding a different and sharper detail later, that her correspondents have gone quiet: not acceptable. Form behaves as though it were a common pool from which a deficit may be repaid anywhere in the poem; sense does not behave that way at all.

5. A draft that renders Marot's courtly teasing in blunt contemporary colloquial English, joking and faintly rude: acceptable, because the teasing arrives intact. A draft that preserves the dictionary sense of every line and loses the teasing, reading instead like a physician's note: not acceptable. But then a third draft, which preserves the teasing perfectly by inventing a new tease — about her dress rather than her illness: not acceptable. I appear to hold that function may be carried across by new wording but never by new content, and I cannot state the difference.

6. A draft that substitutes an English girl's name so that the closing couplet can still rhyme upon a name: not acceptable, though I have just accepted a wholesale substitution of register. Something about aiming the poem at a person who never existed, I suppose; but I am not certain this is more than superstition.

7. A draft made by a colleague who reads no French, working from a literal word-for-word crib that I supplied: acceptable, provided the form and the tease come through. This verdict embarrasses me most of all.

Is there a principle, or a short set of competing candidates, that delivers these verdicts? I should like it stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a draft I have not described, with each clause accompanied by the case above that forced it. Then give me its ruling on four situations I have not yet tried: a companion poem in the identical form, in the lady's own voice, answering Marot line for line; a draft that keeps form, sense, and tease but updates the illness to a modern convalescence; a scrupulously faithful prose rendering, printed facing the French, used as the epigraph instead of any verse; and a version generated by a program that searches English for words satisfying the constraints and arrives at sense only by luck.

I also want an honest accounting of the cost. Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which single one I ought to surrender — I suspect it is either the pair at 2 and 3 or the third clause of 5, but I do not know which, and I would rather be told plainly than have every verdict rescued by a principle too baroque to use. If a simpler principle requires me to overturn two verdicts rather than one, say so and say which two.

Finally: does whatever you settle on commit me to anything about my own fiction in translation? I have had a translator who preserved the content of every sentence and took no notice of my paragraph lengths, and another who kept my rhythms and cut a subplot, and I find that I praised the second and blamed the first without knowing why.

Lead with your bottom line — the principle itself, in your first sentence, as compactly as it can be stated — and only then argue for it, clause by clause and case by case. No preamble, and no restatement of what I have just asked.
