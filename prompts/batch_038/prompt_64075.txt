I am a third-year doctoral student, and on Friday I have fifty minutes of a methods section for eighteen second-year PPE students on methodological individualism and its holist rivals. Most of them have done one term of microeconomics and one of political theory, and two have already announced in seminar that the question is closed: obviously only people act, so anything else is poetry. I have spent a good many years on forums where the debate is treated the same way — one side a truism, the other a category error — and I have come to think that the closed-question version is exactly what makes the topic hard to teach, because the students walk in holding it and cannot see it as a claim at all. I do not want the content from you; I can write that. I want the sequence and the diagnostics. Start by telling me how to prise apart the three things that travel under that single label in my students' heads: a thesis about what there is that acts, a thesis about what counts as an admissible explanation, and a thesis about whether macro-level claims must be translatable into or derivable from micro-level ones. Which of the three has to be separated first, and why — I suspect their confidence is generated by sliding between them, and that until the slide is exposed they will hear every holist argument as the claim that society has a mind of its own, which nobody I know defends. I also want to know where in the hour, if anywhere, Popper's point that endorsing individualism is not the same as endorsing psychologism belongs; I understand the distinction but I cannot see what pedagogical work it does at this level, and I am inclined to cut it.

Then the carrying example. I keep reaching for a bank run, but I now fear it argues the other way: the individual-level facts that do the explaining there seem to be each depositor's beliefs about the other depositors' beliefs, so the micro base is shot through with the very social content the individualist was supposed to eliminate. Tell me whether that worry is well founded, and if it is, what a good example for this purpose has to have and what it must conspicuously lack — I have been considering a traffic jam, a norm about queuing, a hyperinflation, and a shift in the vowel system of a language, and I can see reasons against each but not which reason is decisive. Relatedly, and this is the part I most need planned rather than answered: whatever example I pick, the description of the agents' beliefs will have to use vocabulary like money, votes, tenure, and promises, and a bright student will notice that the reduction is being stated in the terms it was supposed to reduce. I want to know where in the sequence that objection should be invited, whether it should be met or conceded and in what words, and what has to already be in place for either move to land instead of detonating the hour.

Finally, the misconception map, sequenced against your example rather than listed in the abstract. I expect four: that individualism means individuals are self-contained atoms whose beliefs come pre-formed; that a macro-regularity is merely a manner of speaking, hence not a fact; that supervenience settles the matter, so the higher level is not real; and, from the one student who has been reading about corporate group agency for a business-ethics module, that if a firm can be said to believe and intend then the firm can act and individualism is simply false. Say at what point in the order each should be headed off and what single sentence does it, and then tell me which misconception I have failed to anticipate that is more dangerous than all four — I assume there is one, and I assume it is the one that survives the section and shows up in their essays. Close with one question I can put in the last five minutes, answerable in a couple of sentences by someone who has actually grasped the dispute and unanswerable by someone who has only memorised which side the slogan is on, together with what a good answer must contain and what a fluent-looking bad answer will sound like. Also give me an explicit list of what I should decline to get into at this level — I have in mind free will, the reduction of chemistry, the ontology of sets, and anything about collective moral responsibility — and, for each, the sentence I use to decline it without seeming evasive. Please do not suggest readings; I am not assigning any, and I have no way to check that anything you name exists.
