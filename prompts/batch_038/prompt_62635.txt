I have run a reading group on analogy and concepts for years, and the argument between two camps has never advanced an inch; I increasingly suspect the participants are not disagreeing about what they take themselves to be disagreeing about. One camp works in the tradition of Hofstadter and Mitchell's Copycat project: a deliberately impoverished microdomain of letter strings (given abc : abd, complete ijk : ?; harder, aabc : aabd :: ijkk : ?), an associative network of concepts whose link strengths shift with activation, and small stochastic processes that build and demolish partial structures in a workspace. For them, analogy-making and categorization are one process, and conceptual slippage — a group of letters taking over the role played by a single letter — is the visible tip of how all concepts work. The other camp, in Gentner's structure-mapping tradition, holds that analogy is the alignment of relational structure retrieved from a rich long-term memory, with relations systematically preferred over attributes, and that letter strings are a degenerate case with almost no relational structure to align. Each has a standing charge against the other: that the microdomain is too thin to license general claims, and that the relational account redescribes which analogies are good without explaining how any are produced.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that two different questions are wearing one label — what the vehicle of a concept is, versus what makes a mapping correct — with a live empirical question buried underneath about whether response distributions on artificial strings tell us anything about analogy in knowledge-rich domains. A wrinkle: the Copycat authors disclaim any intention of reproducing human answer frequencies exactly, which seems to withdraw the one datum that could arbitrate.

Diagnose the impasse. Is it verbal, methodological, a disagreement about which data count, or bedrock intuitions about what a cognitive model owes us? Argue from what each camp actually says and does rather than surveying the field, and tell me what — a distinction, an experiment, a new class of model — would count as progress, or why none should be expected.
