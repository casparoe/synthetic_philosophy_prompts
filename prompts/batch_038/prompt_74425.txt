So, some background, because it explains why I'm asking. I'm 39 and in March I got a prediabetes diagnosis that was, frankly, not a surprise to anyone. What followed was six months of me discovering that I am extremely good at committing to things on Sunday and extremely bad at executing them on Tuesday, and eventually I fixed a chunk of it with ugly mechanical means: a lockable box in the pantry with the key at my sister's house, a standing bet with a coworker where I forfeit actual money, and deleting stuff off my phone. It worked, which is embarrasing in its own way, because I'd spent years talking and writing about agency as though the machinery didn't matter. A friend who runs a small public-philosophy podcast has asked me on to talk about weakness of will, and I want to send her a page of what I actually believe beforehand so she can aim the conversation at it. Before I send it I want to know whether the page is even internally coherent, because I suspect it isn't and I can't tell where the cracks are from the inside.

Here's the list, roughly in the order I'd present it:

1. I accept the standard descriptive picture. People don't discount consistently: a delay that starts right now gets weighted way more heavily than the same delay starting next month, which is why the ranking you make calmly on Sunday and the ranking you have standing in front of the pastry aren't the same ranking. Not a metaphor, I think it's just true of us.
2. But I don't think that means Sunday-me changed his mind. When Tuesday-me eats the thing, the Sunday ranking is still what he endorses, he just can't get himself to act on it. That's the entire point of calling it weakness of will rather than a revision of opinion: if every failure to stick to a plan is really a fresh judgment that the plan was wrong, the concept has nothing left to describe.
3. When I say someone has botched things here, I'm evaluating the shape of a whole stretch of a life, not a moment. Roughly: does the life go the way the person would want it to go if they were calm, informed, and holding all of it in view at once?
4. I have no patience for the multiple-selves talk. I don't think Tuesday-me is a different person whose interests Sunday-me is exploiting, and I don't think there's anything sinister about binding him. There's one me and one good that's mine.
5. Precommitment is normally rational and often the most rational move available. People who refuse to lock the box on principle, because they want to be the sort of person who can just decide in the moment, are making a mistake and a slightly self-indulgent one.
6. There's an exception I care about, though: it matters enormously who does the locking. My sister holding the key is fine because I handed it to her. A partner, a doctor, or an employer who boxed up my food on the exact same evidence about my future behavior would be doing something objectionable, even if the outcome were identical and even if I'd thank them afterwards.
7. I don't blame people for these failures and I think the sloth-and-discipline vocabulary is mostly useless. Standing in the kitchen at 9pm, the guy could not have done otherwise in any sense that it would have helped to tell him about.
8. Method point: I don't trust people's reports about their own reasons at all, mine turned out to be fiction. But I do think an account that can't accommodate the felt experience of struggling with yourself has thrown away the phenomenon it was supposed to explain.

Tell me which of these actually pull against each other and how badly, worst first, and for each one say what would have to go and what I'd lose by giving it up. Please don't hedge by flagging everything as problematic — I'm pretty sure some of the pairs that look to me like they can't both be true actually can, and I'd like to know which ones those are and why they only look bad. And don't ask me which commitment I'd prefer to sacrifice; I genuinely don't know yet, that's what I'm trying to find out.
