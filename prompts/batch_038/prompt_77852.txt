so I'm prepping a seminar session on what actually counts as corruption, and I'm sick of the examples everyone reaches for — the envelope of cash, the bribed customs officer, the mayor's cousin getting the contract. I want a dialogue built around a case where both sides have something real to say, and I want it to be *this* case specifically, please don't swap in a more familiar one:

A public hospital's orthopedics department, mid-income country. For eleven years the local rep for one implant manufacturer has paid for the whole department to go to the international conference every year — flights, registration, hotel. Twice he's also covered a senior surgeon's mother's private care bills when the public system couldn't get her a bed. In return the department buys that company's implants. Nothing is written down, nobody says the word out loud, and the arrangement is known to everyone including the hospital administration. The implants are good — by most accounts better than the cheaper domestic alternative the new procurement rules would push them toward. Now a compliance officer has arrived from the multinational that holds the hospital's supply contract, applying a bright-line policy: any transfer of value to a clinician who influences purchasing is prohibited, no "modest" or "customary" exception, and disclosure doesn't cure it. Her rule would also kill the conference funding for the junior staff who've never met the rep.

Write the dialogue between the compliance officer and the department head.

what I need from it:

- Neither one is a fool or a villain. Give the compliance officer an actual theory of what's wrong with corruption — not "rules are rules," something about offices and roles, and about the people who are permanently outside the relationship because they'll never be in a position to give a gift or receive one. Give the department head an actual theory too: about recipricity as an obligation-generating system rather than a market, about what a gift network does in a place with no social insurance, and about what happens to corruption when a legible, publicly-known arrangement gets replaced by cash nobody can see.
- Real back and forth. They should be answering each other's actual points, not trading speeches at each other. I want concessions, "fine — but then explain X," and at least one moment where one of them notices that part of the fight is about what the word corruption is doing and part of it is a substantive disagreement that survives fixing the definition. Have them sort that out in dialogue, no narrator's note telling me about it.
- Each of them gets hit with the other's best case and neither gets a free pass. One moment where the bright-line rule makes things worse in a way she can't just wave off (the invisible-cash problem, or the juniors losing the conference, or the domestic implant being worse). One moment where the gift network does real damage to someone outside it — a young surgeon with no connections, a patient who can't reciprocate — in a way he can't wave off either.
- The universality question has to be argued, not gestured at: is her standard a universal moral requirement, or a specifically bureaucratic one that got exported along with the supply contract? Both should get good lines. And I don't want the landing spot to be "well, it's all culturally relative" or "actually the outsiders were right all along."
- The surgeon's mother matters. Someone has to press on the fact that he'd feel bound by that debt whether or not he ever touched the implant choice, and on whether that's a defense or an indictment.
- Don't tie a bow on it. End honest — a stalemate, a narrowed disagreement, or one of them moving an inch on a sub-point.

Call it 1000–1400 words, names and plain dialogue, minimal stage directions. Don't cite anything and don't name real laws, companies, or countries — it should stand on its own.
