I'm 17 and I'm the editor of my high school paper — about 1,800 students, six issues a year. Our district cut the budget so now ads have to cover like 40% of the printing bill, which means I've accidentally become the person who decides what we sell space to. Requests come in constantly and right now we settle them by vibes in a 20 minute meeting, and I don't think thats defensible.

The part I keep getting stuck on: some offers feel gross and I can't articulate why, and some feel totally normal but I'm starting to think those might be the actually bad ones. I've read a bit about repugnant markets and noxious markets and about how selling a thing can change what the thing is, and I want to turn that into something my board can run without me.

So: write us a decision procedure for whether to take an ad, and on what terms. I want the questions in the order we ask them, what counts as a real answer to each one, which questions end it right there with a no, and which ones the procedure can't actually settle becuase they're about what kind of paper we want to be rather than about facts — those should get handed to our advisor or to a board vote, and say which goes where. Assume none of us have had an ethics class, we meet once a week, the faculty advisor signs off, and the principal can veto anything she wants.

Run it on three requests sitting in front of me right now:

- a payday lender two towns over wants a half page in every issue
- a plasma donation center that just opened wants to advertise to our seniors, a bunch of whom are 18
- a college admissions consulting company that's been our biggest advertiser for six years and charges families around $4,000

And tell me straight what it comes out to on each, including the one where I already know I'm going to hate the answer — that company is roughly a third of our ad revenue and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

Then deal with two places I think a basic checklist breaks:

- a company offering to pay for a written piece that reads like our own reporting with a small "sponsored" tag at the bottom. Is that just a fancier ad, or is it a different kind of transaction entirely? Should disclosure fix it, and if not, why not?
- we ran an energy drink ad last year that everyone now agrees was a mistake. Does having taken it last year commit us to it this year? How should the procedure treat precedent, in either direction?

Finally be honest about where it fails: which steps are secretly just my judgment wearing a lab coat, whether an advertiser who knows the list could write copy specifically to pass it, and the fact that we're rating ads one at a time so we could accept twenty individually fine ads and end up with a paper that mostly targets broke people. If there's a way to check the whole year's lineup at once instead of issue by issue, say what that check actually looks like and who runs it.
