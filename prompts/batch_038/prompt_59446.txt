I'm teaching a 75-minute session in a second-year political philosophy seminar — 24 students, mixed majors, maybe a third of them have ever had a poli sci course. We're on legitimacy and political obligation. I want to run something rather than talk at them, and I want them to hit the problem before anybody says the phrase "performance legitimacy" or names a single actual country.

My hunch, which I want the game to test on them instead of assert: judging a regime by how well it delivers isn't a weaker version of judging it by how it's chosen, it's a differently *shaped* risk. Longer fuse, bigger explosion. And under that there's a second problem I actually care about more — you can't score performance without a baseline, and the baseline is itself a political choice, and the people being scored get a hand in picking it. Third thing: the same outcome can come out of a brilliant decision or a lucky one, and the citizens should never be able to tell which. I want that last one built into the rules, not raised in discussion. If they can always see what would have happened otherwise, the whole thing degrades into a normal performance review exercise and I've wasted the hour.

One design idea I keep coming back to, feel free to overrule me: two or three polities that receive literally the same sequence of good years and bad years but judge and replace their rulers under different charters, so the class can watch how fast each one comes apart. Not attached to it.

Constraints: no money, no props beyond index cards, dice and scrap paper. I can't reliably get a second facilitator. 75 minutes including debrief. Every student has to be making decisions, nobody watching from the outside. Someone has to play the ruler and the ruler needs genuine discretion, otherwise there's nothing to legitimate.

What I need from you:

- the setup — roles, group sizes, what each charter actually says, what information is public vs hidden, and the shock sequence (or how it's generated)
- a scoring scheme that makes them care without real stakes: what they're accumulating and why they'd want more of it
- a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75
- the debrief: which moments carry which claim, and what order to surface them in. I want the "wait, we chose our own baseline" realization to come out of a student's mouth, not mine
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how I catch it — in particular what happens if the student playing the ruler is good at it and starts gaming the metric in a way that's clever but leaves everyone else bored
- an honest note on which part of the idea the activity can't reproduce and that I'll just have to say out loud

Thing I'm dreading: I've got a couple of students who grew up somewhere this debate isn't hypothetical, and I don't want the activity turning them into exhibits or quietly assigning them the explaining. Build it around invented polities so it stands on its own, and then tell me whether and when to bring real cases into the debrief.

Format: give me the debrief as a table with exactly these columns — moment_in_game | what_theyll_probably_say | the_claim_it_supports | the_question_to_ask | where_the_analogy_breaks. Prose for everything else.

And at the end, tell me how confident you are that this will actually generate the reactions you're predicting with this group rather than just reading well on paper, and single out the one prediction you're least sure of.
