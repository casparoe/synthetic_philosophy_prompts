I am applying for a six-month funded research leave at a small institute for practical ethics, and I need the proposal document written up. Below is the idea I want built into it.

The familiar complaint that consequentialists are clueless — that we cannot know enough about the far-reaching effects of our acts to apply the theory, since ordinary acts ramify into distant futures, change who comes to exist, and alter the causal background against which later choices are made — is usually answered in one of three ways: consequentialism is a criterion of rightness rather than a decision procedure; remote effects are diffuse enough to cancel out roughly evenly across our options; or we are indeed clueless, but acting on the best partial evidence available is no more objectionable here than anywhere else. My working hunch is that all three treat the extent of our ignorance as a fixed feature of the world, independent of what we do, and that this is what gives the objection its bite. If instead some acts are more clue-preserving than others — if better forecasting, better record-keeping, better measurement of welfare, and more stable institutions narrow the dispersion of futures we will later have to evaluate, while other acts widen it — then a consequentialist can rank options partly by how much they improve her own subsequent epistemic position regarding where value lies, and cluelessness becomes something the theory can work against rather than merely endure.

The proposal should do the following.

1. State the question precisely enough to have a determinate answer. That means specifying what "improving one's epistemic position" has to amount to here — something in the neighborhood of the expected value of the information an act yields about future value — and saying whether that notion can be made determinate without already presupposing the clueless calculations it is meant to displace.

2. Explain why the three standard replies leave this open, and why the question is not simply the criterion/decision-procedure distinction in new clothing. Treat that collapse as a live risk to be argued against, not assumed away.

3. Set out the method: what I would actually do across the six months, in stages, with a stated deliverable at the end of each stage.

4. Identify and confront the central reflexivity worry: applying the clue-preserving principle to itself may demand exactly the remote-effect estimates the principle was invented to make unnecessary. Say what a restriction narrow enough to block that regress would cost, and whether the cost is fatal.

5. Address whether the principle must be agent-relative (it improves my epistemic position) or agent-neutral (it improves the epistemic position of whoever is deciding), and what the answer implies for a theory committed to agent-neutral value claims.

6. Describe what a successful result looks like — what I would be able to claim at the end that I cannot claim now — and what would count as the project's failing.

Please refer to positions and arguments rather than to authors or works; I will supply the citations myself. Target roughly 1,200 words, in the register of a grant application rather than a journal article.
