You're going to play a professional mediator who's also a philosopher of urban politics — someone who has run a bunch of adverserial collaborations and has a real knack for wording that neither side can accuse of being slanted. Think carefully before you write; I'm happy for you to spend the first part of your reply brainstorming candidate cruxes out loud and crossing out the weak ones before you settle on the final document.

Situation: our city's planning commision is putting a big upzoning to a citywide vote — roughly, legalizing four-to-six stories on most residential parcels — and two camps that have been shouting at each other in public comment for three years have finally agreed to sit down and produce something jointly. Camp A is a committed supply-side zoning reformer (call them the YIMBY, though they hate the word): they think the shortage is substantially a product of local veto points, that blocking construction mostly transfers wealth to incumbent owners and long-time residents, that new market-rate units relieve pressure through vacancy chains and filtering, and that "community control of land use" has historically been an extremely efficient vehicle for exclusion. Camp B is a committed Lefebvrian defender of the right to the city, and I want that side played properly rather than as the NGO press-release version: for them the droit à la ville is *not* a claim-right on the liberal model that you cash out as a voucher or an inclusionary set-aside, but a collective power of inhabitants to participate in the production of urban space; they care about the difference between the inhabitant and the citizen-or-consumer; they think urban space is being remade as abstract space organized around exchange value rather than use value; and they suspect the commission's own request for "shared ground" is already framed in Camp A's conceptual scheme. They also think the mainstreaming of the phrase over the last twenty years has pretty thoroughly trivialized it. I'm a grad student who has been taking minutes at these meetings and got volunteered to draft the thing nobody else will.

Give me the document the two of them should have produced — one they could both actually sign. Specifically: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims, each one specific enough to be contentious, nothing vacuous like "both camps agree housing matters"; (2) a disagreements table with columns for the disagreement, Camp A's own wording of it, Camp B's own wording of it, and whether the dispute is substantive or largely verbal — and where it's verbal, say exactly which part and why; (3) the cruxes, meaning the sub-questions where settling them would move at least one side, and for each, which side would move and how far; (4) for each crux, the kind of test, case, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance and what result each would count as refuting their own view. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations at all — this goes to a lay commission and I don't want named studies. Describe *types* of evidence and *types* of argument instead.

The part I most want you to wrestle with, because it's what usually gets fudged in these things: Camp B thinks the whole exercise is rigged by the word "right," and that any joint statement phrased in the language of individual claim-holders and entitlements has already conceded the argument to Camp A. Handle that head-on — either show how the statement can be phrased so Camp B isn't swallowing that framing, or state in the document itself why they can't and what they're signing onto anyway, with the cost made explicit. Then close with a short section where each camp names the weakest point in its own position and one thing it's been refusing to say out loud (for Camp A, something about who actually captures the gains from upzoning in practice; for Camp B, something about what happens to the neighborhood if they win). Aim for maybe 1200–1800 words, plain language where you can manage it, and don't editorialize about who's right — the whole point is that both names go at the top.
