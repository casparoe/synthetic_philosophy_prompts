I am running a 75-minute seminar session for 22 second- and third-year philosophy students as part of a course on the philosophy of action. We have just finished two weeks on the standard analysis of intentional action and on deviant causal chains, and next week turns to the empirical challenge to that analysis: the side-effect asymmetry, on which ordinary people are much more willing to call a foreseen bad side effect intentional than a foreseen good one. My problem is that every student in the room can find the published vignettes in two minutes, and at least a third have probably already read about the effect, so a straight replication will land flat — they will report what they think I want, or what they know the answer is supposed to be. I want an activity in which the asymmetry, if it appears at all, appears in data the class produced itself, on materials they could not have looked up, and in which the students who already know the result are put to work rather than allowed to spoil it. I have no budget and the department's polling software is unreliable, so assume a whiteboard, paper slips, and phones used only as screens.

Design it as a runnable session. First, a fresh matched pair of vignettes — plus a third condition if you think it earns its place — written from scratch and set somewhere neutral: no boards of directors, no environmental permits, no profit motive, since those are precisely the settings of the cases everyone has seen. Tell me what the versions must be held equal on and what may legitimately differ (foresight, stated probability, the agent's skill, whether the outcome functions as a means or as a side effect, the agent's avowed attitude toward it), and show that yours satisfy those constraints. Then the response sheet: I want more than a bare yes/no, but nothing that takes over a minute to complete. Then the logistics for keeping the conditions sealed from one another in a room where everyone has a neighbour, and a tally procedure I can run on the board in under five minutes.

For the debrief, I want the students driven to the fork rather than handed it: is the asymmetry a performance error, a bias in how people apply a concept whose content is morally neutral; or is it a fact about the concept of intentional action itself, so that the competent speaker who calls the harm intentional and the help unintentional is not making a mistake at all? Have them propose the measurement that would discriminate between those, and build in a second round in which they can actually run one of their proposals. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — including what I do if the asymmetry simply fails to show up in 22 people — and be frank about which feature of the effect a single seminar section cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by hand. Keep the whole thing, vignettes included, under 1,000 words; bullets are fine, and each vignette should read in under ninety seconds.
