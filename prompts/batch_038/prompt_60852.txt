I do governance for a mid-sized open source foundation — about 900 people with commit rights spread across 40-some projects, all volunteers except roughly 15 paid staff — and every quarter we recieve ten or fifteen proposals that would change the rules everybody works under: new requirements on maintainers, new enforcement powers for moderators, changes to who votes on what. There's no procedure for any of it. Someone writes a motion in a shared doc, the three loudest people argue for a week, and it lands in front of our seven-person board. I got into Spinoza through a part-time philosophy course I'm taking, and my tutor said something like he was a democrat for reasons that have nothing to do with people being nice, which made me go back and reread him with my day job in mind. There's real machinery there: the identity of right and power (a sovereign's right reaches exactly as far as it can actually make things happen), the argument that you can compel what people do and say but not what they judge or believe, his reason for thinking a big assembly tracks reason better than a small council because individual passions cancel out, the picture of a state's power as just the power of the multitude acting as if it were one mind, his view that the point of the state isn't to make people docile but to let them use their reason without getting hurt, his suspicion of any small body that gets to decide what the rules mean, and the fact that he thinks fear and hope rather than agreement are what actually hold obedience together.

What I want is a decision procedure a rotating volunteer reviewer and I can run on a proposal in about 90 minutes, with no philosophy background needed on their end and no sending anyone off to read Spinoza. Numbered steps in order, what a sufficient answer looks like at each one, where it terminates in a no, where it terminates in a conditional yes (and what the conditions are), and where it stops and hands the thing up to the board because what's left is a question about values rather than facts — the board meets monthly and has to sign off, and the reviewer's output is a one page memo. Be explicit about which of the Spinozist distinctions above have to survive the simplification and which ones you're dropping, and flag the steps that are really judgment calls wearing a procedure costume. Two things I especially want you to be straight about. First, the abuse worry: if right is power, doesn't the procedure just rubber-stamp whatever we can enforce and kill whatever we can't? Show me how to keep that from happening, or admit that you can't. Second, there's a commitment of his I don't think we can inherit — in the democracy chapters he leaves women and anybody under someone else's authority out of the citizen body, and people still argue about whether he thinks that's nature or custom. Don't quietly skip past it; tell me what happens to the procedure if you actually pull that thread, since a huge share of our contributors are contractors and employees whose bosses could in principle tell them what to do.

Then run it on three live proposals. (A) Everyone with commit rights has to sign a statement affirming they accept the foundation's values before each release, and the release is blocked for anyone who hasn't signed; this came up after two maintainers publicly disowned the values doc while still shipping code under our name. (B) Move all cross-project decisions out of the 900-person assembly and into nine elected stewards on staggered five year terms, leaving the assembly one ratification vote a year — the argument being that the assembly is slow and every thread gets hijacked by the angriest three people. (C) Automatic, immediate loss of commit rights for anyone who posts criticism of foundation governance on public forums, no appeal, on the theory that it stops pile-ons before they cost us moderators. That one's mine, I wrote it after a pile-on burned out two of our best moderators, so I want the honest verdict even if the answer is no and even if it's embarrassing. If one of the three should come out as an escalation rather than a yes or a no, put it there and say what the board would actually be deciding when it takes it up. Finish with two or three paragraphs on what the procedure can't do: the failure modes it'll have, the kinds of proposals it'll get wrong, and where a reviewer following it faithfully could still land somewhere I'd consider a disaster. No bibliography or reading list — I can't check it and it isn't the point.
