For a paper I am writing on the dispute over whether knowledge-how is a species of knowledge-that or an ability that does not reduce to it, I have been recording one of our department's senior epistemologists' intuitive verdicts on a fixed battery of cases. Her rulings so far, together with the remarks she made when giving them:

1. A concert pianist, two years after a stroke that left her right hand paralyzed, can no longer play; she can still hear the concerto in her head, demonstrate the fingering in the air with her left hand, and catch her students' errors instantly. — She still knows how to play it. "Losing the use of a hand does not unmake a skill."
2. A retiree who has read a workshop manual cover to cover, can recite the sequence for rebuilding a transmission and draw the exploded diagram from memory, has never held a wrench, and would fail immediately if he tried. — He does not know how to rebuild a transmission. "He knows that those are the steps. That is a different thing."
3. A line cook of thirty years who cannot tell you why she does anything, offers no explanations at all, but works quickly, rescues a broken sauce without hesitation, adapts at once to an unfamiliar stove, and trains newcomers by demonstration. — She knows how. "Being unable to talk about it is beside the point."
4. A perception module trained end-to-end on millions of images that sorts correctly more often than any human on our team, was never given any steps, and cannot explain anything — assume for the sake of argument that it has no beliefs or desires. — It does not know how. "That is a reliable disposition, and there is nobody home to know anything."
5. A surgeon who performs a delicate operation flawlessly and adapts smoothly to anatomical variation, but whose stated rationale is pure superstition: she is convinced the outcome turns on the phase of the moon and the color of her scrubs, and says so cheerfully. — She knows how to perform it. "Bad theory, excellent hands."

Next week I intend to put a sixth case to her, and I want to settle on a prediction beforehand. The case is this: a surgeon at a teaching hospital has performed the same laparoscopic procedure some four hundred times, and an internal audit reveals that for the past three years the robotic system she operates through has been running an undisclosed autonomy layer — her hand inputs are logged and discarded, and the machine carries out each procedure from its own plan, moving the instruments in exact synchrony with her gestures so that nothing feels different to her from the inside. Her knowledge is otherwise untouched: she can still narrate and judge the procedure correctly from video, she performed it flawlessly for years before the layer was installed, and when the audit team switched the layer off on a simulator she completed the procedure without error. The question I will ask her is simply whether she knows how to perform the operation.

Answer in American English, as exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) the single rule her five recorded verdicts best fit, stated so that it yields each of them; (2) a rival rule that fits all five just as well, together with the respect in which the two rules differ; (3) your prediction of her verdict on the sixth case; (4) your strongest reason for preferring that prediction to the one the rival rule delivers; (5) a seventh case you would put to her that would decisively separate the two rules. You may attach familiar labels to the positions if you know them, but I want the analysis, not references or a reading list.
