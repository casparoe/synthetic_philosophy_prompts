I am in the second year of a philosophy of physics doctorate, and next month our foundations reading group is holding a session in which each participant must commit publicly, in writing, to a single sentence stating where they think a live debate actually stands. I drew the Everettian probability problem. My draft sentence says that deriving the Born rule from unitary dynamics alone remains an open problem and that the existing proposals are either circular or question-begging; my co-organiser's draft says that the problem is essentially solved, pending only the preferred-basis business, and that the remaining complaints are philosophical squeamishness. We have argued about this at length without either of us ever putting a number on our position, which I now suspect is why the argument has gone nowhere. Before I submit my sentence, I would like your credence, decomposed, on a proposition stated precisely enough that we can see whether we are even disagreeing about the same thing.

Let P be the following: there exists a derivation of the Born rule from Everettian quantum mechanics — unitary dynamics plus a decoherence-based account of branching, with no probability measure added as a primitive — that (a) does not presuppose the squared-amplitude weighting anywhere in its premises or in its decoherence machinery, (b) explains what a probability claim could even mean in a theory on which every outcome occurs, and (c) would be accepted as non-circular by a clear majority of specialists who work on the Everett interpretation. Please give me your credence in P, along with a rough probability for and one line of justification on each of the following components, and then show me how you combine them — I want to see whether you are multiplying conditionals, taking something like a disjunction over competing strategies, or doing something else entirely. The components I have in mind: the probability that decoherence yields a branch structure definite enough to bear weights at all, without already having the norm smuggled into the definition of a branch; the conditional probability, given that it does, that the decision-theoretic strategy succeeds, meaning that rationality genuinely compels a squared-amplitude weighting rather than merely permitting it, and that the standard circularity complaints and the branch-counting counterexamples have been answered rather than waved off; the conditional probability that the self-locating-uncertainty strategy succeeds instead, on which the agent's post-measurement pre-observation ignorance does the work; the conditional probability that envariance-based symmetry arguments get there; the conditional probability that a frequency-operator or typicality approach does; and the probability that none of these works but that adding the squared norm as a primitive postulate counts as a legitimate resolution rather than an admission of failure — tell me explicitly whether you think that last option should even be inside P or should be treated as a separate, more modest claim. Finally, one term I included deliberately as a check on you: the unconditional probability that the Everett interpretation, or some close no-collapse variant, is true. Say whether that belongs in the decomposition of P at all, and if not, what should take its place, since I have been including it and I am not sure I should be.

Two further things, and then I will leave you alone. First, a distinction of my own that I do not think is standard in the literature, and whose value I want assessed: the difference between saying that Everettian quantum mechanics supplies no measure over outcomes whatsoever and saying that it supplies a glut of candidate measures with nothing internal to the theory selecting among them. My working intuition is that these are not two ways of stating the incoherence problem but two genuinely different problems, and that the choice between them quietly determines which strategies look promising — on the glut reading, the decision-theoretic programme only has to show that one measure is uniquely rational, whereas on the absence reading it looks like a category mistake about a theory that never had probabilities to begin with. Assign a probability to each disjunct, say whether holding them apart raises or lowers your credence in P, and say which of the two readings my co-organiser and I are each implicitly working with, since I suspect this, rather than any technical disagreement, is where the gap between us lives. Second, report your number twice: once for the bare claim that the Born rule is rationally compulsory given unitary dynamics and decoherence, and once for the sociological claim that a majority of specialists would accept such a derivation as non-circular, and tell me which of those two you would be willing to bet on and why the two numbers come apart by as much as they do. Close by identifying the single term your final figure is most sensitive to, what kind of argument or result would move it most, and which term you are least confident in. I do not need references or a reading list — I know the literature — I need the arithmetic and the places where you are guessing.
