I have been an intensive-care nurse for nineteen years and I have no training in philosophy or political science, but I have spent the last several months reading about how nuclear weapons are actually authorized and fired, and something in what I found will not let go of me. As I understand the American arrangement, one person can order their use: the President, acting alone, with no second signature required and, as far as I can tell, no veto available to the Secretary of Defense. The only thing the system verifies at the top is identity — the President reads codes from a card he carries, a duty officer in the command center confirms that they match, and that establishes that the order really comes from him and nothing further. Below that point the safeguards become much denser. A missile crew must open a safe that needs two separate keys, compare the codes in the order against a sealed authenticator, and then turn their own keys simultaneously, with the slots positioned too far apart for one person to reach both; a second crew at another site must do the same. On a submarine, three officers must concur. Yet every one of those steps checks that the order is genuine, not that it is right. I also read about an Air Force officer who was discharged years ago for asking his superiors how he could possibly know that a launch order came from a sane President, and it struck me that the question he was punished for asking is precisely the one the whole machine is built not to ask.

What makes this feel to me like a philosophical problem rather than a policy quirk is that the same institutions insist on the opposite elsewhere. On my unit, an order from an attending physician is never executed on the strength of its origin alone: I am trained and expected to check it against the patient in front of me and against the limits of the drug, and to stop and question it when the dose, the route, or the timing does not fit, and the hospital's rules protect me when I do. The check is on the content, not the identity. The armed forces teach something similar — that a service member must refuse a manifestly unlawful order, and that having followed orders is no defense afterward. So I want to understand what view of authority the nuclear arrangement presupposes, and how it coexists with that teaching inside a single organization. Does it assume that legitimacy resides wholly in the office, so that whatever emerges from a properly authenticated channel is by construction lawful, and that the moral judgment of the act has been made once and for all by the constitutional design rather than afresh by each person who performs it? Does it assume that the men and women down the chain are not moral agents with respect to this one act, in a way no soldier is ever assumed not to be with respect to any other? Or is there some further theory — about minutes rather than days, about the impossibility of deliberating under attack, about democratic authorization — under which the pattern is coherent? Please lay out the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, name the rival views it quietly rules out, and tell me whether the practice is genuinely inconsistent with the rest of military ethics or whether the apparent inconsistency dissolves once the underlying assumptions are stated correctly.

There is a second thing I keep coming back to, which may or may not be connected. Every nuclear-armed government says, in effect, that it possesses these weapons only so that nobody uses them against it, and senior officials repeat that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. Those same governments keep thousands of warheads ready, maintain detailed targeting plans, drill and certify crews to execute them, and spend heavily to replace the delivery systems. The combination puzzles me in a way I lack the vocabulary for. If you sincerely intend never to do a thing, in what sense do you hold a plan and a trained disposition to do it? Is the intention conditional, and if so, what would make a conditional intention of that kind sincere rather than a bluff — and would it matter, for the purposes of deterrence, whether an adversary could tell the difference between a real willingness and a very well-rehearsed pretense? I would like you to reconstruct what account of intention and of threatening the practice presupposes, say whether it is the same account of agency that the authorization chain seems to presuppose or a different and possibly incompatible one, point out where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that quite different theories would license exactly the same arrangements, and describe what would concretely have to change — in law, in procedure, in the physical design of the chain — if we adopted either a view on which the content of the order is checked at the top or a posture that genuinely disclaims any intention to use the weapons at all. Please explain any technical terms you need along the way; I am not looking for references or a reading list, since I would have no way to chase them down, only for the reasoning set out clearly.
