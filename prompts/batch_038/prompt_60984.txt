A friend and I have been arguing for most of the evening about epiphenomenalism, and we have agreed that the next round will begin not with another argument but with a joke — each of us owing the other one, on the subject of the paradox of phenomenal judgment. I would prefer not to be the one who tells a joke that only makes sense to people who have never thought about the problem, so I am asking for your help.

Here is the disagreement, so that the joke can aim at the right thing. My friend holds that epiphenomenalism is self-undermining rather than merely implausible: if experience does no causal work, then my saying aloud that this red looks a certain way to me is produced entirely by neural processes that would have unfolded in exactly the same way had nothing looked any way at all; so the accuracy of my report is a coincidence the theory cannot explain, and the theory itself was not written under the influence of the phenomena it is about. I reply that a charge of that sort can be levelled at any account on which reports are generated by subpersonal machinery, and that the epiphenomenalist is entitled to swallow the coincidence. My friend finds the coincidence intolerable; I find it merely embarrassing. Neither of us budged.

Write the joke I should tell. Constraints:

- It must be short enough to deliver aloud in a single breath — eighty words at most — and must have a recognizable setup and punchline rather than the tone of a wry aphorism.
- The funny thing must be the causal structure: the report is true, but its truth is causally unearned. Someone who has never encountered the term phenomenal judgment should still find it funny; someone who has should be able to say exactly which step of the argument is being caricatured.
- It must not score a point for either side. Do not write a joke at the expense of the anti-epiphenomenalist, and do not write one at the expense of the epiphenomenalist; the target is the shape of the deadlock. Ideally the punchline is a sentence one of us could actually have said this evening without noticing that it conceded the point.
- Do not treat the vocabulary as the joke. Nothing should turn on the words qualia, zombie, epiphenomenal, or hard problem appearing in the punchline. If you use a lightbulb, a bar, or a chicken, it must be doing real work rather than supplying a familiar frame.
- An analogy is acceptable as scaffolding — a critic reviewing films he has never seen, a translator working from a language he cannot speak — but the analogy must preserve the direction of causation, since that is where the joke lives.

After the joke, add two or three sentences telling me which move in the argument it turns on and why that particular move is the funny one. I intend to send the whole thing to my friend, who maintains that the coincidence objection is nothing to laugh at.
