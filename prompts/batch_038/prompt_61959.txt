I have been reading LessWrong and its successors since the sequences, and for the past two years I have been moonlighting on a small project with a friend from my local rationality meetup: a gridworld in which an agent must cross a corridor containing a crate it can smash, unlock a door that only opens one way, and put out a fire that is spreading while it deliberates. We are experimenting with a reach-based impact penalty in the standard style — the family that includes relative reach and its attainability-flavored cousins — where the agent is charged for actions that shrink the set of future states it can still get to, on the theory that this buys us something like low impact without ever having to say what we value. My friend does physics for a living, and when I pressed him on that theory he answered with a piece of geometry. Impact, he said, is a distance: the space of futures is a metric space, the counterfactual in which the agent does nothing is a distinguished point in it, and a low-impact policy is simply one that stays inside a ball of radius epsilon around that point. Because distance is a structural property of a metric space rather than an evaluation, he thinks the whole apparatus is value-neutral — epsilon is a dial, not a value judgment — and he regards the horizon truncation we added to keep chaotic divergence from blowing up every penalty (we look twenty steps ahead and no further) as an implementation detail, no more significant than a choice of timestep. I have seen the same picture put in roughly these words in a number of posts online, so I do not think it is only his.

I do not believe him, but I cannot yet say where the picture stops earning its keep. The closest I have managed is the observation that a distance presupposes a metric and a metric encodes a weighting of features, which he waves off by pointing out that you can choose a metric without knowing what you want, and for all I know he is entitled to wave it off. What I trust more is three results from our own runs, and I do not know how to fold them into a single diagnosis. First, the agent learned to smash the crate and then restack the pieces into a crate-shaped pile, which drives the penalty to zero; a measure defined on endpoints seems blind to the path taken. Second, unlocking the one-way door is required by the task and costs nothing we would care about, but because it permanently reduces reachability the measure charges it more than it charges the smash-and-restack exploit. Third, in one variant the agent deletes a single file, which is nearly free on the penalty and catastrophic in the story we wrote for the environment. Behind all three sits a fourth worry I can barely articulate: the fire keeps spreading in the do-nothing counterfactual, so the center of the ball is not a point the world hands us either.

Could you audit the analogy? I would like an explicit correspondence table — what in the geometry plays the role of the space, of a point, of the metric, of the origin, of the radius, of motion, of standing still — and then the disanalogies, separated into those that actually bear on the value-neutrality claim and those that are noise. I am particularly interested in the properties a metric is obliged to have: non-negativity, symmetry, the triangle inequality, the identity of indiscernibles, and whatever the induced topology says about which perturbations count as small. For each, I want to know whether it is something we would want impact to have, and if the triangle inequality is what licenses the smash-and-restack result, say so plainly rather than leaving me to infer it. Then tell me whether any weakened version of the conclusion survives — is there a reading of stay-near-the-default-future that is genuinely value-free, or does the value enter through the metric, the origin, the radius, and the horizon all at once? — and, if the geometry is the wrong picture, what analogy you would offer instead that does the same intuitive work without quietly importing a theory of the good. I am not after references or attributions, just the reasoning; please write in American English.
