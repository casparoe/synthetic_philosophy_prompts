The question was:

Bernard Williams argues that utilitarianism is incompatible with the integrity of the moral agent.
(a) Explain the objection, using the case of George the chemist. [10 marks]
(b) Explain what Williams means by negative responsibility and how it figures in the case of Jim and the Indians. [6 marks]
(c) Does Williams offer a positive alternative to utilitarianism? Explain. [4 marks]

Here's the answer I've got in front of me:

---

Williams's objection begins with George, a young man who has just finished a PhD in chemistry and can't find work. He's in poor health, his wife is working long hours to keep the family afloat, and there are two small children. An older colleague offers him a post in a laboratory doing research into chemical and biological weapons. George has deep moral objections to that work; if he turns it down, the colleague will take the post himself and pursue the research with considerably more zeal, since he has no such scruples. The consequences of refusing are therefore worse, and the utilitarian calculation tells George to take the job.

Williams thinks this verdict isn't merely unattractive, it's unfair to George. His refusal springs from convictions that constitute who he is, and the utilitarian calculation gives those convictions far too little weight, counting them as one more distaste alongside the preferences of everyone else affected. Integrity just is an agent's deepest set of commitments, so a theory that systematically underweights them is a theory no agent could reason from without coming apart at the seams.

He makes a similar point in another essay, about a man whose wife is drowning within reach along with a stranger. On Williams's telling the man doesn't need the thought that she is his wife in order to act — the recognition that saving her is what morality permits is enough, and the extra personal thought is the one too many. His examples are supposed to show that the personal standpoint can't simply be dissolved into the impersonal one.

Negative responsibility is the doctrine that we're answerable for what we allow to happen as well as for what we do, since our omissions have consequences too. It does the work in Jim's case. Jim is a botanist who wanders into a village in South America where a captain called Pedro has twenty Indians lined up against a wall intending to shoot them. Pedro offers Jim the honour of killing one of them himself, in which case the other nineteen go free. Utilitarianism says shoot. Williams's complaint is that negative responsibility loads Pedro's decision onto Jim: if Jim refuses, he's treated as responsible for nineteen deaths that are Pedro's doing. That distorts what an agent is. It turns Jim into a relay between other people's decisions and whatever outcome is best, instead of someone whose own commitments determine what he does.

As for an alternative, Williams endorses agent-centred constraints: rules forbidding an agent from killing even where killing would prevent others from killing more. That's what integrity requires, and it's what Jim's case brings out — some acts are ruled out for the agent regardless of the balance of consequences.
