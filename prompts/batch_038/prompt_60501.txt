Question 3 (25 points): State the poverty of the stimulus argument as precisely as you can. Then explain what an empiricist about language acquisition would have to establish in order to block it. Finally: does the absence of negative evidence play an essential role in the argument, or could the argument be made out of the positive evidence alone?

Student's answer:

"The poverty of the stimulus argument says the linguistic input children get is too poor to account for the grammar they end up with. There are two kinds of poverty. First, the input is degenerate — false starts, fragments, slips — and it is only a finite sample of the sentences of the language, so there is always more to the grammar than there is to the data. Second, and more important, children get essentially no negative evidence. Nobody tells them which strings are ungrammatical, and when parents do correct their kids they correct them for being truthful or clear, not for producing bad form.

Chomsky's standard example is structure dependence. From 'the man who is tall is in the room' a child could form the yes/no question either by fronting the first 'is' it encounters or by fronting the 'is' that belongs to the main clause. The linear rule would give something like 'is the man who tall is in the room,' which nobody says. Children never make this error, even though they hear very few questions with a relative clause inside the subject, and nobody ever corrects them for it. So they must already know that grammatical rules are structure-dependent. And since structure-dependence holds in every language and every child arrives at it without being taught, it has to be innate rather than learned.

The argument then goes: without negative evidence, a child who lands on the wrong rule has no way of finding out it's wrong, because the sentence that would expose the error basically never shows up in the input. The wrong grammar is compatible with everything the child hears. Yet children converge on the right one anyway, quickly and uniformly. So the knowledge that rules out the wrong grammars is not coming from the data — it is innate. This is basically what Quine meant with the gavagai example: since the evidence underdetermines the interpretation, the mind has to contribute something of its own, and Quine used this to argue that we are born with innate ideas about language.

For an empiricist to block it they would have to establish one of two things. (1) That the input is richer than Chomsky claims. Computational studies of transcripts of child-directed speech have found that the relevant sentence types are not nearly as rare as people assume, and that parents do in fact respond differently to ungrammatical utterances more often than the literature suggests, so negative evidence isn't wholly absent, just indirect. (2) That children learn using general-purpose statistical machinery, since networks pick up structure from distributional regularities without ever being told that something is wrong. If either holds, the argument doesn't get off the ground.

On the last part: yes, the absence of negative evidence is essential. Strip it out and all you have shown is that the input is limited, which is true of any learning problem there is. What does the work is specifically that the missing information is negative — the child can't be told 'that one is impossible.' But this also makes the argument stronger than Chomsky needs it to be. If no grammar at all can be recovered from positive data, then essentially all of grammar is innate, not just the abstract principles, which is why children everywhere acquire language on roughly the same timetable regardless of how much they are talked to."
