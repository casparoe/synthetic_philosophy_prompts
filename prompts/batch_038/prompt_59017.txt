I spent thirty-five years in comparative neurophysiology, most of it recording from the dorsal horn and from transected mammalian preparations — animals whose neuraxis had been cut above the spinal cord and which nonetheless stepped rhythmically on a moving belt and withdrew briskly from a noxious pinch. In retirement I have taken up philosophy seriously for the first time, and Descartes has arrested me, because my own working method was unmistakably Cartesian while my instincts never were: our department treated the question of whether a preparation suffered as a matter settled by anaesthesia protocol and institutional review rather than by argument, which is a way of arranging not to have to think about it. I should add that in the early part of my career it was still common to operate on neonates under paralysis with minimal analgesia, on the reasoning that they would not remember, and that this practice was abandoned only after measurements forced the issue; I mention it because it seems to me to be exactly the kind of episode Descartes' automaton doctrine should be asked to explain. I am now preparing a handout for a reading group of retired scientists — intelligent people with no philosophical training, several of whom will say they are Cartesians without knowing it — and what I want is a decision tree, not an essay and not a history of who said what.

Please build it from the logical space rather than from a list of names: the questions and the combinations of answers they permit first, and only then the positions located among those combinations. Two structural rules. First, the tree must be third-person only: every branch node must be answerable by someone handed nothing but a dossier containing a complete behavioural record and a complete neurophysiological record of the animal in question, with no access to anybody's introspection — including the introspection of the human investigators whose concepts generated the positions in the first place. Nodes should therefore be anchored in concrete cases rather than abstractions, and I would like you to draw on at least these: withdrawal from a pinch in a transected preparation contrasted with the same withdrawal plus days of guarding and refusal to re-enter the room where it happened; conditioned place aversion established by a noxious stimulus that the animal is later shown never to have represented; the scrub-jay that re-caches after having been watched; the octopus taught to unscrew a jar, which later opens jars it has not been rewarded for opening; and the heel prick in a seven-month-old infant. No node may contain the vocabulary of any leaf — no appeal to qualia, to consciousness, to intentionality, or to what it is like to be something — because the point is to sort my readers by what they believe about the cases, not by which label they already favour. Second, run the finished tree four times, on a healthy adult dog, on a common octopus, on a seven-month-old human infant, and on the transected preparation described above, and give me a table of the four verdicts for every leaf.

Aim for seven to nine nodes and ten to twelve leaves. Among the leaves I want to find, at minimum: the strict automaton position on which there is neither sensation nor thought anywhere outside human beings; the softer reading of Descartes on which beasts feel but have no beliefs or desires about the world, so that sensation and cognition come apart; any view that makes animal experience real but derivative, as in the occasionalist tradition; the higher-order-representation view, which denies consciousness to animals precisely because they cannot represent their own states; views that grant it on grounds of neural integration or architecture; the dispositionalist or interpretation-based view on which having pains and beliefs is a matter of there being a real pattern in the behaviour; straightforward ethological cognitivism, on which the animal's distress is what it appears to be and the burden of proof lies the other way; and illusionism about phenomenal states altogether. If any of these turns out to be a leaf nobody actually occupies in the animal case, say so, leave it unnamed, and diagnose why the cell is empty — incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored. For each leaf give one sentence stating the commitment its defenders find most awkward. Then, and this is the part I most want you not to smooth over, list every pair of leaves my third-person rule cannot in fact separate: I suspect that a view which grants phenomenal states and a view which eliminates them will look identical in a dossier from which introspection has been excluded, and if so the tree owes me an honest statement that it is blind there rather than a fudge at the branch that pretends otherwise. Close with a short section on what the instrument costs — which real distinctions it has to trade away in order to be answerable from a dossier alone. Please do not append references, a bibliography, or a suggested reading list; I want the tree, the table, and your candid remarks about its limits.
