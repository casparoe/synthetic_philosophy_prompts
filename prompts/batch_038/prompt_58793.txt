I spent thirty-odd years in paleoclimate work — ice cores mostly, plus a few seasons correlating tephra layers — and since retiring I've been trying to teach myself some philosophy, badly and out of order. Our book club just finished Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel and the discussion afterward got heated about the Black Death in England. One guy insisted that the plague bacterium plus a cooling climate basically ended serfdom on their own, and when I objected that the peasants still had to bargain, revolt, and get away with it for generations, he waved me off with "geography is destiny," as if that settled the matter. The annoying part is I couldn't say what exactly I was objecting to, because I agree with alot of the spirit of it — I've spent my career arguing that climate did things to people.

So here's what I want. Take that slogan apart and give me a menu of the distinct claims it could be making, ordered from the weakest and most-obviously-true to the strongest, and for each one tell me: what it precisely commits you to; whether it's a platitude, a live empirical hypothesis, or just false; whether it's even the kind of thing my background could bring evidence to bear on; and whether it's strong enough to do the job it was being used for in that room, namely shutting down my point about peasant agency. Also tell me which reading people who say it out loud most often mean, since I suspect it isn't the same one every time and that the ambiguity is doing real work for them. I've privately been sorting non-human causes into three buckets out of habit from modeling: boundary conditions that set the range of what's possible (latitude, soil, navigable rivers), perturbations that shove the system (an eruption, a pathogen jumping host), and slow variables that co-evolve with the society they're in (soil exhaustion, deforestation, drifting temperature). Please seperate out whether that three-way split lines up with any of the readings of the slogan, or whether I've built a distinction without a difference.

Two things I especially want addressed, because that's where I actually got stuck. First, writers in this area keep calling volcanoes, climates, and diseases "actors" or "historical agents" rather than merely causes — does that word buy anything at all, and does "geography is destiny" quietly presuppose the agent version instead of the boring cause version? Second, is there a determinism claim hiding in there? My instinct as a lab person is that "destiny" ought to mean something about the outcome being fixed no matter what anyone does, but I can't tell whether anyone who says the slogan means that or is just gesturing at "constraints matter." If there's a version where the constraints bite hard and yet nothing was destined, I want to know what makes it different from the fateful one, and how you'd ever tell them apart in the historical record rather than in adjectives. Write the whole thing in American English, and skip the bibliography — I'm after the distinctions, not a list of who to read.
