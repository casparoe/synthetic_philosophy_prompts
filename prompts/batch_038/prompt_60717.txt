I am drafting a seminar paper on the debate between moral particularism and moral generalism, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency audit of the position I have actually settled on. I will state the commitments in my own words, in roughly the order in which I hold them. None of them strikes me as obviously false, and I would rather be shown where the structure fails than reassured that it is coherent.

1. Reasons are holistic. Whether a feature of an act counts in its favour is not settled by the feature itself: that an act would give someone pleasure is a reason for it in one case, no reason at all in another, and a reason against it in a third. Nothing guarantees that what is a reason here will be a reason anywhere else.

2. That said, I do not think everything is up for grabs in this way. That an act would inflict severe and pointless suffering counts against it in every case in which it occurs, and I would not be moved by a purported counterexample, however carefully constructed.

3. I do not look for moral principles. My reason is not that the exceptions are too numerous to list, but that any finite statement of a principle must be hedged with provisos whose number and content cannot be foreseen in advance, so the resulting statement is not anything I could deliberate with in a particular case.

4. Moral knowledge is closer to perception than to inference. One comes to see that this act, in these circumstances, would be cruel, and the seeing does not depend on antecedently holding a general claim.

5. Competent agents can nonetheless say what speaks for what they did. An agent who could offer nothing at all — no feature of the situation she takes to count in her favour — has not acted for a reason, however well the act turned out.

6. Acts are wrong in virtue of features they have, and this relation is stable: if a feature makes an act wrong in a case where it is present, it makes an act wrong in any case in which that feature is present in the same way. Wrong-making features carry their force across contexts.

7. There has been real moral progress, and I want to describe it accurately. When a society came to see that a long-tolerated practice — chattel slavery, marital rape — was wrong, the change is best described as the correction of a principle that society had been operating with, rather than as the mere acquisition of a new perception.

8. Moral testimony is defective in a way that ordinary testimony is not. One should not believe that an act is wrong simply because a more reliable judge says so, even with good grounds for thinking them more reliable; belief has to track the features of the case.

What I want from you is an audit, not a defence or a lecture. Identify which pairs of these genuinely conflict and which only appear to, and be explicit about the difference: I suspect that at least two of the pairs you might flag are of very different kinds, and I want to know which is which. For each genuine conflict, say what would have to be given up and what the cheapest revision is that preserves the rest of the set; indicate in each case whether the conflict is fatal to particularism as such or only to my formulation of it. Then rank the conflicts by severity and tell me which single revision would resolve the most. If you think a commitment is not in tension with anything but simply false, say so and argue for that directly. Please do not give me a bibliography or a list of further reading — I want the reasoning, not the sources.
