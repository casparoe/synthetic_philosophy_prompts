I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and there's a piece in the works about software agents that commit on people's behalf — booking things, agreeing to terms, telling your doctor you'll take the pills — and underneath it keeps sitting the older question: what makes an undertaking bind at all once you take the law away, and where does the binding actually come from. My editor only wants one clean illustration in the finished piece, but for my own notes I want the four-cell version so I can see which account bites where. So: build me a 2x2. Rows are whether there's an undertaking — one row has the person actually saying "I promise" or whatever you think counts, the other has no undertaking of any kind. Columns are whether the other person relies and eats a real cost, or doesn't rely and eats none. Four vignettes, three or four sentences each, same two people, same task, same amount at stake, and matched on everything else: same knowledge, same window to back out before the other person commits, same distance in the relationship. Constraints — ordinary life, no contracts or courts anywhere in it, no lying or deception in the no-undertaking row (the expectation there has to come from something honest, a confident statement of intention, a track record, a message the person never retracted; if you let a fib in you've built a fraud case and the interesting question evaporates), and nothing should lean on friendship or family or a professional role, because then the obligation just gets borrowed from there.

Then the demonstration, cell by cell. For each of the four, tell me whether there's an obligation to do the thing, how strong it is, and why, according to: a reliance/assurance account where the obligation is generated by the expectation you caused and the cost it lands on; an Atiyah-style view on which the undertaking itself does no independent work and everything comes from reliance or benefit conferred; a normative-power view in Raz's spirit (and I know Raz thinks the power is only worth having where reliance matters, so tell me whether that costs him the no-reliance cell); a practice or convention view that needs a shared institution for the words to do anything; and a rights-transfer view where the promisee ends up holding something like a claim against the promisor. Mark which cell each theory treats as decisive, and name the one theory — if there is one — that actually flips its verdict when you toggle the column instead of just shading the strength.

Two honesty checks, please. First, can all four cells even be built? My suspicion is the no-undertaking row smuggles in an implicit assurance the second you make the expectation as firm and as reasonable as the promise row's, and if that's unavoidable I'd rather you say so and tell me what it does to the design than paper over it. Second, give me the counterattack: how does the best defender of the reliance view explain away the cell where the words were spoken and nobody was harmed at all, and what in my vignette will they point at that's still doing reliance-shaped work — a benefit conferred, a self-imposed reason, a norm they were already inside. And one rule about cheating: don't stipulate verdicts. If a cell binds harder it has to fall out of the theory plus the facts of the vignette, not out of a sentence that says "here the obligation feels weightier." Also, sidebar question, couple of sentences is plenty: in the cell where the person relies but there was never an undertaking, does the let-down party have standing to demand an explanation, or only permission to feel sore about it? I want to know which of your accounts can even ask that.
