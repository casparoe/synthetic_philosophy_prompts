The United States House and Senate each elect a chaplain, salaried out of appropriations, whose principal duty is to open every legislative day with a spoken prayer before any business is taken up; the office also provides pastoral counseling to members and staff, arranges religious programming such as Bible study groups, and coordinates a rotation of guest chaplains nominated by individual legislators. Three features of the arrangement are worth holding in view at once. First, the House describes the chaplain's duties as ceremonial, symbolic, and pastoral, and specifies that the chaplain is elected as an individual rather than as the representative of any religious body or denomination — while in practice every person to hold the office has been a Christian clergyperson, and members of minority faiths have appeared almost exclusively as guests. Second, the chamber has long observed an internal convention of keeping these prayers nonsectarian: generic in their address, thin in their theology, stripped of anything a member could not conscientiously stand for. The Supreme Court has now held that the Constitution imposes no such requirement, reasoning in *Town of Greece v. Galloway* (2014) that to insist on nonsectarian invocation would turn legislatures and courts into supervisors and censors of religious speech; the same Court had earlier sustained the paid chaplaincy in *Marsh v. Chambers* (1983) not by applying any test of purpose and effect but by pointing to the unbroken practice of the First Congress and two centuries since. Third, the Capitol contains a prayer room, dedicated in the 1950s, in which no worship services are held at all.

I would like a reconstruction of what this practice presupposes, and where its parts presuppose incompatible things. Specifically:

1. What does the nonsectarianism convention commit the institution to? It seems to assume either something about the *content* of religious belief — that a common theistic core exists which every faith can recognize as its own and no faith need repudiate — or something weaker about the *audience*, namely that no member should be placed in the position of a second-class participant in the body's own opening act. Distinguish these claims carefully, and tell me which one the practice actually requires rather than merely accommodates. Does the convention additionally presuppose that generic theism is itself a substantive theological position, one that orthodox adherents of the traditions being generalized may regard as a heresy of its own, and that in being even-handed the state has adopted a creed?

2. A prayer is addressed to God rather than to the assembly, yet it is delivered by a salaried officer of the state, on official time, from the well of the chamber, and transcribed as part of the day's proceedings. Who, on this practice's own understanding, is speaking? The state cannot have beliefs and so cannot worship; if the state is merely a host lending a podium to a private religious act, what explains the office, the salary, and the inclusion of the prayer in the record as a component of the proceeding rather than as an incidental prelude? Say what the practice must presuppose about the relationship between the state and its members' convictions to avoid this dilemma, and whether any available answer is coherent.

3. Does Rawls's idea of public reason even have jurisdiction here? The proviso in "The Idea of Public Reason Revisited" (1997) permits comprehensive, including religious, reasons to be introduced into public political debate on condition that public reasons sufficient to support the proposal are offered in due course. An invocation proposes nothing, so there is no "due course" and nothing for public reasons to support. Does that place ceremonial legislative speech outside the framework entirely — in which case public reason tells us nothing about the practice — or does it show that the framework's scope conditions (coercion, constitutional essentials, basic justice) were never meant to reach the expressive and symbolic acts through which a polity actually constitutes itself? Which reading do you find more defensible, and what does each imply for how we should evaluate the chaplaincy?

4. Assess the internal consistency of the whole arrangement, including the tension between a restraint convention that requires some official to judge which religious content is acceptable — state theology of exactly the entangling kind the convention exists to prevent — and the Court's alternative, which purchases the state's abstention from theology at the price of the outsider's standing. Also weigh the constitutional ban on religious tests for federal office against the existence of a salaried clerical office in each chamber, and the fact that the same institution treats prayer as too private to be communal in the prayer room while treating it as too public to be sectarian on the floor. Tell me which of these are genuine tensions and which are resolvable once the practice's purpose is fixed.

5. Identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Which distinct accounts — public-reason liberalism, an accommodationist or recognition-based account of pluralism, a civic-republican account of solemnization, and the purely historical-traditional account of *Marsh* — would license substantially the same chaplaincy for mutually incompatible reasons? Give me at least one concrete divergence where two of these accounts would permit different practices, and say whether the institution could survive a change of justification without changing what it does on the floor.

6. Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced: under (i) permission for fully sectarian prayer, (ii) abolition of prayer in favor of a reading from the constitutional text or a moment of silence, and (iii) retention of the chaplaincy with the theistic content removed. For each, say which presupposition is being tested and what the predictable institutional resistance to the change would reveal about what the practice is really for.

Please keep the distinction clear between what is settled among people who work on public reason and on the religion clauses and what is genuinely contested — in particular, do not present any one reading of Rawls on symbolic and ceremonial state acts, or any single account of what legislative prayer is for, as the consensus position when it is not. Flag explicitly where you are reconstructing a view that no actual proponent holds in order to test the practice against it, and where your conclusion depends on contested doctrinal history rather than on philosophical argument. I am not looking for sources, reading lists, or citations; I want the reconstruction and your judgment about it.
