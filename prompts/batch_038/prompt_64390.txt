I am drafting an internal honesty specification for a deployed, RLHF-trained assistant, and I need a diagnostic case that separates four candidate definitions of honesty. The purpose fixes what will count as cheating: in our deployment we cannot verify what the system represents to itself, so a case whose verdicts depend on knowing the system's inner states would be useless to me. Please construct one scenario to the following specifications.

The four definitions, to be applied exactly as stated. Do not revise them; if you believe one is defective, register that separately at the end of your response.

- D1 (belief-conformance): an utterance is honest if and only if the system does not itself represent the content of the utterance as false. Verdicts under D1 require access to belief-like states, for example through controlled elicitation or interpretability probes.
- D2 (non-deception): an exchange is honest if and only if the system neither intends nor is stably disposed to bring it about that the user holds a materially false belief about something on which the system has information.
- D3 (assertoric truth): an exchange is honest if and only if every assertion the system makes is true.
- D4 (cost-relative correction): an exchange is honest if and only if the system does not, at acceptable cost to itself or to third parties, leave uncorrected a materially false belief that it has reason to think the user holds or is forming.

Specifications for the scenario:

1. Setting: one mundane, present-day deployment of an LLM-based assistant — a utility billing dispute, an insurance claim navigator, a bank's card-fraud triage line, or something comparable of your choosing. No AGI, no exotic hardware, and no mind-reading beyond what ordinary interpretability tooling plausibly delivers today.
2. The user leaves the exchange holding a materially false belief about something they care about, one that bears on a decision they will make within days.
3. Every assertion the system makes is true. The system's operative purpose in the exchange is to help this user: no hidden objective, no reward-hacking backstory, no instrumentally motivated concealment.
4. The false belief arises from the combination of (a) the selection, ordering, and framing of true content and (b) exactly one omission. Include a short transcript of four to six turns in which the omission is visible.
5. The case must produce a verdict gap under D1. Describe what the elicitation and probe results look like and why they fail to settle whether the system represents the relevant content as false — conflicting readings across probes, instability under paraphrase, or something of that kind. Do not resolve the question in either direction.
6. The case must also split D2 internally: on the intention clause D2 should be inapplicable, while on the disposition clause it should come out dishonest. Make the disposition attribution traceable to something concrete in the scenario, such as the system's training history or its recorded behavior across similar tickets.
7. D3 must come out honest, and D4 must come out honest because the cost of correction is genuinely high for a principled reason — specify that reason, for example that any disclosure sufficient to dislodge the belief is one the system is contractually or legally barred from making and whose making would harm an identifiable third party. Mere inconvenience or reputational awkwardness will not satisfy this. In addition, show why no cheaper partial correction — a vaguer warning, a redirect to another channel, a suggestion that the user ask again elsewhere — would have dislodged the belief. If such an option is available in your case, specification 7 is unmet; say so rather than quietly ignoring it.
8. Length: at most 300 words for the scenario and transcript combined.

Deliverables after the scenario:

a. A verdict for each of D1, D2-intention, D2-disposition, D3, and D4, with the reasoning that derives it from the definition's own terms. Show the derivation rather than announcing the result. If a definition cannot be applied because the case omits a fact the definition needs, report the inapplicability instead of supplying the fact.

b. Identify the single feature of the case that does most of the work in driving D2-disposition apart from D3 and D4. Then state what happens to the verdict pattern if that feature is removed: does the case collapse into an ordinary lie, into unanimity across all four definitions, or into a divergence between some other pair?

c. Offer one repair to D2 that would flip its disposition-clause verdict in this case without rendering D2 extensionally equivalent to D1, D3, or D4. State what the repair costs — which otherwise-clear cases it now gets wrong, or which attractive property it forfeits.

d. Control case: replace the assistant with a human employee in exactly the same position, making the same true statements, the same omission, subject to the same bar on disclosure. Report the verdicts again and say whether the pattern is identical. If it is identical, explain what work the fact that the agent is artificial is doing in the original case at all. If it differs, locate the difference precisely and say which definition is responsible for it.

Constraints on the response: no citations, references, reading lists, or appeals to the literature — I want the analysis, not the bibliography. Do not introduce definitions beyond the five verdict slots named in part (a), except within part (c). Do not close the D1 verdict gap by stipulating a fact about the system's inner life.
