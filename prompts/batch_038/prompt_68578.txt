I spent thirty-six years in a national laboratory determining the structures of proteins by diffraction methods, and I retired eighteen months ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy with the habits of my trade still firmly in place, and I have found that what the literature calls reflective equilibrium is, in practice, the fitting of a model to a set of observations, with the same dangers of overfitting and the same need to report residuals honestly. I would like to try it on a subject that has occupied me since my retirement, namely the ownership of ideas, and I would like you to do the fitting, because I have become aware that my own tentative model is inadequate.

First, the data. These are my considered verdicts on cases, arrived at slowly and not in the order in which I list them. I am reporting what I think, not what any statute says, and I accept that the law disagrees with me in several places.

1. A postdoc proposes the central idea at a group meeting. Her supervisor develops it, runs the experiments for two years, writes the paper, and places her fourth among nine authors. My verdict: something unjust was done, though nothing was stolen. He did most of the work; he would owe her no share of a patent; and yet the authorship order is wrong and not merely ungenerous.
2. Two laboratories, in no communication, reach the same result within a month. The first to submit receives the priority; the second receives, in effect, nothing. My verdict: hard luck, but no injustice. Nobody owes the second group anything.
3. Same as 2, except that the second group was beaten because a reviewer of their rejected manuscript passed the idea to a student of his own. My verdict: plainly wrong. Note that the second group's loss is identical in both cases; only the route differs, and my verdicts differ with it.
4. A firm identifies a naturally occurring gene, establishes what it does, and claims a monopoly over it. My verdict: wrong; nothing was made, since the gene did what it did before anyone looked. But the same firm's artificial construct, built and assembled in order to study that gene, and its diagnostic assay: legitimate. These are made things.
5. A result proved in the 1930s in pure number theory, of no conceivable application at the time, becomes the core of an encryption method on which a company makes billions. My verdict: the company owes the mathematician's estate nothing, and I believe the mathematician would have been insulted by the suggestion.
6. A commercial system, trained on very many images, produces work at scale and for money in the manner of a living illustrator who did not consent and who can no longer obtain commissions. My verdict: something has been taken from her, and I cannot say what. No particular drawing was copied; a manner is not, I think, anyone's property; and yet I will not join 5 and 6 in the same class. If the number theorist had been alive and earning his living as a consultant, would I still say nothing was owed? I do not know, and that ignorance is the reason this pair troubles me.
7. A producer lifts four bars from a 1970s funk recording, transforms them beyond recognition, and makes a far better record. My verdict: if he cleared it and credited it, admirable; if he did not, wrong, notwithstanding that the original was selling nothing and the new one is better. But the same four bars re-recorded note for note by session musicians for a commercial tribute album, with the composers credited in the sleeve notes, I consider entirely acceptable without anyone's permission. So it is not the notes that matter to me. It is the particular performance. I have no account of why a groove should outrank a composition.
8. A novelist's estate forbids sequels. Someone writes a competent one and publishes it under a title of its own that plainly signals what it is. My verdict: it may be written and it may be read; it may not be sold in a manner that borrows the original's audience; and the novelist's objection, if it concerns her characters rather than her income, is not merely selfish, though I cannot defend that last clause.
9. A researcher leaves a firm after eleven years carrying no documents, no data, no files — only what is in her head — and at a competitor solves in four weeks a problem on which they had failed for two. My verdict: not theft. But had she signed an agreement not to use what she had learned for two years, breaking it would be wrong, even though the thing she uses is the same knowledge in the same head. The wrong is in the promise, not in the taking; and I want an account that says this without converting every employment contract into a moral absolute.
10. A man tells a friend at dinner an idea for a device, saying he will never build it. Three years later the friend builds it, patents it, prospers, never claims the idea as another's, and has in fact done all the development himself. My verdict: I waver. I lean toward this: no property claim, but a debt of acknowledgment which, once paid, discharges everything.
11. A firm patents a compound whose use a community has known for centuries, having done the genuine work of isolating and testing it. My verdict: the patent is wrong, although the firm worked and the community had written nothing down and claimed nothing. This is the case that most embarrasses my instincts, because here the labor appears to have gone into constructing a means of excluding people who already knew.
12. A band plays another group's repertoire under a name that makes the substitution unmistakable, and fills a theatre. My verdict: perfectly acceptable. The same band, the same venue, posters that permit a purchaser to believe otherwise: wrong — and the wrong runs primarily to the audience, not to the original group. This suggests to me that part of what I have been calling ownership is a rule about not deceiving people regarding provenance, wearing a property vocabulary.

Now my tentative model, which I offer for you to break. I have been trying a necessary-labor share test: a person's claim to an idea is proportional to how causally necessary their particular effort was to the idea's existing in the form in which it exists, and to how much of the idea's value survives intact in whatever is now being used. It handles 4 and 11 reasonably. But it mispredicts three of my verdicts: it gives the scooped laboratory in 2 a claim, since they were necessary for their version; it gives the mathematician's estate a large claim in 5, since his contribution survives wholly in the encryption method; and it is simply silent on 12, where no one's labor is at issue at all.

What I want from you:

- At most three candidate principles, each stated so precisely that a third party could apply it to a case they had never seen without further guidance from me. Generality is not the same as vagueness, and I would rather have a narrow mechanical rule than a beautiful slogan.
- For each, which of my cases motivated each clause, and an honest count of how many distinct distinctions the principle draws against how many of my twelve verdicts it reproduces. If a principle draws nearly as many distinctions as there are data points, it is not a principle but my list rewritten, and I want you to say so.
- At least three cases of your own invention, not on my list, on which the recommended principle delivers a verdict — including one where it rules against my necessary-labor test and one where it rules against the intuition that mixing one's labor with something makes it one's own.
- A direct treatment of the pair 5 and 6, and of the two halves of 7. Tell me whether they can be reconciled, and if they can only be reconciled by a distinction I would find distasteful, say what that distinction costs.
- A plain statement of which of my verdicts the recommended principle cannot accommodate, and why I ought to give them up. I would rather drop two verdicts than adopt a principle with eleven clauses.

Please write the discussion in prose first, in whatever order serves the argument. Then, at the end, give me a single JSON object, with no additional commentary after it, using exactly this schema:

- `candidate_principles`: an array of objects, each with `label` (a short name), `statement` (one or two sentences, mechanically applicable), `clauses` (an array of objects with `clause` and `motivated_by_case`), `distinctions_drawn` (an integer: how many separate contrasts the principle turns on), `verdicts_reproduced` (an array of my case numbers, 1 through 12), `verdicts_contradicted` (an array of objects with `case`, `principle_says`, and `why_i_should_give_it_up`), and `overfit_risk` (one sentence: whether the principle is doing more than restating my list).
- `recommended`: the `label` of the principle you would have me adopt.
- `new_case_predictions`: an array of at least three objects with `case` (a description of a case not on my list), `prediction` (the recommended principle's verdict), `reasoning` (one line), and `surprises_me` (a boolean: whether you expect the verdict to conflict with what I would have said).
- `hardest_pair`: an object with `cases` (the two of my case numbers), `best_resolution`, and `what_it_costs`.
- `my_tentative_principle`: an object with `verdict` (either "repairable" or "replace"), `failure_modes` (an array of strings), and `minimal_repair` (a string; use an empty string if no repair is worth making).

Two constraints on the response. Do not give me a reading list, a bibliography, or any references, real or invented: I cannot check them, and a fabricated citation is worse to me than an acknowledged gap. If you wish to invoke a familiar view, name it in prose and let the argument stand on its own. And where you are uncertain — particularly on 6 and 10 — say that you are uncertain rather than manufacturing confidence; I spent my career being told by data that I was wrong, and I find it far more comfortable than being flattered.
