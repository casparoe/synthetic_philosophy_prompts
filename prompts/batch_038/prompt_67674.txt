I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and each autumn I guest-lead one session of a first-year seminar on how science knows things: fourteen students, seventy-five minutes, no lab equipment, nothing assigned in advance. This year I want them to run into a problem rather than hear about one.

The problem is a pair of purely armchair arguments, symmetric in structure and equally vivid. The first is the tied-bodies reasoning that heavy and light objects cannot fall at different rates, since the lighter would retard the heavier, and the pair — heavier than either alone — would then fall more slowly than the heavy one by itself. The second is the older reasoning that a stone released from a tower top would land west of the base if the Earth turned eastward beneath it, and therefore that the Earth must be at rest. Both persuaded clever people; one lands on a falsehood; and from the armchair they feel exactly alike. That last point is what the students should come away seeing: the same felt necessity is doing the work in both, so it cannot be what earns one of them credit.

Design the session as a runnable activity. Students should commit privately, in writing, to a verdict and a confidence on each argument before anyone is told which is which, under a simple scoring rule that rewards honest confidence and needs no real money; then, in groups, state a criterion for separating truth-tracking armchair reasoning from seductive armchair reasoning and apply it to the pair. Give me the sequencing of the reveal, the materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline.

The debrief should map moments in the room onto the two live accounts of how such reasoning could yield knowledge — that the armchair step only unpacks empirical premises the reasoner already had in hand, versus that some thought experiments deliver genuine non-empirical insight into how nature must be — and should say what each account owes us about the argument that fails. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it.

Two difficulties to face rather than paper over. Contamination: these students already know the Earth turns, so their "intuitive" verdicts will be hindsight in disguise — either show me how the design neutralizes that, or concede it cannot and propose a substitute for the failing argument. And be frank about which feature of the puzzle no classroom exercise can reproduce, and what I will have to supply simply by talking.
