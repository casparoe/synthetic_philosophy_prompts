Our engineering standards panel has a complaint in front of it that I do not know how to think about, and the assignment of circulating the written recommendation before Thursday's meeting has landed on me. I would like you to draft that memo.

First, who I am and what the panel is. I am a staff software engineer at a mid-sized B2B software company, with no formal training in philosophy — I read incident postmortems, not journal articles — and I would prefer that the memo not depend on my getting any terminology right. The panel I sit on has five members: three senior engineers including me, our HR business partner for engineering, and a director from outside engineering who rotates in each quarter. Its charter covers exactly one thing: written complaints that an engineer violated the Change Control Standard, which requires two approvals before any change reaches production and prohibits self-merging. The standard has no emergency exception clause; the only exemption is a waiver granted in advance by the panel chair for planned maintenance. The panel cannot decline to hear a properly filed complaint — it must issue either a recorded finding or a recorded non-finding within thirty days — and it cannot amend the standard, only recommend amendments to its owner. A second recorded finding within twenty-four months triggers a mandatory performance review.

The complaint. Jordan, a senior engineer, was on call on the night of March 12. At 2:14 a.m. Jordan pushed a one-line configuration revert to production without a second approval. The revert undid a feature-flag change deployed the previous afternoon that monitoring had tied to elevated error rates; the panel's incident timeline records twenty-two minutes of degraded customer service beginning before Jordan acted, with error rates back to baseline roughly three minutes after the revert. The review system's required-reviewer check was unavailable from about 11 p.m. to 5 a.m. that night because of an unrelated vendor outage, and the audit log shows Jordan attempted to request a reviewer at 1:47 and 2:02 a.m. with no response, because the request could not be routed. Jordan disclosed the unapproved push herself, in the second paragraph of the incident report she filed at 3:40 a.m.

The complainant. The complaint was filed by Alex, a principal engineer, at 9:10 the following morning. Jordan does not dispute the facts and does not argue that the standard permitted what she did. Jordan's position, given in writing, is that Alex is not in a position to be the one pressing it.

Here is why that is not a trivial point. Eleven months ago Alex pushed a schema migration to production without a second approval during an incident; the panel recorded a finding, issued a documented warning, and the migration caused a ninety-minute outage. Since then Alex has complied — the audit logs show no unapproved merges by Alex in eleven months — and has become the standard's most vocal advocate: a segment at a company-wide all-hands on why the two-approval rule admits no exceptions, himself included, and six blocked merges this quarter citing the standard. Two of those six involved engineers who were mid-incident; in both cases Alex personally located an approver within the hour and filed nothing. With Jordan, in what on paper looks like the same situation, Alex filed. Separately, during a February incident in which the review tooling was working normally, Alex waited forty-one minutes for a second approval rather than push unreviewed.

The panel is split, and I want the memo to engage the split rather than paper over it. Two members say the finding is easy and the complainant is beside the point: it is the panel that finds and records, not Alex, so Alex's history cannot convert a violation into a non-violation, and allowing a respondent's objection to the accuser to defeat a finding would hand every future respondent an amnesty. Two members say that if the panel refuses to look at who is complaining, it becomes an instrument of one person's selective enforcement, and they point to the asymmetry described above. The HR business partner raises a third worry: Alex is not the respondent in this proceeding, will not be present Thursday, and has no opportunity to answer any charge about his motives, so whatever the panel says about Alex risks adjudicating a non-party behind his back. Alex has separately asked to make a short statement at the hearing.

I understand that there is a difference between a rule having been broken and a particular person being entitled to press it, and roughly why the second question is not merely a way of dodging the first. What I cannot work out is what that difference can do inside a written compliance process, where the reproach is delivered by an institution on a deadline, where the person pressing the charge may have changed in the interim, and where the underlying standard may simply have a hole in it: the February and March facts make it arguable that Alex's forty-one minutes and Jordan's 2:14 a.m. revert are the same fact seen from two directions, and that the genuine problem is the absence of any emergency provision. I cannot amend the standard, but the memo can recommend that someone else does.

Please draft the memo — roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, in American English, addressed to the five panel members, written to be circulated forty-eight hours before the meeting. It must:

1. Give a bottom-line recommendation — recorded finding, recorded non-finding, or a finding with a specified remedial consequence — together with the motion I should propose on Thursday and anything the panel should complete within thirty days.
2. Say what to do about Alex: whether the panel should hear the statement he has requested, whether anything concerning him belongs in the record, and how to handle the fact that he is not a party to the proceeding.
3. Include a short section written for the HR business partner and the rotating director rather than for the three engineers, explaining what an objection to a hypocritical accuser does and does not establish and what actually turns on it in this case. Assume no philosophy background in the reader and no willingness to look anything up.
4. State the strongest case against your recommendation in the form its most convincing advocate would give it, not a weakened version. Whichever way you come out, at least one of the three positions above will lose, and I want the memo to show that it lost for reasons.
5. Provide three to five criteria the panel could apply the next time a complaint arrives from someone with a recorded violation of the same standard, written so a future panel could apply them without me.

Constraints: cite nothing. No bibliography, no reading list, no named philosophers, no references to books or papers, no academic signposting; the memo has to stand entirely on the facts above and on reasoning a reader can check for themselves. Do not invent facts — if you need one I have not supplied, whether a provision of the standard, a date, or a prior panel practice, say explicitly that the panel must establish it instead of filling it in. A version of the reasoning, redacted of internal deliberation, will be shared with both Jordan and Alex, so please write it in language that would be fair for each of them to read about himself or herself.
