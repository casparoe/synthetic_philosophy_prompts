I TA a "statistics for policy people" course and every single year the Simpson's paradox week goes the same way. I put up a table, half the room concludes that numbers are meaningless, the other half concludes that you should always just look at the disaggregated data, and neither conclusion is right and I can't seem to talk them out of it. This year I want to hit them with something funnier than yet another worked example — a piece of satire they'll actually remember, that's legible to a 20-year-old non-math person but makes our department's causal-inference person snort.

So: write me a parody client-facing "solution brief" from an invented consultancy whose entire business is choosing the level of aggregation at which a client's numbers look best. Around 700 words, with the full consultancy scaffolding — executive summary, the problem, our method, a worked example, anticipated objections, engagement tiers and pricing — and a small table of made-up figures inside the worked example. Deadpan throughout, no winking, and nobody in the piece is ever allowed to say the words "misleading" or "paradox"; the recoil should land entirely on the reader. The humor has to come from the statistics being exactly right, which means two things. First, the table has to genuinely reverse: the client's option wins overall and loses in every subgroup (or vice versa), and the arithmetic has to actually check out when my students sit down and do it, so pick your numbers carefully rather than gesturing. Second, the subgroup that drives the reversal has to be a causally meaningful feature of the world — sicker patients being routed to the agressive protocol, say, or a call center's easy tickets landing in one queue — not an arbitrary slice, because the whole joke depends on there being a fact of the matter that the consultancy never mentions.

The consultant should handle the causal question the way a real practitioner of this abuse handles it, i.e. by never raising it, so the piece quietly stages why you can't settle which partition to condition on without a causal model, and the difference between conditioning on a confounder and conditioning on a mediator or a collider. Give them a mask-slip in the pricing section: they decline certain engagements, and if you read the reason straight it turns out to be the one situation where the whole business model collapses — a client who has already randomized. They must stay internally consistent about this: they never claim any partition is truer than another, only that some are more useful, and that's the position the satire is actually aimed at. No named statisticians, no dates, no citations, no reading list — a consultancy deck wouldn't have any and I don't want my students hunting for references they cant check. And if you can work in one sentence about how the difference of two weighted averages isn't the weighted average of the two differences, do — that's the line I want to steal for a slide.
