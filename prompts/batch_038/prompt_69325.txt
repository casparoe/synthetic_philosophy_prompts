I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine and I've talked my editor into a long piece on Network — the 1976 Lumet/Chayefsky one — but I don't want to write the usual "wow they predicted reality TV" thing, that story is dead. I want to hang it on Kierkegaard instead, specifically that odd little book from 1846 where he reviews a now-forgotten Danish novel and uses the review to work out his picture of the present age as a reflective, passionless age: the public as a phantom that only exists because there's a press, levelling, chatter, the way the daily paper lets everybody have an opinion about everything from a distance and be responsible for nothing.

So: read the film through that account, seriously, not as wallpaper. Beale gets dumped for bad ratings, announces on air he's going to kill himself, becomes the most-watched man in America by being unhinged, gets a whole country leaning out its windows shouting "I'm as mad as hell, and I'm not going to take this anymore!", loses the audience the moment he starts saying true but depressing things, and finally gets shot by the militants the network hired — with a voiceover explaining he was killed because his ratings were lousy.

The thing I keep coming back to is the rating itself. Kierkegaard's public has no body, no meeting place, no officers; that's part of why nobody can be pinned down by it. Television invented an instrument that claims to measure the phantom and then drives behavoir off the measurement — a phantom with a readout. Does that complete his account or wreck it? Is Beale the single individual who gets absorbed, or was he never one? Where does Schumacher sit? And Jensen's speech, where an actual human being talks in the voice of a total system with no individuals left in it — is the film giving the phantom a CEO, and if so does that make its diagnosis economic rather than spiritual? Plus the awkward bit: Chayefsky indicting TV from inside TV, the way Kierkegaard attacked the press by putting out his own paper at the end of his life. Fatal self-contradiction, or the only honest position?

Then tell me straight where the reading breaks — which parts of the film are doing nothing Kierkegaardian at all, and where forcing the fit would give me a worse film and a mushier philosophy. End with what the picture adds to the argument that the argument can't get on its own. Something like 1200–1500 words, no footnotes and no reading list — I can look that stuff up myself, I just need the thinking. Don't bother quoting either text, my memory of both is vague enough that I'd rather you describe scenes and paraphrase.
