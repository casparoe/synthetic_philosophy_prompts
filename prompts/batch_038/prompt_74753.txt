Write a dialogue, roughly a thousand words long, between two philosophers of history of equal ability who disagree about whether the question "Why did sustained industrial growth begin in Britain rather than in China?" is well posed.

Give them a shared case to argue over. A historian proposes the following answer: around 1800 the most advanced regions of Europe and of East Asia were comparably commercialized and comparably hemmed in by the scarcity of productive land; Britain escaped that constraint through coal that happened to lie near its economic centres and navigable water — while China's largest deposits sat far inland in the north — together with New World colonies supplying land-intensive goods Europe could not have grown at home.

The first character maintains that a contrastive question of this shape is determinate only if three conditions hold: the two terms must be commensurable at the same level of aggregation, since "Britain" and "China" are units of wildly different scale and internal variety, and the live comparison may really be England against the Yangzi delta; the set of alternatives must be closed under the historian's own causal model, so that the same explanans does not equally license "Britain rather than the Netherlands," "Britain rather than Bengal," or "Britain rather than Japan"; and the questioner's interests must settle which of the many candidate difference-makers counts as the answer. On her view, once these conditions are satisfied the question that remains is not the one the historian supposed he was answering.

The second character maintains that the contrastive form is simply the ordinary shape of causal explanation, and that those conditions, enforced consistently, would condemn explanations we rightly accept in epidemiology and geology. Historians, he argues, fix the contrast class empirically rather than by stipulation: the near misses — Song China, Tokugawa Japan, the Dutch Republic — are themselves evidence bearing on which contrast is in play; and a question can be perfectly well posed even if its answer turns out to be a conjunction of factors plus luck.

Both must answer the other's actual moves rather than weakened versions, and at least once one of them should concede something and revise accordingly. Let the exchange turn on the coal-and-colonies case rather than on generalities, and touch briefly on whether the verdict would change if what needed explaining were the rise of modern science rather than of industry. End without a tidy synthesis, but with a clear statement of where the disagreement now stands.
