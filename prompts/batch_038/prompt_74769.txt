Our school's philosophy club read an essay from an online magazine arguing against what it called "anti-aggregationism," the view that no number of trivial harms can outweigh one terrible one. I'd like an accuracy audit before we discuss it, because I can't tell where it's solid and where it's sloppy. The relevant passage goes like this:

"Parfit's mere-addition argument settled the question decades ago: anyone who refuses to add up small harms is committed to the repugnant conclusion. The reasoning itself is simple. Compare a speck of dust in the eye with fifty years of torture. If a slightly worse harm may always be traded for enough slightly better ones, then by transitivity there must be some number of specks worse than the torture; anyone who denies this has a ranking that isn't transitive, and intransitive preferences can be Dutch-booked. The only escape is a sharp lexical line, and there is no principled place to draw one: why would five minutes of headache differ in kind from five minutes and one second? So the numbers count, which is why cost-benefit analysis rests on firm ground. Regulators are always entitled to trade one certain death against enough small risks distributed across millions of people."

Two things in there bother me, though I'm not confident about either. I thought the repugnant conclusion was about enormous populations living lives barely worth living, not about specks in eyes; and I thought there were philosophers who deny that the numbers count without giving up transitivity at all. Please go through the passage claim by claim: which parts a philosopher working on this would sign off on, which are true but carry more weight than the author admits, and which are simply wrong or use a technical term loosely. Then tell me whether the policy conclusion still follows once the errors are corrected, and give me the strongest honest version of the view the essay is attacking rather than its caricature.
