I'm writing a piece that uses the APA's 1973-74 decision to drop homosexuality from the DSM as a case study in the is-ought gap, and what I want is a real case analysis, not the standard science-versus-politics telling. Facts as I have them, so you don't have to guess: Hooker's 1950s study where raters couldn't pick gay men out of a set of projective test results; gay activists disrupting the 1970 and 1971 conventions, Kameny's address to the association, the psychiatrist who spoke from a panel in disguise in 1972; Spitzer's argument that the distress criterion was doing all the work; the board of trustees voting in December 1973 to remove the category and substitute a narrower one for people distressed by their own orientation; dissenting members forcing a referendum of the full membership in spring 1974, which upheld the change with something like 58 percent in favor; the compromise category surviving into DSM-III in 1980 and then being deleted outright in 1987. If you think I've got any of that wrong, say so in one line and keep going — I don't want the analysis hanging on a contested detail.

Now the mapping. Who actually had decision-making authority at each stage, what were the live options, and what counted as evidence for each side? Then sort the premises: which moves on either side were factual claims about an underlying condition, which were value claims about what counts as a harm, and which were neither — changes to the rule that fixes the extension of the term mental disorder. My hunch, which I want you to test and break if it doesn't hold, is that the decisive move in 1973 and again in 1987 was that third kind. The facts about same-sex attraction didn't change and the arguement that suffering matters didn't change either; what changed was the extension-fixing rule, from this trait is a disorder to a trait counts as a disorder only if its bearer is distressed by it, which relocates the pathology out of the person and into the relation between the person and a hostile social environment. If that's right, the distress criterion is self-undermining for a stigmatized trait, because the classification helps produce the distress it then cites as evidence — a looping problem rather than a naturalistic fallacy.

Then run the theory over it. Keep Hume's is-ought apart from Moore's naturalistic fallacy and say whether either charge actually lands on either side, or whether both sides get accused of them rhetorically while neither commits them. Could the removal have been defended with no value premise at all under a value-free account of disease like Boorse's biostatistical theory? Does Wakefield's harmful dysfunction make the episode look like a textbook hybrid where the dysfunction half came up empty and the harm half did all the work — and if so, does that vindicate the hybrid analysis or expose it, given that the harm half got settled by a membership vote rather than by anything with epistemic standing?

Finally, what does the episode show about the theory: confirmation, anomaly, or a case the is-ought framing never anticipated? Be explicit about where it goes silent. It tells you a conclusion doesn't follow from premises of the wrong kind; it says nothing about how to adjudicate a contested thick term, and it doesn't license calling the decision arbitrary just because a value premise was in there somewhere. And tell me where my extension-fixing story strains — I suspect 1987 is the hard case for it, since by then there was no distress-based compromise left to reinterpret. No bibliography and no citations, I just want names and years inline where they help.
