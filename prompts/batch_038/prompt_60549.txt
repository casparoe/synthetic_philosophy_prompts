Consider this proposition: omissions are genuine causes. Concretely, when a lifeguard absorbed in her phone fails to throw the ring buoy and the swimmer drowns, that failure is literally part of what caused the death, rather than a background condition we appeal to when selecting among the real causes. I want your credence in it as a number, together with the decomposition that produces it: (i) the probability that causation is at bottom a difference-making relation rather than the transmission of something along a continuous process, since that largely settles whether there is any work for an absence to do; (ii) conditional on difference-making, the probability that the true omissions can be restricted to a non-arbitrary and reasonably sparse class instead of ballooning to include every absent or merely possible intervener; (iii) the probability that the intuition in the lifeguard case survives being told that it tracks causal selection or contrastive explanation rather than the causal relation itself; plus any further terms you think belong. Then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to and what would move it most — in particular, whether you would treat a finding that people's omission judgments track default-and-normality expectations as evidence against the proposition or only against the intuition behind it. Two colleagues sit at 0.85 and 0.15 on this question; say which term they are most likely disagreeing about, and why.
