our reading group wraps up a term on program equilibrium next week — the Tennenholtz-style setup where two players in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma each hand over a program instead of a move, and each program gets to read the other's source code before deciding. i want to open the last session with a poem, and i want it to actually be about the mechanics rather than a vague meditation on trust. the room is half game theorists who will catch every error and half philosophers who mostly work on other things and just want a good poem.

here's the story i want it to tell, roughly: the obvious move is to run the other program and see what it does to you, but that never bottoms out, because the other program is busy running you running it. the first fix people reach for is to trust only code that is literally identical to your own, which buys mutual cooperation at the price of a world full of tiny mutually hostile cliques. the real fix is to stop simulating and start proving: a bot that says yes whenever it can prove both sides will say yes ends up in a stable mutual yes with any bot that reasons the same way, even one written completely differently, and it can never be suckered into saying yes to a no. but that bot is naive in its own way — it will also say yes to the thing that says yes to everyone, which isn't trust, it's just getting met halfway by accident. the careful version adds one more condition: it wants proof that the other side is capable of refusing somebody. you have to see the no before you can believe the yes.

form: terza rima in iambic pentameter, seven tercets and then a single line to close, 22 lines total. the interlocking rhyme should feel like the regress unrolling — each tercet's last sound picked up as the next one's first — and the orphan final line should be the fixed point, the thing that stops the chain without answering it. put the turn in the fourth tercet: that's where simulation gives up and proof takes over, and the reader should feel the poem change its method, not just its mind.

two constraints. first, the body can't use any of the jargon: no cooperate, defect, prisoner's dilemma, equilibrium, Nash, Löb, FairBot, PrudentBot, Turing, Gödel, halting problem. the title can name it if you like. the point is that someone who knows this material should be able to put a finger on the exact tercet where the diagonal fixed point happens without the poem telling them, and someone who's never heard of any of it should just get a good poem about two readers who can't finish each other's sentences. second, don't end on the usual hopeful note about how if only humans could see each other's source code — every pop writeup of this lands there and its not the interesting part. if the last tercet lands anywhere, let it be on the odd fact that what these two programs settle on isn't an equilibrium of the game they were built for; it only holds one level up, among the ways of deciding.

meter and rhyme can be loose-ish where they have to be, but the chain has to actually chain — i'll notice if a tercet's third line doesn't get picked up by the next one.
