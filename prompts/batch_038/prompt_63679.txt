I direct a three-year research effort staffed by two researchers and myself — one a historian of state formation, one a statistician with forecasting-tournament experience — and our contracted deliverable is a working method for producing defensible probability estimates on century-scale institutional questions: whether a given great power will still exist as such in a hundred years, whether the current international order outlasts the century, and whether a dominant technology firm belongs in a reference class of chartered trading companies, of national infrastructure monopolies, or of neither. We are not writing a philosophy of history. We have to hand a number and an interval to analysts who will be scored against it.

Our core tool is reference-class forecasting: identify a class of comparable past cases, take the base rate of the outcome within it, and then adjust for the ways the case at hand differs. In practice nearly all of our difficulties have collapsed into the following open questions, and I want help deciding which to attack first.

1. Description-sensitivity. "Hegemon," "great power," "empire," and "large agrarian state" yield very different base rates, and we have no non-arbitrary way to choose among them. Is this genuine underdetermination, or merely an underdeveloped protocol for robustness analysis?
2. Unitization. Before cases can be counted, each must be given boundaries, and the start-and-stop conventions appear to move the numbers more than the evidence does.
3. Sample bias. The surviving record is not a random draw, and we necessarily observe from inside a lineage that has not yet terminated. Should the self-locating corrections debated around the Doomsday argument enter a practical method at all?
4. Circularity between views. The causal mechanisms we use to explain institutional failure are induced from roughly the same handful of cases that supply the base rate, so the outside view and the inside view may not be independent sources of evidence — in which case the standard instruction to anchor on the base rate and adjust for mechanism may double-count.
5. Non-stationarity. The parameters that generated the historical frequencies — growth rates, communication speed, the existence of nuclear weapons — have changed, so the base rate may estimate a distribution that no longer obtains.
6. Feedback. Our target questions resolve after the forecasters are dead, so we cannot accumulate a calibration record against them directly.

My own inclination is that (4) is the deepest problem here and that (3) is an intellectual distraction we should exclude from scope. Rank the six by what genuinely deserves the attention of a team our size over three years, and defend the ranking rather than merely producing it. State which criteria you are trading off — how much downstream work depends on the answer; whether the question can be settled by argument and data we already have, as against data we cannot obtain; whether a partial answer would still change what we ship — and say explicitly where you disagree with my two leanings and why. Finally, identify what specific development over the next year or two would move a question up or down your list, and tell me if one of these is not really a question at all, or if the true bottleneck is something I have omitted.
