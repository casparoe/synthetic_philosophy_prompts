For thirty-eight years I worked on the kinetics of solid-state phase transformations — nucleation, metastable intermediates, hysteresis loops, and the way a system that has taken one branch of a transformation cannot be coaxed back along the other by reversing the external variable. Since retiring I have been reading in the philosophy of history and the philosophy of biology, and I have found myself unable to shake the suspicion that my own discipline supplies the wrong intuition for the question I now care about: whether large-scale history, biological and human alike, is locked in, or whether the same destinations would have been reached by other routes. I convene a small monthly group — two former colleagues, a medievalist, an economic historian, and a palaeobiologist — and we have spent the better part of a year on it without progress, because our two camps keep shifting the meaning of the key words. The first camp, in the tradition of the Burgess Shale argument advanced by Gould in the late 1980s and generalised by some of us to technology and institutions, holds that the space of genuinely available trajectories at any historical node is wide, that small differences are amplified unpredictably, and that the actual sequence is one draw from a distribution far broader than it looks in retrospect. The second camp, in the tradition of Conway Morris's argument from repeated convergence in the fossil record and of the increasing-returns and lock-in literature in the economics of technology, holds that the space of viable end-states is far narrower than the space of conceivable ones, that functional, developmental and competitive constraints channel outcomes, and that a great many famous contingencies would have been filled by something equivalent within a bounded interval.

What I want you to produce is the document my group should have written instead of arguing: the joint statement these two camps could both put their names to after a serious adversarial collaboration, drafted as a text with a title, a preamble identifying the two parties, a dated signature block, and then the following parts in order. (1) A numbered section of agreed claims, not padded out — the parties should agree, for instance, that natural selection is a filter and not a generator of novelty, that the fossil record is a sample rather than a census, that both are making claims about counterfactual histories no one can observe, that lock-in dynamics demonstrably operate in at least some technological domains, and that some apparent convergences turn out on closer inspection to be shared ancestry or shared ancestors' latent variation; state such agreements precisely and say what follows from them for the debate. (2) A disagreements table whose columns are: the point in neutral wording; the formulation the contingentist would accept as a fair statement of her own view; the formulation the convergence theorist would accept as a fair statement of his own view; the sense of contingent or of inevitable that is in play; and a verdict on whether that row records a substantive disagreement, a merely verbal one, or a substantive one that is presently untestable, with a sentence of justification. Before the table, give me the conceptual apparatus the table presupposes: distinguish at least three senses in which an outcome can be called contingent — practical unpredictability, sensitivity to initial conditions, and the genuine availability at some node of trajectories that do not reconverge — and distinguish separately the exact replay, in which the starting microstate is reproduced identically, from the statistical replay, in which one resamples from a plausible distribution of starting conditions, noting that the two camps may be answering different questions and that the answer may differ between the two. Also require every convergence claim to specify its level of description — gross morphology, ecological function or adaptive zone, developmental mechanism, genotype — and its timescale, since the parties may both be right at different levels and the dispute collapses if this is left vague.

(3) A section of cruxes: at least five sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party. For each, give the case or line of evidence both parties agree would bear on it, each party's prediction recorded in advance in his or her own idiom, and the result that would oblige each party to revise. I want at least one crux drawn from replicated laboratory evolution in microbial populations run in parallel for tens of thousands of generations, where the degree of parallelism among independent lines can actually be measured, and including the rare, late, lineage-specific innovation that required a long potentiating history; at least one from the comparative and fossil record, where the independence of the converging lineages is the crux, since convergence claims are only as strong as the phylogenetic independence of the cases marshalled for them; at least one from the history of technology and standard-setting, taking the QWERTY case as the paradigm and asking whether it shows lock-in of an inferior design or a competent design that became better as complements accumulated; at least one from deep time, asking whether the replacement of one dominant terrestrial fauna by another after the end-Cretaceous impact depended on the impactor's timing, angle and target lithology, or whether the replacement was already under way; and at least one that is not empirical at all but semantic or metaphysical, namely what would have to be true for a claim of inevitability to have a determinate truth value, given that in physics I could point to a free-energy landscape and a measure over microstates and in history there is neither. For each crux, state explicitly whether it is one on which the two parties predict differently or one on which they predict identically and disagree only about what the predictions mean — that distinction, I suspect, is where most of our year went. (4) A short appendix on evidentiary hygiene listing items both parties agree should be retired from the debate: the popular chain of explanation that traces the modern railway gauge back through tramways and wagon wheels to the ruts of Roman chariots, and the parties' agreed assessment of whether any part of it survives; the familiar account of one recording format defeating another on the basis of technical superiority, which I understand historians of technology now consider at best incomplete; and the earlier reconstructions of certain iconic Burgess animals that were subsequently shown to have been built the wrong way round or reassigned into body plans already known, together with the general lesson about how much apparent disparity in a fossil fauna is an artefact of preservation and of the interpreter's imagination. (5) A closing section in which each party states, in terms the other accepts as sincere, what would count as evidence against his or her own position.

Two constraints. I want no adjudication, no verdict on who is right, and none of the comfortable conclusion that the truth lies somewhere in the middle; the value of the exercise is in the exactness of the residual disagreement. And I want no bibliography, no reading list, and no quoted passages from any author's prose — I have no way of checking them from where I sit, and a plausible-looking but invented citation would poison the document for my group. Naming a work, an author or an experiment where it genuinely identifies a crux is welcome; putting words in anyone's mouth is not. Finally, address my own prejudice directly and without flattery: tell me where the analogy between a metastable phase in a crystal and a locked-in historical outcome holds, and where it is a category error — I suspect the difference is that my metastable states sit in a landscape whose topology and whose measure over microstates were both given to me by thermodynamics, and that the whole controversy may turn on the absence of exactly that gift.
