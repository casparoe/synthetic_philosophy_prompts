I do evals work at a small AI-safety outfit, and for our internal reading group we've started a slightly masochistic tradition where each person writes a one-page "here's where I actually stand" doc so everyone else can spend an hour trying to break it. Mine is on the agency question — whether these things are agents, simulators, or something I don't have a word for yet — and I've been sitting on the draft for two weeks because a coworker already took a swing at it and I didn't like his diagnosis. So I want a second opinion before I present it.

Here's the list, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

1. I'm a functionalist about mental states. To have a belief or a goal is to be disposed to behave in certain ways across a range of situations, and there's no further fact of the matter hiding behind the dispositions.
2. I think the simulator-not-agent line is basically right about base models: what you have there is an engine for producing plausible continuations, and the agency you see is a character being played rather than a character being had.
3. But I don't think that gets deployed models off the hook. When I'm predicting what an RLHF'd model will do in an eval, I take the intentional stance — I ask what it wants and what it believes — and I think that's tracking something real, not just a convenient fiction I could trade for a lower-level description.
4. A system can't have goals it wasn't, in some broad sense, built or trained to have. Goals that show up out of nowhere don't count as the system's goals, no matter how reliably it acts on them.
5. Agency requires persisting through time with something at stake. A single stateless API call can't be an agent, however clever its outputs are.
6. I think (5) is the kind of thing you can actually measure. My working metric — I call it persona-residual behavior — is how much of a model's dispositions survive when you strip away the role it was asked to adopt, and I think that's roughly what "genuinely agentic" amounts to and that you can estimate it with a decent eval suite run in one sitting.
7. Behavioral evidence is fully sufficient for attributing beliefs and goals, but only weakly suggestive for attributing anything like experience. I'm not willing to make the second inference on the same grounds as the first.
8. Methodologically: what settles these questions for me is which scheme of attribution best predicts behavior. I don't want to be deciding agency from the armchair.

My coworker read the draft and said (1) and (7) are flatly contradictory, and that (2) and (3) can't both be true because either it's a simulator or it isn't. I don't think he's found the real problem, and I'm not inclined to just patch whatever he pointed at. So: give me an audit, not a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, ranked by severity rather than all dumped on me as equally bad? For each real one, say what has to give and what the cheapest revision costs me — I want to know what I lose, not just that something has to go. And separately, flag the ones that only look inconsistent and dissolve once you state them more carefully, because I suspect at least one of his two complaints is like that. Finish with the single change that would clean up the most damage across the whole set, and tell me honestly whether (6) is a real metric or whether I've just dressed up (5) in lab clothes.
