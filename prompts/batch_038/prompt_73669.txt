I'm prepping a session for an origins-of-life summer school and I want a borderline case that isn't one of the four everyone always reaches for — no viruses, viroids, prions or xenobots, and please no Schrödinger-negentropy warmup either. The room is mixed: astrobiology grad students, a couple of philosophers, and two engineers who actually build microfluidics rigs, so the case has to survive being described out loud in about two minutes without somebody in the back row saying that chemistry could never work. I'd like you to act as a philosopher of biology who has also spent real time in a wet lab, and to think carefully before answering: start with two or three sentences of brainstorming about which of my constraints are in tension with each other and how you plan to resolve it, then write the case. I already have a rig in mind and I want you to finish and sharpen it, not swap it out for something safer.

The seed: a continuous-flow reactor running about two years, holding a population of fatty-acid vesicles. Inside each vesicle is a small cross-catalytic network — a handful of short ribozymes and peptides that speed each other's formation — plus an RNA replicator lineage. Three things are deliberately outsourced to the machine instead of being done by anything inside a vesicle. (a) The polymerase that copies the RNA is a purified protein piped in with the feed; nothing in the reactor encodes it and nothing there could evolve it. (b) The feed carries pre-formed, activated fatty acids that just accrete onto the existing bilayer, so the membrane grows by supply line plus physics rather than by any internal synthesis. (c) Division is imposed: vesicles past a size threshold get squeezed through a fixed constriction on a duty cycle and split unevenly, contents distributed by luck. Over two years you get genuine cumulative adaptation — faster replicators, fusion-and-steal lineages, constriction-resistant ones, a hoarder. So it's roughly the Spiegelman's-monster situation (RNA evolving in a tube with a supplied replicase) wrapped in a compartment and given a selection regime.

Constraints on what you build. First, every component has to be something a well-funded lab could plausibly assemble in the next decade or two: no nanotech, no smart matter, no alien biochemistry — and you may not disqualify the system just because humans built it or because it lives on a bench, that's cheating and the engineers will hate it. Tell me which parts of my rig are chemically shakiest and how you'd repair them. Second, the case has to split the definitions in a specific way, and I want you to check that it really does instead of assuming it: alive under a Darwinian/evolutionary definition, not alive under an autopoietic or self-maintenance definition, and for the NASA committee definition — the one about a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution — you have to commit to a verdict under an explicit reading of the self-sustaining clause and defend that reading rather than declaring it ambiguous. Third, no verdicts by stipulation: if you say there's real heredity here, show me what carries information across the constriction and why this isn't just differential persistence of chemistry. Fourth, say what the candidate organism even is — a vesicle, the RNA population, or the reactor-plus-population — and pick one, since the three come out differently.

After the scenario, do the walkthrough: one short paragraph per definition giving the verdict and the reason, then tell me which single feature of the rig is doing the work. Run the removal test on it — suppose in year three some lineage evolves a ribozyme that copies without the supplied protein, or the vesicles start making their own amphiphiles from the feedstock — and say whether the case then collapses into something everyone calls alive, and what that tells us about which clause was load-bearing all along. Then handle the objection my colleague keeps making, which I think is the strongest one available: that any system genuinely undergoing Darwinian evolution will eventually internalize the supplied machinery, because selection favors autonomy, so my rig is dynamically unstable and therefore not a real possibility but a temporary artifact of the feed bottles. Take that head on — either explain why selection can't get there from here in the reactor's lifetime, or concede it and say what the case is still good for. Also tell me whether the response that we're simply in no position to define life until we've found a second instance of it dissolves my case or is itself a dodge; I want a position, not a shrug. Be committed throughout: end by saying flatly whether this thing is alive and which definition you'd sign up to, with your reasons. Flag anywhere you're inventing chemistry that might not work or leaning on a contested reading of a definition. And no bibliography, no citations, no reading list — I know the literature, and you can't check any of it anyway, so describe the views by their content.
