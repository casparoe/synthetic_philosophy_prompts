I am drafting a short critical comment on the republican conception of liberty, and I would like a rigorous assessment of how a defender of that conception should answer the objection I plan to press.

My reading of the terrain has settled into three steps. First, the contrast between freedom as non-interference and freedom as non-domination is extensional rather than conceptual: the two ideals diverge only for an agent exposed to another's arbitrary power yet never in fact interfered with — the slave of a kindly master, say — and since the republican objects to that exposure precisely because it leaves the slave at the mercy of a power he cannot anticipate, non-domination is negative liberty indexed to the probability of interference rather than to interference itself. Second, it follows that the republican remedy is a different instrument rather than a different ideal: a republic suppresses arbitrary private power by installing law and administration, and law and administration are themselves interferences, so republican freedom is bought with negative freedom, and the two cannot both be maximized. Third, Berlin's suspicion of the positive concept of liberty nevertheless has real purchase here, because what the republic is said to secure is the standing of the free citizen — an ideal that may be promoted on a person's behalf even against the preferences that person actually holds, which is why republicans must say something about whose preferences count.

Given all this: what is the strongest reply available to a republican who wants to resist the charge that non-domination is indefinable except by appeal to a perfectionist account of the person's true self? Is the better course to accept the perfectionism and argue that the standing of a free citizen is a genuine human good, or to relativize non-domination to each agent's own avowed interests and deny that any account of a true self is needed?

Please close by saying what would change your mind about the answer you give: which argument or consideration would move you to the opposite verdict on that dilemma, and at which stage in your own reasoning you judge the risk of error to be greatest.
