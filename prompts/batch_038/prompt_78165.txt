I'm writing a dissertation chapter in philosophy of biology on the work analogies do in the adaptationism debates, and my central exhibit is Gould and Lewontin's architectural spandrel. My collaborator, an empirical biologist, leans on it in the discussion of our paper on the human chin: buildings contain spaces nobody designed that later get lavishly decorated, so byproduct traits are real and common, and a spandrel account is the default until an adaptive story is independently supported. A philosopher in our reading group says the same analogy cuts the other way — the mosaics are what catch the eye and they are integrated into the building's use, so calling a trait a spandrel merely defers the adaptive question, since byproducts get co-opted.

Please audit the analogy. First the mapping (a table is fine): arches, dome, the spandrel space, the mosaic, the architect, the blueprint, the range of alternative designs — what does each correspond to in the biological case, and what has no correspondent at all? Then the disanalogies, separating those that actually bear on my collaborator's inference from the ones that are merely ornamental. I most want to know what the source quietly imports that the target lacks — a designer, a plan, geometric necessity, a void awaiting contents rather than a structure with properties of its own — and how much of the argument's force comes from that imported machinery. Also: how should he answer the standing objection that the San Marco features are really pendentives, so that the source is itself misdescribed? Does the default-status conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying it? If it fails, is there a better analogy for non-adaptive byproducts, or is the inference rather than the illustration the problem?

End by saying how confident you are in each verdict, and single out the point you are least sure of.
