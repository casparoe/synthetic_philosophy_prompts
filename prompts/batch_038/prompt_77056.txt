A machined part is ordered to a dimension of 25.000 mm with a tolerance of ±0.020 mm, so anything from 24.980 to 25.020 is supposed to be acceptable and anything outside it unacceptable. An accredited calibration laboratory measures the part, obtains 25.016 mm, and reports that value together with an expanded uncertainty of ±0.008 mm — an interval, 25.008 to 25.024, that straddles the upper limit. The customer has asked not merely for a number but for a statement of conformity, and under the accreditation regime such a statement must record which decision rule the laboratory applied. There is no single mandated rule. Under simple acceptance the laboratory compares only the measured value to the limit and certifies the part conforming, since 25.016 lies inside 25.020. Under a guard-banded rule the limit is tightened by the expanded uncertainty (or by some agreed fraction of it), so the acceptance boundary effectively moves to 25.012 and the very same part is certified nonconforming. Under a third rule the laboratory declines to decide and reports the result as indeterminate. Which rule applies is typically settled in the purchase contract, and the choice shifts risk between the supplier, who may have good parts rejected, and the customer, who may accept bad ones. Meanwhile the certificate also discloses the raw measured value and its uncertainty, so a reader who dislikes the laboratory's rule can recompute the verdict under a different one. Two further details matter: dimensional results are conventionally referred to a standard temperature of 20 °C, because the part's length is not even a determinate quantity apart from that stipulation; and the tolerance itself, 20 µm rather than 19 or 21, is a designer's choice fixed in advance of any measurement.

Write an essay that reconstructs the theory of vagueness this practice presupposes, and assess it. In particular:

1. What does the practice take the predicate "conforms to the specification" to be? Does it treat the boundary as sharp and merely unknown to the laboratory, as genuinely vague and precisified by the laboratory's stipulation, as context-sensitive so that the contract supplies the context, or as something else? Distinguish carefully what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit, and identify any point at which several incompatible accounts would license exactly the same certificates.
2. Is the guard band evidence of a semantic view at all, or is it purely an allocation of asymmetric costs, so that a lab using it holds no theory of borderline cases whatsoever? If the latter, does that make the practice philosophically innocent, or does it reveal that the certificates were never in the business of reporting facts about conformity?
3. Conformity now looks like a relation among the artifact, the specification, the declared decision rule, and a risk allocation, rather than a one-place property of the artifact. Is that a problem? What is lost, if anything, when the same part can be truthfully certified both conforming and nonconforming by two laboratories, each following its rule?
4. Does a sorites worry arise anywhere in this picture — for instance in the designer's fixing of the tolerance, in the continuity of the manufacturing variation the tolerance is meant to police, or in the requirement that a part differing from the limit by one nanometer be sorted into the opposite category from one differing by none? If so, say precisely where the pressure comes from and whether the practice has any resources for answering it.
5. Suppose an industry body proposed replacing binary conformity statements with graded ones, reporting a degree of conformity between 0 and 1 computed from the measurement interval and the specification limits. What would have to change in the certificates, the contracts, and the assignment of liability for such a scheme to function, and what would resist the change? Is the resistance practical, or does it rest on a substantive view about what a specification is for?

Take a position on the central question — what the practice is really committed to — and defend it, rather than cataloguing the options neutrally. Write in American English, in a formal register, and argue from general philosophical and technical reasoning rather than from citations; no bibliography or reference list is wanted.
