You are, for this task, a professional philosopher of art with a working knowledge of W. E. B. Du Bois's writings on art and of the aesthetics of propaganda; please treat what follows as a live argument to be diagnosed rather than as a question in intellectual history.

Some context, because this has stopped being abstract for me. I am a documentary photographer, and this season I was appointed to the grant panel of a regional arts council, where I now score applications under a rubric that separates "artistic excellence" from "message and community impact." Half my working life has been spent being told by critics that what I make is propaganda rather than art, and I took the panel seat partly to argue against that slur from the inside. Then a fellow panelist — a philosopher by training, who writes criticism — produced the argument below, in writing, and circulated it to the rest of the panel with the recommendation that we abandon the two-track rubric. I have read it perhaps twenty times. I find each step individually attractive and I cannot accept the end of it, and I am not able to say where my resistance is legitimate rather than merely self-protective.

His argument, as nearly as I can restate it:

(1) Call a work propaganda if it is made, or if it reliably functions, to move an audience toward some view of how the world is or how it ought to be.
(2) No work of art comes from nowhere. The choice of subject, the choice of who is rendered with sympathy and who is rendered as scenery, the choice of form, and even the decision to leave a given subject unmade all carry such a view, and a work cannot help conveying what its making carries.
(3) Therefore every work of art is propaganda. This is simply what Du Bois was insisting on when he said that all art is propaganda and always must be, whatever the purists protest about it. (I am paraphrasing, not quoting; I do not have the text in front of me and I do not want you to supply wording you are not certain of.)
(4) What is supposed to distinguish art from mere advocacy is autonomy: the work is made, and is valued, for its own sake, apart from any view it advances.
(5) Aesthetic value is the value a work has in virtue of that autonomy.
(6) By (3), no work has autonomy; by (5), therefore, no work has aesthetic value distinct from its value as advocacy.
(7) Value as advocacy is a matter of how well the work gets its audience to the view. So the only question left standing about any work is how effectively it does that.
(8) Hence a work's artistic achievement is its persuasive achievement, and a panel that scores "artistic excellence" separately from "message and impact" is either scoring one thing twice or scoring nothing at all.

What I cannot live with is where (8) leaves us. Under it, a portrait of devastating technical command whose politics I hold in contempt must be scored zero as art, while a crudely printed poster that reliably moves people must outrank it; under it, my own photographs are not better or worse made, only better or worse aimed; and, since (1) and (2) say nothing peculiar to art, the same reasoning makes his own criticism and every philosophy paper nothing but instruments of persuasion, so that (8) itself is offered not as true but as effective — which either refutes it or makes its truth irrelevant to why we should accept it.

I should tell you what I want to keep, so you can price it honestly. I want to keep (3). I want to go on saying in that room that all art is propaganda, because the rhetoric of disinterested autonomy has functioned, in my experience, as a stick for beating work made by and about people who were told to be quiet, and because I think the claim is true and not merely tactical. I also want to go on being able to say that some advocacy is superbly made and some is sloppily made, and to score that difference. What I would rather not be told is that the way out is to deny (3) and reassert that some art really is autonomous; if that is genuinely the cheapest exit, I want to be shown that it is, and shown what it costs me politically as well as theoretically.

Please work through this carefully. Before you commit to a diagnosis, sketch at least three candidate failure points and briefly say what would have to be true for each to be the right one; among the candidates I would expect you to consider are whether (1) and (2) use "propaganda" in the same sense, whether conveying a view is the same thing as being made to persuade a view, whether (5) is a premise he is entitled to or a substantive thesis he has smuggled in, and whether (7) runs together getting an audience to a view and the control of medium exercised in trying to. Do not announce in advance which kind of problem you think this is — a hidden equivocation that makes the argument invalid, or a valid argument with one very expensive false premise — but do end by committing to one.

Then give me: the step you reject and the precise reason it fails; an explanation of why that step looked perfectly acceptable in the company of the ones before it, since that is the part I cannot reconstruct for myself; what the argument's proponent gives up if he rejects (3) instead, or (5) instead, or (7) instead, ranked from cheapest to dearest; and a judgement on whether (6)–(8) are a fair reconstruction of Du Bois's own position or a strengthening of it — my recollection, which may be unreliable, is that he also demanded beauty and truth of the artist and not only rightness of aim, in which case he presumably thought propaganda could be done well or badly as craft, and I want to know whether that is consistent with (5) and (7) or fatal to them.

Finish with a practical sentence or two: if you conclude the argument is sound after all and I should accept (8), tell me what I should propose the panel do with its rubric.

Two constraints on the answer. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I cannot look anything up and I am not asking you to; where you need Du Bois, paraphrase him. And do not hedge into a survey of possible readings: think it through as much as you like, but deliver one diagnosis, defended, in something under a thousand words.
