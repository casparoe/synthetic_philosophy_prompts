I'm revising the opening section of a course on how historians can claim to know anything about the deep past, and I want to build it around the Piltdown affair — not as the usual morality tale about a clever hoaxer and credulous savants, but as a test case for a particular piece of source-critical theory.

The theory is Marc Bloch's broad division of historical evidence into two kinds: the testimony of witnesses who intend to inform you, and the traces that past activity leaves behind whether or not anyone meant them to survive. Bloch's reason for privileging traces — I am paraphrasing, and please do the same rather than quoting — is roughly that a witness may be mistaken or dishonest about what he means to convey, whereas the tax roll, the place name, or the refuse pit was not composed to persuade you of anything, and so cannot lie in the same way.

Now set the Piltdown case against it. The facts I want you to work from, briefly: in December 1912 Charles Dawson, an amateur Sussex solicitor, told the Geological Society in London that workmen at a gravel pit near Piltdown had handed him a skull fragment some years earlier and that he had since recovered further fragments, with Arthur Smith Woodward of the British Museum joining the excavations; Woodward's reconstruction paired a strikingly human-like cranium with an ape-like jaw and was named *Eoanthropus dawsoni*; the combination fitted the brain-first model of human evolution then favoured in England and supplied an early ancestor on English soil; dissent was voiced almost immediately, by Arthur Keith among others, but Woodward and his defenders held the line for four decades, and a second assemblage reported in 1915 from a different nearby location was taken to show that the first was no chance mixture of unrelated bones washed into the same gravel; after Dawson's death in 1916 nothing further turned up at Piltdown; in 1953 fluorine analysis, together with the observation that the teeth had been abraded by a file and the bone stained to simulate mineralisation, showed the jaw to be that of a modern orangutan and the cranial fragments to be far too recent, and later work concluded that a single hand had prepared the whole assemblage.

In no more than 900 words, with your verdict stated in your first sentence:

1. Sort the evidence into Bloch's two categories, then confront the difficulty: the gravel-pit material arrived as a trace wrapped inside a provenance story told by the man who controlled the material, and the trace itself had been staged so as to look like a trace. Does the taxonomy have a place for a planted trace? If not, is that a gap to be closed by adding a third category, or a sign that the two-way division was never tracking the property that makes evidence trustworthy?

2. The second site. State precisely what independence corroboration requires, and why two discoveries from two locations felt like mutual confirmation. Then say what follows when one agent controls every channel at once — and whether that worry generalises, or leaves the ordinary historian's situation (many documents from one archive, many witnesses from one community) untouched.

3. Expectation. The find supplied what its audience most wanted. Argue whether a source-critical method ought to discount evidence in proportion to how well it matches a community's antecedent expectations, and whether such a discount can be made coherent without collapsing into a general suspicion of anything confirmatory.

4. The forty years. Was the failure methodological or instrumental? Resist the easy answer that better techniques were simply unavailable: say what the community could have done with the eyes and arguments it already had in 1913, and what it did instead.

Please do not speculate about who the forger was or why, and do not give me a bibliography — work from what you already know, and where you are unsure of a detail, flag the uncertainty rather than filling the gap.
