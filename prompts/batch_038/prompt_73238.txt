I am a high school junior, and for the past year I have been working through the disputes over what our history classes should say: the arguments in this country that pit 1619 against 1776 as the starting point around which the American story should be organized, and the comparable arguments between countries that fought one another and now disagree about how that war is described in each side's textbooks. I have arrived at a position I would like stress-tested rather than applauded.

My position is that the parties to these disputes are not, in the main, disagreeing about the past. Put under patient questioning, most of them would concede most of the other side's factual claims. What they actually disagree about is the organizing question the course is supposed to answer: whether the point of a compulsory history requirement is to produce people capable of sustaining a shared political project, or people capable of weighing evidence against inherited narratives. On my account, these fights feel interminable because a design question is being conducted as though it were an evidentiary one, and the standard remedies — expert review panels, accuracy standards, line-by-line fact-checking of contested passages — reliably fail because they answer a question nobody is really asking.

Tell me what would count against this. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one I want to know whether a committed defender of the view would concede it as counting against them or explain it away. Specifically: what does my view predict that its rivals do not — the account on which these fights are proxies for broader partisan conflict, the account on which there are genuine historiographical disagreements at stake, and the account that emphasizes state adoption rules and market incentives as what actually determines what gets printed? Where is the view exposed to ordinary observation rather than to theory, for instance in what a survey of the disputants would show or in what actually happens at a district hearing? Can it handle the cases that look like straightforward factual disputes — whether a particular atrocity occurred, whether a document is authentic, how large a number was? And does it survive contact with the international cases, where no single polity is deciding the purpose of a compulsory class and the disagreement runs between governments rather than within one, including those instances where historians from the countries involved have jointly authored a shared text?

I am also worried that my reframing has been qualified into unfalsifiability, since any apparent factual disagreement can be absorbed as really being about framing. Give me an honest assessment of whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, or whether it is what a view about a design question should expect. Close by naming the single piece of evidence that would most move me.
