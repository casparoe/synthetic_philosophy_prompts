An article in a general-interest science magazine claims that physics has already settled whether the future is open. Its central passage runs as follows:

> Special relativity abolished the single, universal "now." Two observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are occurring at this very moment, and neither description is more correct than the other, because the laws of physics pick out no preferred state of rest. Penrose's illustration makes the stakes vivid: two people pass each other on the street, and for the one walking toward the Andromeda galaxy an alien fleet has already set out, while for the other the decision to launch still lies in the future. Since both accounts are equally correct, the departure is simultaneously already real and not yet real, and the only way to square that is to say the event is a fixed feature of a four-dimensional block in which past, present and future alike exist. The reasoning applies to any pair of observers anywhere, so every event in what I call my future is already real for someone. Becoming is therefore an illusion, and free will with it: what you will do tomorrow belongs to the furniture of the universe exactly as much as what you did last year.

I find this rhetorically powerful, but I suspect it moves considerably faster than the physics does. Please audit it. Which sentences would a philosopher of physics working on spacetime sign off on, which are defensible popular simplifications, and which are simply wrong? In particular: what further premises does the argument require — about reality, or about how the "presents" of different observers relate to one another — in order to get from the frame-relativity of simultaneity to the claim that events in my future are real, how much can a single step of relative motion actually reach, and are those premises delivered by relativity itself or imported from elsewhere? What is the strongest reply available to someone who wants to keep an open future without denying special relativity, and what does that reply cost them? And once the argument is corrected, does anything survive of the claim that free will is refuted, or does that conclusion rest on a distinct assumption about determinism that nothing in the passage establishes? For what it is worth, I find the block picture attractive on independent grounds but resist the idea that physics should be doing the refuting of freedom; I would like to know whether that instinct tracks a real distinction or is mere squeamishness.
