I run an annual logic demonstration for first-years, and this year it provoked an argument between two colleagues which I have been asked to settle. I was present but did not intervene, and after going back over what each of them said I still cannot tell which was right about which part. I should like a move-by-move adjudication rather than an overall verdict.

The demonstration, as run: forty students sit so that each can see the card fixed to every other student's forehead but not her own. Each card is either red or blue; in fact all forty are red. Before anything else, the tutor states aloud, to the whole room at once, the rules of the exercise — that every student is an ideal reasoner, that every student knows the rules and knows that every other student is an ideal reasoner who knows the rules, and that at the end of each round any student who has by then deduced the colour of her own card must say so out loud. The tutor then says: "At least one of you is wearing a red card." Rounds one through thirty-nine pass in complete silence. In round forty, all forty students say "red".

Elena and Julian watched this, and afterwards argued as follows.

(1) Elena: the tutor's sentence was theatre. Every student could already see thirty-nine red cards, so every student already knew that at least one card was red. An announcement cannot make a deduction possible if it tells nobody anything they did not know; so whatever happened in that room did not depend on the sentence.

(2) Julian: that inverts it. The content was old news; the publicity was not. Before the sentence was uttered, the fact was known to depth thirty-nine — each student knew it, knew that the others knew it, and so on down — but not to depth forty, and it is precisely the fortieth level that the clock running in that room demands. He backed this with a smaller case: with two red cards and one blue, each red-carded student sees only one red card, and so cannot rule out that the other red-carded student is looking at nothing but blue; the public sentence closes exactly that gap, which is why the pair speak in round two and not round one.

(3) Elena: if depth is what matters, then strengthening the sentence should slow the clock. Had the tutor instead said "at least two of you are wearing red cards" — a strictly stronger claim — the room would on your account have had to sit through forty-one silent rounds, since the sentence now has one further person it might be about. But strictly more information cannot possibly make a deduction take longer. So the depth story must be wrong.

(4) Julian: your principle is sound and your arithmetic runs backwards. With exactly forty red cards and the sentence "at least two are red", everybody speaks in round thirty-nine, not forty-one. More generally, if the tutor announces "at least m of you are red" and exactly n cards are red, the red-carded students all speak in round n − m + 1, because the induction's base case is now reached at n = m rather than at n = 1. Strengthening the sentence accelerates the clock, which is just what the depth account predicts.

(5) Julian then offered what he called a test of his thesis. Suppose the tutor says nothing whatever in front of the room, but calls each student aside in turn and hands her a sealed slip bearing the same sentence, "At least one of you is wearing a red card", with no indication of what anyone else has been given. Every student now holds the tutor's assertion in her own hands, and yet nothing will ever be said: the rounds will pass in permanent silence. That, he said, shows that what does the work in the public version is the sentence's having been uttered in front of everybody, not anybody's having come to know it.

(6) Elena: your dichotomy is false, and your slip experiment proves the opposite of what you claim. In the slip version every student still knows the sentence, and still knows it to depth thirty-nine, exactly as she did before the tutor opened her mouth; so the contrast cannot be between knowledge and publicity, since knowledge is present on both sides. What the public version supplies is depth without limit. And the point can be turned on you: had the tutor handed out the slips privately and then said aloud, in front of everyone, "each of you has been given a slip reading 'At least one of you is wearing a red card'", the room would have spoken in round forty just the same, although the sentence was never publicly asserted as a claim about the room at all. The operative thing is the nesting, not the shouting. Separately, your idealisation is not innocent: nobody actually performs forty nested iterations of "I know that she knows that I know", so what you call the missing fortieth level may be nothing more than a name for the failure of the ideal-reasoner assumption.

(7) Julian: the nesting objection confuses the requirement with the performance. Nothing in the account asks any student to hold forty levels in mind at once; the induction is discharged a round at a time, and each round's inference is one step longer than the last. As for your counter-announcement, I accept it, and it seems to me to concede my point rather than undermine it — which is itself worth explaining.

Please write your answer in British English, and organise it as follows:

(a) a rating for each of the seven moves — clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right — each with a short justification that states the underlying epistemic facts, including the actual depth of mutual knowledge before the tutor speaks and the round on which the students would speak under every variant the two of them describe;

(b) an identification of the single move on which the dispute turns, with an explanation of why the others are downstream of it;

(c) an assessment of how much of the exchange is merely verbal — in particular, whether Elena and Julian use "common knowledge", "depth" and "publicity" in the same senses throughout, and where a shared stipulation would have dissolved a disagreement;

(d) the two control demonstrations that the argument gestures at but never runs: one in which the sentence is publicly asserted but the round-by-round speaking protocol is not common knowledge, and one in which the protocol is common knowledge but the sentence reaches each student only privately, together with a precise statement of each, the outcome you predict, and what each outcome would establish;

(e) a statement of what both of them are missing, including whether Julian is right in (7) that Elena's counter-announcement concedes his point, and whether the demonstration as actually run tells us anything about real students in a real room.

Do not simply declare a winner. I want to know who is right at each step, since that is where I think the argument went wrong.
