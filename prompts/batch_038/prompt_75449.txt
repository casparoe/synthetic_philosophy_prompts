So I'm the unlucky one moderating a little forecasting project where a handful of us have been trying to put actual numbers on how long a great power stays a great power — how many years of "top-tier" status you should expect once a state has had it for, say, forty. Two camps have formed and they've been talking past each other for the better part of a year, and I've decided the only way forward is to make them sign a joint statement before anyone writes another paragraph. I'd like you to draft that statement.

The camps, in their own preferred telling: Camp A says forecasting has to start from the outside view — you build the empirical distribution of durations for comparable polities, you take the base rate as your anchor, and any move away from that anchor has to be justified item by item and bounded, because otherwise you're just laundering a story into a number. They're fully aware the reference class is fuzzy (do you count dynasties, regimes, states, hegemons? do you cut the sample at 1500 or 1648 or 1945?), and their answer is that fuzziness is a cost you manage by nesting and widening classes and reporting how sensitive the answer is, not a reason to bail on frequencies. When someone pushed them on "what if there is no usable class at all," they retreated to a Copernican-style fallback — the Gott move where you use nothing but how old the thing already is, assume your temporal position isn't special, and get something like a 95% interval running from roughly 1/39 to roughly 39 times its age so far — and they insist that's still the outside view, just with the class shrunk to the phenomenon itself. Camp B says this whole exercise is numerology with a histogram. Their position: empires aren't exchangeable draws from a stable population, they interact with each other, learn, and exist inside a world-system whose technology and norms change faster than the units being averaged; the sample is at best a couple dozen wildly heterogeneous cases spread over five centuries; and the choice of class is doing all the argumentative work, since you can always gerrymander membership until the base rate says what you wanted. They'd rather output mechanisms, conditional scenarios, and explicit claims about what would accelerate or arrest decline, and they think a number with three significant figures on top of that is false precision. Both camps, notably, think the other one is unfalsifiable — Camp A says Camp B never commits to anything you could score, Camp B says Camp A's commitments can only be scored wether the polity in question survives three hundred years, which none of us will see.

Here's what the document needs to contain. First, a numbered list of claims both sides can genuinely sign — about what the historical sample can and can't support, about what a base rate is supposed to do in an inference, about what counts as a forecast that could turn out wrong — and for each one, a short note on what work it's doing later in the document, because I don't want a pile of trivially true sentences that only look like agreement. Second, a disagreements table: each row a precise statement of a difference, with a column giving the wording Camp A would accept for their side and a column giving the wording Camp B would accept for theirs, neither side strawmanned, plus a column marking whether you judge the difference substantive or merely verbal (I strongly suspect "base rate" is being used at least three ways across the table — as a frequency in a class, as a prior, and as an anchoring value in an adjustment procedure — and that a real chunk of the fight is that ambiguity, so say so explicitly and say which chunk). Third, the cruxes: the two or three sub-questions where settling the thing would actually move at least one side. I'd guess candidates include whether a reference class can be specified independently of the outcome it's used to predict, whether adjustments from an anchor should be bounded by rule or left to judgment, whether the Copernican/delta-t fallback is a legitimate limiting case or a reference-class assumption in disguise, and whether non-comparability across eras is a real defeater or a universal excuse — but pick your own if you think these are wrong. For every crux, name a concrete test, case, or shorter-horizon domain both camps would accept as bearing on it (backtests on cities, firms, dynasties, sports dynasties, whatever you can defend), and record each side's prediction about that test in advance, in writing, in a form that could be scored by someone who isn't in this group. Don't adjudicate who's right and don't quietly harmonize a disagreement to make the doc prettier — if one camp's position can't be stated without begging a question against the other, put that in the document as a finding rather than fixing it behind their backs.
