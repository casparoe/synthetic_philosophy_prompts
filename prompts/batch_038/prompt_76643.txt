For the past term I have been convening a small reading group on utilitarianism and its critics, and our final session is devoted to Bernard Williams's two most famous thought experiments — the young chemist, out of work and supporting a family, who is offered a post in a laboratory doing research into biological and chemical warfare and is reminded that if he declines, a less scrupulous colleague will take the job and drive the program harder; and the man who swims out to his drowning wife, of whom Williams observes that if he were to offer the fact that she is his wife as a justification for what he has done, he would have had one thought too many. We always end up talking past each other, because half the group hears the second case as a sentimental celebration of love and the other half hears it as a claim about the structure of deliberation. I would like to close the meeting by handing out a single poem, printed on a half sheet alongside the coffee and readable aloud in under a minute, that makes the structural point rather than the sentimental one.

Here is what I have in mind, with fairly strict specifications, because the constraints are the argument. Sixteen lines, loose iambic pentameter, no rhyme required, though the meter should be regular enough that our resident formalist, who counts stresses, finds the deviations meaningful rather than sloppy. The lines alternate between two registers, typographically distinguished: the odd lines are italicized and belong to the agent-neutral voice that counts, compares, and generalizes, while the even lines are the first-person voice of the man in the water. The impersonal lines should grow longer and more syntactically elaborate as the poem proceeds, so that the reader feels that voice encroaching; the first-person lines must never exceed six words. The even lines are restricted to particulars — cold, strokes, a coat, a hand, a name shouted across water — and are forbidden any abstraction whatsoever: the words reason, duty, principle, value, right, wrong, justification, consequence, and because may not appear in them, and neither may since, therefore, or for. In addition, the even lines must never identify the woman by her relation to the speaker: no my wife, no mine, no us, no hers. That relation may be stated exactly once in the whole poem, and it must fall in the final line, and it must arrive inside a justification. Somewhere around the turn — call it lines nine through twelve — the italicized voice should raise the counterfactual that belongs to the chemist's case without naming it: what would have happened had this man not been on that shore, the insistence that the outcome is what matters and is the same whoever brings it about, the suspicion that weighting one life above a stranger's is a kind of self-indulgence. This voice should be intelligent and humane, not a villain; at least one member of my group is a committed consequentialist and should recognize her own reasoning in the italics as something worth saying.

The last two lines must break the alternation. Line fifteen is the first line in which the italicized syntax appears inside the first-person voice — the two registers occupying one line, unmarked — and line sixteen completes the merger by supplying the poem's only permissible instance of a subordinating justification, so that the reader watches the agent-neutral voice finally get the swimmer to produce a reason, after the rescue is over and no decision remains to be made. Nothing in the poem may name Williams, George, utilitarianism, consequentialism, or integrity; the argument has to be carried by the alternation and its failure, not by labels. After the poem, please add a note of no more than four sentences identifying the merged line, saying in one clause which thought it is, and explaining why the alternation had to fail at precisely that point rather than earlier — this is for me, not for the handout, so that I can steer the discussion if someone reads the ending as a reconciliation rather than as the intrusion it is meant to be.
