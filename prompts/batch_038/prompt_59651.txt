Our institute is building an elicitation instrument to test a substantive empirical claim: that some of the choices people describe as impossible to weigh involve options that are neither better nor worse nor equally good than one another, but *on a par* — the fourth formal relation proposed by Ruth Chang against the trichotomy thesis. The design problem is that parity is observationally easy to confuse with three rival readings of the very same stated-choice data:

1. **Strict incomparability** in the older sense, where no single covering value ranks the two options at all;
2. **Vagueness or indeterminacy in the exchange rate**, where there is a determinate fact but the respondent cannot locate it, and the imprecision is in the scale rather than in the relation;
3. **A determinate ranking the respondent will not report**, because stating it would be disloyal, distasteful, or strategically costly.

Vagueness is our main adversary. The behavioural signature we associate with parity — roughly equal standing, resistance to modest improvements, no cycling — is close enough to the signature of an indeterminate exchange rate that a careless instrument will simply measure respondents' difficulty and call it a metaphysical discovery.

Here are the seven candidate tasks we have drafted. All are administered around a single scenario pair, X and Y, calibrated in advance from pilot work.

- **T1 — Increment threshold.** The respondent chooses between X and a constructed Y. Y is then improved by a series of modest, unambiguously desirable increments (a larger stipend, a shorter commute, an extra year of leave). We record the smallest increment at which Y strictly wins. A long plateau with no flip is the candidate parity signature; a flip at some rung is the candidate signature of a determinate rate.
- **T2 — Sorites framing.** The same increment series, but instead of choosing, the respondent classifies each rung as Y better, X better, cannot say, or exactly equal, and rates confidence in the classification. We examine whether the cannot-say region and the exactly-equal region overlap or occupy disjoint stretches of the series.
- **T3 — Forced trichotomy.** The comparison is offered with three response options only, and no escape route. We measure how often respondents reject the frame — requesting a fourth option, refusing, or protesting that the question is malformed — and what they say when they do.
- **T4 — Covering-standard task.** The respondent first states the standard by which X and Y are to be assessed, then judges the pair under that standard and under one or two alternative standards they themselves supply. If the standoff dissolves under one standard and returns under another, that is informative about whether a covering value was ever in play.
- **T5 — Cycle probe.** A sequential design: X versus Y, then improved Y versus X, then improved X versus improved Y, each switch carrying a small stated cost. We look for revealed cycles, and, where they occur, whether the respondent notices them and whether they endorse them on reflection.
- **T6 — Re-run and regret.** After the respondent commits to X, we ask whether they would make the same choice in an identical re-run, whether a residual sense of loss about Y persists, and whether that loss is experienced as evidence of a mistake or as fully compatible with having chosen well. On the parity picture, commitment is supposed to generate fresh reasons, so divergence-without-regret is the target pattern.
- **T7 — Aggregate substitutability.** A scaling question at population level: how many units of value V would offset the loss of a fixed quantity of value W, asked at several magnitudes. We are testing whether the trade-off rate is stable, diverges, or hits a hard lexical floor.

What I need is an assessment of these as instruments, not a defence of the view they are meant to detect. Please do the following:

1. Rank all seven by diagnostic power for distinguishing parity from rival reading (2), vagueness, and explain in a sentence for each why it lands where it does.
2. Rate each task on a 0–10 scale for that specific purpose, where 10 means the task would produce markedly different data under parity and under vagueness, and 0 means the data would look identical either way.
3. For each task, name the single most damaging confound — the feature that would let a sceptic explain the results without invoking parity at all.
4. Items 1–3 should be consolidated into one table with columns: task, rank, score, principal confound.
5. We have a twenty-minute slot and cannot run everything. Say which two tasks to cut, and why the loss is tolerable given what the remaining five still identify.
6. Identify the load-bearing task — the one whose failure would most threaten the whole design — and state what would have to be true of respondents, and of the X/Y pair, for it to work as intended.
7. Finally, tell us whether the instrument as a whole can in fact separate parity from vagueness, or whether it can at best establish that some respondents behave *as if* the trichotomy fails. Be blunt about which of the two achievements is within reach.
