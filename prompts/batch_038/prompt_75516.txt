Spent 40-odd years in a condensed matter lab, retired a couple years back, and promptly fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole — mostly the stuff about how words and theories latch onto the world. Alongside that I sit on a small committee at our hospital trust that signs off on proposals to put automated scoring tools into clinical use. Three or four a month, 45 minutes each, and nobody on the committee has any training in formal semantics.

What keeps me up is a version of the old worry. The tool only ever sees a fixed bundle of measurements, but the thing we say it's tracking — frailty, deterioration, risk of neglect — may not be recoverable from that bundle at all, and its not obvious the categories in the data carve the world the way the proposal assumes they do. Quine's field linguist and the rabbit, the causal theory of reference, operational definitions: seems to me they're all circling the same problem, and I don't think the philosophers have a fix, only a diagnosis.

Can you turn the diagnosis into a procedure my committee could actually run? Roughly: for each term the system is supposed to be aiming at, what do we ask about whether its inputs could ever distinguish that thing from some other thing equally consistent with them; what counts as a good answer; and what we do when there isn't one — reject, demand another data source, restate the target in observable terms, or park a human in the loop. I want it explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about where the procedure simply can't decide anything and we're on our own.

Then run it on two cases for me: a fall-risk score that's supposed to track frailty in elderly admissions, and a sepsis alert that's supposed to track "this patient is deteriorating." And end with one case where your procedure would pass something I think should be failed — I'd rather hear that from you now than from a coroner later.
