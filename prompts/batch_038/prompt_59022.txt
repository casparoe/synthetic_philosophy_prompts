Write a dialogue between a strict conditionalizer, who holds that all rational learning is conditionalization on propositions one becomes certain of, and a Jeffrey conditionalizer, who holds that perceptual learning typically shifts probabilities without ever delivering certainty. Anchor the dispute in one running case: an ornithologist in failing light takes what looks like a green finch and moves her credences over the partition {green finch, blue jay, something else} from (.10, .10, .80) to (.70, .20, .10), becoming certain of nothing.

Let the strict conditionalizer open with the familiar reply — that her real evidence is a proposition about the fine-grained character of her visual experience, of which she can be certain, so that the Jeffrey shift is only the shadow of an ordinary conditionalization — and let the Jeffrey conditionalizer press three objections in turn: that rigidity fails in this very case, because the sighting also bears on her beliefs about which species are plausible in that habitat; that the rule says nothing about where the new partition probabilities come from, so a merely causal perceptual process is being passed off as a reason; and that successive Jeffrey shifts on different partitions need not commute, whereas conditionalization is order-independent.

I want the best available answer to each objection rather than concessions arranged for dramatic effect. In particular, the strict conditionalizer should have a substantive reply to the charge that any update whatsoever can be rationalized as conditionalization on some sufficiently baroque phenomenal proposition, which would leave her view with no normative content at all. End by having them agree on what the dispute actually turns on, and on whether it is substantive or merely verbal. Please argue from the structure of the case alone: do not attribute these moves to named authors or quote from the literature.
