I'm a TA for a third-year course that's cross-listed between archaeology and philosophy — roughly "deep time and the good life," the part where we spend four weeks on the Neolithic transition and then argue about whether it was good for anybody. The prof has asked each TA to contribute one practice essay question for the revision session, with a model answer and a marking scheme, and I drew the agriculture week. My problem is that every single student answer I've marked so far is the same essay: one paragraph of Sahlins's original affluent society stuff, one paragraph of the Diamond "worst mistake" line, a sentence about inequality, and a shrug in the conclusion. They've clearly got the labels down and no idea what the actual inferential work is. So I want a question that can't be answered that way, and I want it to be self-contained: closed book except for the handout, 50 minutes, no bibliography, nothing that depends on remembering who published what. To make that work I've invented a regional sequence with a made-up data table (the valley and the numbers are mine, modelled loosely on the shape of real published sequences, so nobody needs outside knowledge). Here's what I plan to hand them:

**The Tarn-Wadi sequence (fictional).** Three periods from one river valley in southwest Asia.

| | Period 1 (foragers) | Period 2 (early farmers) | Period 3 (established farmers) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean adult stature | 168 cm | 161 cm | 159 cm |
| Enamel hypoplasia ( % of adults) | 12% | 41% | 28% |
| Caries (% of teeth) | 2% | 9% | 14% |
| Periosteal lesions on long bones | rare | 18% | 31% |
| Infant/juvenile share of cemetery deaths | 30% | 38% | 33% |
| Estimated regional population | 800 | 6,000 | 14,000 |
| Settlements / largest settlement | 3 sites / ~150 people | 22 sites / ~2,000 | 45 sites / ~4,000 |
| Gini coefficient of house floor area | 0.15 | 0.38 | 0.52 |

Plus three footnotes I'd print under the table: (a) all the skeletal data come from formal cemeteries, and infants are badly under-represented in Period 1 because of burial custom rather than demography; (b) the Period 2 cemetery was in use for maybe two or three generations and includes one mass grave of 40 individuals; (c) the population figures are back-calculated from a carrying-capacity model, not counted.

The question itself I want to be something like: *On the basis of this table alone, is it true that the adoption of farming made the inhabitants of this valley worse off? Say precisely what "worse off" has to mean for the claim to come out true, what it has to mean for it to come out false, and which reading the evidence in the table actually supports — and then say whether anything in this valley was a mistake, given that the footnotes suggest nobody chose the transition and nobody could have reversed it.* I'd also require them to use the fact that Period 3 is not just "Period 2 continued": hypoplasia falls, periosteal lesions and caries keep climbing, stature keeps falling, and inequality keeps widening. Something has to explain that non-monotonic pattern and I want to see whether they notice that the explanation they give constrains how they read Period 2.

So what I need from you is the whole package. A clean statement of the question, tidied up from my messy version above. Then a model answer of about 700 words — the answer a genuinely strong student writes in 50 minutes, not a survey of the literature. Then a marking scheme out of 25 that separates an A from a B by naming the specific moves rather than vibes: roughly, does the answer see that lesion frequency in a cemetery sample is not a direct measure of health because of survivorship and frailty effects (the students who survive a stress episode long enough to be buried are the ones who get counted, so a lesion-riddled sample can be the healthier one); does it see that the population column and the welfare column are pulling in different directions and that "worse off" needs to be pinned to a person, a cohort, or an average before the table means anything; does it notice that footnote (c) makes the population numbers themselves theory-loaded, so the "there were more of them and they were sadder" story leans on a model; does it handle the Period 3 non-monotonicity instead of flattening it; and does it say something non-empty about the word "mistake," i.e. whether a harm nobody chose, that was locked in by population growth already underway, is still a harm *to* someone and whether the counterfactual (a valley that stayed at 800 foragers) is even available. For each must-hit move give the marks it's worth and one line on how students typically blow it. Then a short list of the specific wrong turns that should cost marks — e.g. treating "more people" as evidence of better lives without saying for whom, reading the infant-death share as infant mortality straight off despite footnote (a), assuming foragers decided to farm or that early farmers could have gone back, sliding between average and distribution when the Gini row is right there, and the shrug-in-the-conclusion move where they say "it's complicated, both readings have merit" without having done the work that would make one reading better. I want the scheme to make that shrug an automatic cap at, like, a C. One more thing: keep the model answer free of names and dates too. If the good answer can only be written by someone who remembers which archaeologist said what, I've written the wrong question.
