I've read Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" (Journal of Philosophy, 1982) on and off ever since it kept circulating on the rationalist forums I used to haunt, and the reading group I run — mostly ex-forum regulars, now tolerably well read in fiction — is taking on George Eliot's Middlemarch next season. I want a reading of the novel through Wolf's essay, and past it.

Wolf's argument, as I take it: moral saintliness is not an attractive ideal of human character. She divides the type into the Loving Saint, whose own interests are simply absorbed into the happiness of others, and the Rational Saint, who still has interests and desires but subordinates them to a recognition of what morality requires. In both cases the non-moral goods of a life — humor, taste, personal projects, the cultivation of a talent, the particular attachments that make someone worth being around — are crowded out, and the result, on Wolf's own account, is someone dull. Her positive claim is that a well-rounded life needs a non-moral ideal to organize it, and that morality is neither the sole nor the overriding value.

Here is the complication I cannot shake. Dorothea Brooke looks like a Loving Saint in the early books and something closer to a Rational Saint after Rome, yet Eliot insists on making her luminous, ardent, and occasionally ridiculous in a way Wolf's type is supposed to be incapable of. Either Wolf's phenomenology is doing quiet work — smuggling in a modern, time-poor picture of the saint as someone optimizing global welfare — or Eliot is idealizing a character whose actual life the novel shows to be a sequence of thwarted and misdirected efforts. I would like you to treat the novel as a test case rather than an illustration, and to work through at least the following.

First, where the two accounts genuinely conflict. Wolf's saints are charged with having no self, no non-moral goods, and no particular attachments; Dorothea, from the prologue's comparison of her to Teresa of Ávila onward, seems to have all three in abundance. Which of Wolf's specific charges does the novel actually meet, and which does it simply refuse to engage?

Second, a third type that Wolf's binary seems to miss and that I think Eliot does dramatize. Call it the Programmatic Saint: someone whose moral life is organized as a project with an explicit theory of change — a target population, a mechanism, a plan for scaling it, and a vocation chosen for leverage rather than for love or duty. Dorothea's scheme to rebuild the cottages on the Tipton estate is this, and so, in a stranger form, is her decision to marry Casaubon: she takes him on as an instrument by which her usefulness will be multiplied through the Key to all Mythologies. The failure mode of this type is not excess of love or excess of duty but excess of plan. Does Eliot diagnose that failure mode as such, or does she attribute the failure to the constraints of a provincial society that gives a woman no legitimate outlet at all?

Third, Lydgate as the control case. He is close to the non-moral ideal Wolf recommends: a life organized around a scientific project rather than around beneficence. Does his trajectory — Rosamond, the debts, the entanglement with Bulstrode, the abandonment of research for a comfortable London practice — vindicate or refute Wolf's claim that a non-moral ideal is the better model? Rosamond, in turn, is nearly a pure case of a life given over to non-moral goods. What does the novel seem to think that life amounts to?

Fourth, the Finale. Without quoting it, characterize its argument: the closing claim that the growing good of the world depends in part on acts history never records, and that things are not so ill with us as they might have been because of the number who lived faithfully a hidden life. Read as philosophy, that looks like a claim about legibility and attribution rather than about character — the effects of a moral life are real but unmeasurable, unattributable, and largely invisible even to the agent. Is that an answer to Wolf, who is complaining about what such a life is like to live rather than about whether it registers in any record? Or does it change the subject? There is also a reading on which Eliot denies that moral impact can be assessed from the outside at all, which would be a reply to the entire apparatus of measuring one's own effect on the world — I want to know whether you think the novel supports that, or whether I am importing it.

Fifth, where the reading breaks. Please be blunt about the places where Middlemarch refuses to serve as a case study. Eliot's moral seriousness is inseparable from her realism and her narrator's irony; and Dorothea's thwarted sainthood is also a critique of an opportunity set, which is a point about social possibility rather than about ideals of character, and one Wolf's essay has little purchase on. Say plainly where I am overfitting.

Write this as criticism, not as summary, and assume the group has read both the novel and the essay; paraphrase rather than quote either one. I want faithfulness in both directions: no flattening the novel into a thought experiment, and no bending Wolf's argument until the novel comes out right. Then end by saying what Middlemarch contributes to the moral-saint debate that argument alone could not supply — some feature of the ideal that only the narrative of a whole life, with its misdirections and its unrecorded effects, can reveal.

Close with a single final line beginning "Verdict:" and containing exactly one of these labels: Dorothea is a Wolf-style moral saint and Eliot refutes Wolf; Dorothea is a Wolf-style moral saint and Wolf's charges survive the novel; Dorothea is a Programmatic Saint, a type Wolf's essay misses; Dorothea is not a moral saint and the novel is not about that; or the comparison is a type error and the novel is not a test case for Wolf.
