I'm in year three of an epistemology PhD and our reading group has ground to a halt on norms of assertion, mostly because my advisor keeps reaching for one analogy and half the room thinks it's doing all the work.

Here's how he runs it. Asserting that p is like promising that p: in both cases you give your word, you make yourself answerable, and your audience is entitled to rely on you. And just as you shouldn't promise to do something unless you know you can do it, you shouldn't assert p unless you know p. He leans on it for a second claim too, which I think matters more: that the knowledge norm isnt a moral rule bolted on from outside to an activity that's already fully constituted, but is internal to what asserting even is. A classmate says the analogy is smuggling exactly that second claim in, and that if you run it honestly you land somewhere else.

Can you put on your expert-on-the-epistemology-of-assertion hat and audit this thing properly? Brainstorm a bit first, out loud, before you commit to a verdict — I'd rather see you generating candidate disanalogies and then culling them than a tidy list off the top of your head.

What I want:

2. Sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually threaten the inference and the ones that are cosmetic. I'm thinking of things like: promises concern your own future actions while assertions concern how things are independently of you; a promisee can release you from a promise but no audience member can release you from a norm of assertion. Is there a version of the promissory parallel that resists this, or is he right?
