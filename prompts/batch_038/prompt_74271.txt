I'm a senior in a history and philosophy of science program, and this term's seminar has just spent three weeks on scientific revolutions. Our last session was supposed to be a wrap-up discussion of incommensurability and instead it collapsed into a standoff between two of my classmates, which I've been trying to think my way out of ever since. One of them argued that the whole doctrine reduces to the trivial observation that different theories use their shared vocabulary in different ways, which hardly anyone denies and which does no philosophical work; the other replied that if it is trivial then it is not the thesis anyone was worried about, since the interesting claim is precisely that the rival camps cannot line up their theories statement against statement and settle the dispute by appeal to some neutral description of what everyone can see. Our TA tried to mediate with the taxonomy example, the one about how the count of the wanderers in the sky goes from seven to six across the Copernican change — the earth gets sorted in, the sun gets sorted out — without anyone having made a fresh observation of the sun. I found that example genuinely illuminating, and I have been trying to explain it to my roommate, who is a classics major with no patience for jargon and who keeps asking why two astronomers looking at the same sky on the same night cannot simply agree on what they are seeing. Since plain exposition has not been working on him, I want to try something else.

Could you write me a villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, iambic pentameter, only two rhyme sounds throughout — that enacts the example rather than describing it? The two refrains should do the philosophical work. Let the first refrain be a line of naked-eye description that an astronomer on either side of the change would happily sign his name to, something like Mars halting and turning back along its path, a report that survives the revolution with its wording untouched. Let the second refrain be a line that states how many wanderers there are, worded so that it can return verbatim every time and so that its truth nevertheless depends on which sorting of the heavens is in force. Then let each intervening stanza quietly re-sort what counts as a wanderer, so that the second refrain comes back with identical words and a changed extension, and no line of the poem ever announces that a redefinition has occurred. The final couplet should land the two refrains side by side, and the reader should feel that their meeting is not an agreement: the words rhyme, the sentences coincide, and the two men are still not saying the same thing. I would rather you keep the rhymes true than settle for near-rhymes, since the whole point is that the identity that holds across the change is exact at the surface and only the sense has moved; if the meter ever forces a claim about the astronomy that is simply false, let the meter give way instead.

Please keep the poem free of any of the cheap versions of the thesis. It should not suggest that the two astronomers inhabit incommunicable private worlds or that they cannot talk to each other at all — the interesting claim is that the younger one can learn the older lexicon the way one learns a second language, and can then say what the older one meant, without there being any neutral vocabulary to translate both into. Nor should it end in a shrug about the choice being arbitrary: both sides appeal to the same standing virtues of a theory — how well it handles what it was built to handle, how much it takes in, how economical and how fruitful it is — and the poem should register that these shared standards are what makes the argument possible and also what fails to decide it. I would rather not have you quote Kuhn or anyone else; I want the idea carried entirely by the verse. After the poem, add three or four sentences of plain prose: name the word whose extension the villanelle re-sorts, say which of my two classmates' readings the form is arguing against, and point out honestly where the fixed form strained against the content rather than serving it.
