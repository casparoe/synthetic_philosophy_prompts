I'm about eight months into a novel whose whole third act depends on a character who can't decide, so I've been collecting verdicts — mine, my partner's, a couple of friends who do philosophy for a living — and my notes are a total mess. What I want is the principle behind them, if there is one, stated plainly enough that I could put it in a character's mouth or, failing that, cross it out. I don't want the history of the problem, I already know the bit everyone pins on Buridan about the ass between two piles of hay. I want to know whether my intuitions cohere, because right now I'm writing scenes against a rule I can't state.

Here's the pile. Two identical glasses of water on a tray, same distance from from my hand: I take one, no agonizing, and I'd call that a perfectly ordinary rational choice, not a lapse. Two flavors at an ice cream counter I've never tried and can't tell apart from the descriptions: also fine, and if someone asked why I picked pistachio I'd happily say there was no reason, I just did. Now the case that matters. Elise has been offered two jobs in two cities and she has exhaustively convinced herself they're identical on every axis she actually cares about — same money, same work, same distance from her sister, she ran the spreadsheet twice. I think she's stuck, and I think her being stuck is tragic rather than ridiculous, which is exactly the opposite of how the water glasses feel, and structurally I cannot tell you what's different. Second problem: the coin flip. If Elise flips a coin to break the job tie I think that's legitimate, even a little moving — she's handing it to chance on purpose. If she flips a coin to choose between two equally qualified applicants for a role she's paid to fill, I think that's a dereliction. Same tie, same randomness, opposite verdicts, no account. And then a third category I don't have a name for: suppose one of the two job offers does have a slightly better long-run trajectory and nobody on earth can tell which — here I don't think Elise is stuck at all, and I also don't think she can be faulted for picking either one, so she's neither paralyzed nor choosing-for-no-reason, she's somewhere else entirely. Related: a hung jury at six-six feels like a legitimate outcome to me, while the jurors each privately thinking "it's a tie, I'll go with the others" feels like a failure; two identical hotel rooms and a keycard that opens either is fine; two identical doors in a story where I want the reader to feel dread is something I'm cheating at and I know it.

So: give me one or two candidate principles, each clause tied to the case that forced it, precise enough to argue with. Then tell me what they say about cases I haven't hit yet — does a tie between two options one of which is irreversible differ in kind from a tie between two reversible ones, can an agent have a reason to hesitate that isn't a reason to prefer, and is a tie broken by a whim any different from a tie broken by a coin? Then be honest about the cost. If saving every verdict above needs something baroque, say so and pick the simplest principle that does most of the work and tell me which of my verdicts it makes me give up, because I'd rather have a clean priniciple and rewrite two chapters than the reverse. And if there's any real distinction in kind available between the haystack case and the job case — something better than "one is about hay and one is about a life" — I'd like it, because that's the whole book.
