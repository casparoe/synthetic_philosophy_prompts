**The context.** There is a long-running argument in the rationalist-adjacent forums I read that runs roughly as follows. An optimizer — a hill-climber, a Darwinian process, a planning agent, a learning algorithm — is a search procedure moving across a landscape in Sewall Wright's sense: a space of candidate solutions with a value attached to each point. The no-free-lunch theorems say that, averaged over every possible assignment of values to points, no search procedure beats blind uniform sampling. Therefore any optimizer that does well does well only because the landscape was already arranged to suit it, and its apparent success is borrowed structure smuggled in from outside the algorithm. Intelligence, on this telling, never creates information about where the good points are; it can only shuffle that information around. So a system that seemed to *find* its own target would be collecting a free lunch the theorems forbid.

The lineage is acknowledged in the thread: this is the same move Dembski made against Darwinism in the early 2000s, arguing that the no-free-lunch results show specified complexity cannot arise without an intelligent source — a treatment that one of the theorem's own authors reportedly dismissed as mush. The thread holds that the Darwinian application was refuted while the general move went untouched, and now redeploys it against machine learning: since a general optimizer cannot manufacture a prior out of nothing, the terminal aims of a sufficiently capable system must be installed by hand, and any proposal on which the system discovers or justifies its own values is asking for the forbidden lunch.
