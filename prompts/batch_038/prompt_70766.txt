Our reading group — six of us, two philosophy graduate students and four clinicians — has been working through a recent trade book that yokes the Buddhist doctrine of non-self to the contemporary debate about whether death can be bad for the person who dies. The book's engine is an analogy borrowed from the Milindapañha, the Pali dialogue between the monk Nāgasena and the Indo-Greek king Milinda, and it deploys it in roughly the following steps, which I have reconstructed from three separate chapters: (1) When the king is walked through the components of his chariot — the wheels, axle, pole, yoke, frame, reins — he cannot locate any chariot over and above them, and is brought to agree that chariot is a mere name, applied in dependence on the parts, picking out no further item. (2) Exactly the same goes for a person: walked through form, feeling, perception, volitional formations, and consciousness, one finds no self over and above them, and the word I is likewise a mere designation applied in dependence on the aggregates. (3) So the difference between a person and a chariot is not a difference in ontological kind but only in the complexity of the parts and in the fact that we happen to care about one of the two. (4) When a chariot is broken up for firewood nothing is destroyed but an arrangement; the name simply stops applying; and since a person is a designation over an arrangement in the same way, when a person dies nothing is destroyed either. (5) Harm requires a subject to whom it accrues, so if there is no such subject — only aggregates arising and ceasing, gathered under a convenient label — death is not bad for anyone, and the fear of death is a grammatical illusion, a mistake of a useful name for a thing. (6) The payoff is therapeutic: seeing this dissolves the dread of death without needing any further argument about deprivation, comparison, or the value of future experience.

Half the group thinks (4) and (5) are a sleight of hand; the other half thinks the objection is squeamishness dressed as metaphysics; one member insists the whole thing is simply Hume's bundle account of the mind in Pali clothing, which may or may not be a point in its favor. I am less interested in adjudicating the book's ethics than in auditing the analogy that carries them, because I suspect it is doing work that nobody has costed. So please do the following. First, give me the correspondence table explicitly — what in the chariot case answers to what in the person case, and in particular what is supposed to play the role of the naming relation, the arrangement, and the taking-apart. Second, separate the disanalogies that actually bear on steps (4) and (5) from the ones that are merely picturesque, and say which are which. Third, identify the features of chariots that the analogy quietly imports and that persons arguably lack — being an artifact, having been assembled by someone for a purpose, having interchangeable and reassemblable parts, having a history that is external to it, and above all not being the sort of thing anything is like to be — and then say whether the argument survives once each of those is withdrawn. Fourth, examine whether the phrase in the same way in (3) can bear its weight: the chariot demonstration is synchronic, about parts arranged at a moment, whereas the analysis of the aggregates is momentary and thoroughly diachronic, so ask what the name is supposed to depend on in each case and whether those are the same relation. Fifth, say whether (5) requires resources the analogy does not supply — in particular the distinction between conventional and ultimate truth, and what becomes of the book's repeated claim that suffering is real while the sufferer is merely conventional if persons are unreal in the way chariots are unreal, since the audience being consoled and the teacher doing the consoling would then be unreal in the same way. Sixth, tell me whether a different comparison from the same tradition would do better or worse here: I have seen the flame passed from one lamp to another used for continuity without identity, and the river used for impermanence, and I want to know what each of those can and cannot carry in this argument. Finally, name the single step at which the analogy is doing the most unearned work, and state what the author would have to add to earn it.

Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — whether the conclusion at (5) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it does not, which step is responsible — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my question, and no general remarks about what analogical argument is. Tables are welcome for the correspondence and for the ranking of disanalogies; prose for the diagnosis.
