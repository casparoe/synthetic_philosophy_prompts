I'm co-teaching a second-year analytic metaphysics seminar next month — 18 students, 75 minutes, and the room is cursed: bolted tables in rows of two, so they can only really work in pairs, plus a document camera at the front. They've done Frege on sense and reference and one week on propositions as the things that are true or false, but nobody's read any idealists and I'd rather keep it that way for as long a possible. What I want is an activity where they run into Bradley's regress with their own hands before anyone says the word Bradley, and in particular where they feel the difference between a proposition and a mere list — that thing where "Socrates, wisdom" is just two items lying on the table but "Socrates is wise" is supposed to be one thing that can be true.

Here's the germ I've been scribbling on, feel free to overhaul it: I print up three kinds of card — object cards, property/relation cards, and a tie card that you're only permitted to use to join two other cards. Pairs have to build a construction that survives a challenge round, where the other pair scores a token if they can either (a) take your construction apart into a shopping list of free-standing items with nothing left over that makes them one thing, or (b) demand to be shown the card that ties your tie to what it ties, and you can't produce one without starting a new tie. Tokens for surviving, no real money, I have about £8 and a working printer. The wrinkle I care about most: the regress only bites if you grant that every act of combining needs a combiner, so I'd like at least one pair — by luck or by design — to stumble into refusing that premise, e.g. treating the tie as something you don't write down at all, or as internal to the terms, or as a gap in the predicate that gets filled rather than glued, and I want the rules themselves to have some way of registering that move rather than me standing up and announcing it's allowed. My advisor thinks the whole argument is a cheat for exactly that reason, and I'd like the class to end up in a position to argue with him instead of with me.

So: full rules, what's actually on each card, the token scheme, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes including setup and teardown, and a debrief that maps specific moments — the moment someone realises they need a card for the card, the moment a pair just refuses and the rest of the room thinks they've cheated — onto the parts of the argument: the tie, the demand for further ties, the assumption that generates the demand, and the available escape routes plus what each one costs. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with 18 people in fixed pairs, and be straight with me about which feature of the regress a card game can't reproduce and that I'm going to have to supply in words afterwards. If there's a fallback version that needs only the whiteboard and whatever index cards they happen to have in their bags, sketch that too.
