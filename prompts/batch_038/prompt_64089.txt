I am at work on a novel whose central figure is a provincial administrator in a patrimonial system: he distributes posts, contracts, and wedding gifts, and everyone in his district regards him as an honest man. To keep myself from writing a caricature I assembled the following argument, and it now appears to prove considerably more than I wanted.

(1) Corruption is the use of a public office for private benefit, and what counts as private benefit is fixed by what the office's constitutive norms require its holder to treat as belonging to the office rather than to himself.
(2) Those norms are whatever the community sustaining the office takes them to be, and there is no standpoint outside a community's role-norms from which to enumerate them.
(3) In the district of my novel, the norms constitutive of the office oblige the holder to provide for kin and clients; failure to do so is what the office itself condemns.
(4) Therefore his gifts, appointments, and contracts are not private benefit but the discharge of the office, and so are not corrupt.
(5) The same reasoning extends to every role whose constitutive norms include reciprocal obligation — a department chair, a journal editor, a parent, an author's decisions about which characters to reward.
(6) Hence corruption is either an empty charge or a term that bites only in communities that have adopted a norm of impersonal office-holding, which most communities never have.
(7) Therefore no cross-cultural standard exists by which clientelism can be criticized, and the universality claimed for impersonal-office standards is false.

I wanted (6). What I did not want is (7) as I now read it: it seems to leave the clients themselves without standing to object to their patron, and to permit any administration to immunize itself by rewriting its own job descriptions. I am most attached to (1) and (3), and I would give up (5) without regret.

Which step fails, or which premise is cheapest to surrender? And what does the surrender cost me — state the price in terms of the claims about the world of the book that I can no longer make, not in terms of which philosophical camp I end up in.
