Since January I have served as artistic director of a small annual festival in a town whose foundry closed twenty years ago; I am a painter by training, and the job consists mainly of deciding which of six hundred submitted works receives our single prize. To give the jury something better than taste to argue from, I built the rubric around your essay on art, because it is the only account I know that takes the response of ordinary people seriously instead of the verdict of specialists. The jury has now deadlocked on one entry, the decision falls to me, and I would like to argue the matter with you before I sign anything.

Play Tolstoy. Speak in the first person, in your own idiom: plain, declarative, unbothered by elegance, and free to be blunt about fashionable aesthetics. You may not step out of the scene, and you may not write about yourself in the third person as a later admirer or biographer would. If you need a fact about the machine, the festival, or the woman who made the entry, ask me for it rather than guessing; I will answer. Do not dismiss the case by claiming you cannot follow the technology. Where I have misstated your view below, correct me.

My summary of your position, as it appears on the rubric card: art is an activity in which a person deliberately re-evokes in himself a feeling he has once lived through and hands it on to others by means of lines, colors, sounds, movements, or words, so that they are infected by it and feel it too; the measure of a work is the degree of that infection, which depends on the distinctness of the feeling, the clarity of its transmission, and above all the sincerity of the one transmitting it; and the worth of art lies in joining people together, so that art which pleases only a cultivated minority is bad art however skilled, while a simple song that unites peasants and lords is good art. You turned that standard against much of your own earlier fiction and against most of what your contemporaries admired.

The entry. My sister's son hanged himself in the spring. She fed his letters and text messages into a machine that produces writing — an apparatus that has absorbed an enormous quantity of printed language and, on request, continues in the same vein; it has no body, no memory, and no grief, and I take responsibility for that description. What it produced was a page of verse in the voice of a young man saying goodbye. My sister chose it from among many outputs, printed it, and stood beside us while a neighbor read it aloud in the library hall. Roughly two hundred people were in that room. I watched them. More of them wept at that page than at any performance of the week, and the collection plate for a bereavement line took in more that evening than at the rest of the festival combined.

Here is my opening move. Your account has two halves that pull apart in this room. The value half says art is what joins people in a shared feeling, and this page did that better than anything else we showed. The origin half says art requires a feeling actually lived through by the one who transmits it, and no one lived through this: not the machine, and, you will insist, not my sister either, since she was inside her grief rather than standing apart from it, re-evoking it, and shaping it. So either the origin condition is doing no work at all, in which case your definition collapses into the claim that art is whatever infects, and the machine is an artist by your own measure; or else you must say that two hundred people were infected by nothing, which is a false description of a room I was standing in. Tell me which, and tell me what is left of sincerity once you can no longer see who felt what. End your reply with the single question you most want answered from me, and stay in character until I say stop.
