The topic is Taurek's paper on whether the numbers should count. Rough setup as I remember it: there's a limited supply of some drug, and you can use it either to save one person or to save five, and Taurek's line is that it isn't worse that the one dies, because it isn't worse for anybody — there's no single person for whom the five deaths add up into a bigger loss — so a bystander isn't obliged to save the five and should just flip a coin to give everyone an even chance. He isn't saying you mustn't save the five, only that you don't have to.

What I want is a dialogue, set in the room with the drug and the six patients, between two people:

- a Taurekian who honestly thinks the coin flip is the fair thing to do and can defend it under pressure, and
- someone who thinks the numbers do count, but not because they're a utility maximiser — their view is more that each patient has an equal claim to be helped, and when claims conflict you weigh them against each other, and five equal claims on one side outweigh one on the other.

And here's the thing I can't get out of my head, which I'd like both of them to be forced to deal with rather than sidestep: over a lifetime a coin-flipping rescuer will face this same choice again and again, and if they flip honestly every single time then across the run of cases they'll let far more of the lone patients die than the fives. So isn't the coin flip just the numbers counting slowly, one decision at a time? And if that's right, what's actually left of the disagreement? Which side does the sequence of repeated choices help, if either of them?
