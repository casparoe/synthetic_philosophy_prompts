I'm in a second-year seminar on minds, machines, and the philosophy of biology, and this week we worked through the typogenetics material in Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* — the toy genetics of four-letter strands and the enzymes whose structure a strand determines. One of my classmates posted a recap of the argument on our course forum, and because I'm drafting a short essay out of that discussion, I'd like to know how much of the recap I can safely build on. Here it is:

"Hofstadter's typogenetics strips genetics down to a toy: strings built from four bases, together with enzymes whose shape is fixed by the duplets of one particular string, so that a string can encode the very enzyme that binds to it and copies it. That is self-replication in miniature, and it is less an illustration of Gödel's theorem than the same theorem wearing a biological disguise. Because a strand is at once an object acted upon and a description of the actor, the system has what it needs to encode claims about itself — and any system rich enough to do that contains claims it cannot itself establish. That is just the diagonal argument, so the cell turns out to be formally incomplete: there are facts about a strand that no strand, however long, could encode. The upshot for minds is the same. Consciousness is this trick of a system turning back on itself, and since self-description outruns any fixed set of rules, the anti-mechanists were right that no program can be a self. Hofstadter's version has one advantage over the logician's version: it shows the point in wet, concrete machinery, where the choice of interpretation is not up for grabs."

My own instinct is that the closing sentences are not Hofstadter's position at all — he seems to me to stand on the other side of the machine question from the people who argue that minds transcend mechanism — but I can't tell whether the trouble starts earlier, because the biological and the logical vocabulary are each doing work I only partly follow.
