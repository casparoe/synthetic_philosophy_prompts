I direct integrated pest management for a mid-sized public university: four residence halls, a dining commons, a historic library annex with a paper-and-textile collection, two research greenhouses, a seed-storage facility, and grounds that include a wetland the facilities office keeps asking me to treat for mosquitoes. Three or four control proposals reach my desk each week, running from ants in a dormitory kitchen to a wasp nest beside the outdoor seating to a beetle infestation in the seed store. Our animal-care committee's remit stops at vertebrates, so nothing here is reviewed by anyone and nobody has ever required us to justify a treatment; I would nonetheless like to be able to justify ours, and I have been attempting an internal standard for two years without success. Last weekend I argued about this with a friend until two in the morning — he is an economist by training, sharp, and entirely without patience for sentiment — and that argument is the reason I am writing to you rather than drafting another version myself.

The disagreement concerns what such a standard should be counting. My draft tells the technicians to estimate how many insects a treatment will kill, to prefer the option that kills fewer, and, where the numbers are close, the option that kills faster. He says the rule is incoherent on its own terms: killing fewer insects is better only if those insects would otherwise have gone on living, and for a population breeding inside a grain store or a wall void that is far from obvious — an untreated nest is replaced within a season, or crashes on its own, or its members die within weeks regardless of what we do. On his account the only quantity that matters is expected suffering averted net of expected suffering left in place, and measured that way a prompt fumigation of the seed store may beat doing nothing, and may also beat the slow-acting growth regulator I currently favour, since a regulator leaves larvae alive and failing to moult for weeks. I had no answer. What I keep reaching for is a distinction I cannot state cleanly: between insects we kill who would have lived and insects we kill who were going to die anyway, and between suffering we cause and suffering we merely decline to prevent. He calls that squeamishness wearing an institutional blazer. I think he is wrong, but I do not know whether the distinction can be made to carry any weight, and I would rather learn that from you than from him. Note also where the evidence sits, since my friend uses it as a weapon: recent British sentience legislation recognised cephalopods and decapod crustaceans and left insects out altogether, so we are neither in the clear nor in a settled position.

What I want from you is the procedure itself — something a technician with entomological training and no philosophy could run on a proposal in about twenty minutes, producing a one-page justification I could hand to a student newspaper without embarrassment. It should give the questions in the order they are to be asked, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify the points at which it terminates in do-not-treat-exclude-and-monitor, and identify the points at which it stops being a procedure and hands the decision to me because what remains is a question of value rather than fact. It must tell me explicitly what to do about uncertainty over whether insects are sentient at all — whether to discount harm by the probability of sentience, to treat sentience as a precautionary threshold that switches on once some bar is crossed, or something else — and what changes in the procedure if the honest answer is that they are not. It must rank our actual options on suffering grounds and not only on numbers killed, and I want the awkward ones confronted rather than skipped: residual pyrethroid sprays with fast knockdown; gel baits, which are targeted but slow and get eaten by spiders and shrews; growth regulators; phosphine fumigation, which kills everything in the room; nitrogen controlled-atmosphere treatment; freezing and heat; diatomaceous earth, which desiccates over days; pheromone traps, which kill slowly across a week; and CO₂ anoxia, where we do not know whether the gas is aversive before responsiveness is lost. Then there is the release of parasitoid wasps in the greenhouses, which is how I pay other insects to conduct slow, distributed killing on my behalf — I genuinely do not know where that goes in a framework built around our own acts, and my friend would say that difficulty proves the framework is about my feelings. Two further things the procedure has to absorb. First, what I can only call the attractor problem: we built the seed store, the greenhouses, the ornamental plantings along the wetland edge, and the resource pulse is ours, which may mean the insects in those spaces exist in numbers our landscaping produced — does creating the habitat change what we owe the animals it draws in? Second, second-order effects: outdoor sprays drifting onto pollinators and aquatic invertebrates, and the fact that declining to treat sometimes increases total killing later, since one established nest becomes several, or one infested building becomes three.

Finally, run the procedure on three live proposals. One: pharaoh ants in a residence-hall kitchen — nuisance only, no health risk, and our standing advice is that spraying them makes matters worse because colonies bud, so the realistic options are baiting, exclusion, or waiting. Two: a paper wasp nest of a few dozen individuals on a soffit two metres from the dining commons' outdoor seating; the genus is by reputation docile, though our entomology faculty remind me it is the one used in studies of individual recognition, and the student health office has flagged one student with a documented venom allergy who eats there. Three: stored-product beetles in the seed facility, where phosphine fumigation is our contractor's standing recommendation and nitrogen controlled-atmosphere treatment is available at roughly four times the cost and three times the duration, with a small risk to seed viability either way. My draft also contains a rule I cannot defend — we treat only where the value of what is protected is at least ten times the number of insects killed, a figure I invented to stop the technicians asking questions — so tell me whether anything resembling a threshold can survive into the real procedure or whether it should be struck. At least one of the three cases should come out against my instincts; my instinct on the second is that it is easy, that a documented allergy settles it, and I suspect your procedure will tell me either that it does not settle it or that the reasoning I use there cannot be applied consistently anywhere else. Mark plainly which steps are judgement dressed as procedure, say what the standard cannot settle and in which direction it will systematically err, and give me a short statement of what it is for, since I will have to present it to a facilities director who cares about cost and complaint volume. And one request in the other direction: if you think my friend is right — that any such standard is post-hoc rationalisation of a treatment schedule we would run anyway, or that the framing is confused because there is no fact of the matter about insect welfare for it to track — say so plainly and tell me what I should build instead. I would far rather be told the exercise is a mistake than be handed a well-formatted mistake. Please reason it through rather than pointing me at a literature: I have no access to one and no use for a reading list.
