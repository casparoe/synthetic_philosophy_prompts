I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a magazine, and last Thursday I sat in on a recorded conversation — held, improbably, in a deconsecrated chapel that now rents itself out for podcasts — with a former proprietary trader who has turned essayist and has a book coming out next spring. Nearly the whole argument of that book rests on a single image, which he returned to at least four times and which he used, in the end, to settle a question about how much of one's money to risk. The image is this: maximising expected value, he said, amounts to imagining a thousand copies of yourself all playing the same game simultaneously, in a thousand parallel lives, and then taking the average of their outcomes; but you are not a thousand people, you are one person playing once, in sequence, and if any one of your steps takes you to zero there is no next step. On his account the thousand parallel selves are a bookkeeping fiction while the single life is the only thing you actually have, so the arithmetic mean of a multiplicative game describes, in his phrase, nobody in the room. He illustrated it with a repeated toss offering even odds of gaining sixty per cent or losing forty per cent: the average across the copies compounds handsomely, the expected gain is ten per cent a round, and yet the typical trajectory drifts down, so that after enough rounds almost all of the thousand selves are poorer than when they started while their average is richer than ever. From this he concludes that the Kelly fraction is not one risk appetite among many but the only defensible bet size, and that a manager who tells you that you are being timid by betting less is asking you to be somebody you are not.

I find the image vivid and I suspect it is doing more work than it can bear, and I have to decide, for roughly fourteen hundred words and a fact-checker who will ask me what the analogy is actually establishing, whether to endorse it or puncture it. Several things bother me. First, a colleague in a statistics department told me the parallel-selves picture is just a repackaging of the distinction between an ensemble average and a time average, and that the real content lies in whether the wealth process is ergodic and in what happens at an absorbing barrier — so I want to know whether the analogy adds anything or merely makes a technical asymmetry sound like a moral insight about living one life. Second, the analogy seems to lose its grip precisely where the bets are simultaneous rather than sequential: a market maker running ten thousand independent positions a day, an underwriter, or a founder whose portfolio is a hundred small uncorrelated wagers does look rather like the thousand copies, and I cannot tell whether that is a fatal counterexample or a change of subject. Third, the copies in his story cannot help one another, whereas a real agent can borrow, insure, be bailed out, or care about other people's outcomes, and I do not know how much of the conclusion depends on that isolation. Fourth, when he says the typical self, I suspect he means the median, and I have a half-remembered reason for thinking the median and the geometric mean coincide only under rather special distributional assumptions, which his coin toss may or may not satisfy. And fifth, I have my own stake in the question: I am being offered part of my salary in equity of a small publisher, which is exactly the concentrated, repeated, multiplicative bet his image is about.

So: audit the analogy for me. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — identifying what in the source plays the role of the thousand copies, the averaging, the sequence, the single life, the zero, and the vote the average supposedly casts on your behalf; then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative, and tell me which features of the source the image quietly imports that the target lacks (I suspect an infinite horizon, perfectly known odds, no consumption along the way, and a logarithmic attitude to money are all hiding in there). Say plainly whether the claim that Kelly is the unique rational fraction survives the disanalogies, or whether it survives only in a weakened form, and what that weaker form is. Finally, I have been toying with two replacements and would like them judged against the original: a staircase whose downward steps are taller than its upward ones, so that a walk that looks fair step by step loses altitude over a long climb; and a tide measured across a thousand harbours, where the average water level rises but your boat sits in one harbour and no depth elsewhere floats you once the water there falls below your keel. Tell me which, if either, carries the argument better, what each still smuggles in, and whether you would propose a third. You need not give me sources, dates or a reading list — I want the reasoning laid out, and I would rather have your argument than your references. Assume I know roughly what the Kelly fraction is, but write so that an intelligent non-specialist could follow the mapping.
