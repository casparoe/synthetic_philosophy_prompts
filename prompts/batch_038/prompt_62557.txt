I am preparing a diagnostic handout for a joint graduate seminar whose enrolment splits roughly evenly between set theorists and philosophers of mathematics. The session's topic is Skolem's paradox, and in past years the discussion has collapsed into participants reciting the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem at one another, because nobody notices how differently they are treating models in the first place. So I want a decision tree that sorts a reader by their underlying commitments rather than by their stated opinion about the paradox.

Please observe the following requirements.

1. The entry point should be a bare acceptance of the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem applied to a first-order axiomatization of set theory, together with the fact that the axiomatization proves the existence of an uncountable set. Assume nothing else is given.

2. Each internal node must be a single question the reader can answer from a concrete scenario or a methodological preference, without knowing which leaf the answer leads to. In particular, do not ask directly whether cardinality is relative, whether there is a determinate universe of sets, or anything else that is simply the thesis of one leaf rendered in interrogative form.

3. At least four nodes must probe what I take to be the real fault lines, and I want these posed as live choices rather than absorbed into the background: whether the countability of a model is a feature of the model or of the vantage point of whoever is doing the counting; whether a bijection that exists outside a model but not inside it counts as a bijection for the purposes of the argument; whether one is entitled to restrict attention to well-founded or transitive models, and what further set-theoretic commitments that restriction buys at what price; whether the metatheory in which the counting is carried out enjoys any greater security than the theory being modelled; and whether a systematic mismatch between what an axiomatization says and what is so should be charged against the axiomatization or received as a discovery about its subject matter.

4. The leaves must include, at minimum: the view that there is no paradox and nothing to revise, because the model's own term for uncountable is not doing the work the metatheorist's term is doing; the view that the phenomenon is a reductio of adopting first-order logic as the framework for set theory, so that the remedy is a richer logic; the view that it is instead a reductio of the metaphysical picture of a single determinate set-theoretic universe, so that the remedy is a plurality of backgrounds; the view that set-theoretic notions, cardinality included, are relative to a domain and there is no further fact about which domain was intended; and any further leaf you judge to be occupied that I have not listed. Mark a leaf "unnamed" if the combination of answers reaching it is coherent but undefended, and add one sentence diagnosing whether it is unoccupied because it is unstable, because it is unattractive, or because it has merely gone unexplored.

5. Attach to each leaf one sentence stating the most awkward commitment it inherits: the question its defenders would least like to be handed in seminar.

6. Close with two short appendices. The first should identify any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, whether because distinct answer patterns land in different leaves without reflecting a real disagreement, or because a real disagreement is one your questions fail to track. The second should list three or four answer patterns that look available on the tree but that you judge no consistent position to occupy.

Keep it usable: a participant should be able to complete it with a pencil in about ten minutes, so no path through the tree should exceed seven questions.
