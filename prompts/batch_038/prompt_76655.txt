I teach an MSc module on the formal foundations of distributive justice and I am setting this year's two-hour unseen paper. Candidates answer two of four questions; mine is worth 25 marks and should be answerable in about 45 minutes. Calculators are permitted, notes are not. Candidates have had Nash's axiomatisation and Kalai and Smorodinsky's 1975 alternative in lectures, together with both characterisation theorems and the geometric recipes, and they have done problem sets on each. What I want is one compulsory question on the choice between the two solutions that cannot be passed by reciting that Nash imposes independence of irrelevant alternatives while Kalai–Smorodinsky replaces it with monotonicity. That recitation should be necessary and nowhere near sufficient.

The question is to be built around a concrete problem, which I have already worked out and which I would like you to check before writing anything else. Let the disagreement point be d = (0,0), and let S be the convex hull of (0,0), (0,6), (2,5) and (7,0), closed under free disposal, so that the Pareto frontier runs from (0,6) to the kink at (2,5) and then down to (7,0). My workings: the ideal point is (7,6); the ray from d through it meets the second frontier segment at (49/13, 42/13), roughly (3.77, 3.23), which is the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution; the Nash product is maximised at (3.5, 3.5) on that same segment. The interpretive point I want drawn out is that Kalai–Smorodinsky gives each player 7/13 of the way from d to her own ideal, whereas Nash gives player 1 1/2 of his and player 2 7/12 of hers.

Then the crux. Let T be the convex hull of (0,0), (2,5) and (7,0), closed under free disposal: that is, S with the single Pareto-optimal point (0,6) destroyed. In T the ideal point is (7,5), the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution moves out to (49/12, 35/12), roughly (4.08, 2.92), and the Nash solution is unchanged at (3.5, 3.5). Player 1 therefore gains strictly, and gains more than she would have received under either solution in S, purely because player 2's best attainable outcome has been eliminated — and it costs player 1 nothing, since (0,6) was never going to be selected. I want one part asking candidates to explain why Nash is blind to this contraction and Kalai–Smorodinsky is not, and what each axiom is buying. I want a final part in which candidates assess a verdict I shall print in the rubric, composed from a script I marked last year: "Independence of irrelevant alternatives is the requirement that a bargain not be manipulated by destroying options; monotonicity is the requirement that a bargain respond to what the parties might aspire to. Since the two cannot both be satisfied, and the second is a demand of fairness while the first is a technical convenience, Kalai–Smorodinsky wins." A strong script should recognise that the incompatibility claim is correct and follows from the two uniqueness theorems applying to the same domain, that the gloss on monotonicity is roughly fair, and that the dismissal of IIA is not: Nash bargaining has its own manipulation channel in the disagreement point, which is precisely why Nash needed a separate account of threats, so neither solution is immune and the real question is which parameter of the problem a player is able to move. The best scripts should also say what a solution immune to both channels would have to look like, or argue that there cannot be one, and should note that in my example the gain to player 1 is not a defect that Kalai and Smorodinsky overlooked but monotonicity operating exactly as designed.

Please write the following sections, in this order:

1. **Arithmetic check** (about 150 words): confirm or correct each figure I have given — both ideal points, all four solution points, and the three fractions.
2. **The question as it will appear on the paper** (about 300 words): parts (a) to (d), each with its mark allocation, summing to 25, plus a one-line rubric reminding candidates of both geometric recipes so that the item is self-contained.
3. **Model answer** (about 800 words): the full working, and a treatment of (c) and (d) that states what each side of the dispute is committed to rather than pretending the matter is settled.
4. **Marking scheme** (about 300 words): marks distributed part by part, naming the specific moves each mark requires and the points that distinguish a first-class script from a competent one.
5. **Where scripts go wrong** (about 250 words): four named failure modes you would expect from candidates who have memorised the two axiom lists, and, for each, the shortest diagnostic that reveals it.

Do not include a reading list, a bibliography, or any citations beyond the names of the two solutions and the two axioms.
