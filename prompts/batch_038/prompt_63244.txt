I'm on the programming commitee for my town's public library — five volunteers, we choose the book for our monthly "read it, then come argue about it" evening. A retired professor who's been showing up for years has proposed Hume's little essay "Of Suicide," the one that only got printed after he died. Now we've got a real fight.

One member is flatly opposed: two people in the county killed themselves this year, she says the research on how detailed public discussion can spread suicide is solid, and she thinks the essay is essentially a very well-written case for permission that could hand somebody a rationale. A second member thinks it's an insult to grieving families to host it at all, on the grounds that Hume treats our obligations to the people who'd be left behind as basically nothing. The professor pushes back that they haven't read it closely: that it's an argument about duties rather than rights, that a big chunk of it is actually a theological argument about providence that most summaries skip over, and that assuming adults can't handle a 250-year-old text is exactly the patronizing attitude our mission statement says we're against.

Useful context: we've never turned down a proposal. The closest we came was a evening on assisted dying two years back, which pulled sixty people and one angry letter to the local paper that blew over in a week. The branch director says she'll back whatever we decide but needs three weeks' lead time to sort staffing, and our next open slot is in April.

Can you draft the memo I'll circulate before Tuesday's meeting? I need an actual bottom line — host it, decline it, or host it in some modified shape — plus conditions the library could realistically carry out and a rough timeline working back from April. And please don't give me generic harm-reduction boilerplate. The memo has to say what Hume's argument actually consists in and whether each side's description of it holds up, because these two members will vote based on what they believe the text says, and if the memo gets the text wrong I've wasted my time. It should take the contagion worry seriously and at full strength — that's the best thing on the other side — but also be straight with me about what that worry does and doesn't license here, given that this is a facilitated discussion of a philosophical essay and not a news story or a website.
