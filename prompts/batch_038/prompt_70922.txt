For like fifteen years now I've been reading the rationalist blogs — LW, the sequences, SSC, the forecasting forums — and three years ago I finally built the thing I kept wishing existed: a public register of open questions and known unknowns. People submit entries, we list them, researchers and forecasters mine the list for projects. It's gone okay but the intake side is a mess and I think I've found out why.

We get about forty submissions a month. Six volunteers screen them, none with any philosophy background (one's a nurse, two are undergrads, one does infosec). Ten minutes per submission is the budget, and I sign off on anything they escalate. Entries are public, permanent, and indexed by search engines — we dont quietly retract, because a register you can edit after the fact isn't a register.

Here's the problem. Our intake form has a checkbox that says, roughly, that the submitter takes their entry to be in principle knowable by someone — the idea being that if it isn't knowable its not an open question, it's just noise. Then I went and reread about Fitch's paradox of knowability, the result that apparently came out of an anonymous referee report in the 40s (Church, they found out later) and got published by Fitch in the 60s: if every truth is knowable then every truth is already known, because the particular claim "p is true and nobody knows p" can't be known by anyone without thereby going false. Which means our checkbox is doing something much weirder than I thought, and the exact class of entries I find most interesting — the ones about what nobody knows — might be the unhostable class.

So: write me a screening procedure my volunteers can actually run. Questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exits (reject / rewrite and resubmit / accept / escalate to me), and which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume — say so out loud instead of dressing them up.

It has to do these things:

- Sort "nobody knows this" into the kinds that are genuinely different: ordinary unknowns; unknowns that are contingently out of reach (records burned, private states of people who are dead); unknowns that are Fitch-style, where knowing the entry would falsify it; and unknowns that get destroyed by being published (the dual-use security case). Plain-English tests for each, no modal logic — these people have never seen a diamond operator and shouldn't have to.
- Tell me what to do with the checkbox: keep, kill, or rewrite into something actually true. And tell me whether every accepted entry has to be indexed to a date and to a named community of knowers, because I suspect "nobody" is doing a lot of unearned work.
- Say which premises of the Fitch argument are load-bearing for whatever you land on, which distinctions absolutely have to survive the compression into a ten-minute checklist, and which ones I can throw away without the procedure quietly becoming something else.
- Say where the analogy breaks. Fitch is about truth and knowability, not about publishing lists, and I don't want a procedure that smuggles in a philosophical result it hasn't earned.

Then run it on three submissions:

1. Infosec person: there's a memory-safety bug in a widely deployed open-source library, nobody outside the vendor knows, and no attacker has found it yet.
2. Grad student: nobody currently knows whether the headline effect in a well-known failed replication was real or an artifact of the original analysis pipeline.
3. Mine, which I've been sitting on for a year because I knew it was trouble: there is at least one true claim about the causes of the 2008 crisis that no living person knows and that no surviving record could ever support.

On 3 be blunt. I expect the procedure to make me throw out my favorite entry, and if it does I want the reasoning, not a cushion.

Finish with what the procedure can't do: the false positives it'll generate, the entries it'll wrongly wave through, and the cases where a volunteer genuinely can't tell and I have to be the one who decides.
