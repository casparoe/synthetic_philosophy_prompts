Taking a seminar on promissory obligation right now and I've sort of talked myself into a view I want stress-tested before I write my final paper on it. I'd rather find out it's junk now than in front of the whole seminar.

Here's what I think. Promises don't have any binding power of their own — there's no special normative-power trick where uttering "I promise" manufactures an obligation out of nothing. What actually happens is that promising hands someone a reason to reorganize their plans around your doing X, and the duty you're under is just the ordinary duty not to screw over someone who reorganized their plans on your say-so. So the strength of the obligation tracks the reliance cost: how much worse off the promisee ends up for having counted on you. My verdicts, so you can see the shape of it:

- promised a friend I'd help them move, they didn't rent a truck and burned a whole Saturday waiting → very binding, obviously
- promised some person in a forum I'd mail them my zine, they lost nothing but a few minutes of mild hope → basically not binding, it's more like being a jerk than breaking a promise
- promised my grandmother on her deathbed I'd look after the cat, when she was past the point of relying on anything → binding, but only derivatively, because my siblings had already arranged things around it
- promised to do something I was already morally required to do anyway → little or no extra obligation

What would count against this? Give me the map of its exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. I want the intuitions and case-shapes that a reliance-cost account can't describe, and I want you to say which ones a committed defender of my own view would concede as counting against it — where would I be the one saying "yeah, that's a real problem" instead of explaining it away. I have a suspicion there's something about release and waiver that I can't handle, i.e. that the promisee can just let me off and then the duty vanishes even though the reliance cost already got spent, but I haven't been able to pin down why that's supposed to be so bad for me. Tell me if that's actually a defeater or if I'm overthinking it.

Then the honesty check: does my view risk anything at all? Someone in the seminar said my fallback move — that reliance cost includes the lost opportunity to have made other plans, so every breach costs something — turns the proportionality scale into decoration, since everything gets a nonzero score and the ordering never bites. Is that right? Is a view insulated that way actually broken, or is that just the price any reductive account of an obligation pays?

Finally, what does my view predict that the rivals don't — the assurance-style account (we read Scanlon's Principle F) and the practice-based ones that make the duty fall out of the institution of promising? I'm hunting for a class of case where those come apart from mine in a way I could put in the paper and defend. No reading list, I already have one and half of it unread.
