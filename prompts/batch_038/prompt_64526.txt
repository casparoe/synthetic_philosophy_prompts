So — I'm maybe 60% of the way through a novel set in 1860–61, mostly Glasgow and then Boston, and one of my two protagonists is a free Black compositor who sets type for an antislavery paper and goes along to hear Douglass lecture; the other is a Garrisonian who thinks the Constitution is a pro-slavery compact and won't vote or hold office because of it. The whole book lives or dies on whether their argument sounds like two men who have actually read the document rather than two encyclopedia paragraphs shouting at each other, and I'm not a lawyer, so I've been sitting with the Glasgow speech ("The Constitution of the United States: is it pro-slavery or anti-slavery?", 26 March 1860) trying to get Douglass's own moves straight before I let my compositor borrow any of them. Here's the chunk I keep loosing the thread in. It's from an abridged anthology version, so the ellipses are the editor's cuts, not mine — I'm not asking you to reconstruct what's missing, only to say whether the cuts look like they'd change the argument:

> 1st, Does the United States Constitution guarantee to any class or description of people in that country the right to enslave, or hold as property, any other class or description of people in that country? 2nd, Is the dissolution of the union between the slave and free States required by fidelity to the slaves, or by the just demands of conscience? Or, in other words, is the refusal to exercise the elective franchise, and to hold office in America, the surest, wisest, and best way to abolish slavery in America?
>
> To these questions the Garrisonians say Yes. They hold the Constitution to be a slaveholding instrument, and will not cast a vote or hold office, and denounce all who vote or hold office, no matter how faithfully such persons labour to promote the abolition of slavery. I, on the other hand, deny that the Constitution guarantees the right to hold property in man, and believe that the way to abolish slavery in America is to vote such men into power as will use their powers for the abolition of slavery. . . .
>
> In all matters where laws are taught to be made the means of oppression, cruelty, and wickedness, I am for strict construction. I will concede nothing. It must be shown that it is so nominated in the bond. The pound of flesh, but not one drop of blood. . . . The Supreme Court of the United States lays down this rule, and it meets the case exactly—"Where rights are infringed—where the fundamental principles of the law are overthrown— where the general system of the law is departed from, the legislative intention must be expressed with irresistible clearness." The same court says that the language of the law must be construed strictly in favour of justice and liberty. . ..
>
> [T]he Constitution declares that no person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law; it secures to every man the right of trial by jury, the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus—the great writ that put an end to slavery and slave-hunting in England—and it secures to every State a republican form of government. Anyone of these provisions in the hands of abolition statesmen, and backed up by a right moral sentiment, would put an end to slavery in America.

Four things are tripping me up and I want them argued out, not summarized. (1) The method looks self-undermining to me. He says "I am for strict construction. I will concede nothing," and then he gets the due-process clause, jury trial, habeas corpus and the guarantee of a republican form of government to do an enormous amount of work — none of which mentions slavery, and all of which is being stretched toward a conclusion the framers of 1787 would have screamed at. Is there one principle that makes both halves cohere (strict about some kinds of clause, generous about others, with a rule telling you which is which)? If so, state it the way Douglass would have to state it for it to be a principle rather than a preference, and say what it costs him. If it can't be made cohere, say that plainly, because my Garrisonian character needs a real reply and not a strawman. (2) The Shylock business. "It must be shown that it is so nominated in the bond. The pound of flesh, but not one drop of blood" — I know that's Portia in The Merchant of Venice, but that scene is usually played as a piece of gleeful, slightly queasy legalism that ruins a man, and Douglass is casting himself in the clever-lawyer role against slaveholders. Does he seem aware of how the allusion reads, and is he grabbing Portia's knife knowingly — i.e., granting the slaveholders' own literalism and then beating them with it — or is he just reaching for a familiar quotation? My compositor has to quote this line in dialogue and I need to know whether he says it proudly or uncomfortably. (3) What's packed into "the great writ that put an end to slavery and slave-hunting in England," and does his American argument depend on that English precedent transferring? England had no written constitution and no positive law authorizing slavery; America had both, plus clauses the slave states insisted on. Can the absence-of-positive-law logic survive being imported into a document that at least gestures at fugitives and at three-fifths representation? (4) The last sentence is the one I genuinely can't parse, and it's the one the novel needs. "Anyone of these provisions in the hands of abolition statesmen, and backed up by a right moral sentiment, would put an end to slavery in America" — where does the antislavery-ness actually live, in the words or in the men holding them? If it's in the men, my Garrisonian wins: the identical text in Taney's hands produced Dred Scott, and the identical due-process clause has been read to protect slaveholders' property. So tell me how Douglass gets to say the document itself is antislavery rather than merely that decent men can make it do decent things, and whether that claim is doing honest work or smuggling in the moral sentiment it then credits to the text. One small mechanical point while you're in there: when he writes "To these questions the Garrisonians say Yes," he's answering both numbered questions for them, but his own "I, on the other hand, deny..." seems to take on only the first one — is the second being answered by the rest of the sentence, or is he sliding?
