In discussions of how to supervise AI systems, "process-based supervision" — evaluating and rewarding each intermediate step of a system's reasoning or plan — is routinely treated as a paradigm distinct from "outcome-based supervision," which evaluates and rewards only the end result, and as the more robust of the two: harder to game, better at credit assignment, and less vulnerable to a system that arrives at an acceptable answer by unacceptable means.

I want a sustained argument for the deflationary thesis that this contrast does not carve nature at any joint that matters. Argue for the following two claims, and say plainly where each of them begins to break down:

1. **Reduction.** What is called process supervision is outcome supervision applied to a re-described outcome. A process reward is an outcome reward whose "outcome" is the trajectory taken as a single object to be evaluated, with the score factored into part-scores and then aggregated. There is no third thing here — only a change in the granularity at which one and the same outcome-evaluation is decomposed.

2. **Misattribution.** The virtues credited to process supervision — resistance to gaming, improved credit assignment, tolerance of right answers reached by wrong means — are not produced by the process/outcome contrast at all. They are produced by a different variable: whether the evaluation decomposes into parts that can be checked independently of one another and independently of the final result. Hold that variable fixed and switching a supervisory signal from outcome-labeled to process-labeled changes nothing; vary it while holding granularity fixed and the supposed virtues migrate to the side that the standard vocabulary places in the wrong box.

To make the second claim concrete rather than merely verbal, construct two matched pairs of cases. In the first pair, give me a system supervised only on its final answer and a system supervised step by step, matched as closely as you can on decomposability, and show that the advantages usually attributed to the second one evaporate. In the second pair, give me two systems matched on granularity but differing on decomposability, and show that the advantages reappear in a configuration the process/outcome vocabulary cannot describe. Assume in all four cases a competent but fallible grader — human or model-based — so that whatever asymmetries you find are not artifacts of granting one side an infallible evaluator.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature and I do not want citations or a reading list; argue the case on its merits, with the mechanics of what signal each system actually receives spelled out in enough detail that a reader could say what gaming it would look like.

Please structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

- **Thesis** (about 100 words): the two claims in your own words, plus the single most important qualification you intend to attach to them.
- **The reduction** (about 300 words): the argument for claim 1, including what "factored into part-scores and aggregated" must mean for the reduction to be substantive rather than a relabeling true by stipulation.
- **The two pairs of cases** (about 450 words): the four cases, each described concretely enough to identify the supervisory signal, the failure mode, and how a system would exploit it.
- **Where the thesis fails** (about 250 words): the strongest objection you can construct against yourself, including any asymmetry between process and outcome supervision that survives both claims, and an accounting of how much of the thesis it costs you.
- **Bottom line** (about 120 words): whether "process-based vs. outcome-based" should be retired, kept as harmless shorthand, or replaced by the decomposability variable — and, if replaced, what you would call the distinction instead.
