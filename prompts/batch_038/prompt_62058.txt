I am giving a talk to a decision-theory reading group in which I set out my own position on the coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation, and I would like the package audited before I present it. My commitments are these:

(1) The coherence arguments are normative rather than merely descriptive: an agent whose preferences over gambles cannot be represented by an expected utility function is not just unusual but irrational.

(2) What earns that verdict is dynamic. Such an agent can be led, through a sequence of choices each of which she prefers to the alternative on offer, to end up strictly worse off having paid for nothing; I count that as a genuine defect rather than a harmless quirk.

(3) The utility function delivered by the representation theorems is unique only up to positive affine transformation, and I accordingly hold that there is no fact of the matter about interpersonal comparisons of utility.

(4) Rationality is structural: it requires coherence among the attitudes an agent actually has, not that she have any particular attitudes. Its requirements are wide-scope.

(5) I am sceptical of the idealisations these arguments are usually run with. They should be assessed against an agent's actual preferences over options she has actually considered, not against the dispositions she would acquire under unlimited reflection.

(6) I reject the independence axiom. Allais-style cases convince me, and I am content to replace it with a non-linear weighting of probabilities in the rank-dependent fashion.

Please tell me which of these genuinely pull against one another and which pairs only look as though they do. For each real tension: how severe is it, which commitment has to give, and what would I lose by giving it up? Then name the single revision that would repair the most damage. I have deliberately not indicated where I suspect the trouble lies, or which item I would be most willing to abandon.
