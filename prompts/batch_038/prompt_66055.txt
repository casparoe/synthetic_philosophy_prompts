A classmate in my comparative philosophy seminar constructed the following against the Jain doctrine of many-sidedness, and I have not been able to locate where it breaks. Take the doctrine on its own terms: an assertion about how things are must be prefixed by *syāt*, marking that it holds only conditionally, from some standpoint (*naya*) and in some respect, and that the object taken whole outruns any assertion of it and is inexpressible (*avaktavya*).

1. If a statement is true only conditionally, from some standpoint, it is not true without qualification.
2. The Jain thesis — every statement is true only conditionally, from some standpoint — is itself a statement.
3. So the Jain thesis is true only conditionally, from some standpoint.
4. The absolutist standpoint our opponents occupy is a standpoint from which the thesis does not hold; if truth is always standpoint-relative, the thesis fails from there.
5. Hence the thesis is true from the Jain standpoint and not true from the absolutist one: thesis and negation both hold.
6. The same reasoning runs for any pair of contradictories, so long as each is asserted from its own standpoint.
7. So no assertion rules out any other, and there is no such thing as disagreement about how things are.
8. A position that renders no assertion impermissible is not a position but a mood.
9. Therefore *anekāntavāda*, if coherent at all, is a temperament rather than a doctrine.

Which step should be given up, and what does giving it up cost the Jain? If forced, I would rather keep (2) than (8), but I want to know what I am paying, and whether (4) or (6) is doing the real work. In your diagnosis, please separate what is settled among people who work on Jain logic from what is genuinely contested; I do not want one sympathetic reconstruction of *syādvāda* handed to me as if it were the standard reading.
