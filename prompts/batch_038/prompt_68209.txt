I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a broadsheet, and last month I was sent to cover a two-day workshop on paraconsistent semantics. One of the postdocs sketched an argument on a board that I have not been able to shake, and I do not want to describe it in print until I understand where, if anywhere, it fails. Let me say at once that I know the familiar reply to explosion worries: the dialetheist refuses disjunctive syllogism, so a true contradiction does not license arbitrary conclusions. I checked this one against that reply, and it is advertised as not needing it — no appeal to a contradiction entailing everything appears anywhere in it. Its conclusion is not that dialetheism collapses into trivialism but that dialetheism cannot be stated, which if right is rather worse.

Some stipulations first. Call a sentence *just true* if it is true and not false; *just false* if it is false and not true; a *glut* if it is both; a *gap* if it is neither. Let N — the postdoc called it the Notary — be the sentence "N is not just true".

Here is the argument as I reconstructed it from my notes.

(1) Dialetheism is the claim that some sentences are gluts. That claim is worth asserting only if the gluts form a proper subset of the sentences; if every sentence were a glut the view would be trivialism, which nobody, least of all a dialetheist, accepts.

(2) To say that the gluts form a proper subset is to say that some sentences are true and false while others are true only. That requires the notion *just true*, or something equivalent to it.

(3) Either that notion is available in the language the dialetheist is offering a semantic theory of, or it is not; there is no third possibility, since a notion is either expressible in a language or it is not.

(4) Suppose it is available. Then we may form N, and we may assume the transparency of the semantic vocabulary involved: a sentence is true exactly when what it says obtains, and false exactly when what it says does not obtain. Transparency is no optional extra here — it is what makes the Liar paradoxical at all, and surrendering it would delete the very phenomenon dialetheism was invented to accommodate.

(5) If N is just true, then what N says obtains, so N is not just true. This case is closed.

(6) If N is just false, then what N says fails to obtain, so N is just true. This case is closed.

(7) If N is a glut, then, since N is true, what N says obtains and N is not just true — consistent so far; but since N is also false, what N says fails to obtain, and so N is just true. This case is closed.

(8) If N is a gap, then N is not true, so N is not both true and not false, so N is not just true — which is exactly what N says; so N is true after all. This case is closed.

(9) The four cases in (5) to (8) exhaust what may be said of a sentence, and all four have been closed. So no semantic status can be assigned to N without contradiction.

(10) A semantic theory that cannot assign a status consistently to a sentence of the very language it is a theory of is not a correct theory of that language: correctness here just consists in getting the statuses right, and for N there is nothing to get right.

(11) Therefore *just true* cannot belong to the object language. But then the dialetheist's thesis — that some sentences are gluts and others are not — cannot be expressed in the language her theory concerns; she can only gesture at it from outside. And any outside language capable of saying which sentences below are gluts needs its own *just true*, so the same argument re-forms one level up. The distinction the view depends upon is never drawn, only postponed.

(12) Either way, dialetheism is unstatable: on the first horn its own semantic vocabulary cannot be housed in the language, on the second the language that houses it can never state the view's central claim.

(13) A position that cannot be stated cannot be asserted, believed, or argued for; and what cannot be asserted cannot be true.

(14) Therefore dialetheism is not a possible position.

My difficulty is that I cannot tell whether this is a genuine refutation or a piece of sophistry wearing a lab coat, and my editor wants the column either way. So: tell me which step or steps you would reject and why, and what each rejection costs the person who makes it. If it is more honest to treat the argument as valid and ask which plausible premise is least expensive to give up, treat it that way. For what it is worth, I should most like to keep (4) — I have no appetite for writing that the paradoxes only look paradoxical because we fudged transparency — and I should most like to sacrifice (13), which strikes me as doing unearned work. But I want your diagnosis rather than a confirmation of my prejudices.

Two things my readers will need, since they are intelligent but not specialists: gloss any technical term on first use, and say what a gap theorist and a hierarchical (Tarskian) theorist would each make of the same argument, because if the trouble is not peculiar to dialetheism then the piece cannot be written as though it were. Please write in British English throughout, and please do not include references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want your own reconstruction, and I have no way of checking citations.
