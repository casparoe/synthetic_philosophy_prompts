So I sit on a charter-revision commission in a midsized city (pop. ~180k, wall-to-wall single-family zoning, a bus network that's actually decent), and last November we had three competing housing measures on the same ballot, which turned into the worst argument of my year. Our charter has a conflict clause: if two measures that conflict both pass, the provisions of the one with the most yes votes control. The measures were A, upzone the whole city to allow four units per lot as of right; B, change nothing, keep current zoning; C, allow four units per lot but only within a half-mile of a frequent-transit line, with everything else going through discretionary review plus a formal neighborhood objection process. Between the exit poll and the returns we could reconstruct the electorate as three blocs pretty cleanly. 34% ranked A > B > C (the build-everything people, who hated C because they figured the objection process would hand a permanent veto to the loudest block associations and lock it in). 31% ranked B > C > A (preservationists, who'd take a little corridor density over a blanket upzone). 35% ranked C > A > B (transit-first people). Every bloc cast an insurance yes on its second choice and a no on its last, so the totals came out A 69%, C 66%, B 65% — all three passed, the conflict clause handed it to A, and the city now has the blanket upzone that two-thirds of voters rank below C. Head to head, A beats B 69–31, B beats C 65–35, and C beats A 66–34, and a ranked-choice version of the same vote would have picked C.

A friend from the commission — smart guy, reads far too much social choice theory — says the whole thing proves the enterprise is a fraud, and he laid it out for me like this:

(1) A ballot measure has democratic authority only if its outcome expresses what the electorate actually wants.
(2) What the electorate wants is a fact about the distribution of individual preferences that is fixed before, and independently of, whatever rule you use to report it.
(3) Here the same distribution of preferences yields a different winner under a different rule — the conflict clause gives you A, ranked choice gives you C — so the winner tracks the rule, not the preferences.
(4) So A's passage doesn't express what the electorate wants, and by (1) it carries no authority.
(5) The same goes for any vote with three or more alternatives, including the council elections that would decide the identical question if we scrapped the initiative process altogether.
(6) And even where there are only two alternatives, the drafter picks which two: "allow fourplexes citywide, yes or no" and "let each neighborhood decide for itself, yes or no" are different questions about the same underlying conflict, so the voters never choose the option set either.
(7) Therefore no vote, direct or representative, ever expresses what the people want, and the authority of elections has to come from somewhere else entirely or from nowhere at all.

Here's my problem. I don't want to just reject (7) — I basically agree the will of the people is a piece of mythology, I've said as much at public comment, and I do think the conflict clause handed us the wrong winner. But he wields it in ways I cant follow. He wants to abolish the initiative process on the strength of (7) while keeping courts free to strike down whatever measures survive it, even though judges get appointed by presidents confirmed by senates elected under the exact aggregation he says is void; and he keeps insisting ballot measures are worse than legislatures, which (5) says they aren't. And if (7) is right I don't see what stops me concluding a coin flip carries precisely as much authority as an election, since neither expresses anything — I'm confident he'd reject that, but I can't find where his premises block it.

Something feels like it slips between (3) and (4), and maybe again between (5) and (6), but I can't name it and he's very fast in a debate. Which step do you reject, and why did it look fine in the first place? And the part I actually care about: if I give up (2), what am I out? I'd assumed something like (2) is what makes democracy worth preferring to a competent dictatorship, so tell me what's left to say once it's gone. Also whether the obvious repairs — ranked choice, or splitting C into two separate questions, one about how much density and one about who decides — are available to me without buying (2) back, or whether they just move the problem somewhere else.
