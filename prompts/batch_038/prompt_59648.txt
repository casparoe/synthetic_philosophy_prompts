I'm a third-year bioethics PhD student and I somehow have a seat on our academic medical center's assisted-reproduction advisory panel — I'm the junior member, which mostly means I get asked to write things up. Next month we vote on a proposal from the fertility clinic inside our network: they want to start offering polygenic embryo screening to IVF patients who end up with more than two viable embryos. Not for any single-gene condition — the vendor's composite score blends polygenic risk for schizophrenia and type 2 diabetes with a predicted-educational-attainment score, and parents would rank their embryos on that composite. Two members want to approve it outright, one wants a flat rejection, and the chair has asked me to draft the written opinion that circulates before the vote. I keep going back and forth myself, so I'd rather see the argument built properly than wing it.

The part I actually think decides the case is a wrinkle in the clinic's own draft that I haven't seen anyone argue about. They've included a tie-break lottery: if two embryos land within a fixed band of each other on the composite score, the clinic must randomise between them instead of taking the higher one. The band is deliberately set wide enough to swallow the typical within-family difference, which on the vendor's own validation numbers is small. The stated rationale is that score gaps too small to be meaningful shouldn't be allowed to drive the decision, and that this also blunts the objection that we'd be endorsing a ranking of possible children. One member thinks the lottery is a fig leaf that just makes the objectionable part look more modest; another thinks it's the best feature of the whole proposal; a third thinks it's incoherent becuase if the differences really are that small then there was never a reason to select on them at all, so we should refuse the service rather than launder it.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. It has to land on a concrete recommendation — approve, reject, or approve subject to conditions — and if it's conditions, they need to be things a clinic could actually operationalise, not aspirations. It also has to take the strongest case against your recommendation seriously enough that the members who've already put their views in writing can't dismiss it in a line. No bibliography or reference list, nobody on this panel reads those, but do use the real philosophical vocabulary where it earns its place: the difference between saying parents have a reason to pick the child expected to have the best life versus an obligation to; what the non-identity problem does and doesn't leave us able to say about harm when the unselected embryo never exists; and Habermas's worry about what being the product of a deliberate parental selection programme does to how the child can understand herself later, and to the symmetry between persons that our idea of equal standing leans on. Also handle the access problem honestly — this would run around six grand out of pocket and nobody in our network is subsidising it.

Please use exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Recommendation** — 4 to 6 sentences. The bottom line, the vote I'm asking for, and the one reason that's doing most of the work.
2. **What's actually being proposed** — about 150 words, neutral, including the tie-break lottery described fairly.
3. **The strongest case for approving** — about 250 words.
4. **The strongest case against** — about 250 words.
5. **Does the tie-break lottery fix anything?** — about 250 words. This is the section I care about most. I want a clear verdict on whether the lottery is doing genuine moral work or only cosmetic work, and why — including whether a threshold that cancels small differences can coherently sit inside a view that says any expected difference generates a reason.
6. **Conditions** — numbered, only if you're recommending conditional approval. Each condition should say who has to do what, and how the panel would be able to tell they did it.
7. **What would change my mind** — about 100 words: the two or three specific pieces of evidence or argument that would flip this recommendation.
