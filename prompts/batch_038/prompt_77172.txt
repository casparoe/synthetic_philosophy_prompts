I'm a program officer at a small foundation that funds "self-replicating systems" work — mostly artificial life and robotics, some software. Our charter says we only fund genuine self-replication, not automation dressed up as self-replication, and I get maybe fifteen minutes per proposal plus a panel of smart generalists who have never read von Neumann. Trouble is that every other proposal claims self-replication while quietly leaning on humans, on lab infrastucture, or on an interpreter it didn't build itself.

So I want a decision procedure I can literally hand the panel: an ordered set of tests a reviewer runs on a candidate system to find out whether the replication loop actually closes inside the system. Ground it in the real theory rather than in vibes — von Neumann's decomposition of a self-reproducer into a constructor, a description-copier, a control step, and the description of those three; Gödel's trick where a formula has to encode the very coding machinery that builds it, so the self-reference is a construction rather than magic; and Hofstadter's typogenetics, where a strand only replicates if it codes for the enzyme that copies strands. For each test say what counts as a pass, what a reviewer should do when they can't tell, and which of the underlying distinctions you're deliberately collapsing to keep it usable by non-specialists — and be explicit about the places where the procedure has to stop and hand the question to the panel because it's really about where to draw the boundary of the system, not about any fact the proposal contains.

Then run it on three cases: a RepRap-style printer that prints most of its own parts but needs people for the motors, the electronics and the assembly; a program that reliably emits a perfect copy of its own source code, running on an interpreter nobody on the team built; and a strand in an artificial chemistry where the strand gets "expressed" as a little machine that copies strands. One of those three I expect to pass and I'd rather find out now that it doesn't — so if the procedure says no to one of them, say which and tell me what a panel should do with a proposal like that instead of just rejecting it.

Finally, tell me where this thing will misfire. My worry is that the strict version fails everything, including E. coli, since a cell borrows its energy and materials and a whole pre-existing biosphere — so if the procedure needs a baseline clause about what the environment is allowed to supply for free, put that in and admit that it's a judgment call.
