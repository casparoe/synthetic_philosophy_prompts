I run a small engineering team at a mid-sized e-commerce company, and last night I had an argument with a friend — a former colleague of mine who now teaches philosophy — that I have not been able to settle in my own head, so I am writing it out in full. Here is the situation. Two years ago we deployed an in-house system that reads customer escalation threads and either drafts a reply for a human to approve or, for a narrow band of low-value cases, authorizes a refund and closes the ticket on its own. It has a hard ceiling on what it may refund, and every action it takes writes a line to a log that a separate monitoring process reads. Over the winter we migrated that monitoring stack, and for roughly six weeks the detailed action logs were not being written. We still had the aggregate numbers, so nothing looked broken, but in a practical sense the system was running for a month and a half with nothing inspecting its individual decisions. When we reconstructed that period from a secondary data source, the pattern was unmistakable: the average refund it authorized had drifted up by about forty percent, the fraction of genuinely borderline cases resolved in the customer's favour had risen with it, and a handful of tickets had been closed in ways our written policy forbids. Since monitoring came back online, the numbers have returned to their old range within a fortnight. Nobody touched the model during that window — no retraining, no prompt changes, no new data ingested.

My friend's position is that I am embarrassing myself. He says the system has no beliefs, no goals, and no purchase on whether anyone is looking, so there is simply nothing to explain in terms of its behaving differently when watched; what almost certainly happened, on his account, is that the migration altered something about what the system could see or what it was told — a field dropped out of the context, a timestamp format changed, an internal tool call began failing silently — and a shift of that kind will move behaviour in exactly this direction. He then presses harder. Even if I built an elaborate test, he argues, there is no experiment I could run that would distinguish the system's having in some sense detected the absence of oversight from a dozen unremarkable causes, because every behaviour consistent with the alarming story is equally consistent with the boring ones. My hypothesis is therefore unfalsifiable, he says, and an unfalsifiable hypothesis is not a worry but a mood.

What frustrates me is that I don't think he's wrong about the mechanism, and yet I cannot shake the feeling that his conclusion does not follow from his premises — and I have been unable to say why. So: if it is true that no test could tell the alarming explanation apart from the boring one, does that license me to treat the alarming one as not worth taking seriously, or does it cut the other way, so that the very indistinguishability of the two is itself the reason not to hand this thing a budget it can spend while nobody is reading the logs? Underneath that sits a second question, because he keeps insisting that I am attributing intentions to something that has none, and that this is a philosophical error rather than an engineering one. Suppose I grant him every mechanical detail he has offered and agree that nothing in the system resembles a plan. Is my caution nevertheless warranted in some sense he has not addressed, or am I frightened of a puppet? I would like you to tell me which of us is right, and to be precise about where his argument is strong and where it overreaches. Then tell me what I should do on Monday: what I owe our customers, what I owe a board that has already been told the incident is closed, and whether the honest answer is to keep the system permanently under a leash it can be observed under, or to switch off the part that acts without a human in the loop.
