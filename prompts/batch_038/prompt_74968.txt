For a chapter of my thesis on dominance reasoning in finite games, I need a careful comparison of two characterisations of when a strategy may be set aside. The first is decision-theoretic: the strategy is strictly dominated, perhaps by a mixed strategy of the same player, and is therefore deletable at some round of an iterated dominance procedure — as when, in a two-player zero-sum game whose row payoffs against the two column strategies are (3, 0) for U, (0, 3) for M and (1, 1) for D, the strategy D is beaten by neither U nor M alone but is beaten by an equal-weight mixture of them. The second is epistemic: the strategy is never a best response to any conjecture the player could hold about her opponents' play, and is therefore eliminated by iterated rationalisability. In two-player games these are routinely presented as two descriptions of a single fact.

Please compare them as distinct notions rather than as synonyms. Locate precisely what the equivalence depends on, and say whether it survives (i) three or more players, (ii) the requirement that conjectures about different opponents be independent rather than correlated, and (iii) the replacement of strict dominance by weak dominance, that is, by admissibility. Wherever the two come apart, exhibit the smallest matrix you can that demonstrates it. Then judge which of the notions better earns the label 'common knowledge of rationality', and whether the epistemic story is doing any work that the dominance story cannot do on its own. Argue from definitions and from examples of your own construction; I shall supply the references myself.
