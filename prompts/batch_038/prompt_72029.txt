I am preparing a review essay on whether the Darwinian framework survives the trip from genes to culture, and my method so far has been to collect cases and record my own verdicts on whether the item in question looks like a genuine unit of cultural selection. Here they are, with the reason I find myself giving in each.

1. A fiddle tune learned by ear in one village, never written down, passed on with small alterations each time: a replicator. Copies get made, variants differ in how readily they are picked up, and which variant you happened to hear shapes what you pass along.
2. A medieval sauce recipe that survives only because scribes kept copying it faithfully into new manuscripts, and that no one has actually cooked in six centuries: still a replicator, though a dormant one. The copying continued; only the effect on behaviour stopped.
3. The QWERTY keyboard layout: a replicator, even though it spread through lock-in and compatibility rather than through being good at anything. Nothing selected for it, but it was very evidently selected.
4. A cautionary tale about contaminated food, which travels because it reliably provokes disgust and gets retold from memory: not a replicator. What is transmitted is the hearer's disposition; the narrative is rebuilt each time out of standard parts, and it lands on the same shape in unrelated traditions because the minds doing the rebuilding are built alike. On this view nothing is copied at all — representations merely converge on attractors.
5. A slang expression that spreads precisely because few people use it, so that its own success destroys the condition of its success: a replicator, and the cleanest case on my list, since here a property of the item itself drives its own copying rate.
6. A creed of voluntary celibacy, or of martyrdom, that spreads widely while lowering the reproductive success of those who hold it: the paradigm selfish replicator. Host interest and item interest come apart, and the item wins.
7. A practice sustained only by sanction — a handshake, a required form of address — where what keeps it in circulation is that people punish those who drop it: not a replicator. The unit here is a norm surrounded by an enforcement apparatus, and it is better described as an equilibrium than as a lineage.
8. Decimal place-value notation, or the wheel, spreading because people see that it works and adopt it on purpose: not a replicator. The variation is directed by insight, and retention is explained by human problem-solving rather than by differential copying.

The tentative principle I have been testing these against is: an item is a unit of cultural selection just in case (i) it is copied with enough fidelity that variants remain distinguishable, (ii) features of the item itself causally influence how many copies get made, and (iii) differences in copying rate between variants are inherited by their successors.

I can see that the principle is in difficulty and would like help locating exactly where. Cases 1 and 4 strike me as the same phenomenon written down twice — in both, transmission proceeds by imperfect reconstruction, and in both, the work is done by a standing feature of human cognition — yet I want opposite verdicts, and I cannot say what distinguishes them. Case 3 satisfies (ii) only if lock-in counts as the item influencing its own copying, which feels like a stretch. Case 7 may satisfy all three clauses, since sanctions are a feature of the practice that raises its rate of uptake, and I am not willing to lose the intuition that norms evolve; yet case 5, which I did count, involves a frequency dependence that seems structurally similar to the enforcement in case 7. And clause (iii) may be asked to do too much at once: my recollection is that the person who coined the term doubted that cultural items are copied with anything like genetic fidelity, while a later body of work argues that Darwinian reasoning requires heritable variation in success but not particulate, high-fidelity inheritance — in which case low fidelity is an objection to one theory of cultural items, not a reason to abandon selection talk. I cannot tell whether both halves of that can be held together, or whether accepting the second forces me to retract case 4 and case 8.

So: is there a principle, or a small family of candidate principles, that delivers most of these verdicts? For each candidate I would like it stated precisely enough to be applied by someone who has never seen my list; the case that motivates each clause; its verdicts on cases I have not listed — the design of a national flag, a liturgy sung in a language the congregation does not understand, an acronym that outlives the organisation it stood for, a joinery technique transmitted by apprenticeship rather than by description, and a superstition that persists because it is unfalsifiable; and an honest statement of which of my eight verdicts it cannot accommodate. If cases 1 and 4 turn out to be inseparable, say so and tell me which one I should give up, and why that is the cheaper loss. Two further questions I would like answered along the way: whether the contrast between an item's being selected and its being selected for does real work in this domain, since several of my verdicts seem to turn on it; and whether anything in my reasoning actually requires these items to be thought of as selfish, as opposed to merely differentially retained.
