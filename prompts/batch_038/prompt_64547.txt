A hedonism reading group has run a two-year standoff between two camps on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the problem of saying what, if anything, all pleasures have in common — and the moderator has asked you to draft the joint statement the two camps should have produced by now.

The camps, stipulated for this exercise, are these.

**Camp A, the Felt-Quality camp.** All pleasures share a single kind of phenomenal character: a hedonic tone that is present as a component of each pleasure, though it differs in intensity and is carried by wildly different sensory materials. The apparent heterogeneity of pleasures is an artifact of three things: the diversity of the objects and activities that carry the tone; the frequent mixture of the tone with other, contrary components; and the poor introspective access we have to a component that is most easily detected in comparison rather than in isolation. What unifies pleasure is thus something intrinsic to the experience, available in principle to careful attention.

**Camp B, the Pro-Attitude camp.** There is no such component. Pleasures are unified relationally and functionally: to have a pleasure is for some state of affairs or sensation to be the object of a favorable attitude of a specific sort — being pleased that, enjoying, wanting the experience to continue. The felt-quality camp mistakes a common grammatical and functional profile for a common phenomenology, and its own case list proves the point: the pleasures that survive scrutiny as pleasures are those the subject is favorably oriented toward, whether or not any shared feel can be isolated. Camp B concedes that a weaker fallback is available to it — that pleasure is a disjunctive kind unified only by the theoretical role it plays in welfare hedonism — but insists this is a fallback, not its preferred thesis.

Both camps accept, as shared background, that the affective-neuroscience literature on hedonic impact versus incentive motivation gives good reason to think these two dimensions can come apart in non-human animals, and that human subjects can rank unfamiliar pleasures by amount without being able to state what the shared dimension is.

Both camps also agreed in advance to treat the following eight constructed cases as the fixed data set for the dispute. Do not substitute familiar textbook examples for them; the point of the collaboration is that both sides are on the hook for these.

1. **Cold plunge.** After hard exertion, immersion in very cold water. The subject reports the episode as good, describes it entirely in the vocabulary of strain, shock, and near-pain, and reports that if the aversive edge were removed the episode would lose what made it worth doing.
2. **The click.** The quiet rightness of a solution falling into place mid-puzzle. The subject cannot localize it anywhere in the body and reports that the feel disappears when attention is explicitly turned onto it, returning when attention goes back to the puzzle.
3. **Comeuppance.** Pleasure at a rival's public humiliation. The subject insists the feel is indistinguishable from the feel of hearing that a friend has recovered from an illness, and that nothing differs but the object.
4. **Two cadences.** Pleasure when a much-loved piece resolves exactly as expected, and pleasure when an unfamiliar piece resolves far better than expected. Opposite predictive structures, identical verdict.
5. **Nostalgia.** A bittersweet recollection. The subject refuses to decompose it into a pleasant part and an unpleasant part, says it is pleasant on the whole, and says it would be ruined by removing the ache.
6. **The opera.** The subject books the same performance again next season, reports wanting it to go on, and simultaneously and sincerely reports wanting it to be over. Both reports are stable across repeated questioning.
7. **The priced glass.** The same wine presented as cheap and as expensive. The subject sincerely and stably reports a difference in how it tasted — not merely in how they feel about the tasting — and the report survives full debriefing about the manipulation.
8. **The split ledger.** Two sub-cases. (a) A compulsive episode the subject reports, while in it, as intensely pleasant, and afterward reports not having wanted and not wanting again. (b) A long walk with an old friend, reported at the time as low in felt intensity, which the subject strongly wants to continue and strongly prefers to have had more of.

Produce the document, with the following parts.

**I. Agreed claims.** A numbered list of substantive claims both camps can sign. Include at least: claims about what welfare hedonism requires of its account of pleasure for the theory to be non-trivial; claims about which of cases 1–8 each camp currently thinks its own account handles better, where the two camps in fact agree on that ranking; and any claims about introspection, report reliability, and the relation between felt intensity and motivational pull on which they find they were never actually in conflict. Each claim should be worded so that a partisan of either camp would sign it without reservation, not so weakly that signing it costs nothing.

**II. Residual disagreements.** A table with exactly these columns: `ID` | `Question at issue` | `Camp A's formulation, in words Camp A would sign` | `Camp B's formulation, in words Camp B would sign` | `Substantive, verbal, or mixed` | `What would move it`. For each row, the two formulations must be genuinely answerable to the same question — if they are not, say so in the final column rather than pretending they are. Below the table, add a short paragraph per row stating the strongest reading of the opposing formulation that your own side can accept, so that each camp is on record as having charitably understood the other.

**III. Cruxes.** Identify the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. At minimum, address these three, and add any others you find: whether trained comparative attention converges on a component describable independently of any object (bearing on cases 2, 3, and 7); which of the two dissociable dimensions — hedonic impact or motivational pull — can carry the comparative work that hedonism needs, namely to make claims about which of two lives or two days contains more pleasure determinate (bearing on cases 1 and 8); and whether bittersweet experience must be decomposed into two simultaneous items whose amounts are netted, or can be a single item that is pleasant on the whole (bearing on cases 5 and 6). For each crux, give: a test, argument, or case that both camps accept as bearing on it; Camp A's prediction about the outcome of that test, written down in advance; Camp B's prediction, likewise; and for each camp a revision trigger specifying what outcome would force it to abandon its thesis outright, what outcome would force only a retreat to a weaker version, and what that weaker version is.

**IV. Verbal disputes.** State which portions of the two-year argument you now judge to have been merely verbal, and why. Candidate ambiguities to consider include: whether a hedonic quality is claimed at the level of experience types or of experience tokens; whether intrinsic means intrinsic to the token experience or merely non-relational; whether a feel must be a sensation-like item or may be a manner in which something else is experienced; and whether two experiences having the same feel means they are indiscriminable in pairwise comparison or that they share a common component. For each ambiguity you identify as live, record the stipulation both camps adopt for the remainder of the project, and say which of the disagreements in Part II shrink or disappear under that stipulation.

Write in the register of a document both parties will put their names to: no adjudication of who is right, no dramatic dialogue, no diagnosis of why the debate stalled. You do not need to cite or summarize the literature; the case battery above is the shared data, and the positions are fully specified here.

After the document, append a brief structured summary as a single valid JSON object with exactly these top-level keys and no others:

- `agreed_claims`: array of objects with keys `id` (string), `claim` (string), `why_not_vacuous` (string, one sentence on what signing it costs each camp).
- `disagreements`: array of objects with keys `id` (string), `question` (string), `camp_A_wording` (string), `camp_B_wording` (string), `status` (one of `substantive`, `verbal`, `mixed`), `what_would_move_it` (string).
- `cruxes`: array of objects with keys `id` (string), `crux` (string), `shared_test` (string), `A_prediction` (string), `B_prediction` (string), `A_abandons_if` (string), `A_retreats_to` (string), `B_abandons_if` (string), `B_retreats_to` (string).
- `verbal_disputes`: array of objects with keys `ambiguous_term` (string), `reading_A` (string), `reading_B` (string), `adopted_stipulation` (string), `disagreements_dissolved` (array of disagreement `id` strings).
- `cases_both_concede_are_hard`: object mapping each of `case_1` through `case_8` to an array of camp labels (`A`, `B`, or `both`) that concede the case is a serious difficulty for their own account.

Every array must be non-empty. Output the JSON in a fenced code block and nothing after it.
