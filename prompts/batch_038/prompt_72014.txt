Been going back and forth on whether the revealed preference idea actually says anything, or whether "she chose it, so she preferred it" is just a tautology wearing a lab coat. This came up in a policy seminar where someone argued against a soda tax on the grounds that the purchase is itself the best available evidence that the soda makes the buyer better off, and there's simply nothing else to go on. I couldn't refute it, but I didn't buy it either, so I went home and wrote down my gut calls on specific patterns of choice to see what they add up to:

1. A friend of mine orders tea when the café has two drinks on the board and coffee when it has three. Same prices, same everything. I want to say there's no real preference being revealed there at all — the menu is doing the work.
2. Same friend at dinner, offered pie: takes it when there's most of a pie left, declines it when it's the last slice. I have no problem with this one. It seems normal and even a bit admirable. But its formally the same shape of thing as (1) — an extra option on the menu changes what gets picked — and I cant articulate why one bothers me and the other doesn't.
3. Someone flips between taking a sure $50 and a 50/50 shot at $110 purely depending on whether you describe it as a gain or as avoiding a loss. Arithmetically identical. That's not a preference being revealed, that's a bug in the description.
4. Someone picks the same sandwich whether or not the café also sells salad. Obviously fine, obviously informative.
5. Picks A over B, B over C, C over A. Incoherent. I'd say there's no ordering in there to reveal.
6. A smoker who buys cigarettes every single day and tells me she wishes she'd never started. I'd say the purchase reveals something true about her, while flatly denying it reveals what's good for her. But with the guy in (3) I'm not willing to say the choice reveals anything true about him. I don't know what the difference is supposed to be.
7. Someone who takes $100 today over $110 in a month, and who also — asked a month in advance — would take $110 in 13 months over $100 in 12. That troubles me, but I'm not willing to call it a mistake about what they want. It feels like their want genuinely changes as the date approaches.
8. My roommate deleting the apps off his phone on Sunday night. That looks like choosing not to choose, and it's the most rational thing he does all week.

My working principle is roughly: a choice reveals a preference only when the person understands the options as they actually are, has the information they'd need, and wouldn't want to reverse the choice later. I already suspect that's a mess, because "wouldn't want to reverse it" smuggles in the very preference it's supposed to be testing for.

So — is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, one per intuition I can't fit, that delivers these verdicts? State each one precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case, and say which of my cases motivated each clause. Where nothing fits, I'd rather be told which verdict has to go, and why, than have more conditions bolted on. Also tell me what the surviving account predicts for a case I didn't list: opt-out organ donation, where something like 90% of people end up enrolled because they didn't fill in a form. Is the 90% revealed to prefer donating, or is that question just nonsense? And does the seminar soda-tax argument survive whatever principle you land on?

Underneath all of this, the thing I can't resolve: is "she prefers A to B" ever more than a redescription of "she chose A"? If it isn't, what's left of utility except a bookkeeping device — and why do I feel like I'm saying something about my friend's inner life when I say she prefers her job to mine, but not when an economist says the same thing about a stranger? Take a run at that too, even if it's messier than the rest.
