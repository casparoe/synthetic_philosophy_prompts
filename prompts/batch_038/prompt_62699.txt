My flatmate is doing a conversion masters in philosophy and last night over dinner she insisted that Duhem–Quine holism and the underdetermination of theory by evidence are basically the same thesis said twice — her words were "if you can never test a hypothesis on its own then obviously the evidence can't single out one theory, so it's one point, not two". I said I thought they were two different claims that just get stapled together, and she said I couldnt explain the difference, so now I want to be able to. Can you compare the two properly? I'd like the contrast spelled out in terms of what each thesis is actually a claim *about* — one seems to be about the structure of testing, the other about a relation between a body of evidence and a set of rival theories — and I'd like to know whether either entails the other, or whether you could coherently hold one and deny the other. Both directions, if they do come apart, because I suspect they behave asymmetrically.

Here's the case I keep reaching for, and please tell me if it's a bad one. The water in a village well tests positive for something unpleasant. Theory A says it's leaching from a septic field uphill; Theory B says it's a natural mineral stratum below. Under the sampling routine they currently have — half a dozen drawpoints, quarterly, measuring only the contaminant concentration itself — the two theories imply exactly the same numbers, so no result from that routine can favour either. But start measuring isotope ratios, or sample the day after heavy rain, or sink a monitoring borehole downhill, and the two come apart immediately. Someone in our reading group argued this shows underdetermination is a phantom: empirical equivalence is only ever equivalence relative to today's instruments and today's auxiliary assumptions, and since both keep changing, no equivalence is ever stable enough to do the sceptical work it's being asked to do. Is that right, or does it miss something? Specifically, work out whether that move is one a holist is *entitled* to make — it feels to me like the holist should be the happiest person in the room about it, because holism lets you rebuild the whole web instead of defending a single hypothesis, but I can also see an argument that the holist's flexibility cuts the other way and gets you global underdetermination all over again.

It's also worth saying something about Quine himself: in Two Dogmas he seems to travel from the holistic point to something very like the underdetermination thesis, and in later work he states that thesis outright, but I can't tell whether that's a legitimate inference or a quiet change of subject. Aim for maybe 700 to 1000 words, pitched at someone who's done one year of philosophy, knows what a modus tollens is, and has never read Duhem. And write it in British English throughout, spellings included; I'm going to read bits of it aloud at the group and I don't want it sounding like it was written in California.
