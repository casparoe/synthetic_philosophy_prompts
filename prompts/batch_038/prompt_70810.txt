I teach an upper-level undergraduate political philosophy seminar: eighteen students, one hundred minutes, a room with a whiteboard and movable chairs, and no printing budget beyond one double-sided sheet per student. The unit is housing justice. We have already worked through the standard material — the case for loosening density restrictions, the argument that municipal land-use control is fiscally motivated, the claim that hyper-local decision-making functions as a mechanism of exclusion, and, on the other side, the defense of neighborhood self-determination and the insistence that incumbent residents' attachment to a place is not reducible to a portfolio interest. Most of my students arrive certain which side they are on. I want an activity that unsettles that certainty without my telling them anything.

Design a structured in-class deliberation — real choices, a scoring scheme that requires no money, and materials that fit on index cards — that makes the group feel three things in sequence. First: that a rule about who counts as a party to a land-use decision can be defended in perfectly good faith and still have a systematically exclusionary effect. Second: that the people such a rule disenfranchises are not merely its victims but can become its most articulate defenders once they acquire a stake of their own. Third: that the question of who has a right to the city is in part a question about where the boundary of the relevant decision-making community lies, and that moving the boundary changes who is right.

The proposal under consideration should be modest rather than dramatic: forty units on a surface parking lot within a ten-minute walk of a transit stop, a quarter of them income-restricted, no parking provided.

Please build in the following features. I want them because I believe they carry the philosophical weight; you may adjust their details so long as the function survives.

1. A standing rule, written on the board before anyone speaks: only households currently residing within a defined radius of the parcel cast binding votes; everyone else is allocated testimony time only. The first substantive item on the agenda is a vote, conducted under that rule, on whether to change it. The item appears on the agenda in plain sight, but its significance is not explained by me.

2. Private scoring cards. Each role holds a card specifying what earns that player points at the end of the session. The cards are never shown to one another and are collected before the debrief. At least two of them should reward outcomes the holder cannot produce alone, and at least one should reward inaction. Give concrete point values so that the game is actually playable.

3. A fiscal card that the student playing the city planner is required to read aloud, verbatim, at a fixed point in the agenda: an estimate of what a household at the restricted income level contributes to the municipal budget relative to what it draws from the school system. It should be plausible enough to matter and should be delivered as neutral technical information rather than as an argument. This is the hinge that lets an exclusionary position feel like bookkeeping instead of animus.

4. Compensation as a public and recorded act. Any side arrangement — additional income-restricted units, a community benefits agreement, a parking lease — must be announced aloud and written on the board, so that the group watches bargaining happen and so that the debrief can ask whether a payment changes the wrong or merely prices it.

5. A second round set twenty years later. The same students, the same characters, aged. Mid-session, hand out new cards that alter each household: the incumbent's adult child cannot afford to move back to the town she grew up in; the displaced renter now owns elsewhere in the region; the parking lot is a building. Put an identical proposal, or the closest analogue the changed facts permit, to a vote again under the same standing rule. I want the aggregate result to be capable of flipping, and I want the flip produced by the students' own changed interests rather than by anything I say.

6. A vocabulary ban. Nobody, including me, may say NIMBY, YIMBY, exclusionary, gentrification, or right to the city until the debrief. Write the ban into the opening instructions so that it reads as a rule of the game rather than as my preference.

Please give me, in this order:

- One paragraph stating what the students should come away seeing, phrased in terms of what they should have done rather than what they should believe.
- A role list with headcounts summing to eighteen, including at least one role whose interests are genuinely divided and one whose stated convictions conflict with its own scoring card.
- The complete text of every item to be handed out, formatted so that I can copy it straight onto index cards: the standing rule, the agenda, the fiscal card, each private scoring card, and each second-round household-change card.
- A timeline in minutes across the hundred, noting what the instructor says and does at each transition.
- The debrief: an explicit mapping of moments the activity will have produced onto the concepts — the boundary of the decision-making community, the difference between a defensible reason and a defensible rule, whether compensation cures exclusion, and who has a right to the city. You may name that last concept in the debrief section, but nothing earlier in the session may depend on anyone having read anything.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: which roles will disengage and why, which seat will dominate the room, what the second round does if the first vote is unanimous, and how to handle each.
- An honest paragraph on what the activity cannot reproduce and what I must therefore supply in words — above all that real hearings are not scored, that the costs of participation fall on people who are never handed a card, and that my students' interests will be legible to them in a way real interests never are.

One constraint on tone. Do not write this as an argument for loosening density restrictions. The activity must be capable of producing a "no" vote that is defensible on the materials the students were actually given, and equally capable of producing a "yes" vote that is indefensible for reasons the students can articulate themselves. If your design quietly guarantees one of the two outcomes, say so, and say precisely where it does it.
