> Douglas Hofstadter's account of the self, developed in his 2007 book *I Am a Strange Loop*, grows out of a piece of grief: his wife Carol died of a brain tumor, and Hofstadter found himself unable to accept that she had simply stopped. His answer is that a self is not a thing but a pattern — a self-referential loop of symbols that a brain builds in the course of modeling itself — and patterns are the sort of thing that can be realized in more than one place at once. Anyone who has lived closely with another person knows this: after enough years you carry a working model of them inside you, vivid enough to predict what they would say, to laugh at their jokes in your own head, to feel their disapproval. On Hofstadter's view that model is not a pale copy but a partial instance of the very same pattern, so souls come in degrees, and a person is distributed across everyone whose brains have taken them in. This is why death is less absolute than it looks: the loop stops running in one skull but keeps running, at reduced bandwidth, in others, which amounts to a kind of immortality that requires no supernatural commitments. It is also, essentially, Derek Parfit's point that identity is not what matters, since what survives is the pattern rather than the person. And because a self is a pattern rather than a substance, it is substrate-independent in principle — if we could scan a brain in sufficient detail and run it elsewhere, the person would go on, and Hofstadter accepts this. The practical upshot is consoling. Grief, on this picture, is largely a failure to recognize where the person actually is; the dead are not absent but relocated, and the more widely a life has been absorbed by others, the more of it remains. What we should therefore do with our deaths in view is not to secure an afterlife but to make ourselves legible — to be loved well and remembered accurately.
