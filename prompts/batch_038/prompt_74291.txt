So our CS club meets Thursday after school and this week it was P vs NP, mostly because our teacher put on one of those animated explainer videos and then gave his own version of it. His version went like this: think about Sudoku. Filling in a blank 9x9 grid is brutal, you sit there for an hour trying stuff and backtracking, but if I hand you a completed grid, checking whether it's legit takes like two minutes, you just scan the rows and columns and boxes. That gap, he said, is exactly what P vs NP is about — easy to check, hard to find. And then he went further and said that since literally anyone who has ever done a puzzle can feel that gap in their bones, P not equal NP is basically already settled, and people who still hold out hope for the other answer are just refusing to accept something obvious. He said it kind of jokingly but also kind of not.

My friend who does math competitions was not having it. She said the analogy doesn't argue for anything, it's just an example of the thing wearing a costume, and that pointing at one puzzle and saying "see, checking felt easier" proves as much as pointing at one haystack and saying "see, finding the needle felt harder than holding the needle." She also said something about how a person with a pencil is not the same kind of object as whatever the theory is actually talking about, and that the teacher is trading on psychology to make a claim about algorithms. I could see both sides and I left the meeting still not knowing who's right, which is annoying because I volunteered to write this up for the club blog and people reading it have never taken anything more mathy than algebra 2.

Can you actually audit the analogy for me? Like give me the mapping explicitly — what plays the role of the grid, the person checking, the two minutes, the hour of backtracking, and what each of those is supposed to correspond to on the theory side. Then tell me where the mapping holds up and where it snaps, and separate the breaks that actually matter for what my teacher used it for from the ones that are just nitpicking. I'm specifically curious about a few things: whether the analogy can even tell the difference between "this one instance was hard for me" and "every instance is hard for every algorithm," why it never seems to mention that real solvers crack huge hard problems all the time in practice, and whether the felt obviousness of the asymmetry is doing any actual evidential work or just restating the conjecture in a cozy way. Then the verdict — does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the whole thing? And if the everyday-puzzle genre is just doomed here, is there a better analogy you'd swap in that a sophomore could follow, or is the honest answer that there isn't one? Sorry for the essay, I just want to get this right before thursday.
