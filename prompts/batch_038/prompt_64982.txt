Hi — I'm a retired soil chemist, and in the last couple of years I've fallen hard for early modern philosophy. Berkeley's Querist has got me stuck. Please play the part of a professional philosopher who knows the 18th-century money debates cold, and think carefully rather than reaching for the usual Berkeley cliches.

Write a dialogue — a dozen or so exchanges — between (1) a Querist-type who really means it when Berkeley asks whether the true idea of money "be not altogether that of a ticket or counter" and whether money "be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry," and (2) a hard-money merchant who thinks that whole way of talking is a recipe for ruin. Begin with two or three lines of brainstorming about where the actual fault line sits, then go into the dialogue.

Here's the wrinkle I want them to fight over. A closed little island: one public counter-office, one foreign ship a year. The office doubles the counters twice in ten years — prices dont move, and the idle half of the island starts draining bogs and planting. Then it doubles them a third time, and prices jump by something like 40 percent while most of the new counters just sit in gentry coffers. The Querist has to explain why the first two doublings were harmless and the third wasnt, without giving up the ticket theory. The merchant has to explain the first two without conceding that metal content was beside the point all along.

Both should be at the top of their game — no straw men, and they must answer each others actual moves rather than trade manifestos. End on the one question that stays unresolved; my hunch is its about what "proportioned to industry" can even mean when nobody can measure industry except in counters.
