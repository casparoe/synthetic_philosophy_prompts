I'm a third-year PhD student in history, currently in our department's interdisciplinary methods sequence, which ends with each of us presenting a chapter of our own work to the whole cohort and the faculty who run it. My chapter defends a fairly combative methodological thesis, and I want to walk into the room knowing exactly where it will be attacked.

The thesis, in brief:

1. A claim that some outcome was historically inevitable is not a claim about how likely that outcome was. It is a claim about robustness: that the outcome would have obtained across a wide class of feasible interventions on the antecedent conditions, where the width of that class is what does the real work. Inevitability, on my account, is a modal-dispositional property of a situation, and — unlike probability — it is in principle estimable by simulation, provided one has a model of the system and an explicit statement of the intervention class.

2. The familiar rule that counterfactuals must involve minimal rewrites of the actual past is standardly justified as a plausibility constraint. I argue that this is the wrong justification and a costly one. The rule is really a comparability constraint: one holds as much fixed as possible so that differences in outcome are attributable to the intervention rather than to collateral changes in the causal structure. Plausibility is a by-product, not the aim.

3. The standard historicist objection — that counterfactuals are unfalsifiable and therefore not history — equivocates between epistemic indeterminacy (we cannot determine the truth-value) and semantic indeterminacy (there is no truth-value). Practitioners assert the first and conclude the second, and most of the rhetorical force of the objection depends on that slide.

4. Consequence: the great-men-versus-structures dispute is largely a disguised disagreement about how wide the intervention class is. Structuralists intervene narrowly on individual agents; voluntarists intervene widely. Neither side ever states its class explicitly, which is why the debate never terminates.

The audience will be about fifteen people, and it is not a philosophy audience. Roughly: six or seven quantitative historical sociologists and cliometricians who run agent-based and comparative-historical models and who think in terms of identification, estimands, and sensitivity analysis; two narrative historians of nineteenth-century Europe who regard formalization as a category error and will be courteous but immovable; one visiting philosopher of science who works on interventionist accounts of causation and who will be the most technically dangerous person in the room; and the remainder graduate students of mixed methods who mostly defer to the faculty.

Please write me a prep sheet rather than a defense of my thesis: the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this room — not the ones a generalist philosophy seminar would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do if I answered it badly. For each, give me (a) the objection phrased the way that particular subgroup would phrase it, in their own vocabulary; (b) a reply I can deliver in about two minutes; (c) what I should concede up front, before the concession is extracted from me; and (d) an honest note on where the reply is soft — where it amounts to a redescription, a promissory note about future work, or a change of subject.

I would like the answer to be committal. Tell me which of the four claims above is load-bearing and which I could abandon without losing the chapter; rank the objections by genuine force rather than by how often they are voiced; and if one of my replies is a dodge, say that it is a dodge. Please do not pad this with a reading list — I know the literature on counterfactuals and on historiography, and what I need is the local shape of this particular exchange.

Finally, include the question I am hoping nobody asks: the one objection that, if it comes from the philosopher of causation in the second row, I do not presently have an answer to. And if you think my framing is itself confused — if claim 2 or claim 3 rests on a distinction that will not survive contact with how these people actually talk, or if a prep sheet for a room like this cannot be written honestly without seeing the chapter itself — say so plainly at the top rather than producing something diplomatic around it.
