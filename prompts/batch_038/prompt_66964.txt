Last month I attended a departmental finds seminar as a visitor and took detailed notes on an hour and a half of disagreement about a single deposit. I am not an archaeologist, I have no stake in the dispute, and I never formed a view about who was winning. I would like the argument adjudicated as it actually went.

The deposit, as it was described. Two seasons of excavation at a peat-cutting site produced forty-three socketed bronze spearheads and nothing else: no ingots, no scrap, no tools, no ornaments, no pottery, no bone. They lay in three tight clusters within about two metres of one another, in a waterlogged peat lens at the foot of an escarpment, with no settlement, enclosure, field system, or burial within four hundred metres. Dating places all three clusters within roughly fifty years of each other, in the middle centuries of the second millennium BCE. Every one of the forty-three is bent: the blade deformed sideways at its junction with the socket, always toward the same face, at angles clustering tightly around ninety degrees, with sharp cracking at the folds rather than the rounded stretching a slow deformation would leave. None shows wear consistent with use.

Dr. Vogt holds that the spearheads were deliberately disabled and then deposited as a votive offering — a transfer to recipients who are not there to receive them. Dr. Marr holds that the assemblage is a metal cache awaiting the crucible and never retrieved, that the bending is incidental to bundling, and that the appeal to ritual terminates inquiry rather than advancing it.

The exchange went roughly as follows.

1. Marr: before positing intention, exclude the mundane. Metres of overlying peat compressing thin-section bronze is a known agent of deformation, and parsimony says to begin there.
2. Vogt: compaction has no preferred direction relative to the artefact's own geometry. These bends are all at the same point on the object, all toward the same face, all near ninety degrees, and single-event rather than gradual, judging by the cracking. Substrate pressure does not produce that pattern. Hands do.
3. Marr: I concede the bending was deliberate — but deliberate is not sacred. You bend a blade so that forty-three of them fit into a hide bundle; you bend it to sort a batch; above all you bend it so that whoever picks it up cannot use it. Each is practical and each predicts precisely your evidence. Your argument has a gap in it: from someone did this on purpose to someone did this for the gods, with nothing supplied in between.
4. Vogt: every disablement account leaves the metal recoverable. You bend it and keep it, or bend it and melt it. This metal went into saturated ground with no settlement near it and no reason to return, and stayed. A costly, non-recoverable, tightly selective transfer with no practical return is what giving something to invisible recipients looks like in the stratigraphy.
5. Marr: "Selective" is unearned. A founder's cache of newly cast spearheads is also nothing but spearheads. Both hypotheses predict a homogeneous assemblage, so homogeneity cannot discriminate between them; you are scoring a shared prediction as though it were a private one. And your cost argument assumes the depositors experienced the loss as you would. In an economy without money, withdrawing prestige metal from circulation may be exactly how wealth is made visible: the loss is the display, and the display is social, not theological. Grant that and your offering collapses into conspicuous expenditure, which requires no gods.
6. Vogt: then you have conceded the substantive claim — that this was a deliberate, non-utilitarian, non-recoverable transfer. Whether the recipients were ancestors, deities, or rival claimants is a further question about belief; the signature in the ground is the same in each case, and that signature is what I am inferring.
7. Marr: that is a switch. At move four, invisible recipients were what explained the absence of return; now social display explains it and the recipients have become optional. A hypothesis that survives by relocating to whatever the alternatives have not yet covered is not being confirmed but insulated. Nor have you secured your three events: three clusters within fifty years, in mobile peat, is compatible with one cache split across three pits, or with a single deposit reworked by flow.
8. Vogt: accept a single event. But note that none of the forty-three carries use-wear. These were not withdrawn from service at the end of a working life; on the most economical reading they were cast for this. Manufacture-for-immediate-destruction is unintelligible as a commercial arrangement — one does not cast stock one intends to write off at once — and wholly intelligible as offering the best one has.
9. Marr: that inference runs the other way. Unworn blades are what an unfinished batch looks like. Had they been taken out of service you would expect edge damage, resharpening scars, repairs, or owner's marks — the biography a weapon accumulates while it belongs to somebody. Brand-new, generic metal is the signature of stock, not of service.
10. Vogt: then explain the place. Caches intended to come back go into dry, findable ground near where people live. Across this region the metal you are describing turns up repeatedly in bogs, springs, and channels, at the same locales over centuries. Retrieval failure is random; repeated place-selection is not.
11. Marr: wet ground preserves what dry ground destroys, and preserves it where modern drainage has not cut through. The regional preference for water may be a property of our sampling frame rather than of their behaviour. You are reading a taphonomic filter as a religious map.

At that point the session ran out of time.

Please work from the evidence exactly as I have set it out; I am not asking for parallels from other sites, for the state of the literature, or for any references. What I want is: (a) a rating of each of the eleven numbered moves on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating; (b) an account, for each of the two, of where they concede more than the argument forces them to and where they defend ground they should have given up; (c) a determination of which of the disputed claims — deliberate bending, non-recoverability, single versus multiple depositional events, manufacture-for-deposition, preference for wet locales — the described evidence genuinely settles and which it leaves underdetermined, and, for each underdetermined claim, the specific further hypothesis it fails to exclude; (d) what both of them are missing, including any third explanation of this deposit that the evidence as described would treat differently from the two on the table; and (e) two or three analyses plausibly runnable on these forty-three objects and their surrounding matrix, together with the result from each that would favour which side.
