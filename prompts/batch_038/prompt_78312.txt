For the past two years I have kept a fairly disciplined journal, mostly as a way of working out what I actually think about things, and somewhere along the line I stopped trusting the exercise. The pattern is always the same. On a given evening I can say, quickly and with real conviction, why I turned down an invitation, why a remark from a colleague rankled me, or whether I was in a bad mood that afternoon. Then, weeks later, evidence turns up — a message I sent, a comment from my partner, an entry I had forgotten writing — showing that my confident account was at best a reconstruction. I have no academic training in philosophy or psychology; I read around the edges of the subject and think on my own, which may be why I cannot get past the following problem. I would like it explained to me properly, as a conceptual matter, rather than handed a survey of who has argued what. I am explicitly not after a reading list or a list of names.

The problem is that I cannot tell whether introspection is failing at a job it could in principle do, or whether the whole image of a job is mistaken. Working on my own, I have come up with three pictures, and I would like you to tell me whether they are genuinely different pictures or whether I have merely described the same view three ways. The first I think of as the window: my mental states are settled facts about me, introspection lets me see straight through to them, and when it works the fact that I am looking does not enter into the account at all. The second is the mirror: the facts are settled just the same, but the reflecting surface is warped, fogged, or badly angled, so what I get back is a distorted image of something determinate. The third is the sculptor's studio: there is no finished object sitting there before I look, and the act of attending to my irritation, or of putting it into words in a journal, is part of what makes it the particular irritation it turns out to be. The first two pictures make my errors ordinary empirical errors. The third seems to make some of them not errors at all, and I cannot see how it is supposed to avoid collapsing into the claim that anything I sincerely say about myself is true.

What I want is an explanation of the conceptual issue that separates these pictures, and of what actually turns on choosing between them. Please address the hard cases directly, because I suspect they are where the pictures come apart rather than where they agree. Start with pain or with a sudden headache: if it hurts, it hurts, and there seems to be no room at all for a gap between how things seem to me and how they are, which is exactly the room the mirror picture depends on. Then take the well-known finding that people asked to explain a choice between two indistinguishable options will invent a reason and defend it even after being shown the choice was arranged; is that a mirror failure, or is it evidence that there was no reason there to be mirrored? Then take my own moods, where I am often blind until someone points it out and it immediately becomes obvious — why should an obstacle disappear the moment another person names it, if the obstacle were only in my detecting apparatus? And finally, the difficulty that seems to me to paralyze the whole question: if looking at my own mind can change what is there, how could I ever check, since any check would itself be an act of looking? It is like trying to find out whether the light in a refrigerator goes off when the door is closed. Given that, is there any evidence that could discriminate between the window and the sculptor's studio, or is the choice being made on purely conceptual grounds? Two further things I would like settled. I keep encountering the phrase privileged access and have never been able to tell what it is supposed to guarantee — please separate out the distinct claims that get bundled into it, and say which of them survive being regularly and confidently wrong about my own motives. And if the honest answer is that different stretches of the mental call for different pictures — that sensation behaves one way, mood another, and reasons for action a third — say so, and explain what would make that a principled division rather than a retreat. If one of my three pictures is simply confused, I would rather be told that plainly and shown where it breaks.
