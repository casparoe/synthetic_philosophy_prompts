I am a Sixth Form student in England writing an Extended Project Qualification dissertation, and I would like your help with what my supervisor tells me is the real philosophical work of the project, rather than the part I originally thought was.

My question is: when a machine says that it is having an experience, under what conditions should that report count as evidence that it is? My supervisor's objection is that I cannot answer that until I have specified what a theory of consciousness must deliver in order to be usable for that purpose at all. The specification, she says, is the dissertation. That is what I want you to write: the desiderata for an adequate theory of consciousness, relativised to my purpose.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of mind who has supervised undergraduate dissertations and is willing to be exacting, but who is explicit about where disagreements are substantive and where they are merely verbal. Begin by thinking carefully: before you produce the list, write a short paragraph identifying which phenomena in this area are genuinely contested and which only look contested because different camps use the same words for different things. I would rather have a small number of sharp requirements than a long list of platitudes. If you think one of the requirements I suggest below is confused, or is a prejudice of a particular camp rather than a demand on any adequate theory, say so and replace it. I would rather be corrected than flattered.

The angle that makes my project unusual, and that the requirements must serve: I am not trying to settle the metaphysics of consciousness. I need a theory that will support third-person assessment of a system whose inner life I cannot share, and whose functional organisation I can in principle inspect completely from outside. So please divide the requirements into two tiers.

The first tier should be requirements that bind any theory of consciousness whatever it says about phenomenal properties: what it must explain about our reports, what it must preserve about the apparent directness of introspection, what it must do with the cases where report and behaviour come apart, and what it must be consistent with from the study of perception, anaesthesia, sleep, and disorders of awareness.

The second tier should be requirements that bite specifically on illusionism: the family of views holding that we are systematically misled about possessing intrinsic phenomenal properties, and that the appearance of such properties is something to be explained rather than taken at face value. For this tier I want you to address at least the following, though please add others you think matter more:

- The demand that the view explain why the illusion is compelling, stable and near-universal rather than local and easily corrected, given that we usually call something an illusion only when it can be dispelled.
- The demand that it not smuggle the thing it denies back in under a new name at the point where it explains why we seem to have it.
- The demand that it say what introspection is actually getting right or tracking, if not what it seems to be tracking.
- The distinction between being under an illusion, misdescribing a genuine state in misleading vocabulary, and being outright deluded. I suspect this distinction does a great deal of work and that views differ over whether they may collapse it. Please say whether an adequate theory must respect it.
- Whether a theory in the second tier inherits an obligation to explain everything the realist about phenomenal properties explains, or whether it is entitled to explain less and call the remainder a confusion.

Then I need three things from you about the list as a whole. First, tell me which pairs of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, with the reasoning; in particular, whether preserving the apparent authority of first-person reports is compatible with applying the theory to a system I can only inspect from outside. If those two cannot both be met, my project is in trouble and I need to know now rather than in March. Second, tell me which single requirement eliminates the largest number of candidate theories. Third, mark clearly which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp.

Finally, and briefly, assess four approaches against the list: global workspace accounts, higher-order thought accounts, predictive accounts on which experience is a form of controlled inference about the causes of one's own sensory input, and integrated information accounts. For each, say which requirement it fails, or meets only by stipulation. Keep this to a few sentences per approach; the list is the deliverable and the assessment is only there so I can see the list biting.

Please number each requirement and give it a short name, so that I can refer to them later in my text. If your honest conclusion is that no current theory satisfies the specification, say so plainly and identify the requirement responsible. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations: my school library and my supervisor will supply sources. What I need from you is the reasoning.
