Two years ago I was made redundant from a logistics firm, moved back to the town I grew up in, and signed up for an evening class in eighteenth-century Irish writing chiefly to get out of the house on weeknights. That is how I came to read George Berkeley's *Querist* — the pamphlet of several hundred numbered queries he published in the 1730s, after he was made Bishop of Cloyne, mostly about why Ireland was poor and what might be done about it — and that, somewhat to my own surprise, is how I ended up drafting the economic rationale for a town currency which our council has now actually funded. We launch it on Saturday at the market: the first two thousand notes go over a trestle table on the steps of the old corn exchange, and I have been asked to say a few words before the first one changes hands. Six minutes, spoken aloud, standing. In front of me will be perhaps a hundred and fifty people — the shopkeepers who agreed to take the notes, several of them reluctantly; two councillors; a reporter from the county paper who described the scheme in print last month as a compliment to the town's insolvency; a primary-school class promised a trip to the sweet shop afterwards; and, in the front row, my father, who was a bank manager for forty years, who has told me at length that this is Monopoly money, and who is nevertheless the man who explained to me when I was eleven that almost none of the money in the world was ever metal in anyone's hands. I want the speech to reach him without arguing with him.

The reason I want it built on Berkeley rather than on the usual local-currency patter is that his account of money is stranger and better than the patter. Two things about our scheme matter for this. First, each note is redeemable one for one at the credit union, and I need the room to understand that the reserve exists precisely so that nobody ever has to touch it: if everyone redeems their notes we have not succeeded, we have merely given people back their own money with extra steps. Second, the notes carry a quarterly stamp — a holder has to buy a small stamp every three months to keep them valid — because we want them spent, not kept. As for Berkeley, from what I took from the class and the text: money is not wealth and never was, and no people can be called poor where the common sort are well fed, clothed and lodged; there has never been an industrious nation that was poor or an idle one that was rich; money is useful only so far as it stirs up industry and lets people participate in one another's labour; considered in itself it is neither a commodity, nor a standard, nor a measure, nor a pledge, but — this is his own phrase and the only one I want quoted — "a ticket or counter"; he even asks whether the denominations could be kept and commerce carried on with the bullion gone altogether. He insists that smaller sums circulating quickly are equivalent to larger sums circulating slowly, which is exactly the case for our stamp. And, the part that struck me hardest, he thinks appetite and fashion rather than reason move people: fashions create wants, wants set industry going, and he wonders aloud whether the legislature ought to take a hand in making fashions instead of leaving the business to tailors and vintners. Please do not attribute anything further to him beyond what I have said here, and do not supply references or suggested further reading; I need the speech, not the scholarship.

So: write the speech as it will be delivered, around seven hundred and fifty words, with at most one bracketed stage direction (the moment I hold a note up). It must not say that the town is now richer by the amount we printed, that the notes are backed by anything of value, that they are anything like gold, or that they will hold or gain value — the reporter is listening for exactly that sentence. It must not rest on community spirit, togetherness or trust, because if the notes depended on how we feel about one another we would already be finished; Berkeley's claim is colder and, I think, more encouraging, which is that they work if they change what people do on a Tuesday. It must say plainly what happens if nobody spends them, and make that a consequence of the theory rather than an embarrassment to it. It must explain in one sentence a shopkeeper can repeat to a puzzled customer why she should take the note, without using words like velocity, multiplier, liquidity or seigniorage. It should contain at least one line that my father would recognise as simply true of the business he was in for forty years, delivered as an observation and not as a gotcha. He was a bishop and it shows — the argument is moral, about drones and idle hands rather than about technique — and I would like a trace of that register without it turning into a sermon; name him once, at most twice, and never in a way that warns the room a lecture is coming. And I would like it to end on something other than an invitation to feel good about ourselves: at the front of the pamphlet there is a verse from Ezekiel about the high tree being brought down and the dry tree made to flourish, and I keep thinking of it in connection with the three empty units on Market Street — use that image in your own words if you can make it land without being solemn.
