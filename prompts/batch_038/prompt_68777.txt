Three weeks from now I'm leading the 40-minute ethics session for the new volunteer intake at the hospice where I've been driving people to appointments and sitting with patients for about six years. I've never taken a philosophy class — I just read Kant on and off by myself — but I've decided I want to actually argue his side on lying: that lying isn't ever permitted, and that the whole murderer-at-the-door thing is not the knockout blow everybody assumes it is.

The room will be roughly fifteen new volunteers. Nearly all retirees, a handful of people in their thirties, nobody with any philosophy beyond whatever they got at school. Several of them spent years at home caring for a husband or a parent with advanced dementia before they came to us, so their views about lying to someone who's confused are personal and settled, not theoretical. The person I'm most worried about is a ward nurse who's doing the session as well. She's done some person-centred care training and talks about joining the patient's reality instead of correcting them, about a fib as a kindness, and she'll bring real cases with names attached. There's also a retired headteacher who enjoys trapping you with escalating examples.

Here's roughly my argument, so you know what they'll be aiming at:

1. Kant's reason isn't rule worship. A lie only works because the other person lets you into their thinking — they take what you say and build on it. So when I lie, I've borrowed their trust to steer them somewhere I picked for them. I'm using them like an instrument instead of dealing with them as a person.

2. On consequences, you never actually know what a lie sets in motion. My memory of the essay where he answers that Frenchman Benjamin Constant is that he says something like: if you tell the truth and the worst happens, that's on the murderer, but if you lie and your lie is what gets your friend caught, you're answerable for it. Correct me if I've mangled that.

3. The bit I think rescues the whole position: Kant never asks you to say something false. You don't have to answer at all, and you can say something true that lets the other person draw the wrong conclusion on their own. So the murderer at the door doesn't force a lie on you, it forces you to be careful and to keep your hands off the falsehood.

What I want is the prep sheet for that room. The objecions they'll actually raise, in the order they'll come and sorted by how much damage each one does — not the ones a philosophy seminar would raise, the ones a retired carer and a hospice nurse raise, phrased the way they'd phrase them, including the nurse's own vocabulary where she has one. For each: what I should give away immediately, what I should refuse to give away, a reply that fits in about two minutes, and where that reply is softest. Then tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and the one thing that has to survive the evening for the talk to have been worth giving.

Be blunt with me. If you think this thesis is simply a loser in front of this particular room and I'd do better to defend something narrower, say that first rather than handing me eight objections I can comfortably swat. And if any of my three points is my own invention rather than Kant's, tell me which one — nobody's ever checked my reading and I'd much rather hear it from you than from the nurse. Not after a reading list or book recomendations, just the prep.
