I am a retired plant physiologist. For thirty-eight years I measured stomatal conductance and drought response in crop plants; I retired four years ago and have taken up philosophy since with an intensity my former colleagues find mildly alarming. I now chair the scientific review panel of a small private foundation that funds translational work on conditions commercial sponsors ignore. The trustees chose me partly because I am not from medicine and hold no conflicts of interest. The panel has five members — two clinicians, a health economist, a patient representative with no scientific training, and me — and none of us has any training in ethics. We meet twice a year for ninety minutes and allocate roughly €1.2 million across twelve to eighteen applications; individual asks run from €50,000 to €700,000. The trustees accept our decisions unless the written justification is incoherent.

You should treat me as your client and yourself as an expert: a philosopher with a serious working knowledge of population ethics, distributive justice, and the practical literature on allocating scarce research money, who has done this particular translation before — turning a contested principle into something a committee of non-specialists can run without quietly misusing it. Think carefully before you write. Begin by brainstorming the candidate principles that could govern a decision of this kind and the way each might be operationalised, then discard. Show me the discarding briefly, because that is where I expect the real content to be.

The difficulty recurs every cycle. Some applications are monster-like in the sense the philosophers use: a very small number of patients stand to gain so much from the money that, on a straightforwardly aggregating view, the entire cycle should go to them. Others are the mirror image: thousands of people stand to gain a modest amount each. We have no framework, and we decide by who speaks last.

Two years ago we gave 61 per cent of a cycle to a programme addressing a condition affecting eleven patients in this country. The science was sound and the programme has since delivered a real result. I remain convinced the decision was made badly, and I want a procedure that would have exposed the flaw. I deliberately will not tell you what I take the flaw to have been — partly because I want an independent diagnosis, partly because I am not sure of it myself. The facts of that meeting were these. The parents of four of the eleven patients attended and spoke for twenty minutes. The applicants supplied their own estimate of the benefit per patient and we accepted it without asking how it had been derived. We considered this application last, after ranking the others, and so never ranked it against them. And nobody asked what the eleven would look like in five years had we declined.

For each application, the dossier gives us only the following: the number of people affected in this country and the incidence; the natural history and severity of the condition; the applicants' own estimate of the probability of technical success within three years; the amount requested; the applicants' own estimate of the benefit per person if the work succeeds; and a statement of what other public or commercial funders are doing in the area. We cannot commission evidence, and we cannot ask applicants for anything outside this format. Every step of your procedure must be answerable from those fields and nothing else.

One further thing. I have been working with a distinction of my own, and I want your procedure either to preserve it or to tell me plainly that it will not survive. I think we run together two features of a claim that behave quite differently. One is the magnitude of the benefit to each person who receives it — how much better a single life goes if the work succeeds. The other is the concentration of the benefit — how few people it must be divided among to produce that magnitude. A claim can be extreme in the first respect without being extreme in the second, and the reverse. My working hypothesis is that the resistance we feel in the monster case — the sense that one enormous claimant must not be allowed to absorb everything — is really a response to concentration, and that magnitude on its own is innocent: were a thousand people each to stand to gain enormously, I suspect we would not feel the same pull to restrain the claim. If that is right, the procedure should constrain concentration and leave magnitude unregulated. If it is wrong, say so, say where the intuition actually bites, and say what should be constrained instead. I am not attached to the distinction; I am attached to getting it right.

What I want, in this order.

First, a prose section of no more than about nine hundred words in which you (a) state the normative principle the procedure implements, in terms a layperson can follow; (b) say which rival principles you considered and what specifically you discarded from each, and why; (c) give your diagnosis of the failure two years ago and name the question that would have exposed it; and (d) rule on my magnitude/concentration distinction. Take a definite position on each. I want a committed answer, not a survey: I would rather receive a wrong procedure I can argue with than a balanced one I cannot use. If you think my distinction is confused, say that it is confused and explain the confusion; do not preserve it out of courtesy to the chair. Do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I cannot check them and I do not want them. You may name a philosopher's device where a name helps a non-specialist recognise it, but build nothing on authority. I want the reasons, not the pedigree.

Second, the procedure itself as a single JSON object with exactly this schema:

- `governing_principle` (string): the rule the procedure implements, in two or three sentences.
- `principle_committed_over` (string): the main rival you rejected, and the single decisive reason.
- `past_failure` (object): `diagnosis` (string); `missing_question` (string, phrased as it would be read aloud in the meeting); `encoded_in_step` (integer, the `step_id`).
- `magnitude_vs_concentration` (object): `ruling` (one of `survives_as_stated`, `survives_with_revision`, `collapses`); `explanation` (string); `what_is_constrained_instead` (string, or `nothing`).
- `steps` (array, at most nine objects): `step_id` (integer, order of application); `question` (string, phrased to be read aloud); `sufficient_answer` (string, what the dossier must contain for the step to pass); `fails_when` (string); `type` (one of `factual`, `judgment`, `values`); `decision_rule` (string, what happens on pass and on fail, including whether the procedure terminates here); `handoff` (string, and if `type` is `judgment` or `values`, who decides and by what mechanism — vote, chair's ruling, referral to the trustees); `failure_mode` (string, how the step misfires and how an applicant could game it); `time_minutes` (integer). The `time_minutes` values must sum to ninety or less. At least one step must be of type `values`, and at least one must terminate the procedure with a no.
- `case_runs` (array of three objects, `case_id` `A`, `B`, `C`): `verdict` (one of `fund_in_full`, `fund_at_reduced_scope`, `reject`, `defer_for_information`); `trace` (array of strings, one per step, recording what the step returned); `decisive_step` (integer); `confidence` (one of `high`, `medium`, `low`); `cuts_against` (string, which panel member's stated preference the verdict disappoints, or `none`); `weakest_link` (string, the step most likely to be wrong on this case and why).
- `limits` (array of strings): what the procedure cannot settle, and the kinds of application about which it is silent.
- `gaming_resistances` (array of strings): concrete amendments to our dossier instructions.

Third, run the procedure on these three applications from the coming cycle. Treat the numbers as fixed inputs, not as estimates to be revised.

Case A. An adeno-associated virus gene therapy for a paediatric neurodegenerative disorder that is invariably fatal before age six. Nine children in this country are known to be affected and roughly four are born with it each year. On the applicants' account, a therapy delivered before symptom onset would be expected to preserve near-normal development. Ask: €690,000, 58 per cent of the cycle. Applicants put the probability of a demonstrable therapy within three years at 20 per cent and describe the work as a prerequisite no commercial firm will undertake because the market is too small. The dossier says nothing about what becomes of the work if we decline, beyond that assertion.

Case B. A multi-site implementation programme to reduce post-operative delirium in patients over eighty undergoing hip-fracture repair: orientation, sleep protection, early mobilisation, medication review, family involvement. About 4,000 such operations a year nationally. The applicants hold that the package prevents delirium in something like one patient in eight who would otherwise have it, and that an episode costs the survivor a few months of function and some fraction of a year of life. Ask: €145,000, 12 per cent of the cycle. Probability of success high; the difficulty is implementation, not discovery. Two clinicians on the panel described this as unglamorous work at our last meeting and I expect them to vote against it on instinct.

Case C. A programme to develop a therapy that would halt, though not reverse, vision loss in adults with an inherited retinal dystrophy. About 400 people in this country are affected, most losing useful sight over ten to twenty years from onset. Ask: €420,000, 35 per cent of the cycle; applicants put the probability of a usable therapy within three years at 50 per cent. Two features must be treated explicitly. A mid-sized pharmaceutical firm has a compound in preclinical development against the same target and has told the applicants' institution that it expects to reach trials within five years, so our money buys time rather than existence. And I am personally attached to this application: I know two of the affected families and I think the applicants are unusually good scientists. If your procedure rejects it, say so in one flat sentence rather than softening the verdict into a deferral, and tell me in the `weakest_link` field whether you think my attachment is the reason the verdict is unwelcome to me or whether it is unwelcome for a better reason.

Finally, be candid about the procedure's own failure modes. Tell me where it will misfire on a real application, where a determined applicant who reads our published criteria could game it, and which distinctions from the underlying theory you have dropped as unusable in a ninety-minute meeting and what we lose by dropping them.
