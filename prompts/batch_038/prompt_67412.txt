Last month I sat through a workshop session in which a prison historian told the room that the archival record does not support the picture of the nineteenth-century penitentiary most of us absorbed from *Discipline and Punish*: timetables were honored in the breach, hierarchical observation was chronically understaffed, examinations were perfunctory, and the cellular regime spread at least as much from budget pressure, overcrowding, and administrative convenience as from any logic of docility. My reply at the time was the one everyone gives — that Foucault's claim was never that the machine ran smoothly, and that his treatment of delinquency explicitly folds the prison's documented failures into what the apparatus accomplishes. Since then I have become less certain that this reply is a defense rather than a surrender, and I would like the case against my position laid out properly. The position is this: Foucault's account of disciplinary power is a general analytic rather than a local history. Power on this picture is exercised rather than possessed, capillary rather than lodged in a sovereign; it produces its objects — the delinquent, the hysteric, the schoolchild, the soldier — instead of repressing pre-existing ones; and the techniques that carry it, namely hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, the examination, and the distribution of bodies across space and time, travel promiscuously among the prison, the school, the barracks, the workshop, and the hospital. Knowledge of these populations and the mechanisms acting on them are mutually constitutive, so there is no neutral observational standpoint from which to describe either. The commitment I most want pressure applied to is the last one: institutional failure is not evidence against the analysis, because the gap between the reformers' stated aims and the effects actually produced is precisely what the analysis was built to expose.

So what would count against it? I want a map of exposure, not a survey of Foucault criticism. Please address four things. First, which historical or archival findings would be decisive rather than merely inconvenient — for instance, an institution that implemented the full battery of techniques and generated neither a new body of knowledge about its population nor any measurable normalization of conduct; or a colonial prison system in which the metropolitan reform vocabulary was absent and the exploitative logic explicit, which seems to reintroduce exactly the interested agents the account is meant to dissolve; or evidence that a given technique diffused along identifiable chains of identifiable decision-makers. Second, the conceptual defeaters: whether the productivity thesis can be distinguished at all from the bare functionalist claim that whatever persisted must have functioned; whether coupling power and knowledge leaves Foucault any ground from which to treat his own archival findings as knowledge rather than as further effects of power; and whether the absence of a subject renders the account unfalsifiable in principle rather than merely difficult to test. Third, what the disciplinary analysis predicts that its rivals do not — the juridical-sovereign model, the instrumentalist class account, the rational-choice story about reform as cost-efficiency, and the straightforward humanitarian narrative. Fourth, how much of the position has already been conceded by Foucault's own later work, in which governmentality and the conduct of conduct take over from discipline, and in which the final volumes of the history of sexuality, published in 1984, the year of his death, describe individuals working on themselves in a way that looks a great deal like the agency the earlier account appeared to foreclose. Is that a retraction in all but name, or a compatible extension?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say explicitly which ones a serious Foucauldian would concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss as beside the point, and on what grounds. For my own part, what would move me most is not a critique of Foucault's politics but a specification of what the archive would have to look like for the productivity thesis to be false; if no such specification can be given, say so plainly and then tell me whether that is a defect. A genealogical diagnosis is not a law, and it may be that the standards for falsification differ for a view of this kind, or that a certain measure of insulation is the price of its ambition — but I want that judgment argued rather than assumed. Please write the answer in American English, and do not pad it with biographical context or a summary of the book: assume I have read it closely and am asking only about where it is vulnerable.
