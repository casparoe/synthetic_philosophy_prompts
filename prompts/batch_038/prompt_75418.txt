You are a philosopher of medicine with serious expertise in the naturalism/normativism debate over the concepts of health and disease, and you are also careful about the methodology of classification. Begin by brainstorming: list the dimensions along which the cases below differ — statistical typicality, evolutionary function, harm, etiology, the subject's own standpoint, responsiveness to treatment, the identity of the classifier — and say which of these a principle could make load-bearing. Only then proceed.

I teach a seminar on the concept of disease and have arrived at the following verdicts. Treat them as data to be systematized, not as a defense of any existing theory.

1. Untreated asymptomatic hypertension in a 48-year-old man: a disease, though he feels nothing and would likely feel nothing for a decade.
2. A pathogenic BRCA1 variant in a healthy 30-year-old woman: not a disease but a risk — despite being a deviation from the species-typical genome with a very large effect on expected outcomes, and despite properly licensing prophylactic surgery.
3. A glycated hemoglobin in the band officially designated as prediabetes: not a disease either. I cannot articulate any principled difference between this and case 1.
4. Grief six weeks after the death of a spouse, meeting the symptom and impairment checklist used for depressive episodes: not a disease.
5. Precisely the same symptoms and the same impairment, arising without bereavement in someone whose life is going well: a disease.
6. Profound congenital deafness in a person flourishing within a signing community who does not want it corrected: not a disease. Identical hearing loss acquired at age eight after meningitis in a hearing family: a disease.
7. A healthy child at the first percentile for height who would be taller if treated: not a disease, notwithstanding that height is socially rewarded and that treatment works.
8. When a guidelines body lowered the blood-pressure threshold and millions of people thereby became patients, I think those people were already diseased — the committee tracked a fact. When homosexuality was declassified, by contrast, nobody was cured: the committee had simply been wrong, and the change was a correction rather than a discovery.
9. My problem case, on which I keep changing my mind: a heritable variant, recently characterized, that abolishes the capacity for fear. Bearers show no detectable pathology on examination, do modestly worse than average on statistical life expectancy because of accidents, mostly like being as they are, and in the environment in which the variant became common left more surviving offspring than non-bearers.

Cases 1 through 3, and the two halves of case 8, are the ones I take a satisfying account to owe me an explanation of rather than a dismissal; case 9 is the one whose verdict I want derived rather than assumed.

Please deliver: one candidate principle, or at most two, stated precisely enough to be operational — as a set of necessary and sufficient conditions or a prioritized set of clauses — with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. State the principle's verdicts on case 9 and on two further cases I have not raised: stage-two chronic kidney disease in an eighty-year-old whose renal function is entirely typical for her age; and a psychiatric diagnosis that responds reliably to treatment but has no identified biological marker and no consensus on its boundaries. Then report honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot save. Where a simpler principle overturns one of my verdicts while a more complex one saves all of them, tell me which reflective equilibrium you recommend and what each costs.

Finally, take up the meta-question: do these cases show that the term is being asked to do two distinct jobs that no single analysis will discharge? If so, is the fault in the concept, in my verdicts, or in the assumption that one analysis is owed? And do not settle the matter by mapping my intuitions onto a familiar camp label — naturalist, normativist, hybrid — as though the label were an argument. If one of those camps does fit my verdicts, demonstrate it by deriving the verdicts from the theory.
