I am moderating the Q&A for a panel on the ethics of financial returns, and our guest is a philosopher who has written a book arguing that Aristotle's theory of money is not a primitive curiosity but a live alternative to price-based accounts of value. On her reading, need or demand is the real single standard that holds exchange together, money exists only by convention as a stand-in for it and can be altered or rendered useless at will, exchange requires a proportionate equality between unlike products, and interest is the mode of acquisition Aristotle condemns most sharply as contrary to nature, because its gain comes from the currency itself rather than from what currency was invented for.

Write me eight questions that press the argument rather than asking her to restate it. At minimum I want one on whether need can be measured across persons and across kinds of goods without quietly reintroducing prices as the measure; one on whether Aristotle's own concession that things so unlike cannot be commensurable in the strict sense gives away the game; one on how much of his critique of money-making survives if one rejects his teleology of natural ends and the claim that the wealth a household legitimately seeks is bounded; and one on whether the case against interest extends to other returns — dividends, rents, index funds — or collapses as soon as money's productive uses become indirect. For each question, give the answer she is most likely to give and the follow-up that makes that answer hard to maintain.

Close with a brief note marking which of your questions rest on readings of the texts that are genuinely disputed among specialists and which turn on objections the literature agrees Aristotle faces. I don't want a minority interpretation presented to an audience as though it were settled.
