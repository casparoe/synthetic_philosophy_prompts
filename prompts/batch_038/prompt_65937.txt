After thirty-seven years as a physicist in national metrology, I have taken up philosophy in retirement, and next month I shall present a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on automation and human flourishing. My thesis, in outline, is that skilled work is meaningful not because of what it produces, nor because of the income or recognition attached to it, but because it closes a loop: the worker acts, perceives the consequence of the action, and corrects, on a timescale short enough for the consequence to be attributable to the act. Automation that removes effort leaves such loops intact; automation that removes the loop cannot be compensated by leisure, by a guaranteed income, or by transferring the worker to a supervisory role, since supervision inherits the loop only in attenuated form — one watches a system that largely behaves itself. Policy should therefore be judged not by jobs preserved but by loops preserved: whether a redesigned process leaves a residual channel in which human judgement is causally efficacious and its effects attributable.

The room will be mixed, and hostile in several registers: two labour economists, who will demand an operational definition and press the historical claim that each wave of automation generates tasks I cannot foresee; a sociologist of work, for whom meaning is conferred through recognition, status and collective identity, and who will treat feedback loops as an engineer's category mistake; an Aristotelian perfectionist and a preferentist about well-being; a controls engineer from industry, who will call the proposal unimplementable; and a disability scholar, who will observe that friction and attribution are precisely what exclude people.

Prepare my question-period sheet: the eight objections I shall actually face from this audience, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased as its proponent would phrase it rather than as I would; for each, a reply deliverable in two minutes, a note on where that reply is softest, and what I ought to concede at the outset. Close with the question I most hope nobody asks.
