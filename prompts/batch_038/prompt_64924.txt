Read a piece by some philosopher-blogger trying to explain the anthropics debate to normies, and I want to know how much of it to trust. Their summary runs:

"SSA and SIA start from the same place: reason as if your own identity were drawn at random from a pool of observers. SSA takes the pool to be everyone who actually exists, past present and future, and that's what generates the Doomsday Argument — finding out you're somewhere around the 60 billionth human bumps up the probability that the pool is short. SIA instead says the pool is everyone who *could* exist, weighted by how likely each of them is to exist, so your own existence is evidence for a big world. That's why SIA kills the DA: the boost toward large worlds exactly cancels the birth-rank update, and your credence ends up where it started. The cost is Bostrom's presumptuous philosopher — because priors scale with observer count, a theory predicting a trillion times more observers than its rival comes out a trillion times more likely on no evidence at all, so SIA lets armchair philosophy settle scientific disputes. Bostrom's fix was to stick with SSA but narrow the reference class to observers who are your epistemic peers, which just trades one arbitrariness for another."

I've generally leaned SIA (thirder intutions, big-world priors, etc.) but the armchair thing bugs me. Can you go through this claim by claim and tell me what someone who actually works on anthropic reasoning would accept, what's a fair popular simplification, and what's just wrong? And does their last line survive once you fix the rest?
