My father was declared dead by neurologic criteria in a New Jersey hospital four months ago. My family invoked the state's provision requiring the attending physician to take a patient's religious beliefs into account before making that declaration; it did not help us, but it delayed the declaration by a day and produced a note in the chart, and it planted a question I have been trying to answer ever since: what sort of legal object is a death determination, exactly? Below is a draft essay.

DRAFT — "Two Registers"

The Uniform Determination of Death Act, a model statute approved in 1981 and since enacted by most states, defines death disjunctively. An individual who has sustained "irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brain stem" is dead; so is one who has sustained irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions. The usual gloss treats the two disjuncts as two windows onto a single underlying event, and the standard biological rationale supplies that event: the brain is what integrates the body into an organism as a whole, so its total and irreversible failure is the loss of the organism.

I think the gloss is a rationalization, and that the statute is not tracking one event at all. Consider the second disjunct as it operates in donation after circulatory death. There, a patient whose heart has stopped is declared dead a few minutes later while the brain remains anatomically intact. The declaration turns on no neurological fact; it turns on irreversibility, and in that setting irreversibility is secured not by physiology but by a prior agreement not to attempt resuscitation. The same arrested heart belongs to a dead person if the team has decided not to restart it and to a living person if the team is about to. At the level of the criterion, death is doing normative work.

Once this is visible in the circulatory disjunct, the neurologic one looks different too. The statute says all functions of the entire brain; the bedside examination asks about coma, brain-stem reflexes, and apnea. No one measures the hypothalamus. Critics have pressed this for decades, and defenders reply either that the statute means clinically ascertainable functions or that the loss of the brain's integrative role is what matters even where scattered tissue persists. Both replies concede the point: the operative content is set by what the determination is for, not by what the body is doing.

My proposal is that we stop reading the statute as a claim about a natural kind and read it instead as a jurisdictional instrument — closer to a discharge in bankruptcy than to a statement of fact. A discharge does not describe insolvency; it settles who may press claims against whom, on criteria that are part economic and part policy. A death determination likewise settles a bundle of things at once: whether support may be withdrawn without consent, whether an estate opens, whether withdrawing support is homicide or routine care, and whether organs may be procured. On this reading, brain death determinations are safe harbors with a biological trigger condition, not verdicts about organismic status.

The view has a cost I have not yet figured out how to pay. If a determination is a safe harbor, then a family that says he is not dead is not obviously making a factual error, and the physician's declaration loses the authority that makes it conclusive. The New Jersey accommodation, which I first took for a strange local quirk, becomes the honest version of what every state already does — and it is not clear that this is a recommendation.

END DRAFT
