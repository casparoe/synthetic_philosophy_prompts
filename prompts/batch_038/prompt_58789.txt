I TA an interdisciplinary seminar in the spring on comparing mass atrocities — half the students are history majors, half are philosophy or poli sci, and the first session always goes the same way: somebody puts two events side by side, somebody else says you can't do that, and then twenty minutes get spent arguing about what "can't" meant. The instructor wants a short diagnostic we can hand out in week one so people find out where they actually stand from their gut reactions to cases, before they've read anything and before they know the labels. Ten minutes max, no prep, and it has to sort them by what they believe rather than by what they call themselves. Could you write it? Eight or nine questions, each one a concrete situation with two or three answer options — something like a government that knowingly keeps a water-treatment system understaffed and foreseeably causes tens of thousands of deaths a year, versus a much smaller massacre carried out deliberately by named people following a plan; a museum wall text that lists death tolls in descending order; being asked on the record, at a public commemoration, whether event A was worse than event B; a comparison where one side's victims are your own grandparents' generation and the other's are strangers you've never thought about. Nothing should ask about a theory directly — no "do you think wrongness is commensurible?" — because everyone will just pick the sophisticated-sounding option and learn nothing about themselves.

Then I need a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named leaves, and I'd rather the tree be built out of the logical space first than out of a list of authors. The dimensions I care about are roughly: whether the badness of mass killing sits on one scale or on several that don't trade off against each other; whether numbers ever dominate every other feature; whether the perpetrator's state of mind changes the rank itself or only our attitude toward it; whether deaths caused by omission and by standing structures count as the same kind of thing as killings; whether the standing of the person doing the comparing matters — an outsider ranking other people's catastrophes versus a survivor ranking their own; whether refusing to compare is objectionable because of what it does to the victims or because of what it lets happen next; and whether an atrocity nobody remembers anymore is worse, or just differently bad. Leaves I'd like to see if they're reachable: a single-scale view where the tally basically settles it; a pluralist who'll compare along dimensions but won't produce a ranking; a threshold view where past a certain magnitude everything is simply "the worst" and further ordering is idle; a view on which the act of ranking wrongs the victims in itself, quite apart from its consequences; a view on which refusing to rank is the more dangerous practice because it shields harms still going on; a claim that some one atrocity is non-comparable in kind rather than merely very bad; and whatever cells you think are genuinely empty, with a line on whether they're empty because the view is incoherent, because nobody wants to be seen holding it, or because nobody's thought of it yet. Descriptive labels for the leaves please, and only mention actual philosophers in passing if it genuinely helps — half the room won't have read them.

Last thing: flag any pair of leaves your questions can't really pull apart, and tell me which single question is doing the most work in the whole instrument. And give me your own read on the key — how confident are you that it sorts people the way you claim, and what's the one leaf assignment you're least sure about, where you suspect a real student's answers would land them somewhere they'd flatly refuse to accept?
