I chair the committee in my department that awards two-year internal fellowships supporting one postdoctoral researcher, and this cycle I am applying rather than adjudicating. I would like you to draft the proposal — approximately 1,500 words, written for a panel that includes philosophers from other subfields and two members with no philosophical training — on the following half-formed project. Please sharpen it rather than merely expand it, and write it as the document itself, without any prefatory remarks about how you composed it.

The project. Normative realism is standardly characterized as the view that there are reasons obtaining independently of what anyone happens to care about. But realists disagree sharply about how far that independence can be pushed before the very idea of a reason loses its grip, and my suspicion is that the disagreement is muddied because three distinct conditions are routinely conflated:

(i) an *existence* condition — what it takes for there to be a reason at all;
(ii) a *guidance* condition — what it takes for a reason to be capable of moving some agent to act;
(iii) an *access* condition — what it takes for a reason to be discoverable by someone.

On my reading, the internalist tradition descending from Williams effectively collapses (i) into (ii), while the robust realism of the Parfitian stripe invokes (iii) only intermittently and at a cost it never states. Mackie's queerness worry, I think, gets its real traction precisely from cases in which a reason would have to obtain with (ii) and (iii) both failing — a demand that seems to float free of anything that could make it a demand.

To isolate those cases I have provisionally distinguished three categories, and I want the proposal to test whether the distinctions are worth their upkeep:

- **orphan reasons** — reasons that obtain, if any do, but that no actual agent can ever recognize or act upon;
- **residual reasons** — reasons generated by a commitment, practice, or relationship that has since lost every participant;
- **vacant reasons** — reasons that would bear on agents who might have existed but never will.

These labels are my own coinage; I make no claim that they are in circulation, and the proposal should not write as though a literature already speaks in them.

The proposal must deliver the following:

1. A precise statement of the research question, phrased so that it has a determinate answer, together with an enumeration of the answers it could receive.
2. An account of why the existing positions leave that question open: what the internalist and the externalist each owe, and where exactly the conflation of (i)–(iii) is doing unacknowledged work for one side or the other.
3. The method. This should not be a promise to read carefully. I want a stated procedure for constructing cases in which two of the three conditions come apart, and a discrimination test: for each pair, a case designed so that competing accounts of reasons yield *different* verdicts about whether a reason obtains. Work one specimen case out in full — for instance, a linguist who records the last fluent speaker of a language and extracts a promise from her graduate student that the recordings stay sealed until the student's own death; both are now dead, and the tapes sit in an archive whose access policy was set decades later by people who never knew either party. Ask whether a reason to honour that promise still obtains, and whether the answer shifts depending on whether the tapes survive, whether anyone could ever learn of the promise, and whether anyone could now act on it.
4. A realistic scope: twenty-four months, myself at roughly twenty percent and one postdoc full time. Give a staged plan with a deliverable at month twelve that would retain value even if the central thesis collapses.
5. At least two explicit failure conditions, including the live possibility that the three-way distinction is genuine but idle — that nothing in ethics, epistemology, or the theory of practical deliberation turns on keeping the conditions apart — and say what evidence would tell us that this is what has happened.
6. A short section answering the non-philosophers on the panel: what is actually at stake here. Candidates include obligations to the dead, the status of claims made on behalf of future people who may never exist, and whether realism can answer the charge that its reasons do no explanatory work. Be candid and do not inflate the significance.
7. A reflexive note on whether the project's own idiom prejudges its outcome: to speak of orphan or residual *reasons* is already to grant that the existence condition is satisfied, and the proposal must say how the investigation can remain neutral between realism and its denial.

Please include no bibliography, reading list, or citation apparatus; I will supply references myself. Name positions and arguments freely, but attach no page numbers and no quotations.
