Three things, in ascending order of how much they're actually bugging me.

**1. the loaded bits.** I looked up a few but I don't trust myself on the ones where the dictionary answer isn't the point. What is she doing with "Goshen of mediocrity" — I know Goshen is the bit of Egypt where the Israelites get to live undisturbed, so is the joke that this career is a protected enclave where the normal standards don't apply? And "unctuous egoism" as opposed to just egoism, the "spiritizing alembic" (i.e. spiritualizing), the "writer of imaginary conversations", the Amphitryon at dinner. Especially I want to know what "made reason-proof by being baptized with the name of piety" is meant to describe mechanically — is that a claim about how a belief gets caused, or about how it's held once it's there?

**2. the main thing.** I've been scribbling in the margin that there are two seperate complaints fused together here and I can't tell if she sees the difference. One is about the hearer's psychology: a passion gets relabelled as piety, and then it can't be argued with. The other is about the *setting*: no hissing, no walking out, no press critic taking notes, nobody authorised to reply. The first one sounds like a claim that the belief is bad; the second only sounds like a claim that the belief is unaudited. And I don't see why unaudited should mean bad — if this guy only ever said true things from that pulpit, the exact same arrangements would be in place and nothing would be wrong, would it? So is she committed to something like "an unchallenged assertion is ipso facto unwarranted", which feels way too strong, or is the weaker point enough for what she's trying to do — which is to say his theology is not merely untested but false? Related: that line "certain that those of his hearers who are not sympathizing are not listening" keeps collapsing on me when I try to unpack it. Read one way it's trivial (the bored ones tune out), read another way it's doing a lot of work (the ones who are still in the room are already onside, so his assertions never meet resistance), and read a third way it's just an accusation that the sympathizers are too far gone to hear the gaps. Which of those is it, and does the argument need the strongest one?

**3. is she hoist by her own petard.** "Like the writer of imaginary conversations, he may put what imbecilities he pleases into the mouths of his antagonists, and swell with triumph when he has refuted them" — that's pretty much a description of what this essay is doing to Cumming. He gets no say in how he's characterised, she invents the recipe for his career in the imperative ("let him fish for souls..."), and the reader of the Westminster Review can't answer her back in the same breath. So what's the asymmetry she's entitled to? I can sort of feel that there is one — a printed attack can in principle be answered by a printed reply, by another periodical, by the fact that her claims about his sermons are checkable against his published sermons, whereas nothing said from the pulpit can be answered inside the situation where it lands — but I can't tell whether that's a real principled difference or just me being generous to my assigned author. Can you make the strongest version of the objection that she's doing the same thing, and then tell me whether it lands?

Ideally what I get back is a walkthrough of the passage in order, with the conceptual stuff spelled out rather than name-checked — like, actually say what turns on whether the criticism is about the content of his beliefs or about the epistemic situation they're formed in, bc I think that distinction is going to matter for the rest of the essay and for how she talks about religion later on.
