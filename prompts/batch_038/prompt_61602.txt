Consider a means-tested, work-tested out-of-work benefit of a kind that operates in several countries, with the following features. I set them out explicitly so that nothing turns on recalling the machinery of any one jurisdiction, and so that you can treat the design itself, rather than its local legal history, as the object of analysis.

1. Entitlement depends on job search and not on need alone. Each week the claimant must complete a fixed number of logged job-search activities — applications, registrations with agencies, meetings with a work coach — and the log is the operative evidence of compliance.
2. Payment does not depend on the outcome. A claimant who does everything required and finds no work is paid in full; a claimant who finds work ceases to be paid.
3. Breach is met with total withdrawal of the award for a fixed period — say thirteen weeks — rather than with a proportional reduction. There is a recognised excuse for good reason (illness, a caring emergency, transport failure), but the burden of establishing it rests on the claimant and it must normally be evidenced by a third party: a doctor's note, an employer's letter.
4. While sanctioned, a claimant may apply for a hardship payment. It is smaller than the normal award and it is a loan: it is recovered from the claimant's benefit after the sanction ends, in instalments, for as long as recovery takes.
5. Some decisions turn on a mental state. A claimant may be found to lack a genuine intention to look for work; the finding is made from the pattern of behaviour in the log, and there is no route to contesting it other than by producing more behaviour of the right kind.
6. Where the department has overpaid because of its own administrative error, the whole sum remains recoverable from the claimant, whether or not the claimant was at fault and whether or not it was spent in good faith. Where the department has underpaid or wrongly stopped an award because of its own error, the arrears are restored, but nothing compensates the claimant for the period of want.
7. Deliberate misrepresentation is criminal and may be prosecuted; honest error is not criminal, yet it produces exactly the same recoverable debt.

Several of these features strike me as difficult to reconcile, and I should like them treated as data to be explained rather than as anomalies to be explained away.

First, reconstruct the theory the scheme presupposes. Is the payment a wage for the labour of searching, a contribution-based insurance benefit, a subsistence entitlement with behavioural conditions attached, or a gift whose condition is the visible performance of deserving it? What has to be true about work, and about the relation between claimant and taxpayer, for sanctions of this particular shape to be intelligible as anything other than punishment?

Second, separate strict presupposition from good fit. Which of features one to seven would be licensed equally well by several rival theories — by an administrative concern with the tractability of proof, say, or with the cost of investigating fraud — and which become explicable only on one specific view of desert or responsibility? Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and where it commits its operators whether or not they intend to be committed.

Third, assess its internal coherence. Feature two makes outcomes irrelevant to payment, yet feature five makes an unobservable intention the object of assessment and treats logged behaviour as its only admissible evidence: does the scheme in practice operate as though the intention simply were the performance of the logged acts, so that the appeal to a mental state does no work at all? And features three, six and seven appear to allocate the cost of contingency asymmetrically. The world's unpredictability — a cancelled bus, a sick child — is charged to the claimant unless they can purchase proof otherwise, while the department's unpredictability is charged to the claimant as well. What account of fault, control and reciprocity would render that asymmetry principled rather than merely convenient? Does it presuppose that the burden of disorder tracks position rather than agency — that whoever is the one asking must absorb whatever the world fails to deliver?

Fourth, feature four seems to me the strangest element of all, and I should like it given the most attention. What conception of the person is required in order to treat someone with no present income as a borrower? It appears to presuppose a diachronic subject constituted as a ledger, whose current deprivation is an entry to be balanced against a future income stream that has not materialised and that, on the scheme's own terms, nobody owes them. Is that presupposition compatible with the picture of the claimant implied by features three and five, which is of a present-tense bundle of behaviours assessed week by week? And what does it imply about whether the debt is expected to be repaid at all — is the loan a financial instrument, or a moral one, whose point is that the claimant should experience the hardship period as owing rather than as receiving?

Finally, name the rival theories the scheme quietly rules out, and set out what would have to change in its design if one of them were adopted in its place. I want the answer at the level of machinery rather than rhetoric: the unit of assessment, the object of proof, the direction of the burden, the treatment of departmental error, the length and shape of any penalty, and the status of support given during it.
