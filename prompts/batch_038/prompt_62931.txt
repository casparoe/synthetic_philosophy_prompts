I cover tech policy and online speech for a general-interest magazine, and I've been trying to build a piece that isn't just "both sides have a point" — the plan was to anchor it on Mill's harm principle, since literally everybody on this beat invokes it like a magic spell. The problem is that when I actually ran my own intuitions through it, they came out scrambled, and my editor is (rightly) not interested in my confusion. So here's the deal: below are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases, and what I want to know is what principle, if any, they add up to — stated precisely enough that I could tell whether it actually generates the verdicts or just sounds like it does.

The cases. A sociologist publishes a study claiming a certain ethnic group is over-represented in violent crime, loaded with caveats about confounders: fine, even though I fully expect it to get stripped of its caveats and used to stoke hostility. Someone posts the identical claim and then appends the researcher's home address: not fine — but notice that the part I object to is the address, not the claim. Mill's own example, where the opinion that corn dealers starve the poor is acceptable in the press but punishable when shouted at an angry mob standing outside a corn dealer's house or handed around that mob as a placard: I agree with both verdicts, and I can't say what does the work, since the words are the same and the mob's psychology is arguably the mob's own business. A recommendation engine pushes that study into forty million feeds, unaltered: I think that's worse than the address post, which I cant defend, because the harm per exposure is obviously smaller. An unlabeled deepfake of a candidate saying something he never said, four days before an election: not fine. An obvious satire deepfake of the same candidate the same week: fine — even though the affect on attention, belief formation, and spread looks structurally identical to me, and all the difference seems to live in intent plus "everyone can tell," which doesn't sound like a harm-based difference at all. Fraudulent ads for a fake cancer cure: not fine, but I don't think I need any free-speech principle to say so, it's just fraud. A serious book arguing that a minority should be expelled, published legally, no imminent violence: fine, but uneasily. A venue cancelling a speaker because of credible threats from protesters: fine — yet the state cancelling the very same talk: not fine. And knowing defamation of a private figure that costs them their job: not fine, and I'm comfortable with civil liability.

So: is there a coherent principle behind this mess, and which of these verdicts does it force me to give up? I care about the clause-level detail — what counts as a harm, whether the audience's own free choices break the causal chain or just relocate responsibility, whether scale and amplification change the *kind* of thing happening rather than just the quantity of harm, and whether the speaker's intent can matter if the downstream effects are the same. Then run whatever you come up with on a case I didn't list: an offshore troll farm posting thousands of individually accurate, individually trivial negative comments about one small business until it goes under. And one more thing that's been nagging me, which you should tell me honestly whether it's right: I suspect half my verdicts aren't coming from the harm principle at all, but from a different Millian argument — the one about how silencing or distorting discussion robs us of the chance of the truth getting heard, so the operative question isn't "does this hurt someone" but "does this foreclose or corrupt the process by which we'd find out we're wrong." If that's true, my list is tracking two different principles and calling it one, and I'd want to know which verdicts flip when I switch frames. No bibliography or citations, please — I just want the reasoning laid out.
