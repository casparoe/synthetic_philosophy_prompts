Each round has a set question, and one member grades another's response. **Question:** Explain the objection that utilitarianism fails to take seriously the separateness of persons. Does that objection show that utilitarianism is false, or does it show only that one common argument for utilitarianism fails?

**Response under grading:**

The objection comes from Rawls and was taken up by Nozick. Its core is that utilitarianism applies to society a principle of choice that makes sense only within a single life. It is rational for me to sit through a painful dental drill now for the sake of greater comfort later, because one and the same person undergoes both: the cost and the benefit land in the same life, and so the cost is compensated. Utilitarianism extends that reasoning across people. It treats a harm to me and a benefit to you as entries in a single ledger, as if society were one large person whose experiences happen to be distributed over many bodies. But there is no such person. Nobody experiences the net. The one who loses does not receive the gain that is said to justify his loss, and gains accruing to others cannot compensate him for anything, since they are not his. So the extension fails at its first step — and with it utilitarianism, which has no foundation left once the impartial-spectator style argument for it is gone.

Two further points. First, the objection is really Nozick's utility monster case in general form: the monster is simply the clearest instance of a being whose satisfactions are permitted to swallow those of separate individuals, so anyone who rejects the monster is committed to rejecting aggregation. Second, Parfit's work on personal identity reinforces the objection. He argues that identity is not what matters and that the boundaries between lives are shallower than they appear; if even the unity of a single life is weaker than we assume, then the distinction between trade-offs within a life and trade-offs between lives cannot be as sharp as the utilitarian needs it to be — which is precisely the objector's point.
