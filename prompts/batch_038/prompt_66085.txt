Having spent some forty years in experimental condensed matter physics, I have come to philosophy late, and I find the literature on bounded rationality oddly stratified: everyone invokes Herbert Simon, yet the proposals that travel under that banner strike me as differing enormously in how much of the classical picture — a rational agent as one who maximizes a well-specified objective — they actually leave standing.

I would therefore like you to sort the following into tiers according to that single criterion: the degree to which the framework preserves rationality as optimization and merely adds frictions, versus the degree to which it replaces optimization with something structurally different.

- Simon's aspiration-level satisficing and sequential search
- Radner's insistence that the costs of acquiring information and of computing be counted within the optimizing framework itself
- Rubinstein's modelling of agents as finite automata, and of games of bounded reasoning
- the heuristics-and-biases programme of Kahneman and Tversky
- Gigerenzer's fast-and-frugal heuristics and ecological rationality
- rational inattention, in which the agent optimally allocates a limited information-processing capacity

Assign each a score from 0 to 1, where 1 means optimization survives essentially unscathed, place each in a tier, and justify each placement in a sentence or two. Then tell me which of the six a physicist should regard as the most intellectually consequential — that is, which one forces a genuine revision of what we mean by rational choice rather than a change of variables in the same equation. If in your view two of them are not real competitors but one idea written in different notation, say so plainly and explain why the distinction is being preserved anyway.
