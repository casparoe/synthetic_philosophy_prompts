I'm working on a paper for a graduate seminar on theories of distributive justice, and before I commit anything to the draft I want to settle a dispute that consumed most of last week's session. The topic was the priority view and the leveling-down objection. A classmate of mine — call him R. — argued that the whole debate can be dissolved by an analogy with the diminishing marginal utility of income. On his telling, prioritarianism is simply utilitarianism with a concave transform applied to each person's wellbeing before the summing: an additional unit of wellbeing counts for less the higher the level at which it arrives, exactly as an additional dollar buys less satisfaction for a wealthy person than for a poor one. The concavity, he insisted, is in the moral scale rather than in the world. When someone objected that a concave transform is not invariant under positive affine rescaling — that you must fix a zero point and a unit before you can even ask which transforms are concave — R. took this as confirmation rather than as a problem: if prioritarianism is committed to a particular way of scaling wellbeing, then its disagreement with telic egalitarians is a technical disagreement about the choice of scale with no independent normative content, and the leveling-down objection cannot get a grip on a view that is, at bottom, a monotone aggregation. He conceded that the analogy is imperfect, but added that an imperfect analogy is no objection to a conclusion the analogy was never needed to support.

The self-described relational egalitarians in the seminar had their own picture, which R. treated as the mirror image of his. Theirs was that distributive equality is not a shape in an allocation at all but a relation among persons — the way equality of standing in a democracy is not a fact about how votes tally but about nobody being anyone's subordinate — so the leveling-down objection attacks a distributive-pattern view they never held and therefore misses them entirely. R.'s reply was that this merely relocates the question: a relation of equal standing still has to be cashed out somehow, and once it is, it either reduces to a pattern in distributions, in which case it inherits the objection, or it concerns something other than distribution, in which case it is not a rival to prioritarianism at all. I have about a week before the draft is due, and I find myself unable to say which of these two exchanges actually landed.

What I want from you is an audit of both analogies rather than a verdict on prioritarianism as such. For each, give me the explicit correspondences: in the income case, what plays the role of the dollar, the utility function, the concave transform, and the interpersonal comparison; in the standing case, what plays the role of the tally, the citizen, the relation, and the leveling. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on the conclusions R. and the egalitarians were drawing from the ones that are merely interesting. I am especially unsure whether the money-versus-wellbeing difference does any work here — one is an instrument whose marginal value declines for contingent, person-specific psychological reasons, while the other is supposed to be the morally relevant magnitude itself — or whether that is decoration. Tell me what each analogy quietly assumes about interpersonal comparability, and about whether wellbeing comes with a natural zero and a natural unit. Then assess R.'s maneuver specifically: is converting the scale-dependence point into his conclusion legitimate, or does it beg the question against the prioritarian by presuming that the only thing the weighting function could be doing is fixing a scale? Finally, say which conclusion, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table — in particular, whether prioritarianism's immunity to the leveling-down objection is owed to the analogy or to something structural about the view that the analogy merely illustrates — and, if there is a better analogy for what prioritarianism actually claims, what it would be.
