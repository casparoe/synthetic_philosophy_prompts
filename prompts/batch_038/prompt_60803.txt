I convene a final-year undergraduate seminar on moral responsibility (16 students, one 75-minute session) in the second week of a unit on blame. The students have already worked through the standard accounts of what blame is — judgment, sentiment, protest, holding to account — but they have not yet encountered the literature on whether a blamer can lack the standing to blame, and I want them to arrive at that problem from inside an activity rather than from a lecture. Please design the session for me in British English throughout.

The activity must satisfy the following requirements.

1. No framing vocabulary. The words 'standing', 'hypocrisy', 'tu quoque' and 'blameworthiness' may not appear on any handout, card or instruction sheet, and the students must not hear them until the debrief. The intuition is to be generated by the mechanics.

2. Sealed records, not real confessions. Each student receives a sealed card assigning them a fictional minor transgression of their own together with the norm it violates. I do not want anyone disclosing anything true about themselves. Write out at least four of these cards in full, including the fictional act, the norm it breaches, and what the card entitles its holder to do in each round.

3. A finite blame budget, and a contested depletion rule. Each student holds a small number of blame tokens to spend when judging the transgressions of others. The crux I want you to build in is this: tokens are forfeited when a student is shown to have committed the very fault she is blaming, and I want two depletion rules running simultaneously in opposite halves of the room — one under which the forfeiture is local to the norm breached, and one under which any forfeiture drains the student's standing across the board. Give me the exact rule text for each half, and explain, in the debrief notes, which of the two better models which family of theory about standing, and why running both in parallel is worth the administrative cost.

4. Four rounds, in this order. (a) Accurate blame delivered by someone with the identical fault. (b) The same, except that the blamer has since repented and demonstrably reformed. (c) The blamer's own fault is structurally similar but belongs to a different norm — I have been calling this the normative neighbourhood case, though the label is my own coinage and I want you to tell me whether round (c) really isolates what I think it isolates, or whether it silently collapses into a question about degrees of similarity; supply a diagnostic question I can ask the room to find out which. (d) Two contrast figures: a blamer far worse than her target who nonetheless applies the standard ruthlessly to herself, and a blamer barely worse who quietly exempts herself.

5. Deliverables. A materials list confined to paper, envelopes, tokens of some cheap physical kind and a whiteboard; a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75 minutes; the verbatim wording of the instructions you would read aloud at the start; a reveal protocol that prevents round (a) from turning into public shaming; the ledger sheet on which tokens are recorded; and a debrief that maps each round onto a specific position in the philosophy of blame. The debrief must draw the distinction between the claim that the hypocrite's blame is morally criticisable and the claim that the target is entitled to protest it or that the blamer lacks the status to address her at all, and it must raise the further question of whether standing is a property of the blamer alone or of the pair. Where you attribute a view to a named philosopher, do so only where you are confident of the attribution, and say plainly where you are not rather than inventing a source. I do not want a reading list, a bibliography or any citations.

6. Failure modes. Identify at least three things that will predictably go wrong the first time this is run — for example, students refusing to spend tokens, the session degenerating into gotcha-hunting, someone identifying too closely with an assigned fault, or round (c) overrunning — and say concretely how I should intervene in each case.

7. Exit slip. Two short written questions, to be answered in the last three minutes, whose answers would let me tell after class which theory of standing each student's intuitions actually tracked.

Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce: in particular the fact that real blame carries real relational costs, that the second-personal authority to protest is not something a token system can instantiate, and that the students are themselves in a room where they are being observed and assessed by me. Tell me what I must supply verbally to fill each gap, and where in the timeline to supply it.
