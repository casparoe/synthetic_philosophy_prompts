Our little community cinema is starting a monthly series of genuinely devestating films — I program it, and I've been asked to do the opening remarks on night one, about four minutes. Two things about the room. It's mixed: film regulars, a couple of students, some people who came because a friend dragged them. And this season we're co-hosting with a local bereavement charity, so maybe a third of the audience has lost someone in the past year, a few of them very recently.

I want the remarks to answer the obvious question — why would anyone pay to be made miserable — but there are three things I refuse to say. Not "it's only a story, so it's safe," because the draw is precisely that it isn't safe, it lands. Not catharsis, and not any shade of "you'll feel lighter afterwards" — I've watched that fail on people and I won't promise it to a room like this. And not "great art teaches us about suffering," since half the room already knows more about suffering than any of these films do.

The bit I most want you to get right: whatever account you land on has to be *one* account, covering both the man whose wife died in March and the woman who came because she likes Hungarian cinema and is here on a whim. Anything that works by saying the film resonates with your own loss splits the room in two and leaves the second person unexplained, and I don't want to fudge it with "different people get different things out of it."

It also has to absorb one objection inside the speech rather than leaving it dangling: if being sad together is what's on offer, why not just sit in a room together and be sad about our actual losses — no film, no tickets, no director's commentary? And the answer can't be "because the film is beautiful."

No philosophers named, no use of the word catharsis, and please write it as the programmer, not as a lecturer — it should end with people wanting to stay for the film rather than go home and think about it.
