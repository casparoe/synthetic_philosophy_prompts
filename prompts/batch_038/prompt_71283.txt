I've inherited a second-year module that's half philosophy of rational choice, half public-policy reasoning, and the previous lecturer buggered off in March having written exactly one of the four exam questions. So I need to finish the final one. It's meant to be a 45-minute essay worth 20 marks, and the cohort have had two lectures on bounded rationality: one on satisficing and aspiration levels, one on search costs and when to stop looking. They haven't been set any primary reading at all, which means the question and the model answer mustn't cite anything or name-drop papers — no bibliography, no "as Simon argues in 1956" business. Everything has to come out of the case itself plus the concepts I taught.

Here's the case I want the question built around. Feel free to tidy the wording, but keep the numbers and the structure, because the marking scheme has to bite on the specific details:

Northfield borough council is recruiting one housing officer. Sixty applications arrive. Scoring each one properly takes about 40 minutes of a manager's time. The panel agrees a rubric out of 10 and adopts a rule: read applications in arrival order, and offer the job to the first candidate who scores 7 or above.
(a) Candidate 9 scores 7.4 and is hired. Reading stops there. A year later, during an internal audit, the council scores the remaining 51 applications it never got to, and candidate 34 turns out to have scored 9.5.
(b) The following year, for a different post, nobody in the first 25 applications clears 7, so the panel drops the threshold to 6 and hires someone at 6.4. Two years after that, with a strong field, they raise it to 8.
(c) My friend, who does econometrics, says the whole fuss is empty: given that each application costs 40 minutes, a threshold of 7 is simply what maximising expected value net of search costs would tell you to use, so the council was maximising all along and "satisficing" is just a relabelled maximiser.

I want the question to stop students writing the thing they'd otherwise write — "maximisers maximise, satisficers settle for good enough, and in the real world we don't have time to maximise" — and then stopping there. So it should force a stand on three things, and I'd like these as the sub-questions with marks allocated: whether (a) is a genuine objection to satisficing or a misdescription of what rationality requires, given that the council never saw candidate 34 and could not have; whether the shifting thresholds in (b) show that the aspiration level was never a normative standard in the first place, or show instead that rationality here is procedural rather than a matter of getting the best outcome; and what would have to be true for my friend's reply in (c) to go through, plus — this is the real crux — what is left of satisficing as a rival view if it does go through, whether there's a residue that survives the collapsing move or whether it really is just maximising with the costs written in.

Please give me: the exam question exactly as students will see it (a short stem with the case, then the sub-questions, marks totalling 20); a model answer of around 700 words that actually argues rather than surveys the options; and a marking scheme built on five must-hit moves, each with the marks it's worth and one line on how students typically fumble it. Also flag the answers that look good on a skim but should be marked down — I'm thinking especially of one that says satisficing is "more realistic" without saying whether realism is a virtue of a normative principle or of a psychological theory, and one that treats (a) as settled by pointing out that reading all sixty would have cost forty hours. If a student can hit all five moves without ever taking a position on (c), the marking scheme is wrong, so make (c) do real work.
