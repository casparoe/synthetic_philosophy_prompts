I run an interdisciplinary reading group at my uni — a mix of philosophy grads and a few very keen undergrads — and next term we're doing Christopher Nolan's *The Prestige* alongside personal identity. Everyone's seen the film, so I don't need plot summary. What I need is a real reading I can build a handout and two sessions around, and I'd rather it be honest than tidy: not a film-as-illustration job.

The obvious move is Parfit's teletransporter, and I've seen it done badly a dozen times: Angier's machine copies him, so the film is about whether the copy is really him, cue *Reasons and Persons*, everyone nods. I want more than that. Here are the things I keep coming back to — take them seriously rather than just listing them:

1. Angier's act isn't the standard teletransporter. The machine leaves the original intact and puts a second Angier on the balcony, and the original drops through the trapdoor into a water tank and drowns within a minute or two. So every single night is a Branch-Line case where the branch gets closed almost immediately, and closed on purpose. I've started calling this the Drowning Solution to the fission problem: enforce non-branching by killing the branch. Does Parfit have to treat that as a metaphysically interesting manoeuvre at all, or is it purely a crime with no identity-content? And what does closing the branch buy Angier on Parfit's account, given that identity was never what mattered?

2. Dying, Angier admits he's no longer sure which of the two he is — whether he's the man who stepped into the machine or one of the many. Parfit wants to say that question is empty. But here the branches diverge within seconds and diverge catastrophically: one of them gets the experience of drowning and the other gets the applause. Does the emptiness verdict survive that, or does the film expose a limit — that the question is only empty when the branches are roughly equally good to be? A grad student in the group will push on this and I want to be ready.

3. The Borden trick is the real rival and I dont think anyone has done it properly. Two bodies, one public person, alternating, one of them always living underground as Fallon, right down to amputating the same two fingers so the bodies keep matching. They accept branching permanently and never close it, and they fake the bodily criterion as a stage prop for the psychological one. Is that a fission case in Parfit's sense, or something he has no slot for — a single person deliberately constituted out of two seperate people who trade places? Can Relation R be spread across two bodies that take turns, and if it can, does Borden's arrangement amount to survival better or worse than Angier's? I genuinely can't tell which of the two tricks Parfit should prefer, or whether his framework can even rank them.

4. Root, the out-of-work lookalike Angier uses first. Near-identical body, no psychological continuity at all — the case that should matter least to Angier on Parfit's view. Yet what undoes him is standing under the stage listening to Root take the bow. Explain that resentment. It looks to me like evidence that what Angier cares about isn't identity and isn't R either, but something like being the one who is seen, and I want to know whether that's a third thing Parfit's framework is missing or just vanity.

5. The basement: rows of tanks holding a hundred drowned Angiers. Parfit's question is prudential — will I survive? — and the film keeps a visible ledger of what that cost, which the prudential question has nowhere to put. What can Parfit say about the ledger? Is there anything in his treatment of our relations to our future selves, or in the population-ethics part of the book, that lets you say the surviving Angier committed a hundred murders rather than one long survival? And is each two-minute copy harmed by being made at all? I know that runs straight into non-identity territory; engage it rather than waving it off.

6. Cut me some slack here but don't: I keep wanting to say the pledge/turn/prestige structure maps onto Parfit's dialectic — the ordinary belief about identity, the machine, and the reveal that identity was never what mattered in the first place. Tell me whether that's an insight or a cute nothing. If it's nothing, say so plainly.

And I want the resistance, not just the fit. The film never says how the machine works — no scanner, no blueprint, no account of the cause — so the narrow-versus-wide point about the right kind of cause can't even get a grip on it. And I suspect the film's actual subject is total devotion and the audience's appetite for being fooled, not survival at all; nobody in it is doing metaphysics, and reading them as though they were might flatten the thing. Argue with me if you think that's wrong.
