I am a third-year PPE student, and in two weeks I will present a seminar paper to a joint philosophy-of-economics and health-economics reading group. My thesis is that interpersonal comparisons of utility are not merely possible in principle but empirically tractable, because they can be anchored to a yardstick that is not itself a utility. The mechanism I propose is a single common elicitation: each respondent states how much of their own remaining life expectancy they would surrender in order to avoid a standardised duration of a specified painful condition. This yields, for each person, a rate of exchange between suffering and time, and I argue that comparing two people's rates tells us whose suffering is worse without ever assigning cardinal numbers to anyone's utility.

The room will hold about a dozen people: health economists who work with quality-adjusted life years and disability weights and are professionally sceptical about preference elicitation; two philosophers of economics who are welfarists but take Robbins's methodological ban to have closed this debate; one attendee who holds that comparability is already secured by the imaginative act of occupying another's position and will therefore find my entire apparatus redundant; and one senior ordinalist. No psychologists.

Please prepare my question-and-answer sheet: the eight or so objections this particular room will raise, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased in the vocabulary of the subgroup that raises it, with a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is thinnest. Distinguish the objections that are fatal if I fumble them from the ones I can safely concede in a single sentence. Close with the question I will be praying nobody asks.
