I am a retired invertebrate zoologist. For thirty-four years I worked on the functional morphology and shore ecology of intertidal gastropods, mostly in the tropics, and I have come to the philosophy of biology late and from the bench rather than from the seminar room. A department near where I live has invited me to give a paper on where I now stand about the adaptationist debate, and before I write it I would like my position audited rather than defended. I shall set out my commitments plainly and ask whether they are jointly tenable.

Some background on the one empirical case I shall use, since it does real work in my thinking. The character that has occupied much of my career is the thickened, flared apertural lip that adult males of a neritid snail develop on wave-exposed shores. I showed that lip thickness predicts resistance to dislodgement in a flow tank, and that lip mass tracks exposure across nine shores over three years. I have always called the lip an adaptation to wave action. I have also always known that lip thickening co-varies with overall shell growth rate, and I never eliminated the alternative that the lip is simply where deposition is slowest when accretionary growth is fastest — an allometric consequence of the growth process rather than a target of selection. In candour, I do not think that alternative was ruled out; I think it was set aside.

My commitments:

1. I read the spandrels critique of Gould and Lewontin (1979) as making a methodological point rather than a statistical one. What it objects to is not the claim that many traits are adaptations, but a practice in which adaptive hypotheses are generated and accepted without ever being exposed to the alternatives. I accept that objection.

2. Accordingly, I hold that no character may be called an adaptation until the non-adaptive alternatives have been actively ruled out — phyletic history, developmental constraint and allometry, random drift, architectural by-product — rather than merely acknowledged. Noting an alternative is not testing against it.

3. I nevertheless hold that natural selection is the only force in evolution that produces fit between organism and environment. Constraint, drift and by-product status limit, redirect and subtract; they do not design. I take this to be an ontological claim about the sources of form, and I do not believe it competes with (1) or (2).

4. On the concept itself I am a strict historical user of the word. A trait is an adaptation for a role only if selection shaped its lineage for that role; a trait that now performs a role but was not built for it belongs to a different category, and I do not regard the two as continuous. Present usefulness is not sufficient.

5. I am also a sceptic about origins. For most taxa, and for nearly all soft-bodied or fossil-poor ones, the circumstances in which a character first arose are simply unrecoverable, and a science that makes its central explanatory concept depend on that record is a science that cannot use the concept. The question worth asking about a character, to my mind, is why it is present now — what it contributes to the organisms that have it.

6. I hold that architectural by-products are pervasive in organisms and that a by-product can in principle be a complete account of a character's presence. But I also hold that no by-product remains a by-product for long: once a structure exists and does something, selection will act upon it, and in a lineage with any geological time behind it I do not believe there are pure spandrels. On my view every persistent character has an adaptive component.

7. I hold that the deepest error in the adaptationist programme is atomism — treating the organism as a set of independent traits each owed its own separate explanation — and that the organism is an integrated whole whose parts constrain one another. I have not worked out how the standard in (2) is to be applied to anything other than one character at a time, and my own comparative work in the case above is character-by-character.

8. On evidence I hold that the failure to find a character optimal tells us nothing: imperfection is entirely compatible with selection having done the best available. But I also hold that a good fit between a character and a well-specified environmental demand, demonstrated functionally and patterned across independent sites, counts positively in favour of the adaptive explanation.

Please tell me:

(a) which pairs or groups of these commitments are genuinely in tension, and which only appear to be, with the appearance accounted for;

(b) for each genuine tension, what specifically would have to be given up or weakened, and what the cost of that revision would be — I am not asking for a verdict on who won the adaptationism debate, only on whether this particular package can be held together;

(c) what my own verdict about the apertural lip amounts to under my own criteria, given that I cannot honestly say the allometric alternative was excluded;

(d) which single revision would restore the most coherence across the set, and whether that revision leaves me holding a position that is still recognisably mine rather than someone else's.

Please rank the tensions by severity rather than presenting them all as equally serious, and do not recite the history of the debate to me; I know it. I am not asking for references and would prefer you supply none — where you need to characterise a view held by others, do so in your own words.

One further request, about your own footing. I have no independent means of checking what you tell me and would rather not have to acquire one. So for each judgement you make — that a tension is real, that an appearance dissolves, that a given revision is the cheapest — state how confident you are in it. Then single out the one point in your entire audit about which you are least sure, and say what would settle it.
