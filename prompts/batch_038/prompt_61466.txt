I'm a retired atmospheric chemist — thirty-five years mostly building instruments to measure trace gases, so I've spent my career arguing about whether a signal is real or an artifact. Three years into retirement I fell into philosophy, mostly philosophy of science and some epistemology, and I also took on a monthly Q&A column for our regional astronomical society's magazine (about 3,500 members). I get maybe fifteen letters an issue, I pick three, I have about half an hour each and 500 words, no research assistant, and no library access worth the name. The editor signs off on everything; she's a retired schoolteacher and gets nervous in two directions at once — she doesn't want the magazine looking like it endorses astrology, and she doesn't want members writing in to say they were mocked in print. Several of our dues-paying members practice astrology and are perfectly pleasant people about it.

The letters I need help with aren't about the sky. They're about Kepler. Roughly: "the man who worked out the laws of planetary motion cast hundreds of horoscopes, so your dismissal of astrology is just scientism," or "Kepler defended astrology against the scoffers of his own day, so you're on the wrong side of history." My stock answer for two years has been some version of *great scientists can be wrong outside their field, and Kepler's astrology was a historical curiosity.* Lately I've read enough Kepler in translation to worry that this answer is lazy and maybe flat-out wrong. He wrote a pamphlet in 1610 — the one whose title is something about a third party intervening — whose whole point was a warning to people who were rejecting astrology wholesale that they were throwing the baby out with the bathwater and undermining their own professions while doing it. He argued that rejecting a thing completely because of its imperfections is ill-considered, since on that standard medicine wouldn't survive either. But he also cut the subject down hard himself: he said he'd put the popular predictive rules to "the philosophical test" and had no use for them, he refused to let himself be used as a marketplace entertainer, and his own account was that what mattered was the geometry of the aspects between the planets — and he located that geometry not up among the stars but down here, at the point that collects the rays, with something like a perceiving faculty in the earth and in souls doing the responding. He was also blunt that the foolish daughter astrology was what kept the sensible mother astronomy from starving.

So I don't want an essay about Kepler. I want a procedure I can run on one of these letters in half an hour, every month, for as long as the letters keep coming. Specifically:

- The questions to ask of the appeal, in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each and where a given answer kills the appeal, where it merely weakens it, and where it moves me on to the next question.
- Where the procedure stops being a historical question — what Kepler thought — and becomes a philosophical one about what a great scientist's belief does and doesn't do for the credibility of a claim, and therefore something I should hand back to the reader as a live disagreement rather than settle by authority. I want those handoff points marked explicitly.
- What to do when I simply don't know what Kepler thought about the specific thing a letter raises and can't check it. I'd rather the procedure tell me to say so than let me invent something plausible.
- Which distinctions have to survive being compressed into 500 words and which ones I'm allowed to drop, given that my readers aren't historians.
- Which steps are genuinely judgment calls wearing a procedure costume. I spent my life watching people present calibration choices as if they were facts, so please don't do that to me here.
- What the procedure can't do at all — where the decision is mine as a person writing to a named member of my own society, not a matter of getting the history right.

Then run it on three actual letters:

1. A member writes that more than eight hundred of Kepler's horoscopes survive, that his prognostication for 1595 called a peasant uprising, a Turkish invasion and a bitter winter, and that all three happened — "so the man had a working method, and three for three is not luck."
2. A member writes that my stock reply is precisely the position Kepler's 1610 pamphlet was warning against, and that if I'm going to keep invoking him I should be honest that he would have filed my column under bathwater-throwing.
3. A professional astrologer in the membership writes that they abandoned zodiac signs, houses and mundane prediction years ago and now practice only a Keplerian astrology — aspects between planets, correlated against their own decades of weather and temperament records — and asks me to say in print that this is a legitimate empirical research program that deserves the society's cooperation rather than its mockery.

My guess is that at least one of these comes out against me, and probably the third one does. Don't soften it to spare my feelings; I want the procedure applied straight, and then I want you to say plainly that this is the answer I wasn't hoping for.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I can't chase them and you'd only be guessing at page numbers. Where you're working from a general picture of Kepler rather than something solid, just flag it as such in the text. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in the claims you've made, separating the ones about what Kepler actually held from the ones about how a procedure like this will behave in practice — and single out the one step you're least sure of, with a sentence on why it's shaky and what would settle it.
