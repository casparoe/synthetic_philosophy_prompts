The question as it appeared on the paper was this: Putnam's Twin Earth case is standardly said to show that meaning is not fixed by what goes on inside a speaker's head — that, in his own phrase, "meanings just ain't in the head!" Set out the case and the argument Putnam draws from it. Then explain what, if anything, the case shows about the contents of beliefs rather than of words, and discuss one objection to the conclusion. The question is worth 25 marks. Our scheme for the course is that 21 to 25 marks indicates an excellent answer with real command of the material, 16 to 20 a good answer that is accurate but perhaps limited in ambition, 11 to 15 an adequate answer with significant gaps or errors, and anything below 11 a fail.

Here is the script.

Putnam's argument begins from a piece of science fiction. We are to imagine a planet very like ours, whose inhabitants are molecule-for-molecule duplicates of us and whose language is indistinguishable from English in every respect that could be detected from the inside. There is one relevant difference: the liquid that fills its lakes, falls as its rain and comes out of its taps is not H2O but a different compound, which Putnam abbreviates as XYZ. XYZ is by hypothesis colourless, tasteless and drinkable at ordinary temperatures, so that nothing in the everyday experience of the Twin Earthlings marks it off from water; and it is important that in 1750, when nobody on either planet knew any chemistry at all, the extension of 'water' on Earth was already H2O and on Twin Earth already XYZ, since what fixes the extension is not anyone's knowledge but the actual constitution of the stuff in the environment.

Putnam then compares an inhabitant of Earth, Oscar, with his duplicate. The two are in precisely the same psychological state: the same dispositions, the same mental images, the same beliefs, the same readiness to apply the word 'water' to the liquid in their glasses. Yet the extension of 'water' differs between them, and so, Putnam concludes, the meaning differs. Since two speakers who are psychologically indistinguishable can differ in what their words mean, meaning cannot be a matter of psychological state alone; something outside the head must be doing part of the work.

Two features of his account of how that work is done deserve mention. The first is that a natural-kind term like 'water' is fixed by the underlying structure of the samples it applies to, so that the meaning of 'water' is, in the end, H2O: to state the meaning of the term is to state the chemical formula, and any two communities that attach different formulas to their word have different meanings for it. The second is that most speakers are in no position to state a formula, so Putnam introduces the division of linguistic labour. The ordinary speaker's competence is parasitic on a subgroup in her community whose job it is to settle which samples belong to the kind; I cannot tell an elm from a beech, but my word 'elm' nonetheless picks out elms, because there are people in my community who can tell them apart and to whom the question could be referred. Relatedly, Putnam treats natural-kind terms as having an indexical component, in the way that 'here' depends for its reference on where the speaker is standing: what makes Oscar's word pick out H2O is partly that the relevant stuff is the stuff around him.

The same strategy extends well beyond natural kinds. Burge's arthritis case is Putnam's argument applied to a different sort of example. A patient believes that he has arthritis in his thigh, although in his community the word is reserved for inflammations of the joints. His belief is therefore false, whereas in a counterfactual community whose word applied also to inflammations of the surrounding tissue, a duplicate patient in the same intrinsic state with the same dispositions would have had a true belief. Since the difference in truth value is not matched by any difference in the two patients' heads, the content of the thought is fixed from outside in just the way Oscar's meaning is. What both cases show, then, is that the physical environment determines content.

Kripke makes a parallel point about names. He asks us to suppose that the man we call Gödel never in fact proved the incompleteness theorems; the proof was produced by a forgotten Viennese called Schmidt, whose manuscript Gödel acquired and published under his own name. In that situation, Kripke says, the name 'Gödel' would really refer to Schmidt, since Schmidt is the one who satisfies the description we associate with the name. Reference is settled by how the world actually is rather than by what is in speakers' minds, which is the conclusion Putnam reaches for 'water' by a different route.

The consequences are not confined to language. Beliefs are individuated by their contents, so if the meaning of a term depends on the environment, the content of a belief expressed with that term depends on it too. Oscar believes that water is wet; his duplicate, though there is no difference between them to be found, believes something else. It follows that no amount of introspection could reveal the difference to either of them: to find out what one's own words mean, and hence what one's own beliefs are, one has to leave the head and investigate the world, in the same way that one has to do chemistry to find out what water is. Some philosophers have drawn the further conclusion that a speaker can be systematically mistaken about the contents of her own mind, though this is not a step Putnam himself is willing to take.

One objection to the whole argument is that it is question-begging, since it is only from our own position, already knowing that water is H2O, that we describe Oscar as talking about H2O and his duplicate as talking about something else. But Putnam can reply that the fixing of extension does not depend on what anyone knows: the 1750 case shows precisely that the extension was settled before anyone was in a position to describe it.
