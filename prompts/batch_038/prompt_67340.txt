I'm grading take-home essays for my second-year intro to medieval Jewish philosophy and I've got about forty of these to get thru by Friday. The question I set was: "Maimonides explains the place of animal sacrifice in the Torah by appealing to the historical situation of the Israelites at the time the Law was given. Explain his argument, and say whether it commits him to holding that the sacrificial cult is religiously obsolete." Here's one answer:

"Maimonides' treatment of the sacrifices in the third part of the Guide is the clearest case of his explaining a commandment by its historical context rather than by its inner meaning. The problem he faces is this: if sacrifice is really the proper way to worship God, why does the Torah go into such detail about it and then effectively let it disappear with the Temple? His answer is that at the time of the Exodus every nation understood worship in sacrificial terms, since it was the only form of divine service anyone recognized, and the Israelites had just come out of Egypt steeped in that way of thinking. God could not simply tell them to stop, any more than a parent can expect a child to drop an ingrained habit overnight; Maimonides compares it to the forty years in the wilderness, which were needed to produce a generation capable of the land rather than to punish the previous one. So the Torah handled the sacrificial instinct allegorically: it kept the outward form but transferred it exclusively to God's name and to a single place, draining it of its idolatrous content while giving the people the worship they were actually capable of. Sacrifice was therefore a concession to a historical situation, a stage on the way to the purer worship of prayer and knowledge. Two consequences follow and Maimonides accepts both. First, the sacrificial cult is obsolete: it belonged to a stage that has passed, which is why he is content to let it lapse after the destruction and sees its restoration as nothing the messianic age requires. Second, the reasoning generalizes: if the form of worship was fitted to the capacity of a particular people at a particular time, then the principle behind it is that worship should be fitted to the capacity of whoever is practicing it now, which is why Maimonides' argument keeps getting taken up by later reformers of ritual."
