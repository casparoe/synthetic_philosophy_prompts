The claims as I understand them (from the 1990s econ work — Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, and a separate Banerjee paper, plus a later Avery and Zemsky one): a line of fully rational Bayesian agents each gets a private signal about some binary state and each sees the actions of everyone ahead of them but not their signals; after a couple of people, everyone rationally ignores their own evidence and copies; the group can lock onto the wrong action with a probability that doesn't go to zero no matter how long the line gets; the resulting consensus is bizarrely fragile, so a small piece of public information or a few outsiders acting against the trend can break a herd that looked stable for years; more observers can make the aggregate worse rather than better; and once you put a price or some other aggregating mechanism in the loop, the herding can get prevented or at least discounted for.

The concrete thing bugging me: at my company, design docs and postmortems go through a review queue where you can see who already approved before you comment. Two colleagues pushed back and said the visible approvals are a feature, because a staff engineer's judgment is real information and you shouldn't throw it away. That objection sounds econ-shaped to me and I suspect my whole team is re-enacting the cascade debate in a conference room, but I can't tell if my side or theirs is the confused one.
