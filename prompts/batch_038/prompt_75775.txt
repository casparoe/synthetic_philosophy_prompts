THE NOTES

§1 Setup. Bradley's complaint, as I read it: if a stands in R to b, and R is just one more item sitting alongside a and b, then nothing in the list {a, R, b} accounts for R's actually holding of a and b — a list of items isn't a fact. So you posit a further tie R₁ to bind a, R and b together; but R₁ is now itself just another item alongside {a, R, b}, so you need R₂; and so on. Part of my paper's job is to say what makes that regress vicious rather than merely long. The line I mostly accept and will be arguing from is the standard one: the regress is vicious because each stage of the analysis calls for a further stage, so the analysis never gets completed. I want to stress that mere non-termination can't be the criterion on its own — the natural numbers don't terminate and nobody thinks that's a problem — so the viciousness has to come from the demand-structure, from each stage requiring the next.

§2 My proposal, call it the signature view. Applying R to a and b is not relating three things by means of a fourth thing; it's performing an operation, and operations come with a signature — an ordered list of argument slots specifying what goes where. So the tying step is essentially R-to-a-then-b, and its order and slot-types are built into it rather than added on. Here's the blocking move: an operation cannot take, as one of its own arguments, anything standing for an operation of its own signature. So the second step of Bradley's chain is not a step I'm heroically refusing to take — it isn't a well-formed step at all. R₁ would need among its arguments something standing for the R-to-a-to-b operation, which its signature rules out. Viciousness, for me, then means this: the sequence isn't endless, it stops being a sequence. The chain is truncated by ill-formedness at step two.

§3 Why the same machinery should handle the ordering problem. There's the issue I associate with a 2000 Philosophical Review paper by Fine called "Neutral Relations" (I'm going from a seminar discussion, so check the attribution rather than trusting me): if a relation is neutral with respect to the order of its arguments, then that a loves b and that b loves a are built out of the very same relation and the very same two objects, and a relation that doesn't care which slot its relata go in leaves nothing to tell them apart — so they come out as the same fact, which is absurd. Hence relations can't be neutral; their argument places have to be somehow non-neutral. My gloss on the scope of this, which I want in the paper: the ordering problem only really bites for symmetric relations, since when R is asymmetric the difference between aRb and bRa is already guaranteed by R itself. On the signature view the ordering problem is a corollary rather than a separate argument — the same relation and the same objects can be fed into differently signed operations, and that's the whole difference between a-loves-b and b-loves-a.

§4 False propositions, plus a claim about Frege. If a proposition is a signed operation, a false one is an operation that is perfectly well formed but doesn't get executed — the world declines to cooperate. I think that's an advantage, because a view on which unity is delivered by a real relation holding among real constituents looks able to build only unities that actually obtain, and then it's hard to say what a false proposition is made out of. (This needs much more than a paragraph and I know it.) Relatedly I want to say that Frege's saturated/unsaturated distinction is normally read as a merely logical or semantic point about names and predicates, but that on my account it's doing metaphysical work: unsaturatedness is what an operation's signature looks like from the inside. I also want to gesture at that remark in the Tractatus about complex signs, the one where the spatial arrangement of the names is supposed to do the representing rather than any further sign (I'd have to look up the number) — an ancestor of the signature view, though I don't want to overclaim the ancestry.
