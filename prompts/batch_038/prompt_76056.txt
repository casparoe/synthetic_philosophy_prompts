Our department's work-in-progress group met last week, and two of my cohort-mates ended up in a standoff over whether accuracy-first epistemology can really vindicate Conditionalization. I've reconstructed the exchange below; I couldn't tell who had the better of it at any given stage.

Ravi opened with the familiar worry: the expected-accuracy argument for Conditionalization assumes the agent knows in advance precisely which evidential proposition she will receive, so it cannot handle agents who are uncertain about their own evidence — for instance, someone awaiting a lab printout who isn't sure she'll be able to read it, since it may come out smeared and leave her only a disjunction of the two possible readings. Mira said this is exactly what the plan-based dominance argument repairs: evaluate whole plans for responding to any of the evidence you might get, and the plan that conditionalizes on whatever you actually receive is never accuracy-dominated, smeared-printout cases included. Ravi answered that she was trading on an ambiguity — the dominance proof still presupposes a known partition of candidate evidence, and the printout agent's uncertainty is uncertainty about which partition she is in; for her, the accuracy-best plan can require hedging toward a compromise credence rather than conditionalizing at all. Mira said he was conflating levels: dominance is assessed over dispositions, not over particular token episodes of belief-formation, and a standing disposition to conditionalize-on-whatever-you-get is perfectly plannable. Ravi's last move was that if that is the reply, accuracy-first has stopped grounding a diachronic norm and has become an instrumental claim about agents who happen to have planning capacities; it now tells us what good planners should do rather than what anyone should do, and the foundational ambition is gone. Mira granted that accuracy-first needs a separate argument that agents ought to be plan-having at all, but insisted this is a capacity-assumption like any other, and that the resulting norm remains accuracy-based rather than pragmatic.

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of Ravi's and Mira's steps, say whether it succeeds, and if it fails, locate precisely where the inference breaks. Then say which of them has the better of the exchange overall, and identify any premise the two are treating as settled — about which accuracy measures are admissible, about the work plans are doing in the argument, or about what a diachronic norm is supposed to deliver — that is really where the disagreement lives.
