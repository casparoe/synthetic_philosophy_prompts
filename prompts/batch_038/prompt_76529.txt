Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about whether introspection is a source of knowledge at all, and we got nowhere in an unusually clean way: he is a thoroughgoing deflationist who holds that every self-report is an interpretive inference drawn from one's own behavior, situation, and folk theory, differing from third-person attribution only in the quality and quantity of the evidence; I kept retreating to pain, on the ground that when I have a headache I do not infer anything, I simply have it, and that a view which cannot preserve even that much has bought consistency at the price of the phenomenon. He replied that the feeling of immediacy is itself just another deliverable of the interpretive process, and that I was citing the datum as if it were an argument. Neither of us budged, and I now suspect we were each assuming an answer to a question we had never formulated: what would a theory of introspective access have to do to be adequate?

So I want the specification before I read any more of the literature, and I want it written for a specific purpose, since adequacy is relative to one. I am drafting the conceptual framework section of a methods chapter for a study that treats participants' experience-sampling reports as its primary data — beeper interruptions, structured prompts about what the subject was experiencing immediately beforehand. That means a theory of introspection is useful to me only if it can tell me when such a report is evidence of something, what would count as a participant becoming more accurate rather than merely reporting differently, and whether there is any fact of the matter about accuracy to become more accurate about.

Write the desiderata: the list of requirements any adequate theory of introspective access to one's own mental states must meet, each with the case or consideration that generates it. Organize it as you see fit, but I want the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the empirical results it must accommodate, all separated out rather than blended into one narrative. For each item, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp — and if it is a camp preference, name the camp and say what they gain by insisting on it.

Below are the candidate requirements I had in mind going in. Treat them as raw material, not as a checklist to be rubber-stamped: reject, reformulate, or split any of them that are ill-posed, and add whatever I have obviously missed.

1. The asymmetry between first- and third-person authority. It must be explained, or explained away with a diagnosis of why it looks so compelling. If the theory says the difference is one of degree rather than kind, it owes an account of why the degree-difference is so stable and why it feels categorical.

2. Immunity to error through misidentification. There are self-ascriptions — my having a headache right now, in the ordinary case — where I cannot be right that someone has a headache and wrong that it is me. A theory should say which class of self-ascriptions enjoys this, why, and how the class survives the empirical cases below, or explain why the whole notion is a philosopher's artifact.

3. The appearance/reality gap. Perception tolerates a distinction between how things appear and how they are; if introspection is modeled on perception, then a theory owes an account of what a false introspective appearance would consist in, given that in the paradigm cases there seems to be nothing to appear wrongly. If it denies the observational model, it should say what the alternative model is, and what does the explanatory work that the observational model was doing.

4. Confabulation about one's own reasoning. Nisbett and Wilson's 1977 result and the content/process distinction it drew — access to contents but not to processes — must be accommodated. A theory should say whether that distinction is principled or convenient, and whether there are also errors about contents.

5. Preference reports that survive the substitution of the object. In the paradigm where subjects choose the more attractive of two photographed faces, are handed back the photograph they did not choose, fail to notice the swap, and then give detailed and confident reasons for a preference they never expressed, a theory must explain the confident reasons without rendering all preference self-report worthless. This is the case my friend leans on hardest, and I want to know what a requirement built around it should actually say.

6. The disagreement between two competent investigators about the same subject's accuracy. There is a published collaboration — a philosopher and a psychologist, using a beeper-and-prompt protocol on a single intensively studied participant, with the participant's reports and interviews examined jointly — in which the two authors ended up on opposite sides about whether that participant's introspective reports were accurate, and said so in their respective closing remarks. A theory of introspective access must have something to say about how two people with the same transcripts, the same access to the participant, and no incentive to disagree could fail to converge on whether she was right about her own experience. Either there was a fact of the matter that one of them got wrong, in which case the theory should say what would have settled it; or there was not, in which case the theory should say what it means to call a report accurate at all. I flag this one because I cannot tell whether it is a genuine constraint on the metaphysics of introspection or a methodological artifact about underdetermined evidence, and I want to be told which, and why.

7. Introspective training. In Hurlburt's sampling work, participants initially deny having any thinking that lacks words or images and, after coached practice in describing what they find at the beep, come to report such unsymbolized thinking. A theory must have an account of what makes the trained report better than the untrained one rather than merely different — otherwise the notion of introspective skill, which my own study depends on, is unintelligible. This connects to item 6: whatever makes training improve accuracy should be the kind of thing two investigators could in principle disagree about the presence or absence of.

8. Self-ascriptions that help make their own object true. Deciding, avowing, committing, resolving an inchoate feeling into an articulated one: a theory must not treat every introspective judgment as tracking a fully determinate prior fact, but it also must not slide into the view that we invent our minds by describing them. It should locate where the line falls.

9. Misattributed arousal. When experimentally induced physiological arousal is attributed to whatever plausible source the situation supplies, the subject is wrong not only about the cause of a felt state but plausibly about its character and valence. Errors of this kind must be possible on the theory's account, and the theory must not make them so easy to come by that no report is ever safe.

10. Transparency. We answer questions about our own beliefs by attending to the world rather than to ourselves. If this is a mechanism, a theory should say why it works, what its limits are, and whether anything analogous holds for sensations — where it conspicuously does not seem to.

11. Subjects without the relevant concepts. A creature or an infant with no concept of belief plausibly knows that it hurts. A theory that makes self-knowledge conceptually demanding must not imply that such creatures have no knowledge of their own states at all, or must say explicitly that it accepts this and why that is tolerable.

12. The possibility of self-blindness. Could a rational creature lack introspective access to its own attitudes entirely and rely on inference from its own behavior, in exactly the way my friend says we all in fact do? A theory should say whether this is a coherent possibility that must be ruled out by argument, a coherent possibility that is merely false of us, or an incoherent description of a creature that could not count as having attitudes at all. Item 12 and my own pain-intuition pull in opposite directions, and I want to see which one the theory sacrifices.

13. Moore's paradox as a datum. Believing that it is raining while believing that I do not believe it is raining must come out as impossible, or at least as radically unstable. I want to know whether that is an explanandum, a theorem derived from other requirements, or a piece of grammar doing unearned work.

Then, and this is the part I most want: tell me which of these requirements are genuine and which are the theoretical prejudices of a camp wearing the mask of data — my own suspicion is that item 2 and possibly item 13 belong to the second category — and identify any subset that is jointly unsatisfiable. If some of them cannot all be met, the list is itself the result, and I would rather have the incompatibility stated precisely, with the minimal pair or triple that generates it, than have the tension papered over by a distinction that collapses under pressure. Say also which single requirement, taken seriously, does the most to eliminate candidate theories, and which one everybody invokes but nobody can defend.

Close with a short scorecard — no more than a few lines each — for the main contenders as you understand them: the observational or inner-sense family, transparency-based accounts of self-ascription, higher-order thought and higher-order perception views, interpretivist or self-interpretation accounts like my friend's, constitution or agency-based views, and pluralist positions that deny the phenomenon is unified. Keep it brief; the weight belongs on the criteria.

Two further things I need from you, and they matter more than tidiness. First, keep a firm line between what is settled among people who work on this and what is genuinely contested. I have read enough of this area to know that its apparent consensus is often just shared vocabulary, so where a requirement is one that only some camps accept, say so plainly and do not write as though the whole field endorses it. If nothing is settled, say that too. Second, where you are uncertain whether an item is a real requirement, mark the uncertainty rather than smoothing it out, and flag any place where my framing above already begs a question against my friend — I would rather know that now than discover it in the argument.
