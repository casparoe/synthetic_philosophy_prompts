I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student — decision-theory-adjacent, though my dissertation is on something else entirely — and I've somehow ended up as the part-time "risk person" for a small regional resilience fund. We hand out maybe fifteen grants a year, $50k to $400k each, for things like levee upgrades for small towns, heat-refuge networks, drought contingency for farming co-ops. Every application arrives with a benefits figure and a probability attached, and almost none of the probabilities are worth anything. The modelling is thin, the base rates are tiny, and half the time the consultant clearly wrote a number down because the form has a mandatory field for one. The board votes, and the votes go in the published minutes with reasons attached. Nothing gets to the board without a written justification from me, and the board's one actuary gets to append a dissent.

To be clear about what I don't want: I know Ellsberg. Don't walk me through the three-colour urn, I've taught it. And I don't want a survey of who said what about whether ambiguity aversion is rational — I've read enough of that and it doesn't help me on a Thursday afternoon. What I want is the translation: a procedure I can actually run.

So:

1. Give me the procedure itself, as an ordered set of steps with the question asked at each one, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where it stops at a recommendation versus where it hands the thing upstairs to the board because it's a values question and not a facts question. Assume I have no stats training past one grad methods course, and that the actuary is skeptical of everything I say.

2. The heart of it should be a test for when a single point estimate of a probability is good enough to reason from and when it isn't — i.e. when the fund should be treating its uncertainty as a spread of possible probability assignments rather than a number, and which decision rule to run in that case. Worst case? Something less paranoid? How do you choose between them without cheating? Be explicit about what each candidate rule costs us, meaning which good projects each one turns away.

3. The board refuses to hear "we don't know." They want one expected-value cell in the spreadsheet, always, for every proposal. Right now I fudge it with something I made up and have been calling an ignorance discount: take the expected benefit, multiply by (1 − x), where x is supposed to track how much the experts disagree with each other. It's crude but it looks defensible and it outputs a number, which is the only reason it has survived this long. Tell me honestly whether it's any good. Two things I specifically want: can a linear discount like that even express ambiguity aversion, or does it just collapse into being extra risk-averse about a bigger number, which is a different thing with different consequences? And what does it do to the people we ask — if reporting disagreement gets your own project discounted, do you still report it? If the whole idea is bad, give me the least-bad single-number replacement and say plainly what it's hiding.

4. Some of our ignorance is cheap to fix: a $5k survey, one pilot season, borrowing a hydrology report from the neighbouring catchment. Fold that in. How should the procedure decide between deciding now under ambiguity and spending money and a year to convert the problem into a risk problem first? My hunch is that ambiguity aversion changes this answer in a way that straight expected-value thinking doesn't, because resolving ambiguity is worth more to us than its effect on the mean benefit. If that's right, say it properly and show me why. If I'm wrong, tell me I'm wrong.

5. There's the usual objection that ambiguity-averse preferences can be pumped — that if you'll pay a bit to dodge a bet with unknown odds, someone can walk you through a sequence of swaps that leaves you worse off than where you started. Ours is a sequential world in practise: renewals, phase twos, follow-on grants in the same catchment, and the board remembers what it approved last time. Does my procedure expose the fund to that? What does dynamic consistency cost us here, and is it worth buying? Please don't just tell me to be consistent — I want to know what giving up consistency actually gets us.

6. Mark every place where the procedure looks mechanical but is really me making a judgement call. I'd rather flag those in the minutes myself than have someone find them two years later.

Then run the whole thing end to end on three real-shaped proposals:

- A town of about 4,000 wants $180k for a levee upgrade. The consultant's model says it avoids an expected $900k of damage over 25 years, but the flow record for this river is only 40 years long and there's a live dispute among the engineers about whether the last decade is even representative of the climate the levee will face.
- A farming co-op wants $90k to trial a drought-tolerant variety that's widely grown two countries over and nowhere in our region. Nobody can give me a local yield distribution, and the co-op's own estimate is visibly optimistic in a way I can't prove.
- A city wants $400k for a heat-refuge network. The benefit is close to unquantifiable — mostly deaths avoided, some of it just comfort — and there is essentially no modelling behind the number in the form. But everyone on the board already wants to fund it, and I'm the one who has to write the justification. Give me the procedure's honest output even if it's just "your rule says yes, and here's how thin the reasoning under it is." And if the procedure says no, or says pilot-first-then-decide, tell me straight, because I will not like it and I'll probably argue with you about it.
