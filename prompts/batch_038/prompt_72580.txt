I teach a decision-theory seminar for advanced undergraduates — sixteen students, one 75-minute session. Design a money-pump activity they run on themselves in two phases, where the second phase is meant to undercut the conclusion they draw from the first.

Phase 1: induce genuinely intransitive preferences over three lotteries and then charge a small fee at each step of the trading cycle, so that participants discover on their own that they have ended up strictly worse off. Give me the exact prize structures, the token currency, the fee, and how tokens convert into small real prizes.

Phase 2: run the same pump twice more — once on three-person committees that settle each trade by majority vote, and once on individuals who were permitted, before the cycle began, to buy a device binding them to a complete plan rather than choosing step by step. The committee version should be arranged so the group is pumpable in a way no individual member is; the commitment version should make the purchase price itself something the group has to argue about.

I want them to leave seeing that the pump's force depends on assumptions about how an agent governs herself across time, not on intransitivity by itself.

Include rules, materials, the exact numbers, a timeline, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the assumption it tests. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — I expect several students to see the cycle coming and refuse the opening trade — how to handle that, and be frank about which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself.
