For thirty-nine years I earned my living measuring things: I directed a laboratory in solid-state chemistry, and my professional life rested on the assumption that a sample prepared identically twice will behave identically twice. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy seriously, and for the past year I have sat in on a monthly reading group in my town. Last month we worked through the seventeenth discussion of al-Ghazālī's *Incoherence of the Philosophers*, the one on causality, alongside Averroes's reply. Two members — I shall call them Helena and Ravi; she spent her career in theoretical chemistry, he is a retired schoolmaster who has read a good deal of Arabic philosophy in translation — argued for the better part of an hour, and neither would yield. I want an adjudication, and I want it move by move rather than as a general impression.

(1) Helena opened by saying that al-Ghazālī's central claim has been vindicated by physics. His point, as she read it, is that observing one event follow another never discloses that the second occurs *by means of* the first — only that it occurs *with* it — and that the necessity the philosophers read into fire and cotton is a projection of ours. Quantum theory, she said, has withdrawn whatever empirical warrant that necessity might have had: for an individual decay or detection there is no prior physical condition settling the outcome, so the Peripatetic's "by its very nature" is now unsupported by the science that was supposed to supply it.

(2) Ravi said this substitutes a doctrine that merely sounds similar. Indeterminism is not occasionalism. Al-Ghazālī does not simply subtract necessity from the world; he adds an agent — every event is produced directly by a will that knows what it is doing, and created things have no efficacy of their own. Quantum mechanics supplies no agent. What it supplies is a probability law of extreme precision and, between measurements, an evolution that is deterministic. A theory that predicts how likely each event is to a part in a trillion is a poor candidate for the thesis that things have no natures at all.

(3) Helena answered that deterministic evolution of the state rescues nobody, since it terminates in no single outcome; the probabilities are brute, and nothing in the physical situation says why this result rather than that. Where physical explanation runs out in precisely that way, there is room for the agent al-Ghazālī posits. She added that a good deal of contemporary philosophy of physics agrees with him on the deeper point: on the Humean accounts now widely held, laws govern nothing and are merely the most compact summaries of what in fact happens, so nature contains regularity without necessity — which is his conclusion.

(4) Ravi said she had made two distinct errors. First, an explanatory gap is not an agent, and al-Ghazālī is no gap-filler: he insists the customary course is fixed and dependable, that fire does burn cotton, and that knowledge of the world is unimpaired by the denial of necessity. If her vindication requires gaps in the physical order, it requires a doctrine he did not hold. Second, Humeanism is the opposite shape of view: it deletes the modal vocabulary while keeping the entire pattern of events as fundamental, whereas occasionalism deletes the efficacy of events while inserting a will. Subtracting necessity and adding agency do not amount to the same position, and offering the first as evidence for the second equivocates on the word "necessity."

(5) Helena replied that if that is so, al-Ghazālī is scarcely an occasionalist, which was her original point in other words. He concedes, she said, that created things do have natures, bestowed and sustained by God, and that fire is such as to burn what contacts it; what he denies is only that the sequence is metaphysically unavoidable, since God may withhold the effect. On that reading his quarrel with the philosophers narrows to whether the order of nature is necessary or merely reliably ordained — and on the narrower question physics speaks directly, having shown that it is not necessary. She added that Averroes's charge, that abolishing necessary connection abolishes knowledge, is therefore much overdrawn, since al-Ghazālī keeps empirical knowledge intact and merely relocates its ground.

(6) Ravi said the charge was never about ordinary empirical belief, which al-Ghazālī does preserve, but about demonstration and about the intelligibility of the agent: if nothing has a nature, a statement about fire is not even a statement about fire; and if the philosopher's reasoning is nothing but an occasion for God's creating a conclusion in him, then neither party's argument is his own, which saws off al-Ghazālī's branch as readily as his opponents'. He closed by observing that the conservation principles of physics — those Noether showed to follow from symmetries — are exactly what the kalām background refuses: persistent structural constraints on what may happen, in a picture on which accidents do not subsist for two moments and the world is re-created at every instant.

I could see force on both sides and said nothing. Please go through the six moves and rate each one — clearly right, partly right, or mistaken — with a sentence or two of justification, rather than giving me a global verdict first. Then tell me: on which specific claims Helena is correct and Ravi wrong, and the converse; who has the better of the exchange overall; and whether your verdict would shift if one read al-Ghazālī's concession about bestowed natures as a genuine doctrine of secondary causes rather than as a tactical retreat. Finally, tell me what both of them left out of account. I suspect at least one substantial omission and possibly two, but I should prefer to see whether you find them to hinting at what they are. I am not after references or suggested further reading; I want the reasoning.
