I work on a small team building a personal assistant that adapts over time to the people who use it, and at a design review last week a senior colleague twice invoked the proverb "actions speak louder than words" to close down a proposal of mine. The proposal was that we should give some weight to what users say they want — their written comments, their answers to an occasional survey, the things they tell the assistant in the course of a conversation — alongside the behavioural signals we already collect: which suggestions they accept, what they do next, how long they dwell, whether they come back. His position was that behaviour is revealed preference and talk is noise, so the additional collection machinery would be expense without information. I did not have the presence of mind to say much at the time beyond gesturing at weakness of will, and afterwards a friend who works in behavioural economics told me that I had been right to object but for muddled reasons. I would like to return to the next meeting with the slogan properly unpacked, because I suspect at least four distinct claims are being run together underneath it.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the different precise theses that proverb could be standing for in a setting like ours? I am interested in the whole spread from the near-trivial to the radical: claims about the comparative reliability of two measurement channels; claims about self-deception, social desirability and the general unreliability of introspective self-report; the stronger game-theoretic claim that talk is uninformative because it is costless and hence not credible in equilibrium; the still stronger revealed-preference thesis on which a preference simply is a pattern of choice, so that there is nothing over and above behaviour for words to track or fail to track; and the quite separate practical claim about which source should be believed when the two conflict. For each entry, please say what would have to be true for it to hold, whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false as stated, and — this is the part I care about most — whether it would in fact license my colleague's conclusion that verbal reports deserve no weight whatsoever in the system.

Please also take account of several features of our setting that the textbook version of this debate does not seem to cover. Our behavioural data is generated inside an interface we designed, so a user's choices partly disclose our defaults, ordering and friction rather than anything about her; much of it is pooled across a very large population, so that "what users do" is a frequency-weighted mixture of impulses, habits and passing situations rather than anyone's considered ends; and most of the talk we would collect is addressed to a machine that the speaker knows will act upon it, which cuts both ways. Some of it will be strategic performance for the system, but a good deal of it looks to me like aspiration — people telling the assistant who they would like to be rather than who they are — and I genuinely do not know where aspirational self-description belongs in any ordering of channel reliability. Tell me which two readings are doing the real work in an argument of his shape, whether he is entitled to move between them, and which single reading would most change the weighting if it turned out to be correct. A short closing paragraph on what the menu implies for our design decision would be welcome, but the deliverable is the disambiguation with verdicts attached, not a recommendation about the product.
