I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a piece about the way counterfactual best cases quietly determine what people believe they are owed. I have been talking to divorce mediators, to plea-bargaining defence lawyers, and to delegates in climate-finance negotiations, and all three keep describing the same structure: a number that was never achievable nevertheless sets the terms of the settlement. For the launch weekend I have been given a 75-minute slot at a city ideas festival — about 24 ticket-holders, mixed room: two or three schoolteachers, at least one professional economist who will enjoy catching me out, and mostly readers with no formal training. I want the slot to be a game rather than a talk, built around the contrast between the Nash bargaining solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution: that Kalai–Smorodinsky is picked out by the line from the disagreement point to the ideal point and therefore satisfies monotonicity, whereas Nash satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives, so a deal that sits on the menu and would never actually be signed moves one recommendation and leaves the other untouched. Nobody in the room should hear either name, or the word axiom, until the final ten minutes.

Here is the apparatus I have drafted; please check it before I print it. Each pair bargains over tokens, and each token converts into something small at the end — my materials budget is roughly forty pounds, so keep the payout cheap. If a pair fails to agree before the timer runs out, they fall back on their walk-away amounts: 4 tokens for the party I will call A, 6 for B. On the table are six printed deal-cards, listed as (A, B): (5, 33), (10, 30), (14, 27), (18, 24), (21, 20), (25, 12). Half the pairs also receive a seventh card, (44, 7) — technically a deal, since B still clears the walk-away by one token, but one that no B in the room will ever sign. My own arithmetic says that with six cards both procedures recommend (18, 24); that the seventh card leaves the Nash-product recommendation exactly where it was; and that it moves the equal-proportional-gains recommendation to (21, 20). Verify all of that, and if I have it wrong, give me a replacement menu, still in whole tokens and still physically payable, where the divergence is real and large enough to see. Two worries I cannot resolve myself. First, the menu is a finite list of indivisible deals, so the ray from the disagreement point to the ideal point will generally miss every card: I need a stated rounding or tie-breaking rule so that the recommendation an impartial referee ought to make is well-defined for the participants asked to choose it. Second, adding the seventh card makes the set non-convex, which I know sits outside the textbook assumptions, so tell me whether the demonstration survives that or whether I am smuggling in a distraction.

Then give me the runnable thing: a materials list I can print on a single sheet; the complete rules; a stage in which every participant privately records which card an impartial referee should impose before any talking begins, so that I can separate fairness judgement from bargaining leverage in what I write up afterwards; the negotiation itself; and a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75 minutes and still works if only a dozen people turn up. Follow it with a debrief that maps particular moments in the room onto the two rules, reveals the two menus side by side, and lets people work out which side of the divergence they were on. Then a candid list of what will go wrong the first time I run it — I expect at least one B to brandish the phantom card, one pair to blow up into the walk-away, and the economist to ask why I am treating tokens as cardinal utilities — with a line of handling for each. And finally a frank statement of which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce. I suspect the deep one is that no participant ever sees both menus, so the phenomenon exists only in the comparison across the room, which would make the payoff of the whole evening a statistical fact rather than a felt one. Tell me whether that diagnosis is right, and if it is, how to make a room-level regularity land as a discovery rather than as a chart.
