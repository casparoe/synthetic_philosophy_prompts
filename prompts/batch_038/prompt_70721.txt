In about a month I'm giving a talk at our university's affective science workshop. Roughly a dozen people: mostly psychologists and neuroscientists who work on fear conditioning, interoception, and autonomic measures, one philosopher who defends a judgment/construal theory of emotion, and one grad student who's a committed constructionist. My thesis is that emotions are perceptions but not perceptions of value — they're non-conceptual perceptions of affordances in roughly Gibson's sense. Fear is the body detecting the flee-able layout of a situation, anger detects the push-back-able, shame detects the exposed-and-can't-hide. I argue this explains why infants and animals have emotions without any evaluative concepts, why recalcitrant fear (terrified of flying while fully believing flying is safe) isn't a straightforward case of irrationality, and why an emotion can be mistaken about the world in a perception-like way rather than a belief-like way.

Prep me for the Q&A. I want the eight-ish objections this particular room will raise — not the ones an ethics or aesthetics crowd would — in the order they'll hit, phrased in each group's own vocabulary (the physiologists will want to know what's measurable and what prediction changes, the constructionist will go after whether theres anything for the perception to track in the first place). For each: a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is thinnest. Then tell me what to concede in the first five minutes to defuse the worst of it, which single answer has to land for the talk to work at all, and what question I should be quietly hoping nobody asks.
