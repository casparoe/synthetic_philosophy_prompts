I am a second-year doctoral student in philosophy, and this term I am enrolled in an interdisciplinary seminar on concepts and categorization, co-taught by our philosophy and psychology departments. The seminar requires each of us to present a draft of the final paper at the department's work-in-progress colloquium, which draws the seminar participants plus the usual departmental crowd. Mine is scheduled in three weeks, with roughly twenty-five minutes of discussion, and I want to be prepared rather than merely fluent.

My paper takes its starting point from Hofstadter's Letter Spirit project: the attempt to model, computationally, the creative act of designing letters, whose task is to render the lowercase alphabet in styles that differ radically from one another yet remain recognizably the work of a single hand, working with forms the project calls gridletters, drawn on a coarse grid, and with the thought that what binds every version of a letter together is something like a spirit or halo rather than a template. My aim is not to interpret that project but to generalize from it. My thesis is that letterform identity has no context-independent answer: what makes every 'a' an 'a' is neither a shared feature set nor graded proximity to a canonical prototype, but a family-resemblance region the reader constructs, whose boundaries shift with the task (proofreading a novel, deciphering a shop sign, transcribing a charter, deciding whether a logo's letterform is a deliberate distortion) and with the style of the surrounding inscription. From this I argue that a large class of definitional projects in philosophy rest on a false presupposition — they seek a determinate intension for concepts that, like letterforms, have only halos — and that the honest response is not skepticism but a turn toward asking what a given halo is for and relative to which practices.

My argument runs: (i) the double-storey 'a' of ordinary printed text, the single-storey form children are taught to write, the 'a' of a blackletter face, and the 'a' of a geometric sans-serif whose bowl is a perfect circle share no non-trivial feature set that is both necessary and sufficient; (ii) nor are they united by similarity to a stored prototype or exemplar, since a reader with no exposure to blackletter can pick out its 'a' on first encounter, and designers routinely produce letterforms that are simultaneously farther from and closer to the standard than any similarity ranking can order; (iii) identity must therefore be a matter of what a reader does with a form in a context; (iv) the same holds for many concepts beyond letters.

The audience will be about fifteen people: four or five researchers in visual word recognition and letter identification; two experimentalists who work on category learning within the dual-system framework contrasting rule-based and information-integration categorization; one computational modeler who works on generative models of typefaces; a visiting historian of book design and typography with no philosophy training; and three or four philosophers from my own department, one of whom defends epistemicism about vagueness and one of whom works on artifacts and social ontology and is deeply suspicious of any account that treats a convention-governed artifact as though it were an open-textured term like game.

What I need is a briefing document for that discussion period, not a defense of the paper. Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered overall by how much damage each would do, and for each say who is most likely to press it; phrase it in that person's own vocabulary and register. For each, supply a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, an honest note on where the reply is soft, and a judgement about whether I should concede something in advance or hold the line. Two objections I have already anticipated and dread: the one from mirror-invariance and letter-reversal errors, which appears to show that letter identity is fixed by an orientation-sensitive recognition mechanism rather than constructed; and the charge that I have simply restated family resemblance with a computational veneer. Tell me whether these are as damaging as I fear and how best to answer them, but do not let them crowd out the objections I have not thought of. Close with the question I should be hoping nobody asks — the one that exposes the weakest joint in the argument — and with one modest reformulation of the thesis that would let me absorb several of these objections in my opening five minutes without giving up the generalization that makes the paper worth writing.
