For the past eight months I have been facilitating a working group attached to a constitutional reform charity. Its brief was narrow: to assess a single proposal, drafted by one of our members, under which every adult would retain an unweighted vote, but a certification panel — drawn by lot from the top tenth of scorers on a twenty-item test of basic institutional and economic knowledge — would hold a suspensive veto over primary legislation, exercisable once per bill and overridable only by a fresh majority in the Commons. The group has produced nothing, and I have come to suspect that it never really had one disagreement to begin with.

Three positions have hardened.

The economist who drafted the proposal maintains that the opposition is aesthetic rather than argued: we already gate consequential decisions behind demonstrated competence — jurors, doctors, pilots, judges — and nobody calls that an insult to the excluded. His sharpest claim is that objecting to a knowledge test is really objecting to a hierarchy of status, and that this is a complaint about how a system feels rather than about what it delivers. He adds that bad policy kills people: housing permission delayed, energy mispriced, hospital capacity deferred. If the filter measurably reduces that toll, refusing it is a decision to accept the larger figure, and the refusal should be costed as such.

The labour historian answers that the jury and licensing analogies misconstrue what an election is for. Electoral decisions are not answers to questions posed by an authority; they are partly how a body of people constitutes itself as an authority at all. She points to the Condorcet-style claim that groups of individually fallable but better-than-chance judges tend to converge on the right answer, and says it is beside the point, since the theorem tells us nothing about who may be counted as a judge, and since the relevant question is not which arrangement maximises correct outcomes but which one the outvoted can accept without being told that their standing as citizens is conditional. Legitimacy, on her account, is what the economist keeps forgetting to include in his arithmetic.

The legal aid solicitor says both of them are arguing about a system that does not exist. Her question is who, in ten years' time, sits on the certification panel, who sets the test, and who challenges the marking. She notes that every competence qualification ever written into electoral law has been turned, within a generation, against the group most recently admitted to the franchise. She is not, she insists, making a point about political philosophy; she is asking for a staffing schedule.

One oddity has struck me. Both sides cite the same historical record of literacy and comprehension tests in electoral law, and draw opposite conclusions from it: the economist sees a discredited instrument whose abuse does not refute the principle, the historian sees the principle revealed by the use it was predictably put to. Neither treats this as a disagreement needing resolution.

My working hypothesis, which I hold loosely, is that the three are answering different questions under a shared vocabulary — the economist asking which procedure yields the best outcomes, the historian which arrangements can be justified to those bound by them, the solicitor which are capturable — and that the word legitimacy is doing quite different work in each mouth. An alternative is that the dispute is empirical in disguise: everything may turn on whether the panel would shift policy systematically in a specifiable direction, a matter that could in principle be measured, with the historian's objection vindicated and the economist's efficiency case undercut if it could.

What I want from you is the diagnosis, not a verdict on epistocracy. Identify what kind of disagreement this actually is — substantive, verbal, a plurality of questions passing as one, an empirical question in normative dress, or some combination that varies across the three pairs of positions, since I suspect the economist and the solicitor are not disagreeing in the same way as the economist and the historian. Support each claim with what the parties actually say rather than with a survey of the literature on either side. Then state what would count as progress on each layer you identify: a distinction to be drawn, a study to be run, a question to be replaced, or an argument that some layer is bedrock and will not move, together with your reason for thinking so.
