I have recently taken up a postdoctoral post that sits between two literatures on resilience, and how to characterise the relationship between them has become an awkwardly personal matter: I drafted a methods chapter describing the two traditions as incommensurable in something like Kuhn's sense, read it out at a departmental seminar last week, and was told by one colleague that I had inflated a merely verbal dispute into a metaphysical crisis, and by another that if I were right I would have no business recommending one framing over the other in the policy report I owe our funder at the end of the year. I should like the conceptual ground clear before I revise.

The case, stripped of detail. One tradition treats resilience as a property of a system at equilibrium: what matters is how quickly and by how direct a path the system returns to its prior state after a perturbation, and the paradigm evidence is a controlled disturbance with a measured return time. The other treats resilience as the capacity to absorb disturbance without crossing into a qualitatively different regime: what matters is how much stress a system can take before it reorganises, and the paradigm evidence is a long observational record in which a regime shift can be seen to have happened. Practitioners of each read one another's papers, agree about a great deal of the underlying data, and can usually explain what they mean. But the terms they lean on hardest — stability, equilibrium, recovery, the identity of the system across time — do not appear to line up one to one, and a given lake or woodland can be resilient in one sense and fragile in the other, with neither tradition treating that as a contradiction.

What I want is an explanation of the conceptual issues, not a survey of Kuhn's career and not a map of the literature. Please write in British English. I have no use for citations, references, or reading lists, and I would rather you did not guess at them; I want the distinctions argued through from their structure. In particular:

1. Separate the strands that the word incommensurability bundles together in Kuhn's writing — the methodological strand concerning the absence of a shared algorithm for theory choice, the perceptual strand concerning proponents of rival views seeing different things in the same situation, and the semantic or taxonomic strand concerning terms that cannot be mapped onto one another. Explain why he took these to be separable, such that a case may exhibit one without the others, and what each strand would have to look like in practice for us to say it is present.

2. Take up the question of scope. Explain the difference between holding that a whole theory-language resists translation and holding that a limited set of terms within it does, and explain Kuhn's later distinction between translating a text and learning a second language, together with what that distinction is meant to buy him. Then say whether local incommensurability, if that is all we have here, is compatible with rational comparison, and identify what is doing the comparative work when someone has acquired both ways of talking.

3. Diagnose my case. Which strand or strands does it actually exhibit, and how much of what looks like incommensurability is really ordinary scientific ambiguity, vagueness in the extension of a term, or the theory-ladenness of observation? Give me something I can apply rather than a verdict: a short test or checklist with explicit conditions, run through the resilience example.

4. Address the objection that the whole notion is self-undermining — that we cannot identify a way of talking as genuinely different from our own unless it is already largely translatable into ours, so that a claim of untranslatability either collapses into a claim about mere differences of emphasis or defeats itself. Explain the force of that objection, what responses remain open, and how much of Kuhn's position survives them.

5. Finally, the practical one. Is it true that if two frameworks are incommensurable, choosing between them is arbitrary? Please explain carefully the difference between there being no decision procedure and there being no reasons, and say what my funder-facing report may legitimately claim when it prefers one framing for a specified purpose.

One further caution: I do not accept that 'talking past each other' is an adequate folk gloss on any of this. Please tell me where that phrase misleads, if indeed it does, and what would be a better ordinary-language description of the failure.
