I have been trying to determine whether my objections to certain markets amount to anything principled, and I would like the pattern behind my verdicts tested rather than merely restated.

The cases, with my gut reactions:

1. Paying an app to hold my place in the queue for a passport appointment: acceptable.
2. Paying a stranger to camp overnight so that I can buy concert tickets at face value when they go on sale: not acceptable — and I cannot articulate how this differs from (1).
3. Selling a kidney to a stranger: not acceptable. Selling blood plasma twice a month for cash: acceptable.
4. Paying a woman to carry a child, with all expenses covered: not acceptable. Adopting a child and paying an agency its fees: acceptable.
5. Selling my vote in a municipal election: not acceptable. Selling the development rights attached to my land to a neighbor: acceptable.
6. Charging forty dollars for a case of bottled water at a ski lodge: acceptable. Charging the same amount after a hurricane, to people with no alternative supply: not acceptable.
7. Paying healthy volunteers for a Phase I drug trial: acceptable. Offering prisoners triple the rate for the same trial: not acceptable.
8. A corporation buying the naming rights to a stadium: acceptable. A school district selling the naming rights to its gymnasium: not acceptable.
9. Paying my niece five dollars for every book she finishes: not acceptable, even though it plainly works.

My tentative principle, which I will call the buyback test: a sale is objectionable just when the seller cannot afterwards recover what they sold. A kidney cannot be bought back; plasma is regenerated; a vote once sold is gone for that election; a school cannot recover what its gymnasium's name used to mean. I know this is too crude — it seems to condemn ordinary wage labor, since my hours are equally unrecoverable — but I cannot see how to repair it.

Please answer in the following sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

**Bottom line** (two or three sentences): whether a single principle can capture all nine verdicts.

**The pattern** (about 150 words): what my verdicts appear to be tracking, stated as a diagnosis rather than as a list.

**Where the buyback test breaks** (about 250 words): the cases it handles, the cases it gets wrong, and the wage-labor objection in particular.

**Candidate principles** (about 450 words): two or three, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have never encountered, with every clause tied to the numbered case or cases that motivate it.

**Predictions** (about 200 words): apply your leading candidate to three cases not on my list — selling the right to tattoo an advertisement on one's forehead; paying teenagers for each day they go without their phone at school; a company buying a village's water rights and reselling them at a profit — and say whether a verdict of mine that conflicts with the prediction should count against the principle.

**What I should drop** (about 150 words): which of my nine verdicts each candidate cannot accommodate, and which single verdict you would advise me to abandon.
