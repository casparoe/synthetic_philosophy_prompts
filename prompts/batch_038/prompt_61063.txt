ok so I spent three hours last night arguing with my friend Dez about Kelly betting and geometric rationality and I need to get it out of my system in a form that isnt an argument.

Quick version of where we landed. There's a deal on the table, repeated over and over: a spotter sells you a tip before each trip, and if you take it you stake some fraction of your whole float. Half the time the trip pays +60% on what you staked, half the time it costs you 40% of what you staked. Stake nothing and you just bleed slowly — harbor fees, diesel, call it −1% a trip. Bet the whole float and the average across all the possible versions of you says +10% a trip, gorgeous on paper. But follow it through one actual life and betting everything shrinks you: up 60% half the time, down 40% half the time compounds to about −2% a trip. The fraction that actually grows you is somewhere near 40% of the float, worth about +2% a trip. Dez's position: that's just "assume your utility of money is the logarithm of money" wearing a physics costume, there's no new content, and dressing it up as being about one life over time instead of across outcomes is branding. My position: it isn't branding, because ruin here is absorbing — hit zero and there is no next trip — and no taste parameter is doing any work, the shape of the situation is.

Then the part where I think Dez half won, and the part that made me want a story instead. Suppose you don't have forever. Suppose you need one specific lump sum — enough to buy out your brother's share of the boat, say — by a date about thirty trips out. Then the growth-optimal 40% is not what you should do. You overbet on purpose, maybe you take the coin flip on the entire float, because falling short by a mile and falling short by an inch are the same outcome to you, and the policy that's right across decades is wrong across one deadline. Which looks like it proves his point: the answer moved the second the utility function moved.

Write me a short story, 1300–1700 words, that puts that dispute inside people.

- Two characters who are related or have known each other a long time, on a working waterfront. The deal above has to be real in their world — translate it into whatever they're actually staking, float or catch or fuel or the boat itself — and the numbers have to survive the translation. Don't quietly turn it into a gamble that grows you no matter how you bet it.
- One of them has played the slow policy for years and it worked, or it didn't. The other one has the deadline. Neither of them gets written as an idiot.
- Somebody should say the word ruin in the ordinary, non-technical sense, and it should land wrong.
- No equations, no terms of art — no "Kelly," no "ergodic," no "expected utility" — and no scene where one character explains the theory to the other. A reader should be able to reconstruct the argument purely from what people do.
- Optional, use it only if it earns its place: the tip isn't free information, it's a person, and you can play with what a partial unreliable signal is worth. There's an old result to the effect that the fastest your money can grow per round, when you bet well on a noisy signal, equals the information that signal carries, measured in the same units people measure information in. I remember that roughly, not exactly. If you use it and garble it, flag it in the commentary rather than smoothing it over.
- The ending should not decide who won the argument. It should leave me less sure than I was, not more.
