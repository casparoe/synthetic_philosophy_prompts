I sit on a three-person comms ethics panel at a public health nonprofit and I'm usually the tiebreaker, so I've been trying to get my own line on manipulation versus legitimate persuasion down on paper before the next round of campaigns. Here's where I've landed:

1. Manipulation is about the mechanism, not the motive. An influence is manipulative to the degree that it produces the change through emotion, habit, inertia, or social pressure rather than through reasons the person could actually weigh. And it isn't automatically wrong — you decide that separately.
2. But you do need intent. Nobody manipulates by accident. If you weren't aiming to get around their reasoning, it isn't manipulation no matter how much you in fact got around it.
3. The permisibility test is retrospecive endorsement: if they'd say, knowing exactly what you did and why, that they're glad you did it, it's fine.
4. Case verdicts I'm confident about: graphic photos on vaping packs aimed at teens — fine. Doom-and-grief storytelling in climate fundraising — manipulative, but fine, the cause is just. Auto-enrolling patients as organ donors with an opt-out — off limits, even though basically everyone says they support donation when you ask them. And the one I can't fit anywhere: our palliative team's scripted "what matters to you at the end?" conversation is fine, but I think it stops being fine once we coach staff on a warmer tone and slower pacing for delivering it, even though the words and the intention are identical in both versions.

Go through it. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real ones by severity, say what would have to be given up in each case, and tell me which single revision would clean up the most damage. Exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion.
