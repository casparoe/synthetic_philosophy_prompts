For eleven years I have followed the forum where my former thesis supervisor posts the rulings she gives on her term's problem sets, and I have got into the habit of trying to reverse-engineer her decision rule from the samples before I read her own gloss. Her exercise never varies in form. She stipulates a toy world by giving nothing but its total distribution of local, particular matters of fact — what her students call the mosaic — together with one or more candidate generalisations about that world, and the class must say which candidates are laws of nature there and which are not. She forbids them to answer by naming schools of thought; the ruling is meant to come out of the world as described.

This term's seven rulings, as I have transcribed them.

1. Gridworld. An infinite square grid of cells, each lit or dark, evolving in discrete steps. Everywhere and always, a cell is lit at the next step if and only if exactly two of its four orthogonal neighbours were lit at the previous step. No exceptions anywhere in the world's history. Candidate: that rule. Ruling: a law.

2. Gridworld with a speck. As above, except that at one step, in one cell, in one place, the rule fails once; thereafter the grid evolves from the altered state by the same rule, the failure is never repeated, and nothing else in the world accounts for it. Candidates: the rule as stated; the rule with an exception clause naming that cell and that step. Ruling: the unqualified rule is a law, the exception-clause version is not, and the speck is an accident. She adds that the ruling would have gone the other way had the failures been many, or patterned.

3. Aurium and balium. The world contains two elements. Every sphere of aurium in the mosaic is under a hundred kilometres across; nothing in aurium's constitution limits its size, and it is simply that no larger aurium sphere ever formed. Every sphere of balium is likewise under a hundred kilometres across, because balium above that mass promptly flies apart, and the mosaic shows this happening again and again to fragments that grew too large. Candidates: no aurium sphere exceeds a hundred kilometres; no balium sphere exceeds a hundred kilometres. Ruling: the second is a law, the first is not. She stresses that both hold without exception throughout the mosaic, so exceptionlessness is not what she is tracking.

4. Two halves. The universe divides into two halves that never interact. In the left half cells evolve by rule P; in the right half by rule Q; P and Q are equally simple and mutually incompatible. Candidates: P holds everywhere; Q holds everywhere; P holds in the left half and Q in the right. Ruling: the third is a law, the first two are not. Pressed afterwards, she says she would just as readily describe the world as having two laws, P holding here and Q holding there, neither of which generalises to the whole.

5. The cheap predicate. The mosaic admits two complete, true axiomatisations. The first uses only predicates she elsewhere treats as unobjectionable and runs to eleven clauses. The second runs to six, but its central predicate is a gerrymander: a disjunction pairing a kind of particle with a kind of event at the far side of the galaxy, united by nothing except that the disjunction happens to shorten the derivation. Ruling: the eleven-clause system states the laws and the six-clause system does not, notwithstanding that it is shorter. Length, she says, is a proxy, and must be counted in the right currency.

6. The coin world. The mosaic consists of a billion events of two types, evenly split, with no further structure of any kind — no ordering, no correlation, nothing from which any event's type could be derived. Candidate: each event, when it occurs, has chance one half of being of the first type. Ruling: a law. She adds that she is not thereby taking a position on whether anything in the world produces the distribution, and that a student who said the candidate is a law because it belongs in the best description of the mosaic and a student who said it is a law because the events are so produced have both answered the question she asked.

7. The phase change. The mosaic obeys rule R for its first billion years and rule S thereafter, the switch occurring at a determinate instant, with nothing at that instant except the switch. Candidates: R holds always; S holds always; R holds before the boundary and S after; a single rule covering the transition. Ruling: the third is a law, the first two are not, and there is no law governing the transition. The boundary, she says, is brute, and a world of this kind is not thereby a world in which some law is being violated.

Next week's case is the one I want your prediction on. I have reconstructed it from a student's paraphrase, so treat my wording as fallible.

The untried configuration. A finite gridworld. Throughout the entire mosaic — its whole history, beginning to end — a cell is lit at the next step if and only if exactly two of its four orthogonal neighbours were lit at the previous step. Call this g1. One configuration never arises anywhere in the mosaic: no cell ever has all four of its neighbours lit at once. The world's initial state and its evolution simply never produce it. Now consider the rival g2: a cell is lit at the next step if and only if the number of its lit neighbours is even and non-zero. g2 agrees with g1 about every cell at every step in the world's history and differs from it only about the configuration that never occurs. Stipulate, since it is a toy, that in the privileged vocabulary the two are exactly the same length, use predicates of exactly the same standing, and entail precisely the same claims about every actual event, so that any systematisation containing one can be exchanged for a systematisation containing the other at no cost whatever in simplicity or strength.

The twist: late in the world's history its engineers build a device which, if switched on, would force a single cell to have all four of its neighbours lit for one step. They never switch it on. Nothing in the mosaic records what would have happened had they done so.

Her question to the class is whether g1 is a law of that world, whether g2 is, and whether the built-but-unused device bears on the answer.

What I would like from you:

(a) Set out the candidate decision rules that are jointly consistent with all seven rulings. State each as a general criterion rather than as a label, and say which of the seven each is strained by, if any. I expect at least one candidate to be killed outright by ruling 5 and at least one to be bruised by ruling 2, and I should like to see you say which, and why the bruised one survives at all.

(b) For each surviving rule, derive its verdict on the untried configuration — g1, g2, both, neither, or indeterminate — and separately, what that rule makes of the device.

(c) Your best guess at her actual verdict, with a credence, and the crux you would bet on: why the rule you favour fits the seven rulings better than its closest rival does. Not a survey of the landscape.

(d) Design one further toy world in her format — a mosaic plus candidates — that would separate your top two rules. Say what each of the two would rule, and confirm that no third surviving rule splits the same way.

(e) Flag where my transcription may be doing work I have not noticed. In particular: whether rulings 4 and 7 are the same ruling twice; whether the disclaimer in ruling 6 is as neutral as I have reported it; and whether the format of the exercise — specifying a world by nothing but local particular facts — already answers a question she means to leave open. For what it is worth, my prior after seven terms of this is that she is some kind of Humean, but rulings 2, 4 and 6 each look to me like places where that prior is load-bearing in a way I could not defend under questioning, so please treat the rulings as the data and me as a biased reporter.

I am not after the standard tour of the debate and I have no use for a reading list; the toy worlds are self-contained by design, so work inside them.

Format: open with the bottom line, a short paragraph answering (c). Then a single table covering (a) and (b), with the columns rule (a short name of your own coining), criterion (one or two sentences), strained by (ruling numbers, or none), verdict on the untried configuration, status of the device, and your credence that this is the rule she runs. Then (d) and (e) in prose. Close with a JSON object using exactly these keys: predicted_verdict (one of g1, g2, both, neither, indeterminate, other); verdict_confidence (a number between 0 and 1); device_bearing (one of settles it retroactively, would reveal it but not make it, irrelevant, unsettled until used, other); leading_rule; runner_up; discriminating_case (an object with keys mosaic, candidates, leading_rule_rules, runner_up_rules); transcription_doubts (an array of strings). Keep every string value to a sentence or two.
