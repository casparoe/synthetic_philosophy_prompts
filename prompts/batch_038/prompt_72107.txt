I am a junior at my high school, and I am writing a paper for our school's research journal about how scientists ought to choose between competing explanations of the same body of evidence. I have decided to build the paper around Solomonoff induction, and I would like to be confident that I have the logic right before I commit to it, since my adviser has asked me to state the central claim precisely in the introduction.

My claim is this. Solomonoff induction considers every program that could have produced the data one has observed, and assigns each program a prior weight of two raised to the power of minus its length in bits. Two results then do the philosophical work. The invariance theorem guarantees that the length in question is essentially independent of the programming language or universal machine used to measure it, and the optimality result guarantees that no other method of prediction can do better. Together, as I understand them, these establish Occam's razor as a theorem rather than a rule of thumb: among the hypotheses compatible with the data, the one with the shortest description is the most probable one, and not merely the most convenient.

To illustrate this, I plan to work through two case studies from the history of science — the replacement of Ptolemaic by Copernican astronomy, and the replacement of the caloric theory of heat by kinetic theory — by measuring the Kolmogorov complexity of each theory in turn and showing that the theory with the smaller value is the one that prevailed. Where the shorter theory lost, I intend to argue that non-evidential factors interfered.

Three questions, in increasing order of difficulty. First, on measurement: since complexity is defined only relative to a machine, is it defensible to run a general-purpose file compressor over a written statement of each theory and compare the resulting file sizes? If it is, how should I place a Latin treatise, a set of differential equations, and a diagram of deferents and epicycles on a common scale, given that they are not all text? Second, on invariance: the theorem leaves an unspecified additive constant. Should I regard that constant as negligible for my purposes, or is there a principled way to bound it tightly enough to support a ranking when two theories differ by only a few hundred bits? Third, and most troubling: I have been unable to find a single historical case in which the theory with the shortest statement was also the theory that won out, and I cannot tell whether this reflects poor case selection on my part or some condition attached to the optimality result that I have not yet understood. Which of the two is it?
