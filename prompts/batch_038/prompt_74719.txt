I'm drafting an argument about Simpson's paradox for a philosophy-of-science course, to motivate the idea that causal inference needs more than associations. The problem is that it now appears to end in causal skepticism, and I can't accept that ending.

(1) In the standard kidney-stone case, open surgery looks worse than the less invasive procedure overall, yet does better both among patients with small stones and among patients with large stones, because stone size influences both which procedure a patient receives and how well they do.

(2) In such cases the aggregate association is an artifact of that third variable, while the within-subpopulation comparisons track what the treatment actually does.

(3) So when an aggregate comparison and a disaggregated comparison conflict, the disaggregated comparison is the one to believe.

(4) A finer partition is never worse evidence than the coarser partition it refines: it preserves everything the coarse comparison supported, since the cases compared are the same cases, while additionally holding one further fact fixed.

(5) Any dataset admits of indefinitely finer partitions, one for each further variable we can measure or might have measured — clinic, physician, week of admission, comorbidity score — and there is no general procedure for telling in advance whether a given partition will reverse the association we started from.

(6) Therefore, for any aggregate association we cite as causal, a finer partition may exist on which it fails, and we are never in a position to rule out that it does.

(7) So no finite dataset licenses an unqualified causal claim; it licenses at most the claim that the association holds relative to the partition we happened to settle on.

I want to keep (1) and (2), since they are just the ordinary diagnosis of confounding, and giving them up surrenders the reason the paradox is interesting at all. What I cannot live with is (7). Tell me which step I should reject, why that step looked acceptable where it stands, and what the rejection costs me. In particular: is (3) a principle I'm entitled to in general, or does it hold only for some variables and not others — and if the latter, can the restriction be stated without already assuming causal knowledge of the kind (7) says the data cannot supply?
