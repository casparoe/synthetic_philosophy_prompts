I am preparing a handout for the seventh week of a graduate seminar on minds and machines. The assigned reading is Lucas's 1961 paper, the Gödelian argument in Penrose's Shadows of the Mind, and three critical responses, and my students reliably leave the week conflating at least four objections that are not the same objection. What I want from you is a decision tree that lets a student locate their own position and shows them how much of the logical space the debate leaves empty.

The root should be a commitment every student can accept before branching: state the incompleteness result in the form the argument needs (a consistent formal system with effectively enumerable axioms, strong enough for a decent amount of arithmetic, contains a sentence unprovable in it that is true provided the system is sound), together with the claim that a Turing machine's reasoning is governed by such a system. Add one line noting that the second half of the root is itself an idealization, since it is closer to what the argument must establish than to what it may assume.

Every subsequent node must be a single question about a move in the argument, phrased so that a student can answer it without knowing which position the answer leads to. At minimum the following forks must appear somewhere in the tree. Decide their order on the grounds of which is most discriminating, prune any that turn out not to fork, and add any you consider more fundamental than these:

- whether the argument requires that the system be one we can identify, or only that some system be us;
- whether it requires the system to be known consistent, merely consistent, or sound, and whether the soundness at issue is the system's or ours;
- whether the conclusion is positive (we are not Turing-computable) or dialectical (whoever asserts we are machines owes a specification, and any specification offered can be turned against them);
- whether the reflective capacity invoked is a one-off act of insight or a repeatable procedure, and if repeatable, whether iterating it generates a hierarchy of reflection that outruns the original system and forces the argument to be re-run at each stage;
- whether the step from unprovable to true requires a determinate, mind-independent conception of arithmetic truth;
- whether the argument is best run on Gödel sentences or on the variant formulation in terms of algorithms for ascertaining that computations do not halt, and whether that choice alters which objections bite;
- whether non-algorithmicity of mathematical insight generalizes to the claim that we are not machines at all;
- whether machine in the conclusion means Turing-computable or physically realizable, and hence whether there is room for a physical process that is not a computable one;
- whether a student who takes us to be inconsistent or systematically fallible reasoners belongs at a distinct leaf, and whether that leaf can retain the premise that we see the truth of the Gödel sentence;
- whether the argument is available to actual finite mathematicians or only to idealized ones, and whether the idealization is a premise the mechanist need not grant.

At least ten leaves. Each should carry the vector of answers that reaches it; a name if the position has a standard one, and unnamed if it does not; one sentence naming its most awkward commitment; and one sentence saying why a competent philosopher would hold it rather than something adjacent. Attach a proponent's name only where you are confident, and if you are not, describe the view and leave the name off rather than guessing. Where a combination of answers is coherent but, so far as you know, undefended, keep it as a leaf and mark it as unoccupied territory, with a one-line diagnosis of whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Then two further pieces. First, flag at least two pairs of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say whether some further question would separate them or whether the collapse is real. Second, write three sorting questions for the seminar, each a concrete situation rather than a label, and say which of them the tree treats as decisive and why. Three situations to work from: a colleague produces a complete listing of a program together with a proof that the program's reasoning is captured by a consistent formal system, and claims the program is you; a colleague produces only a proof that some program is you, without saying which; and you are told that you are a Turing machine, that this is true, and that no one, including you, could ever establish it.

Render the tree as a compact indented outline that would fit on a single page, keep the whole thing to roughly 2000 words, and give me no bibliography and no reference list. I will supply citations myself.

Finally, a critical note of about 250 words. Tell me which branch node carries the most weight in your tree, and why you think that same node is the one most likely to be a badly drawn distinction. Tell me which leaf you judge most defensible and what specific argument or consideration would move you to a different leaf. And tell me where the tree is sharper than the debate it maps: which two leaves are really one view described twice, or which of the forks I listed is a pseudo-fork that separates nothing. If one of them is, route around it and say so rather than manufacturing a distinction to fill my specification.
