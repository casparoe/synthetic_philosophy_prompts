I have just finished a year as secretary to a constitutional review commission in a country with middling levels of institutional trust, and we have been asked to recommend what posture toward the state our new institutions should presume in citizens and actively cultivate. Two commissioners have argued about this from the first session to the last: a Danish public-administration scholar who holds that the Scandinavian default of trust toward government is the more reasonable attitude for a citizen to hold, and an American constitutional lawyer who holds that the American default of suspicion — distrust built into the machinery rather than left to mood — is the more reasonable one. Both have worked extensively outside their own countries, neither is a nationalist, and neither is a crank. I should like you to reconstruct their argument at its very best as a written dialogue, with speaker labels, of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words. Please write it in American English.

To keep them out of the weeds of data disputes, assume both accept the following without argument: Nordic countries have for decades sat at or near the top of international surveys of trust in public institutions and of trust in fellow citizens, and the United States has consistently sat well below them on both; American trust in the federal government has fallen from something like three-quarters of the population in the late 1950s to around a fifth or less today; Nordic administration is by no means verification-free, running extensive audit, ombudsman and transparency machinery and relying heavily on pre-filled tax returns and randomised post-payment audit rather than exhaustive checking of every claim in advance; American institutions, for all the distrust, deliver a great deal and have absorbed severe shocks without coming apart; and whether Nordic trust produced good government or was produced by it is genuinely unsettled, with the causal arrow plausibly running in both directions and reinforcing itself. They may not cite studies, name researchers, quote figures beyond those above, or appeal to national character — I want argument, not authority, and I do not want a bibliography, a list of sources, or suggestions for further reading at the end.

What I care about is that they actually answer one another. Each must engage the other's most recent point rather than restate a position, and each must concede something substantive at least once. Specifically, the Dane should press the thought that suspicion is not merely a belief but an expensive institutional technology with characteristic failure modes of its own — capture of the auditing apparatus by the parties it is meant to check, procedural paralysis, and a citizenry trained to expect bad faith that recruits and trains officials accordingly — while the American should press the thought that Nordic trust is a contingent and possibly fragile achievement of small, historically homogeneous societies with a particular administrative inheritance, that its efficiency is purchased by accepting a tail risk that arrives late and is hard to reverse, and that institutionalised distrust is the better bet under uncertainty precisely because it does not depend on the virtue of whoever happens to hold office. Both should be made to confront two further problems. The first is the regress: every scheme of distrust must terminate somewhere in a trusted node — courts, a free press, electoral administrators — and every scheme of trust retains some verification node, so the real disagreement may be not how much trust but where the residual trust is lodged and what happens if that particular node is itself corrupted. The second is a distinction they should draw explicitly: trust as an evidence-tracking belief about reliability, versus trust as a normative expectation that helps to bring its own object into being. That distinction should force them to say whether the question I have set — which attitude is more reasonable — is being asked in an epistemic sense, meaning which attitude fits the worldwide base rates of government performance, or in a practical sense, meaning which attitude yields better outcomes when it is actually held, and to say what should be done if the two senses come apart. Finally, each should be made to state the characteristic error of the other's attitude rather than merely its average cost: catastrophic betrayal that is discovered too late, on one side; chronic underperformance, low compliance and slow crisis response, accepted in advance, on the other. Do not resolve the argument. End with one of them naming what they take to be the crux and the other refusing to accept that this is where the disagreement lies.
