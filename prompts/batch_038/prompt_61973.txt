I sat in on a friend's decision-theory reading group last night and two of the regulars got into it about money pumps, and I genuinely couldnt tell who was winning. Here's the case they were arguing over, roughly as they stated it: Dev prefers camera A to B, B to C, and C to A — a strict cycle, with each gap a lot bigger than a dollar. He currently owns C. A dealer offers three swaps in sequence, each for a $1 fee: C for B, then B for A, then A for C. Dev takes all three and ends up holding C again with $3 less.

Then:

(1) Mara said that's a proof that cyclic preferences are irrational. He accepted every step voluntarily and finished somewhere he'd have paid to avoid. Textbook self-defeat.

(2) Ivo said that just assumes he's myopic — that he takes each swap without seeing the tree. A sophisticated Dev backward-inducts: at the third swap he compares accepting (C, -$3) with declining (A, -$2), and declines, because he prefers A to C and keeps a dollar more. Work backwards and he takes the first two swaps and ends up with A for a net $2, which he prefers to C. No loss, no pump. So the example condemns myopia, not intransitivity.

(3) Mara said: look at what your sophisticated agent does at the third swap. He turns down C for A — the one swap in the cycle he says he'd want. So on your story his preferences aren't what shows up in his choices any more; you saved him by changing what the preferences are allowed to do. And the rescue is contingent anyway, since it needs him to see the whole sequence in advance. Reveal one swap at a time, or make him a bit forgetful, and he's pumped. Whether a preference is rational cant hinge on the agent's forecasting talent.

(4) Ivo said seeing the tree isn't a heroic assumption, it's the standard one in any dynamic problem. And "can be made to hand over money in some environment" is a terrible criterion of rationality: a perfectly transitive buyer facing a take-it-or-leave-it seller gets his whole surplus extracted too. Being on the losing end of someone else's bargaining power isn't incoherence.

(5) Mara said extraction leaves you with the best deal available given the other party's power, whereas the pump leaves you somewhere you'd pay to undo, and you got there purely through your own choices. And here's the tell he's dodging: for a transitive agent who likes money, greedy step-by-step choice and planning the whole thing ahead give the same result — every swap he accepts is an improvement in the full bundle, and transitivity chains those improvements, so he can never be walked in a circle. Myopic and sophisticated coincide exactly when the preferences are acyclic. The fact that you have to import a special second-order capacity to stop the bleeding is the pump working, not the pump failing.

(6) Ivo said: then your conclusion is much weaker than where you started. All you've shown is that greedy-plus-cyclic is bad, and you owe an independent argument that real agents are greedy rather than planners. And what you're leaning on — that the right model of agency is foresee-and-plan — is a substantive normative thesis about diachronic rationality, not something the pump derives. You assumed it and dressed it up as a result.

Can you adjudicate this turn by turn? For each of the six, say whether it lands, half-lands, or misses, and why — including whether Ivo's backward induction in (2) is actually correct as stated, and whether finishing with A at a net $2 counts as having been pumped. Then: who's more right about the thing they actually care about, whether the pump shows cyclic preferences to be irrational? And what should one of them have said that neither of them did?
