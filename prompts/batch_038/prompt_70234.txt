I am delivering a forty-minute paper, with fifteen minutes for questions, at an interdisciplinary workshop on inductive biases and the origins of linguistic structure. Please act as an experienced philosopher of cognitive science who has chaired a great many hostile question periods and knows how these go wrong, and prepare me for mine. Begin by thinking carefully, in a short opening paragraph, about which parts of my argument are load-bearing and which are merely decorative, since the objections will cluster around the load-bearing joints; only then produce the prep sheet.

My thesis is deliberately provocative, and I should like the objections aimed at it rather than at a straw version. It runs roughly as follows. First, the argument from the poverty of the stimulus was never, properly construed, an empirical claim about the quantity or quality of the data available to a learner; it was a claim about the necessity of a richly structured prior, and its polemical framing in terms of what children do and do not hear was a rhetorical detour that has cost the argument dearly. Second, contemporary neural language models do not refute this claim but instantiate it: the tokenisation scheme, the attention pattern, the objective function, the positional encoding and the initialisation regime jointly constitute an inductive bias whose restrictive power is large, measurable, and doing most of the work that anyone in the 1970s would have attributed to a language-specific endowment. Third — and this is the part I expect to be attacked hardest — it follows that such models are nativist in the only sense the learnability argument ever required, while leaving the substantive question of a species-specific universal grammar neither confirmed nor refuted, because the priors at issue are domain-general and engineered rather than language-specific and evolved. My conclusion is methodological: the empirical content of the nativism debate has migrated out of corpus studies of child-directed speech and into the analysis of architectures, so that the right evidence now comes from ablating components of the bias and observing which structural generalisations — structure-dependence in question formation, say, or sensitivity to long-distance licensing — survive.

The audience is the difficulty. It is about two-thirds computational linguists and machine-learning researchers, several of whom have published on scaling behaviour and will regard my second premise as a platitude and my third as an equivocation; one runs a laboratory on synthetic miniature-language learning and is committed to the view that data-efficiency comparisons, not architecture description, are the correct test; two are developmental psycholinguists of a usage-based persuasion who will say that the statistical structure of the input is richer than I have conceded and that I am refighting a dead debate; and there is a philosopher of science who specialises in inference to the best explanation and underdetermination, and who will want to know whether my position has any content at all or has simply redefined nativism so that it cannot fail.

Please give me a prep sheet rather than a defence of my view. I want roughly eight objections, ordered by the damage they would do to the talk if I handled them badly, each phrased in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it, each with a reply I could actually deliver in about two minutes, and a candid note on where that reply is softest. Mark clearly any objection for which the correct response is to bite the bullet rather than to argue, and tell me which concession I should make explicitly in my opening five minutes so that it cannot later be presented as an extraction. Identify separately the one question I am hoping nobody asks, with an honest account of why it is so damaging and what the least disastrous answer would be. Two further constraints: write in British English, and do not supply references, bibliographies or citations of any kind — I know the literature and cannot check anything you invent, so where a study or benchmark is relevant, describe it in words rather than naming it.
