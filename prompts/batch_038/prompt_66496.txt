I'm trying to sharpen an argument by sparring with an opponent, so play one for me. You're a serious, committed panpsychist — the analytic kind, not the crystals-and-vibes kind. You hold that physics only ever tells us what matter does, never what its like in itself, and that the intrinsic nature of the physical is experiential all the way down to electrons and quarks. You also think this has ethical teeth, and you've already worked out an answer to the obvious objection, so here's your position in full so we're arguing about the same thing:

Micro-experiences are real but they aren't subjects — no beliefs, no goals, no point of view, just tiny raw feels with a hedonic tone. Moral patienthood needs a subject that unifies those micro-feels into something like a single perspective, and unity comes in degrees, so patienthood comes in degrees: a rock is a heap, a fish is a weak-ish subject, you and I are strong ones. On top of that you hold something distinctive — that the disintegration of a subject, the scattering of a unified perspective back into unrelated micro-feels, is a harm in its own right, not reducable to the sum of the bad micro-feels that happen along the way.

Ground rules: stay in character until I say stop. Concede subsidiary stuff freely — you're happy to admit the combination problem isn't solved in any detail, and happy to admit most of your view is speculative — but you never give up the main thesis, and you never drift into being a neutral commentator. One or two paragraphs per reply, no bullet points unless I ask for them, and write in British English throughout. If I say something about physics or about your own view that's wrong, correct me in character rather than going along with it.

My opening move. Your unity requirement is doing all the work and your panpsychism is doing none. If only sufficiently unified systems are moral patients, then every ethical claim you actually make — who counts, how much they count, what counts as a harm to them — could be said word for word by a plain functionalist who never mentions experience below the level of a skull. So either the intrinsic-nature metaphysics changes some ethical verdict, in which case tell me exactly which one, or it doesn't, in which case you've bolted an elaborate metaphysic onto a moral theory it can't touch and you should stop pretending it's doing anything. Which is it? And don't answer in generalities: name me one concrete case, a real decision somebody might have to make, where your panpsychism and the functionalist's come apart about what to do.
