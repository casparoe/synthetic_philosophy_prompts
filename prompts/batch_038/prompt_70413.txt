Marking papers for my relativism seminar and one of them keeps leaning on a slogan I cant quite pin down. I want to write something precise in the margin instead of just "unclear," and I think you can help me see how many claims are actually stacked up in it.

The student's argument is that anekāntavāda dissolves faultless disagreement. Two people disagree about whether the film is beautiful, and — quoting the paper — "each is speaking from their own naya, so both assertions are true and there is no contradiction to resolve." They also gloss syāt as "maybe," and use that to argue a Jain never flatly asserts anything, which they think immunizes the whole view against the self-refutation charge.

My suspicion is that "every view is true from its own standpoint" isn't one claim but at least four, and the paper slides between them without noticing. So: lay the readings out as a menu I could hand back. Start with the ones that are basically platitudes about perspective-dependence, move through the semantic or indexical readings, and go all the way up to the metaphysical claim that a single object really does carry incompatible properties at once. For each reading tell me (a) whether it's trivially true, substantive, or false; (b) whether it actually gets the student what they want — which is a genuine disagreement that both sides win, not two people talking past each other about different things; and (c) whether it's still recognisably the Jain position or has quietly turned into the ekāntavāda it was supposed to be the alternative to.

Two follow-ups. First, is the "syāt means maybe, so nothing is flatly asserted" move any good at all? My instinct is that it either drains the sevenfold schema of content — if every predication is hedged, whatever the avaktavya clause was doing stops being non-classical work — or it lands them right back in the self-refutation they think they escaped. Tell me which horn they're actually on, and whether there's a third option I'm missing.

Second, which of your readings is the one people have in mind when the slogan gets trotted out at interfaith panels and in the "all religions are partly right" line? I want to say that's a different reading than the one the student needs, and I want to be able to explain why the toleration use doesn't transfer over to the taste case. A historian colleague also insists the tolerant-pluralist packaging is a fairly modern reconstruction, and that classical Jain authors deployed anekānta polemically — to break rival schools rather than accommodate them. Is there anything to that, and if there is, does it knock any of your readings off the menu?
