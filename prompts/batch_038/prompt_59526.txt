I am attempting to determine, from my own considered verdicts rather than by adopting a system wholesale, what I am committed to when I say that a claim about the future is or is not already true. Below are the cases, with my verdicts and, where I can identify it, the feature of the case I suspect is doing the work. I would like a principle — or a small set of competing principles — that accounts for the pattern.

Cases and verdicts:

1. An admiral, before any order has been given, says that there will be a sea battle tomorrow. Verdict: neither true nor false; there is as yet no fact of the matter. But I also judge the disjunction — either there will be a sea battle tomorrow or there will not — to be straightforwardly true.

2. The same admiral, the same sentence, but the order has now been issued and is irrevocable, the fleet is under way, and no vessel can physically turn back. Verdict: the sentence is now simply true. Nothing about the future seems to have changed except that fewer of the options remain.

3. A claim computing the return of a comet from present positions and known laws. Verdict: true now, and equally true a thousand years ago, when nobody could have computed it. Unknowability makes no difference to any verdict on this list.

4. A die that has left the cup and is tumbling through the air: it will land six — either true or false, though nobody can say which. The same sentence uttered two seconds before the throw: neither true nor false. My verdict flips across an instant of an otherwise continuous physical process.

5. It will be raining in this city at noon on 4 April 2091. Verdict: there is a fact of the matter, because the atmosphere evolves, so far as I know, deterministically, even though the claim is hopeless in practice. I notice that this looks very much like case 4 before the throw, and I cannot say why they should differ.

6. That I will take the coffee rather than the tea, said of myself in a moment I regard as genuinely open. Verdict: neither true nor false. Yet I also believe that my deliberation is a physical process in a world whose physics may be deterministic, in which case 3 and 5 seem to say the claim is true now. I want both verdicts and do not see how to keep both.

7. Of an atom whose decay the theory treats as irreducibly probabilistic at exactly one half: that this atom will decay within the hour — neither true nor false. But that there is a chance of one half that it will decay within the hour — flatly true now. Any principle I accept must let a chance claim be determinate about an outcome that is not.

8. The battle has now taken place. Looking back, I have no inclination to say that the admiral's assertion in case 1 was untrue, or that it became true only today; I want to say it was true when he made it. This appears to contradict my verdict on case 1, and I cannot tell which of the two I should abandon.

9. A claim that something will happen in a remote epoch, in a universe that ends before that epoch arrives. Verdict: false, not gappy. Openness seems to require that a future actually be there to be open.

10. While I deliberate I cannot treat the proposition that I will do A as settled — but I take that to be a fact about my position as an agent, not a fact about truth values. A principle that explains case 6 by appeal to what a deliberator may believe has got hold of the wrong thing.

My tentative principle: a future-tensed claim has a truth value now just in case the present state of the world, together with the actual laws, entails it or its negation. Please test it against the list and repair it. It already looks wrong somewhere: it seems to make case 6 determinate whenever the world is deterministic; it gives no account of why case 4's verdict flips at a moment I could never locate; it does not explain why the disjunction in case 1 is true while its disjuncts are not; and it says nothing that would make case 7's chance claim come out true.

What I want:

- The principle or principles stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case from the list that motivates each clause.
- An account of what resources the principle needs: branching histories, truth-value gaps with a non-classical treatment of negation and disjunction, a designated actual history among the branches, a notion of settledness indexed to a moment, or something else. Say which of my verdicts each resource is bought in to save.
- Whether the principle makes the claim that it is not settled whether there will be a sea battle itself settled, and whether that answer is one I can live with.
- The principle's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a claim whose causal history is partly quantum-random and partly a matter of undecided human choice; the same admiral's sentence asserted by someone who has privately decided but has issued no order and taken no step; and an announcement that will bring about, through the responses it provokes, the very event it announces.
- An honest statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which single verdict I should sacrifice if I want the simplest adequate principle rather than the most conservative one.
- Where you think my verdicts are tracking epistemic rather than metaphysical indeterminacy, and whether that confusion is responsible for the difficulty in cases 4 to 6 and in case 8.
