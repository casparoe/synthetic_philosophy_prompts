I am in the second half of an interdisciplinary seminar this term — evolutionary genetics in the mornings, philosophy of biology in the afternoons — and our current unit is the units of selection. We have worked through the familiar territory: the mid-century case against group selection, the argument that the gene is the only genuine replicator because it is the only thing copied with high enough fidelity to persist through deep time, the rejoinder that organisms and even colonies can be real interactors when their parts share a common fate, and the literature on transitions in individuality, germ–soma separation, and selfish genetic elements. For our department's undergraduate science-writing journal I have undertaken to contribute a piece of speculative fiction that stages the crux instead of reciting it. I would like you to write that story.

Please treat the following biology as stipulated fact. Do not research it, do not correct it against real taxa, and do not import the life history of any actual organism into it.

- The creature is a modular colonial animal that the inhabitants of a fictional cold-water archipelago call a quilt. A quilt is a sheet of many thousands of zooids; each zooid feeds, and any zooid may bud off a propagule that settles and founds a new quilt. There is no sequestered germ line and no determinate size. Quilts grow at the margin and are torn back by storms.
- When two quilts meet, they either fuse and share a single circulatory fluid, or reject one another and leave a bare, visible seam. Which happens is governed by a compatibility locus with many alleles, so full siblings fuse and strangers usually do not — usually.
- The fluid carries, besides nutrients and propagules, a class of heritable cytoplasmic particles the locals call motes. Motes are transmitted vertically through propagules and also horizontally, at a low but measurable rate, through the shared fluid of fused quilts and through the water column.
- One mote type, A, confers resistance to a warming-associated pathogen and slows the budding rate of the zooids that carry it. Within a fused conglomerate, A-bearing propagules are overproduced relative to non-A ones from the same shared fluid.
- The protagonist is a field biologist with thirty-one seasons of mark–recapture and transplant data on a single bay. Her archive records fusions, seam formations, and splits: the bay's population count has moved between nine and roughly forty-one thousand during her career, depending on the season and on how one chooses to count.

The plot must turn on a decision she has to make and cannot defer. A conservation statute of the archipelago protects individuals, requires them to be counted, and sets a minimum viable-population threshold below which no protection attaches. If the fused conglomerate is the individual, the bay holds three organisms and falls under the threshold. If each quilt is an individual, the bay holds tens of thousands and is protected — but then the entity protected is not the entity that possesses the pathogen resistance, and the resistance is precisely what the statute was written to preserve. Her own transplant experiment, which she designed to settle the matter, yields a result that can be read either way, and the story should make clear why: the horizontal transmission route is what determines whether the mote's fortunes are bound to its hosts' or free to diverge from them, and her experiment did not vary it.

Near the end, introduce a variant mote that suppresses compatibility at the fusion locus, so that its host quilts refuse every fusion and regain a hard boundary. I want the biologist to realize that her thirty-one seasons are a record of an individuality boundary being drawn, blurred, and redrawn from below, by things she could never see.

Constraints on the execution:

1. Between 1,800 and 2,500 words, third person limited on the biologist, past tense.
2. No character may explain the theory aloud. No dialogue may function as a lecture, and no scene may be staged for the purpose of a character asking a clarifying question.
3. Name no real biologist, book, or paper anywhere, and include no epigraph.
4. Describe at least two measurements twice — once in the vocabulary of copying, fidelity, and parts that persist, and once in the vocabulary of whole organisms, their survival, and their reproduction — and let the two descriptions fail to line up without any narrator remarking on the mismatch.
5. The seam is your recurring image. Use it concretely and at least three times, in different lights.
6. The biologist must be wrong about something that matters, and the story must not rehabilitate her at the end. No twist in which she was right all along, and no deathbed clarity.
7. Leave the legal question unresolved. Do not end on an aphorism, a sunrise, or a lesson.
8. Keep the furniture of working science: boats, cold hands, tags that corrode, grant reports due, a technician who has stopped believing the hypothesis. No artificial intelligence, no first contact, no catastrophe fiction.
9. After the story, add an afterword of no more than 150 words, out of character, stating which of the three available descriptions — an adaptation of quilts, a group-level adaptation of fused conglomerates, or the phenotype of a self-propagating element — the evidence in the story actually licenses, and saying why the transplant result does not settle it. Our readers will know what replicator and interactor mean, so you may use those terms here and nowhere else.
