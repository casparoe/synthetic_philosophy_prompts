I have sat for six years on my Trust's Adult Capacity and Best Interests Panel, and I came to that work out of a rationalist online milieu in which pre-commitment is treated as an unqualified good: Ulysses contracts, values drift, the folly of letting a degraded future self overrule a lucid present one. I now find that the folklore I absorbed prepares me badly for the case in front of us, and I need a written opinion to circulate to the eleven members before we reconvene. Please write it in British English.

The facts, as the panel has them. The patient — I will call her Ms R — is 61 and has end-stage kidney disease, unrelated to the genetic dementia she was tested positive for at 37 and which she watched her father go through. She has been on thrice-weekly haemodialysis for fourteen months. At 39 she drafted a lengthy advance decision, witnessed by a solicitor and her GP and formally renewed at 45 and at 50. It contains, among much else, two clauses that matter here.

The first is a cessation clause: that if she reaches the point at which she can no longer reliably name her grandchildren, dialysis is to be stopped and not restarted, and she is to be managed for comfort only.

The second is what I can only describe as an epistemic clause, and it is the reason I am writing. In substance she instructs whoever decides on her behalf that any expression of contentment, or any wish to carry on, that she voices after that point is not to be treated as evidence that her life is going well for her. Her stated reasoning is that such expressions will be the preferences of someone whose standards have collapsed to meet her circumstances, and that she declines to let that person cast a vote in her place. She anticipates, in other words, not merely the substance of her future self's objections but their evidential weight, and attempts to fix that weight in advance.

At 45 she recorded a video message, addressed jointly to the panel and to her future self, which is held by the chair. In it she sets a test: she asks that her future self be put one private question, the answer to which is in a sealed envelope, and says that a correct answer establishes that the woman in the room is still her, and so still bound. Last week the assessment was carried out. Ms R answered the question correctly and unprompted. She then watched the video twice, wept through the second viewing, and afterwards said, in a form the assessing psychiatrist recorded as verbatim, that the woman on the recording had been terrified, that terrified people ought not to be allowed to vote on anything, and that she was not her and did not want to die.

The capacity report is not favourable to her. The psychiatrist's finding is that she lacks capacity for this decision: she cannot retain the information about stopping treatment, she cannot weigh it, and her account of what would follow is confabulated. The same report, however, records three things that the panel keeps coming back to: that her wish to continue has been stable and consistent over eleven separate weeks; that she can give a reason for it, in the ordinary way, rather than merely expressing a preference; and that, immediately after passing the video test, she offered a two-sentence account of why her current view differs from the recorded one, which the psychiatrist describes as coherent though not, in his judgement, capacity-conferring.

There is also a dispute about applicability. Two members say the cessation clause was never triggered: she names her grandchildren on good days, and the consultant's note describes recognition as fluctuating rather than absent, whereas the clause says reliably. The remaining nine have been arguing about the second clause instead. In the family, the elder daughter — who was twelve when the first directive was drafted and is named in it — supports cessation; the husband opposes it; the younger son declines to take a position. The next dialysis session is Thursday morning and the panel meets on Wednesday.

What I would like the memo to do:

1. Reach a bottom line: stop, continue, or continue under specified conditions with a defined review point. Say what is to happen, who is to do it, and by when, in terms a lay member and the chaplain can act on without further discussion.

2. Take the epistemic clause seriously as a distinct problem. Our policy framework speaks only of whether an advance decision is valid and applicable, and has no vocabulary at all for a directive that binds a decision-maker as to how the present patient's utterances are to be weighed. Can such a clause be given effect? Ought it to be? What should our policy say about such clauses in future, in criteria a panel can actually apply at a Wednesday meeting rather than in principles.

3. Address the circularity head-on. The clause treats a reflective rejection of the directive as itself evidence of the collapse it predicts, so that any response the patient makes confirms it. State plainly whether this renders the clause self-sealing and therefore evidentially inert, or whether there is a non-question-begging test of it — and if there is, describe how the panel would run that test on Ms R in the time available.

4. Say what to make of the video. Her own authentication device was passed, and the response it produced was not confusion but a considered reply to the woman on the recording. Does passing the test license the panel to discount her voice, or does it cut the other way?

5. Dispose of the applicability question about the word reliably, and say whether resolving it would change your recommendation or only its footing. Trust legal has already advised on the statutory position, so I do not want statutory analysis.

6. State the strongest case for the outcome you do not recommend, in the terms its proponents on this panel would use, and then answer it rather than displaying it. I should tell you that my own lean is towards cessation, largely because I find the epistemic clause persuasive and I distrust my ability to discount it; if you think I am wrong, say so, and identify the one or two facts which, had they come out differently, would flip your recommendation.

Do not give me citations, case law, or a reading list, and do not speculate about what any authority would say: we have the legal advice and I want reasoning from the facts as set out above. Aim for 900 to 1,200 words, with the recommendation at the top under its own heading.
