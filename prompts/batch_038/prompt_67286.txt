I am a postdoc in an evolutionary-biology group, and I am drafting the section of a joint review that assigns biological systems to levels of selection. My supervisor, whom I will call Professor N., has been remarkably consistent over a decade of lab meetings and graduate teaching about which collectives she is willing to call units of selection, and I have to write up a new case before she sees the draft. I want a reconstruction and a prediction from you, not a survey of the debate; I don't need references or a reading list.

Her verdicts so far, with the remark she made on each when she made one:

1. A monogynous ant species with a permanently sterile worker caste, one singly-mated queen per nest, workers that destroy any eggs laid by other workers, and daughter colonies produced by fission in which a single queen is led away by her own daughters. **Yes** — the colony is a unit of selection and an individual in the literal sense. Her remark: "the workers no longer have a fitness of their own."

2. A polygynous ant species whose nests are started each season by several unrelated queens who cooperate, end up with dozens of queens, and whose workers police one another's eggs just as efficiently as in species 1. **No.** Her remark: "a shared nest, not a body."

3. *Dictyostelium* fruiting bodies, assembled each time from tens of thousands of amoebae drawn from many clones, roughly a fifth of which become the sterile stalk, and in which cheater strains that bias their own allocation to the spore mass are well documented. **No.** Her only addition was that the composition of a fruiting body is a matter of luck.

4. A flow-cell biofilm grown up from one cell, hence perfectly clonal, in which matrix production is a costly public good, non-producing mutants arise and rise in frequency inside the biofilm, and cells slough continuously into an effluent that is discarded, so nothing downstream ever founds anything. **No.** Her remark: "clonality is not individuality."

5. A metapopulation of pond snails: patches colonized by one or two founders, frequent local extinction, and residents free to emigrate to other patches whenever they like. **No.** Her remark: "a place, not a thing."

6. A shoal of reef fish that assembles each morning from the same local population and dissolves at dusk; shoal size has genuinely non-additive effects on predator detection and confusion, and shoals differ markedly in survival. **No**, with no remark.

7. A lichen propagated by soredia, each of which carries both partners, whose photobiont is not known to exist free-living, and neither of whose partners can establish the thallus alone. **Yes** — one individual, two genomes.

The case I have to rule on is devil facial tumour disease in Tasmanian devils. So that we are arguing about the same thing, I stipulate its features: the cancer is transmitted not by a virus but by the living tumour cells themselves, passed mainly in bites; a tumour in a new host is founded by a small inoculum of cells from an existing tumour; those cells are allogeneic to their host and are nonetheless not rejected; the disease is a single clonal lineage descended from one devil's Schwann cell a few decades ago, and a second, genetically distinct clonal lineage now circulates in another part of the island; assume our collaborators' deep sequencing recovers several subclones competing within a single host's tumour, and that the subclone which dominates inside a host is not the subclone best at reaching the next host, because fast-growing subclones tend to kill the host before it has bitten enough neighbours to transmit; and nothing in the tumour polices its own cells — any cell is in principle able to found a new infection.

What I need:

(a) Name the candidate rules — using the standard vocabulary of the levels-of-selection debate where it has one — that are consistent with all seven verdicts *and* with the pattern of her remarks. Give me at least three, say which of her remarks each is meant to capture, and say which superficially attractive rules her track record has already excluded and by which verdict.

(b) For each rule that survives, state its verdict on the devil tumour, and set out explicitly where the rules *diverge* rather than where they agree.

(c) Give your best single guess at what she will say, with reasons drawn from the pattern of her seven verdicts and glosses rather than from which rule you find most plausible in the abstract. If you think those seven genuinely underdetermine this case, say so, and say what the residual uncertainty consists in. If your conclusion is that she is equivocating between two senses of "unit of selection," that is a substantive answer and I want it argued rather than announced.

(d) Name one further case — a real system, or a stipulated one if you need to hold a variable fixed — that would discriminate between your two leading rules, and state what she would be committed to saying about it under each.

(e) Tell me what would change your mind: which single consideration, or which detail about the devil tumour that I may have described wrongly or left out, would flip your prediction — and where your reconstruction of her criteria is most likely to be wrong, for instance if her remarks express a temperament rather than a criterion, or if she is in fact running two rules and switching between them without noticing.
