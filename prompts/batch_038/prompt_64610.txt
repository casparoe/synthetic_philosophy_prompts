I cover bioethics for a general-interest magazine, and a puzzle in informed-consent law has me stuck. When clinicians test whether a patient has capacity to refuse treatment, they look for four abilities: understanding the information, appreciating that it applies to her own situation, reasoning with it, and communicating a choice. Appreciation is where the work gets done — it is what lets a team conclude that a severely anorexic patient who recites the medical facts accurately but insists they do not describe her is not really deciding. Yet the same doctrine holds that a competent patient may refuse for reasons her doctors think foolish, because the test is procedural and outcome-blind.

Now set that standard against decisions that would change who the decider is: a Deaf adult weighing a cochlear implant, a first pregnancy, a psychiatric treatment expected to alter temperament. For those, arguably no one can appreciate in advance what the outcome will be like, and the person who emerges may not share the values of the person choosing. What does the appreciation requirement presuppose about the relation between the deciding self and the self that lives with the decision? Does it assume that evaluative character persists through such a change, so that appreciating the outcome is possible in principle, or could the standard rest on something weaker? Which rival accounts of transformative choice does that exclude — including the view that such a decision is about which person to become rather than about which outcome to get? And why doesn't the requirement, applied honestly, render everyone incapacitated for these choices while still doing decisive work elsewhere? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and tell me what capacity assessment would look like if the presupposition were false.
