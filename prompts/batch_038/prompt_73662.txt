I teach an upper-level political philosophy seminar — 16 students, a single 90-minute session — and all of them will already have read the standard texts on equality. I want to run an activity that makes them feel a problem in luck egalitarianism that I think is almost always lost in the reading: the identification problem. The distinction between brute luck and option luck is clean on the page, because the page already knows which choices were made and which odds were faced. An administrator charged with redistributing according to responsibility sees only outcomes. To separate the negligent from the unlucky, they have to reconstruct a counterfactual — what this person could have chosen, at what cost, on what information — out of testimony, and the testimony comes from the very person whose compensation depends on how it is received.

I want that epistemic predicament, along with the indignity the democratic-egalitarian critics allege comes bundled with it, to live in the mechanics rather than in my closing remarks.

Here is the skeleton I have in mind. Please build it out completely, and tell me where it needs to change rather than simply fleshing out what I have written:

- Everyone begins with an identical endowment of tokens. No real money changes hands, but final scores must be comparable and must matter to the players.
- Play runs over several rounds. In each round a player operates under one of two regimes that produce outcomes which cannot be told apart from the outside: a public lottery whose posted odds are visible to the whole room, and a private menu from which the player silently selects their own odds, paying tokens for the safer ones. After the fact, no observer and no administrator can determine which regime a given player was under, or which option they took. A large enough sample would diverge statistically, which is worth flagging in the debrief, since the adjudicating body only ever sees one case at a time.
- In at least one round, assignment to a regime is itself drawn by lot rather than chosen, so that players run into the regress directly: whether you were in a position to choose at all turns out to be another piece of luck.
- Losses entitle a player to file a claim against a common compensation fund. Payment requires submitting to questioning by a student panel acting as the Board, which can see nothing but the outcome and the claim form.
- Every question the Board asks and every answer it receives is written on a public log. At the end of the session the whole class reads that log aloud.

What I need from you:

1. A complete rulebook: number of rounds, the token economy, exact odds and prices, how the fund is financed and what happens when it runs dry, the Board's decision procedure and the scope of its discretion, and how final scores are computed. It must be runnable with paper, pens, and a whiteboard.
2. The claim form, word for word — the actual text players fill in. This matters more than anything else in the design. It should be constructed so that perfectly truthful answers admit of both a responsible and an irresponsible reading, and so that the act of completing it already begins to feel like something.
3. A round-by-round timeline with minutes, including when the Board is seated and how often it rotates.
4. The debrief: which concrete moments in the play correspond to which parts of the distinction and the critique, in the order they occurred, and what to say at each point. Do not let the technical vocabulary appear on any handout or in any framing speech; the names should arrive only afterward.
5. One design decision I cannot settle, where I want your judgment rather than a list of options: should the Board's audit sometimes be able to catch a lie — meaning the private choice is in principle discoverable, but only at a high price in time and dignity — or should it be strictly undiscoverable, so that the Board's verdicts are guesswork dressed up as findings of fact? Tell me which choice teaches which lesson better, what each costs, and whether splitting the class into two rooms under different regimes would let them compare notes productively or would merely halve the group size for nothing.
6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: the exploits players will find, where the token economy will break, the student who refuses to answer the Board, the panel that turns into either a soft touch or a tribunal, and how to handle each without halting play.
7. A frank account of what the activity cannot reproduce — in particular, whether a game in which students are only pretending to be badly off, for ninety minutes, among peers who know the whole thing is a game, can actually generate the humiliation the objection depends on, or whether it merely diagrammatizes it. Tell me what I would have to supply myself.
8. A compressed 50-minute version, and exactly what has to be cut to get there.

Please write in American English.
