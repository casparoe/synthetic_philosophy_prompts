I'm in a decision-theory reading group and a couple weeks ago I made a claim I can't quite back up, so I want to pressure-test it. The claim: basically every famous puzzle in infinite ethics — the ones where adding a finite amount of value changes nothing, where two outcomes with equally infinite totals can't be ranked, where you're apparently obliged to accept endless small harms for a payoff that never lands — only bites because the utilities are unbounded or the sums diverge. Bound everything, make the series absolutely convergent so every option has a real-number total, and the weirdness evaporates and you're back to ordinary finite ethics. Two people pushed back but nobody could actually produce a counterexample on the spot, which felt unsatisfying, and I've been smug about it ever since. I'm giving a short talk at the next session and I'd rather bring a case than a slogan.

So: build me the counterexample, if there is one. I want a concrete scenario with two courses of action, bounded per-period effects, absolutely convergent totals, and at least three of the standard aggregation rules genuinely disagreeing about which is better — the undiscounted total, some positive discount rate, and the catch-up/overtaking style rule that compares running partial sums and only declares a winner once one option pulls ahead and never hands the lead back. Ideally the undiscounted total prefers the option that discounting likes least, and the catch-up rule refuses to rank them at all. Constraints: (a) the infinite horizon has to be justified by something mundane — a foundation with a perpetual charter, a river authority, a civilisation that just keeps going — not by an immortal person, a supertask, or a god; (b) the agent actually making the choices is finite-lived and never sees the whole sequence, and I want you to say whether that epistemic limitation changes the verdict or is just set dressing; (c) the infinity comes from time, not from an infinite population of separate people being added; (d) three paragraphs of scenario, max, and it should read like a real institutional dilemma rather than a table of numbers with a story stapled on.

Then do the check and show your work. Write down the numbers (a formula or a repeating pattern is fine, I don't need five hundred decimals), state what each of the three rules says and why, and point at exactly which structural feature is doing the work — I'm guessing it's something about how the per-period differences are signed and how fast they decay, but tell me if I'm wrong. Say what happens if I remove that feature: does everything snap back into agreement, and how fast? Then give me the best objection from someone who thinks my smug original claim was right after all, i.e. that the disagreement is an artifact of how the case is set up rather than a real ethical difference between the options. And if you think it genuinely can't be done under these constraints, don't fudge it — give me the argument for why the constraints rule it out, because honestly that would be the more interesting thing to walk in with.
