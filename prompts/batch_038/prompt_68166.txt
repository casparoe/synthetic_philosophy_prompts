I was at a potluck last weekend and two people I barely know got into an argument that ran for like an hour, and I've thought about it alot since. Neither is a philosopher — one's a vet tech, the other does data work for a nonprofit — which maybe matters. Names changed. Roughly how it went:

(1) Ruth said that even if you think there's only a one-in-a-thousand chance any given insect is sentient, the sheer number of them — something like ten to the eighteenth alive at any one moment, she said — means the amount of insect suffering you could plausibly affect is bigger than everything else you could do with your money and time put together, so it should dominate your giving.

(2) Marcus said that's Pascal's mugging in a lab coat: take any tiny probability, multiply by any gigantic number of beings, and you can 'prove' some exotic cause matters more than everything ordinary. If the argument form is that permissive, it can't count as evidence for the conclusion.

(3) Ruth said the mugging case is different because there the number comes from whoever is trying to exploit your decision rule, whereas here the count is an independent estimate from people who study arthropod abundance and have no stake in her giving, and the one-in-a-thousand is her honest credence after looking at the neurobiology, not a placeholder she picked to make the arithmetic land.

(4) Marcus said he doesn't believe anyone actually has an honest credence of one in a thousand about whether insects are conscious — that question doesn't come with three significant figures. You can have a range, or a shrug, but the moment you write down 0.001 and multiply by 10^18 you're laundering vagueness into a precise verdict.

(5) Ruth said she doesn't need precision, because the conclusion is robust: to flip it you'd have to either push the probability below something like one in ten to the fifteenth, or hold that an insect's welfare counts for essentially nothing next to a human's — and neither of those is a neutral default, they're both strong positive claims.

(6) Marcus said the ten-to-the-eighteenth is a snapshot, a headcount at one instant, and adult insect lives run to days, so the quantity of experience actually at stake isn't anything like 10^18 lifetimes' worth. He said she's multiplying a stock by something that behaves like a flow, and the number is inflated by exactly the orders of magnitude she just claimed to be robust to.

(7) Ruth said turnover makes it worse for him, not better: if that many adults are alive on a given day and they live for days, the number born and dying over a year dwarfs the standing population, and anyway most of an insect's life is spent as a larva, which is the long part.

(8) Marcus said she'd just abandoned the entomologists' estimate and was now generating numbers on demand, which is his whole point — the conclusion gets defended by whichever quantity happens to be lying around.
