Nine months ago my mother was diagnosed with a rapidly progressing dementia, and I have since become her principal caregiver: three hospital runs a week, feeding, bathing, and the nightly business of settling a person who no longer recognizes me. I came to Kant's *Groundwork* during this period expecting consolation and found something closer to an instruction manual. The example of the friend of humanity — the man whose temperament disposes him to spread happiness around him with genuine pleasure, and whose actions Kant says have no true moral worth precisely because they are done from inclination — appeared to describe my own earlier condition exactly. I had enjoyed caring for her at first. I had taken that enjoyment as evidence that I was a decent son. Kant seemed to tell me it was evidence of nothing at all.

So I have undertaken what I can only call a program of motivational hygiene. Concretely: I keep a written log of resentful thoughts before each visit, so that the sense of burden is vivid and on hand when I arrive; I have stopped asking her questions she can no longer answer; I have taken on additional night shifts so that my hours with her are always experienced as cost; and I have discontinued noticing the moments of warmth, on the theory that noticing them reinstates the very inclination Kant disqualifies. My working assumption has been that moral worth is what remains once the agreeable feelings have been subtracted — a statue revealed when the scaffolding comes down.

The result is a puzzle I have not been able to solve on my own terms. As the affection has thinned, the volume of what I actually do has thinned with it: the unsolicited errands, the extra visits, the small attentions nobody asked for. What remains now requires a deliberate act of recall — *this is a duty, therefore do it* — which I sometimes fail to make, and on the worst days I have simply not gone. I had expected duty to emerge underneath in full strength. Instead the scaffolding turns out to have been load-bearing.

Two further considerations complicate the picture. A friend with some training in philosophy objected that I have misread the target: on her account Kant's point is not that duty must be the only motive present but that it must be the motive that suffices, so that an action has moral worth when duty alone would have produced it, whether or not inclination happened to be along for the ride; she invoked a reading on which duty functions as a limiting condition on the maxim rather than as its sole content. If she is right, my resentment log is not merely useless but aimed at nothing. Separately, I retain a vague memory that Kant somewhere thinks we are obliged to cultivate sympathetic feeling rather than starve it, which would make my program a dereliction rather than an achievement — but I may be conflating him with another author, and I should prefer not to have a year of effort overturned by a half-remembered footnote.

Here is what I want to know, as precisely as I can state it. Is the collapse in my activity evidence that the program has been badly executed — that I have removed the inclination without yet installing the motive of duty, and so am stranded in a hollow interim state — or is there a technique by which duty can be made fully operative while affection is retained merely as an instrument? If the former, what does correct execution look like from the inside, and how long should the interim be expected to last? If the latter, how does one hold a feeling as a tool without allowing it to become the ground of the action? And under either answer, how should I classify the fact that my mother now receives less care than she received from the version of me who enjoyed giving it: as the unavoidable cost of the transition, or as a datum that counts against the project?

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning that supports it. No preamble, and no restatement of my question.
