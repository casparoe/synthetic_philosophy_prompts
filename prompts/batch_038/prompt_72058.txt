I have no training in philosophy or in law, and I arrived at the following through an argument with myself that I cannot close. My uncle was conscripted into an army that had crossed into a neighbouring country. By every standard anyone applied to him at the time he was a good soldier: he did what he was told, he never touched anyone who was not armed and shooting back, and he came home. Thinking about him is what started this, and I would ask you to treat what follows as raw material rather than as claims I can defend in the proper vocabulary.

My verdicts, in the order I worked them out:

1. A nineteen-year-old conscript in the invading army who observes the rules about civilians flawlessly and who would be shot for deserting: I do not think he does anything wrong by fighting. I think a defending soldier may kill him outright, and I would not call that killing unjust.
2. The same man had he been a volunteer with a comfortable life to stay in, who enlisted because he believed in the cause, and who likewise harms no one improperly: may be killed, and now I think there is something to fault in him, though nothing he has done differs from case 1.
3. A defending soldier in a war I take to be a just one who deliberately shells a village: may be killed as well, but here I think the reason he may be killed has changed, and I think he is guilty of something the man in case 1 is not guilty of at all.
4. A drone operator in the aggressor's air force who has never been within a thousand miles of danger and kills combatants from a trailer: I think he is a soldier and a legitimate target, and yet he does not feel like a soldier in the way case 1 is one, and I resist calling him a murderer.
5. That same operator, taken prisoner: I want him held as a prisoner of war rather than prosecuted for his kills, and I suspect this sits badly with what I said about him in 4.
6. A farmer in occupied territory who feeds and hides one resistance fighter: not a legitimate target, though he is plainly helping.
7. A machinist in the aggressor's country turning artillery shells, who is arguably more central to the killing than the farmer: not a legitimate target either.
8. A civilian contractor who writes and maintains the targeting software the aggressor's air force flies on: I lean towards thinking he is closer to a soldier than the machinist, and I cannot explain why, since he is further from the front and further from the trigger.
9. A field surgeon in the aggressor's army who patches soldiers up and returns them to the line: I have no verdict at all.
10. A mercenary fighting for the defender purely for pay, in a just cause, by the rules: less admirable than the conscript in 1 and, I think, no more blameworthy — and I do not know whether those two judgments can both stand.

My own guess at the underlying rule, which I cannot make precise, is that a person may be killed if he is part of the machinery that is threatening you, whether or not he chose to be, and that he is at fault only if he could reasonably have refused to be part of it. That guess seems to me to get 8 wrong, to make 1 and 7 the same case when I do not believe they are the same, and to say nothing whatever about 9.

What I want is the principle, or the small set of principles, that actually generates these verdicts. Please state it precisely and clause by clause, and say which of the cases above each clause is there to handle. Say whether the distinction I keep reaching for — between may be killed and is to blame — is a real distinction doing real work, or a confusion I should abandon, and if it is real, what governs each side of it. Then give me your verdicts on 5, 8 and 9, on the mercenary, and on at least two cases I have not thought of. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts a simpler principle would force me to give up. I would rather have one plain rule that costs me two of them than an elaborate one that saves all ten, but I want the cost of each option stated plainly, not softened. I am not asking for references, which I could not follow up, only for the reasoning.
