I'm a software engineer on a clinical informatics team. I have no formal training in philosophy, so please treat me as an intelligent outsider, and correct my own framing below wherever it turns out to be mistaken.

Our team builds a model that predicts which diagnostic code a patient will receive at discharge, using the notes written during the admission. After I described our validation approach in an internal talk, a colleague sent me a paragraph from the closing chapter of a popular book about prediction in medicine, and used it to argue that the approach is circular. The paragraph concerns the philosopher Ian Hacking's idea of the looping effect, and it reads as follows:

"Hacking's looping effect shows that the sciences of human beings can never achieve what physics achieves. When psychiatry introduces a diagnostic category, the category does not merely describe people who were already in that condition; it produces them. Patients learn the label, come to understand themselves in its terms, and begin to present in ways that fit it, so the diagnosis is self-fulfilling. Hacking insists the traffic runs in both directions: as patients change, the experts are obliged to revise the category, and the revised category changes the patients again, so there is no stable human nature underneath for psychiatry to discover. That is why a condition such as multiple personality disorder could appear, spread, and disappear within a few decades — these are transient illnesses, brought into being by the culture that named them and dissolved once that culture moves on. The upshot is that psychiatric classification is a matter of convention and power rather than of natural kinds, and that any attempt to validate a diagnosis by showing that it predicts later outcomes is circular, because the outcomes were themselves shaped by the diagnosis."

I want an accuracy audit of this paragraph, not a defense of the book and not a defense of Hacking. Concretely:

1. Work through the paragraph claim by claim. For each one, say whether a specialist working on Hacking or on the philosophy of psychiatry would accept it, would accept it only as a rough simplification, or would reject it — and in the latter two cases, what the accurate statement would be.

2. I suspect two conflations, though I lack the vocabulary to state them cleanly. The first is between a classification bringing a new way of being a person into existence and a classification inventing an individual's suffering. The second is between the looping effect and an ordinary self-fulfilling prophecy. Are these distinctions Hacking himself keeps, or am I manufacturing a defense for a paragraph that is simply wrong?

3. Elsewhere I have seen Hacking contrast interactive kinds with indifferent kinds, where the contrast has something to do with whether the objects of study care what we say about them. Does the paragraph preserve that contrast, or does it quietly replace it with a claim about objectivity and truth?

4. How much of the paragraph is Hacking's, and how much is material imported from other critics of psychiatry — the anti-psychiatry tradition, say, or Foucault's account of knowledge and power? Where the summary blends sources, tell me whose view is doing the work at each point.

5. Does the final claim, that validation by predictive success is circular, follow from anything true in the paragraph? If it does not, please distinguish it from the weaker claim that a looping kind is a moving target, and say which of the two a specialist would endorse and why the difference matters.

6. Close with the strongest modest version of Hacking's thesis that survives these corrections, and with what that version would imply for someone in my position: is it a reason to distrust a model trained on diagnostic labels, a reason to expect those labels to drift over time, both, or neither?

One disclosure: it would be convenient for me if the paragraph turned out to be sloppy, so please do not soften whatever in it is correct. I am not asking for a bibliography or a reading list — I want your reasoning and the conceptual distinctions, not pointers to literature.
