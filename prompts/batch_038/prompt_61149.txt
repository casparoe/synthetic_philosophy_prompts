I teach an upper-division course on language and mind — twenty-two students, one eighty-minute session, a seminar room with movable tables, index cards, and a whiteboard, one teaching assistant, and no phones permitted. By this point in the term they have had the standard tour of the linguistic relativity debate and settled into a comfortable position: the strong version is obviously false, the weak version is obviously true, and there is nothing left to say. I want an in-class activity that unsettles the second half of that. Specifically, I want them to leave able to keep three claims apart — that a language's resources limit what its speakers can think; that they fix what speakers are obliged to say; and that being obliged to say something changes what they habitually attend to and retain — and to have first-person evidence that the third does not follow trivially from the second, and that the second has consequences even where nothing was in principle untranslatable.

I have drafted two candidate registers, and I would like you to tell me which to run, or how to combine them, given the room and the clock.

(a) A spatial pair. In one version, all placement must be described using bearings fixed to the room — the door is always south — and viewer-relative terms are banned outright; in the other, bearings are banned and only viewer-relative terms are allowed. A describer conveys an arrangement of five objects on a four-by-four grid; a partner reconstructs it out of sight. The problem I cannot solve is this: everyone in the room has the same first language, so the bearing group can silently translate from the relative description they would have given anyway, and any difference I measure may be pure translation cost rather than a difference in what was encoded. Fix that, or tell me plainly that the design cannot be fixed in this setting and why.

(b) An evidential register. Every assertion must carry a marker for how the speaker came to know it — saw it directly, inferred it, was told it, or is guessing — and unmarked assertions are penalized. Teams narrate a short sequence of events; an individual test at the end probes both content and source. My worry here is the reverse of (a): the marking degenerates into a rote suffix within two rounds and stops costing anything at all.

What I need is a complete runnable specification: roles, materials, the exact rule card handed to each group, round structure with minutes, and the closing test. Build in whatever makes the constraint actually bite under time pressure instead of dissolving into paraphrase. Four of my students are fluent bilinguals, which may be an asset or a confound — say which and what to do with them. Then give me a debrief keyed to moments rather than to a lecture — when this happens, say this — mapping what they just did onto the three claims, including how I handle it if the test shows no difference between groups, which with twenty-two people is a live possibility, and how I keep the session from becoming a demonstration of a hypothesis they were led to expect. Tell me whether withholding the test until the end is defensible or whether I should announce it up front and absorb the demand characteristics, and make the call for me. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and what to do at the moment it does. Finally, be frank about the feature of the real phenomenon this cannot reproduce: I suspect it has to do with the gap between a restriction imposed on adults for an hour and a system acquired over a lifetime, but I want that stated precisely, along with what I then have to supply by argument to close it. I am not after a summary of the literature or a reading list — I know it. I want the activity.
