For most of my working life I was employed in a national measurement laboratory, where the settled practice was to report an interval together with an explicit statement of the confidence attaching to it, and to regard a bare number as an incomplete report. Since retiring I have taken up the philosophy of probability in earnest, and I have found that the practice I assumed was merely good sense is precisely what one side of a standing philosophical dispute rejects. Two colleagues of mine — one a subjectivist who maintains that a rational agent's degree of belief is always a single real number, however thin the evidence, and one who maintains that a rational agent's belief state may be a whole set of probability functions, so that an interval is not a summary of anything but the belief state itself — have argued about this for over a year. They have now agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration and have asked me to produce the joint statement they would both sign. I should like you to draft it. I confess I lean toward the second camp, which is exactly why the drafting must be such that the first could sign it without feeling caricatured.

The document should contain, in this order:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties can sign. I expect this to be longer than either of them assumes. It should include whatever they agree on about the formal constraints — coherence, the axioms, what a Dutch-book style argument does and does not establish — and about the fact that a singleton is a limiting case of a set, so that one framework contains the other. It should also include the concessions each side is in fact already making: that sparse evidence does not single out one prior among many, on the one hand, and that a belief state which licenses no action is of limited use, on the other.

2. A disagreements table, with columns for: the crux; the disagreement stated in wording each side would accept as a fair rendering of its own position; the case or argument both accept as bearing on it; and each side's prediction about that case, recorded in advance. Each crux must be one whose settlement would actually move at least one of them — say which, and in which direction. Three candidate cruxes come to mind, and you may add or replace them: whether an imprecise agent can be rationally unmoved by evidence that a sharp agent with the same total evidence would treat as significant, and whether that inertia is a vice or a virtue; whether learning something can leave an agent less determinate than she was before, and what turns on that possibility; and whether any decision procedure open to the imprecise agent avoids covertly selecting a single member of her set — and if none does, whether that defeats her view or merely leaves it incomplete.

3. A worked example, which I want used consistently throughout rather than tucked into a footnote. It is of my own devising. A laboratory builds a single instrument of a type that has never existed before: no fleet of predecessors, no intercomparison data, no relevant reference class. Its designers can offer physical arguments bounding its systematic error, but the arguments are of a kind a careful referee would call suggestive rather than probative, and they yield two bounds without yielding any distribution over the region between them. The instrument is used once, on a target whose own properties are poorly characterized, and produces a single reading. What belief state should the laboratory hold about the true value of the measurand? Then the second half, which is the part that interests me: suppose a second, independent measurement is later obtained, and it agrees with the first to well within those bounds. How should each side represent the effect of that agreement, and does the agreement carry the evidential weight that each side's representation implies? The sharp agent's update depends on the shape she posited between the bounds; the imprecise agent's set may narrow far less than intuition suggests, or in some respects not at all. If your honest conclusion is that one side's treatment of this case is unstable — that it delivers wildly different answers depending on arbitrary choices the representation was meant to immunize us against — say so, and enter it as a concession in the statement rather than papering over it.

4. A short closing section stating what each party would accept as a genuine defeater of its own view, and whether there is anything at all that both agree would settle the matter.

Finally, if you judge that some portion of this dispute is verbal — that the two are using the same apparatus to express the same view about evidence under different bookkeeping — identify exactly which portion, say what survives the deflation, and keep the surviving disagreement in the table. I do not want a verdict on who is right, and I do not want references; I want the reasoning laid out in terms both parties could stand behind.
