I am a senior legal officer serving as rapporteur to a standing law reform commission. Our current review cycle covers legacy offences in the category of sexual morality and public decency: roughly fifteen provisions, most drafted in the nineteenth century, several dormant, all of which the legislature has asked us to justify or recommend for repeal. The commission has adopted an explicitly Benthamite frame of reference for this cycle — not because its members are utilitarians in any doctrinal sense, but because Bentham's penal theory supplies a vocabulary for sorting justifications that we currently lack. Two of the three commissioners are lawyers with no philosophical training; one is a former magistrate who regards the whole exercise as an invitation to repeal things the public would not consent to repeal. Every recommendation we publish must be defensible to the legislature, and my analyst team has to be able to apply the framework uniformly across all fifteen provisions in about three working days per provision, with a lawyer signing off before it goes to the commissioners.

What I need from you is a screening procedure my analysts can actually run, not an exposition of Bentham. Please build it around the following, and be explicit wherever you depart from or simplify his apparatus:

1. **The gate structure.** Give me the questions in the order they should be asked, and for each: what evidence or finding counts as a sufficient answer, which disposition follows from a failure (retain as drafted; retain as amended; repeal; or refer to the commissioners), and whether that gate is dispositive on its own or merely cumulative with the others. The gates should track Bentham's own grounds for declining to punish — the cases in which punishment is groundless because no mischief is produced, inefficacious because it cannot operate on the conduct, unprofitable because the mischief it produces exceeds the mischief it prevents, or needless because the mischief could be prevented at a cheaper rate.

2. **The consent gate.** Bentham treats consent that is free and fairly obtained as strong evidence that the consenting party has suffered no mischief on the whole. Tell me how an analyst should operationalise that in a screening, and — critically — what the procedure must import from outside Bentham's theory to assess whether consent was free and fairly obtained, since his own framework does not supply an independent account of that.

3. **The classification of third-party distress.** This is where I expect most of the disagreement in our cycle. Require the analyst to classify every item of reported public distress into one of three boxes: alarm in Bentham's technical sense, meaning apprehension of suffering the mischief that the act itself produces to assignable victims, and therefore presupposing both an identifiable primary mischief and a real chance of the complainant suffering it; derivative mischief falling on persons connected to an identifiable victim; and bare revulsion or antipathy — distress at the thought of conduct in which the complainant is neither victim nor at risk. Then say what weight each box carries, and flag the point at which the procedure runs out of textual guidance and the commissioners must choose an interpretation.

4. **The disqualifying-justification gate.** Include a step that asks whether the sole remaining support for a provision is that the conduct is wrong in itself, or that people of good character detest it — the two principles Bentham regards as rival to utility rather than as evidence within it — and specify what happens to a provision that survives every other gate but fails this one.

5. **The law's own secondary effects.** Require the analyst to enter, on the mischief side of the ledger, the harms the enforcement of the provision itself produces: selective enforcement against unpopular or unrepresented groups, the exposure of complainants and defendants to publicity, the incentive the provision creates for blackmail and extortion around conduct that must be concealed, and the cost of prosecution relative to the mischief prevented.

Then run the finished procedure on three cases, showing the analyst's worksheet at each gate: (a) a dormant provision criminalising consensual sexual acts between adults in private, with no evidence of complaint by any participant in forty years; (b) a modernised provision criminalising the covert recording or photographing of a person's private parts or sexual activity without their consent, where victims describe lasting apprehension about being recorded in changing rooms and clinics; (c) a general provision against sexual conduct in places where members of the public are likely to be unwilling witnesses, supported by a large volume of sincere, stable, and demographically broad complaints. At least one of the three must produce a recommendation I expect to be politically unwelcome, and you should say which one and why the procedure cannot be adjusted to avoid it without giving up something else.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do. I want an honest account of the steps that are judgment calls dressed as arithmetic, the classes of provision it will systematically get wrong, and the specific way in which Bentham's insistence on comparing quantities of pain and pleasure leaves my analysts without a number precisely when the case is most contested. Please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; the procedure must stand on its own terms for readers who will never check it against a text.
