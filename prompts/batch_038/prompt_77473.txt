> State Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as precisely as you can, making explicit what work each hypothesis is doing. Then explain what the theorem does and does not establish about utilitarianism, and how it relates to Harsanyi's separate veil-of-ignorance / impartial-observer argument.

Rough mark scheme: 5 for a correct statement of the theorem, 4 for the role of the hypotheses, 3 for the utilitarianism discussion. Here's the answer, transcribed (about 450 words):

> Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is a representation result about social welfare. Suppose n individuals each have a preference ordering over lotteries on a common set of outcomes, and each ordering satisfies the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms: completeness, transitivity, continuity and independence. Then each individual is represented by a utility function u_i, unique up to positive affine transformation and linear in the probabilities. Now suppose there's also a social preference ordering over the same lotteries which likewise satisfies the vNM axioms, so it's represented by some social utility function U, also linear in probabilities. Finally suppose the social ordering satisfies the Pareto condition: whenever every individual strictly prefers lottery x to lottery y, society does too.
>
> The theorem then says that U is a weighted sum of the individual utilities, U = a_1u_1 + ... + a_n u_n, with every a_i > 0, and that U is itself unique up to positive affine transformation. So Pareto does the substantive work: it's what forces the weights to be strictly positive rather than merely non-negative, and hence what rules out society ignoring somebody entirely. The vNM assumptions on both sides are what buy linearity in the probabilities, which is what makes the adding-up possible; without them you're back in Arrow's world, where the impossibility theorem shows no rule can satisfy Pareto along with independence of irrelevant alternatives and non-dictatorship. Harsanyi escapes Arrow because he works with cardinal utilities over a richer domain of lotteries, so the social ranking of outcomes still depends only on the individuals' ordinal rankings but has more structure to work with.
>
> The theorem is often said to establish utilitarianism, but strictly it doesn't, since it says nothing about the values of the a_i. It leaves open a weighted utilitarianism where some people count more, and rescaling the weights gives an equally valid representation. To reach classical utilitarianism you need the weights equal, which is where Harsanyi's other argument comes in. If a sympathetic observer is equally likely to end up as any one of the n individuals and evaluates each lottery by the expected utility of whoever she turns out to be, her preferences satisfy the vNM axioms, so the theorem applies to her, and the equiprobability assumption makes the weights equal: (1/n) times the sum of the u_i. With n fixed that's equivalent to the sum, so we get classical utilitarianism.
>
> Note that this route never requires interpersonal comparisons of utility. The theorem only ever adds utilities, and adding two people's numbers isn't comparing them; the observer step likewise just uses each person's own utility function. That's supposed to be what makes Harsanyi's defence of utilitarianism better than an intuition-driven one: it needs only vNM rationality for individuals and the observer, plus Pareto and equal chances, and nothing as contentious as the claim that my happiness and yours can be measured on the same scale.
