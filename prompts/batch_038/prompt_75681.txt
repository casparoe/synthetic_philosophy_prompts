I'm auditing a philosophy-of-language seminar this semester purely for fun and my roommate's a poli-sci major, and the two of us got into a genuinely exhausting argument last night about Brexit that neither of us won. What I want is a careful speech-act analysis of the whole 2016–2020 sequence, because I think we were talking past each other about what kind of thing a referendum result even is. The facts as I have them (correct me if I've got any of it wrong, but please don't let the analysis turn on a detail): Parliament passed the European Union Referendum Act in 2015 setting up the vote, and the act didn't say the outcome would be legally binding. Leave won 51.9 to 48.1 on 23 June 2016. For months afterward the government said it would notify under Article 50 — the treaty provision letting a member state withdraw in accordance with its own constitutional requirements — using the royal prerogetive, i.e. without going back to Parliament. Gina Miller and others sued, and in January 2017 the UK Supreme Court held 8–3 that an Act of Parliament was needed first, on the reasoning that leaving would strip rights Parliament had created by statute. Parliament then passed a short authorizing act, and Theresa May notified the European Council on 29 March 2017. Two extensions later, the UK left on 31 January 2020. Separately, in December 2018 the European Court of Justice held that a member state could revoke its Article 50 notification unilaterally, so long as the revocation followed its own democratic process, and that revoking would leave it an EU member on unchanged terms.

Now the analysis, and I want the mapping done before any verdicts. Who counts as the speaker at each step — the electorate, Parliament, the government, the Crown, the UK as treaty party? For each link in the chain give me Austin's three-way split: what was said, what was done in saying it, what was brought about by it being said, and be willing to conclude that at some link there was no illocutionary act at all, just a very expensive opinion poll with enormous perlocutionary consequences. Which of Searle's five illocutionary categories does the referendum result land in, and does that change depending on who you assign as speaker — an assertive reporting public preferences, a directive to the government, or a declaration that, unlike a wedding officiant's pronouncement, lacked the institutional backing to change anything by being uttered? Where does the advisory-versus-binding fight sit in Austin's taxonomy of infelicities: a misfire, because no accepted conventional procedure with settled uptake rules existed, or a perfectly felicitous act of a type that simply isn't force-bearing in the UK's rulebook? And what is the Supreme Court itself doing in Miller — is a court holding that an utterance had no legal effect the same kind of thing as a bishop declining to recognize a marriage, or is the court doing something else entirely, like settling which constitutive rules govern?

Two more things. First, a coinage I've been kicking around and want you to stress-test: an illocutionary relay, where the force of an act is deliberately split across two speakers and two times — a first act that is politically authoritative but legally inert, and a second that carries all the legal force while borrowing its entire legitimacy from the first, with the litigation living in the gap between them. Does 2016–2019 fit that, and if so is it a real problem for speech act theory or just Searle's constitutive-rules point wearing a costume? Second, run the thing backwards: the revocation ruling implies a declaration can be taken back. What does that do to the idea that declarations are irreversible changes to institutional reality, and is a revocable declaration still a declaration? Then be honest about where the theory goes silent. Does the Austin/Searle toolkit have anything to say about a seventeen-million-person speaker, about force that is contested rather than absent, or about the fact that nearly all the public disagreement was about perlocution — what the vote meant and what it obligated anyone to do — and almost none of it about illocution? Tell me which of those the framework genuinely misdescribes instead of stretching it to cover them.
