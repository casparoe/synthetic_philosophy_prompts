I'm auditing a seminar on the physics of information this term and I keep getting hung up on something everyone else treats as settled, so either I'm missing the point or the point isn't as settled as it looks. My friend who does condensed matter thinks I'm being obtuse. Here's the settled part, as I understand it: you set up Szilard's 1929 one-molecule engine, the demon drops in a partition, measures which half the molecule landed in, then lets the partition slide like a piston and pulls kT ln 2 of work out of a single heat bath. And then the punchline: the second law is safe, because before the demon can run the cycle again its memory has to be reset to blank, and Landauer's principle says resetting one bit dissipates at least kT ln 2. Books balanced, exactly. Crucially, the measuring step is treated as free — the Bennett line being that looking can be done reversibly, at arbitrarily little cost, because it's just a copy into a register that starts out blank.

That's the bit that bugs me. The whole framework puts a thermodynamic price on forgetting and no price at all on learning, even though those look like the same operation run in opposite directions: writing a bit into a blank register versus overwriting a register back to blank. If information is really physical, why does the arrow sit on one of those and not the other? And it isn't just theory. In the tabletop versions of this — single-electron boxes, trapped colloids, feedback-controlled bits — the thing actually measured is the measurement-plus-feedback cycle where work gets extracted, and the erasure is what the papers gesture at when they say the second law survives, but the reset sits outside the measured cycle. The books get balanced by an argument rather than by a stopwatch. Meanwhile the older Brillouin-style answer, that the demon pays when it looks because finding anything out requires physically coupling to it, seems to have been retired rather than refuted. And one more thing: the reset only costs something if you had a blank memory to begin with, and blank memories look like a free supply of low entropy — which is exactly the move cosmologists make when they trace the arrow of time to a special low-entropy initial condition. Over there that assumption is treated as a giant unsolved puzzle; over here it's a footnote.

So what does this practice — locating the cost in erasure and nowhere else — presuppose about what information is? Please reconstruct the implicit theory carefully rather than reciting the standard resolution. What has to be true about information for a copy-into-blank to be thermodynamically innocent and an overwrite-to-blank to be expensive: is the relevant property logical reversibility, and is that a fact about the computation, about the physical state of the memory, or about what somebody knows? Which rival pictures does the practice quietly rule out — Brillouin's, epistemic or subjectivist readings of entropy, views on which establishing a correlation between two systems is itself the costly step? Is there a real inconsistency between the measurement-is-free half and the erasure-is-costly half, or between the blank-tape assumption and the claim that the cycle is closed, or can the whole thing be made coherent with some distinction I'm not seeing? Please seperate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to sit comfortably with — I suspect a good chunk of the "information is physical" rhetoric is decoration the experiments and the accounting could survive without, and I'd like to know which chunk. And finish by telling me what would actually have to change, in how those feedback experiments are designed and how their results get reported, if you swapped in one of the rival pictures instead.
