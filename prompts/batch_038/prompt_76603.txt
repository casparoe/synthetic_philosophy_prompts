I'm in a seminar this term on moral psychology and the philosophy of history, and we spent two weeks on Plutarch — Alexander, Pericles, Nicias, then the paired comparison sections at the end of each pair — and I got hooked in a way that has nothing to do with my actual thesis, so I've been rereading Middlemarch through it and want to sanity check what I've got before I turn it into a paper. Here's the distinction I've been playing with, and I want you to stress-test it against the novel rather than just agree with me. Plutarch seems to run two different models of moral learning from a life. Call the first the exemplar model: one life is laid out as an imitable pattern, admiration is the engine, and the work gets done by the reader wanting to be like the subject — basically the exemplarist line in virtue ethics, the stuff Zagzebski and people push. Call the second the syncritic model: the moral content isn't in either life but in the pairing, so setting a Greek against a Roman and then adjudicating trains the reader in comparison rather than imitation, and you learn what courage is by seeing it twice, slightly differently, instead of once. Underneath both sits the hagiograpy problem — the more you smooth a life into an exemplar the less usable it gets, because admiration of the very best flips into the sense that you could never manage it yourself. Idealization has a cost, and I think it's a real cost, not just a stylistic worry.

My claim, tell me if it's dumb, is that Middlemarch takes the syncritic model seriously and then pushes it somewhere Plutarch never goes: into lives nobody will ever write a Life of. The Prelude opens on Saint Teresa as an exemplar and immediately says the conditions that made her kind of epic life possible are gone; the Finale says the growing good of the world depends on unhistoric acts and on people resting in unvisited tombs. That reads to me like it keeps the machinery of the great-person's life and points it at people who by definition can't be great persons, which is either an extension of Plutarch or the abolition of the genre, and I cant tell which. So: read the novel against both models and tell me which one it actually runs on. Where do the pairings do the moral work — Dorothea against Mary Garth, Lydgate against Casaubon, Bulstrode against Farebrother — and does Eliot leave the adjudication to the reader the way the comparisons sometimes do, or does her narrator judge for us so insistently that the book quietly reverts to the exemplar model while claiming not to? I especially want you on the Casaubon material, where the narrator more or less orders us to pity a man who has done nothing pitiable, and on Bulstrode, where the scandal at the assizes seems engineered so that any settled verdict feels cheap. And then the exemplarist question: does the novel show that a life nobody could admire is still morally instructive, which would be an actual argument against admiration being our route into virtue?

Be an honest critic in both directions — I'd much rather hear the reading is forced than get a compliment. If the pairing structure is just a feature of any realist novel with a big cast and not something specifically Plutarchan, say so plainly. And flag the self-undermining move I suspect you'll find: Dorothea's hidden life isn't hidden, we're reading a very long public account of it, so the Finale may be disclaiming exactly the monument the novel spends 800 pages building. Format: open with two or three sentences of bottom line — syncritic, exemplar, or neither, and whether the book extends the Plutarchan project or destroys it — then the reading, organized around the episodes you're pointing at rather than in the order of my questions. Describe the episodes by what happens in them; don't give me chapter numbers and don't quote the novel at length, paraphrase. Hard cap of 1000 words.
