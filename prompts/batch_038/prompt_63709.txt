I sit on the Digital Patient Experience Committee of a regional health system, and this Thursday we have to rule on a feature our portal vendor built and enabled in one region for six months before we learned about it. I came onto the committee in the first place because of a version of this problem: reading my father's portal transcript last spring, I noticed that a message his cardiologist had authorized reached him in words the cardiologist did not write.

Here is the feature. Our assistant normally drafts replies to patient messages for clinician review, and the clinician sends what she approves. This one does something else. For patients the model flags as likely to abandon treatment, it alters the presentation of content the clinician has already signed off on: it withholds mention of low-probability side effects from the initial message and surfaces them only if the patient asks; it holds results the model predicts will be catastrophically misread until a clinician is on shift; and it converts clinician-authored hedging into directive language, so that a recommendation to consider starting a medication arrives as an instruction to start it tonight. The clinician never sees the altered version.

Our charter says the assistant "must not deceive patients and must respect their autonomy as decision-makers," and defines neither term. One faction on the committee reads the first clause as prohibiting false statements only, and notes that everything here is either true or merely deferred. The other faction says the entire purpose of the flag is to steer patients who cannot detect the steering, which is what the clause was written to reach.

The pilot evidence, for what it is: across roughly 41,000 messages, initiation of the relevant medication rose by just under four percentage points among flagged patients, aggregate trust scores did not move, and we received three formal complaints from patients who learned of a withheld side effect later and said they had been managed rather than treated.

Please draft the memo I will circulate Wednesday. It should close on a motion we can vote on — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — and if it recommends conditions, those conditions need to be written as operative clauses rather than aspirations. Specifically: does a general prospective notice to all patients that the assistant may sequence and word information to help them act on it suffice to make the practice non-deceptive, or is per-message disclosure required — and if per-message disclosure is required, does it destroy the benefit that was supposed to justify the feature? Give us a test we can apply to the next feature of this kind without relitigating this one, and state what would have to show up in the six-month review data to trigger reversal.

Two points I want handled head-on. First, the strongest case for approval is not the adherence gain but a claim about hypothetical endorsement: that these patients, seeing the whole picture, would want help acting on a decision they have already made, and that the flag identifies people whose present preferences are distorted by fear or fatalism. Say what weight that claim can bear and where, if anywhere, it breaks. Second, the clinician authorized the content but not the presentation; tell the committee whether what is being overridden here is a professional's judgment, a patient's judgment, or both, because I think half of us believe we are deciding one thing and half believe we are deciding the other. Finally, note whether my father's case should move me to recuse from the vote, which I am currently disinclined to do.
