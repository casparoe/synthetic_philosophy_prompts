I chair the algorithmic oversight committee at a mid-sized public hospital system — nine of us: two ICU attendings, a couple of nurses, a pharmacist, two community reps, a lawyer, a health economist, and me (clinical informaticist; I've read around the philosophy but I'm not a philosopher). Next month we vote on whether to buy a vendor tool that ranks patients for post-op ICU beds when demand exceeds capacity, and our pilot agreement lapses at the end of the quarter, so this can't slip.

The part I'm stuck on isn't the usual accuracy/bias stuff. It's that this tool doesn't hide its value commitments — which I actually like right up until you ask how it combines them.

It scores each waiting patient three ways: expected life-years gained, a prioritarian term giving extra weight to the worse off, and a fair-inn style term looking at how much of a normal lifespan the patient has already had. Then it merges them, and the merging is where we're stuck:

- The vendor assigns each framework a fixed weight it calls a "credence" — 0.5 / 0.3 / 0.2 — and the deck explains these as our uncertainty about which framework is the right one.
- Each framework's score gets min-max normalized to 0–1 across the batch of patients currently waiting, and then the weighted average is taken.
- The prioritarian term has an exponent the vendor calls the equity dial, currently 2.3, tunable by the customer.
- The tool also emits a "conflict score" for how far the three rankings disagree about a given case. Above a threshold it abstains and sends the case to a human. In a six-month pilot at a sister hospital, 18% of cases crossed the threshold. Our human panel meets twice a week and says 18% would roughly double their load. Vendor's suggestion is to raise the threshold; the fallback nobody likes is that on abstention the tool silently reverts to the life-years ranking.

Pilot numbers, for what they're worth: 82% agreement with the human panel; of the 40 cases the panel overrode, a later blinded review judged the model's call better in 11.

The committee is split three ways and I don't think any camp is dumb. The community reps say a vendor setting 0.5/0.3/0.2 is unelected moral legislation — if we're picking weights, they get picked in public by a body answerable to patients. The ICU docs say every triage call already embeds weights, theirs just never got written down, and at least this ones is auditable and appealable. The economist says the normalization makes the weights close to meaningless anyway, since a min-max rescaling is batch-relative, so whether you get a bed partly depends on who else happened to be waiting that night — and we shouldn't pretend arithmetic settles it.

Write the memo I'll circulate before the vote. Recommend one of buy / don't buy / buy with conditions — and if conditions, make them things we could actually put in a contract and monitor afterward: what has to change about the normalization, who sets the weights and by what process, what happens when the tool abstains, what we tell a patient or family whose ranking came out the way it did. Keep the reasons followable for a surgeon and a lawyer with no philosophy background, so no citations, no name-dropping, no reading list — I want the argument, not the references. And give the losing side its real due: whichever camp you end up rejecting, state their strongest case better than I've stated it here, and say why it still loses. One-paragraph bottom line at the top, then the reasoning. Around 700–900 words.
