In March, the two founders of the engineering consultancy where I have worked for eleven years signed the firm over to an employee-ownership trust. I now sit on the nine-member transitional council, and I have been asked to draft the short statement of principles that will preface our new constitution. A colleague on the council has been arguing that we should ground that preamble in John Stuart Mill, and specifically in the posthumous Chapters on Socialism, which he maintains shows Mill endorsing precisely the arrangement we are building. I would like to test that claim before it hardens into our founding document, but I am finding the relevant stretch of argument genuinely hard to follow, and I would be grateful for your help with it.

One disclosure before I begin: I do not have the book with me this evening, so I will not attempt to reproduce any of its wording. What follows is my own sentence-by-sentence rendering of a paragraph in the chapter in which Mill canvasses the objections to socialism — the point at which he stops listing the standard objections and turns to explaining how, in his view, the question actually has to be settled. If you know this passage and my rendering distorts it, please say so before answering any of the questions below, since several of my puzzles may be artefacts of my own paraphrase.

My rendering is this. First, the objections usually pressed against socialism — that abolishing private property removes the incentive to labour, that no such scheme has ever been sustained at scale, that human nature would have to be transformed before it could work — do not on Mill's account amount to a refutation; they amount at most to a showing that the thing has not yet been done. Second, he appears to argue that the comparison cannot be run the way the objectors run it, because the two terms of the comparison are not in the same epistemic position: the evils of the existing order are matters of daily observation, whereas the evils predicted of a socialist order are inferences about a condition nobody has yet inhabited. Third — and this is where I lose the thread — he seems immediately to reverse the polarity, noting that the socialist is in the same position with respect to the benefits he promises, and that a promise of future improvement is worth nothing to a labouring family until it has been realised. Fourth, he concludes, if I read him correctly, that the only way to settle the matter is by trial on a limited scale, that it would be unjust to prevent voluntary communities from attempting their schemes, and that such attempts would either collapse under their own weight or persuade by their results. Fifth, in the pages that follow he introduces a worry that seems to me to undercut the fourth point: that under a fully communal arrangement every member would be exposed to the observation and moral pressure of the whole body, and that the weight of communal opinion might prove more constricting than any dependence on an employer, since a person can leave an employer but not a community.

With that in front of you, here is what I would like to understand.

1. Are my second and third points right? If so, what is Mill doing by running the asymmetry argument in both directions inside the same passage — is he cancelling the two asymmetries out, or weighting them differently? What does the passage ultimately license a reader to conclude about where the burden of proof lies?

2. In the fourth point, what is the scope of the trial he has in mind? Does he mean that communities of the Fourierist type should be permitted to form and compete within an otherwise unaltered market order, or that a national scheme could be adopted provisionally and then judged by its working? My colleague assumes the second, and I am not sure the text supports him.

3. Does the distinction between communism and socialism, as those terms were used in his day rather than ours, carry argumentative weight here? My understanding is that Mill treats a scheme which continues to remunerate capital and distributes the product unequally among labour, capital and skill as a different object of assessment from one which pools everything and divides it equally or according to need. If that is right, which of the two is under discussion in the passage I have rendered, and does his verdict carry across to the other?

4. How does the fifth point relate to the first? Is it a retraction, a qualification confined to full communism, or a separate objection that he regards as more serious than the incentive objection he has just defused? And does he think the danger is intrinsic to common ownership, or contingent on the size and character of the community?

5. The practical question, which is the reason I am writing. A trust of one hundred and forty people, still competing for contracts against conventional firms, still paying differentiated salaries by role, and still allowing members to realise their interest on departure, is evidently not the object Mill is assessing. Is there anything in this passage that supports my colleague's claim that Mill would see our arrangement as the realisation of the ideal under discussion — or is the honest answer that our arrangement belongs to a different category he treats elsewhere in his work, namely voluntary association among workers who own their own capital and compete within a system he does not propose to abolish? I need to decide whether Mill belongs in our preamble at all, and if so in what capacity.

For each question, please give me your best reading; where my rendering admits of more than one, give me the strongest rival reading as well and identify what feature of the text would decide between them. I would like to be able to state Mill's position to the council in a single paragraph without overclaiming on his behalf, and I should add that the book breaks off unfinished, so please tell me if any of my five points belongs to material he never completed rather than to the argument as he left it.
