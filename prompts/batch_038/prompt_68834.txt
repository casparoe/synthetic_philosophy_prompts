I'm a backend engineer, zero history or philosophy training, and after an acquisition I've ended up as the de facto owner of a twelve-year-old internal service. Most of the people who built it are gone. My job for the next quarter is to write the "why is it like this" doc — the actual reasons, not the folklore — and the problem is that every account I collect contradicts some other account. One guy says the weird retry/backoff behaviour was added after a specific bad incident in 2014. Another says it came in through a vendor SDK and nobody ever chose it. A third says he wrote it himself over a weekend. I have git history, old tickets, Slack archives going back about nine years, two postmortems, and maybe six living ex-colleagues who'll do a 30 minute call.

My instincts about which story to trust keep turning out to be garbage. The worst one: I treated three people independently telling me the same thing as strong evidence, then found a 2016 internal blog post all three had read and linked to, so it was one story with three mouths. Historians have apparently been grinding on exactly this problem with evidence way worse than mine — no logs, no version control, witnesses dead for millennia — so I want whatever part of that actually transfers, turned into something I can run. I hit maybe two or three of these claims a week, my manager signs off on the doc, and new hires will read it as fact, so writing down something merely plausible is worse than writing "we don't know."

So: a procedure, in order, that I can work through on a single piece of testimony in about fifteen minutes. For each step, what question I'm asking, what would count as a good enough answer, and what I do if there isn't one. Specifically I want it to cover — how to test whether two accounts are genuinely independent or both descend from one origin; how much weight a hard trace (a commit, a ticket timestamp, a deploy log) carries against a confident first-person memory; what to do when the teller has something to gain or lose from the version they're giving; whether there's anything special about details that cost the teller something to admit; and when I should just declare the question unanswerable and stop repeating the story. Flag the steps that are really judgement calls dressed up as procedure, and skip the bibliography, I'm not going to read it.

Then run it on three cases: (1) the retry/backoff dispute above, where the SDK story and the incident story can't both be true and the git history has been squashed; (2) an outage everybody tells identically, word for word, including the part about the on-call engineer's laptop; (3) a design decision whose only surviving evidence is a four-word commit message that points the other way from what everyone remembers. For at least one of them I want the answer I don't want — the one where my favourite story doesn't survive. And close with what this procedure fundamentally can't get me, so I stop hoping.
