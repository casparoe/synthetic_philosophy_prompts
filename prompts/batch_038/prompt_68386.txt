I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and for the past four years I have taught the required concepts seminar in a graduate programme in science journalism. My students are training to write about minds — animal, machine, and human — and the most common defect in their drafts is a silent slide between levels of description: a passage that begins with what a controller's registers do ends with what the controller believes, and nobody can say whether the second sentence is licensed by the first. This term the seminar has worked through Dennett's distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances, and his proposal that a pattern counts as real insofar as capturing it lets you predict the data more economically than brute enumeration could.

The end-of-term assessment is a closed-book, forty-five-minute timed essay worth twenty-five marks. I would like you to write one question on this material, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question must be built around the following case, which I have devised and which I want kept substantially as it stands. You may tighten the wording, but please do not substitute a stock example: no chess programs, thermostats, Chinese rooms, or philosophical zombies.

A commercial pollination firm runs a swarm of two hundred small autonomous drones across an almond orchard. Two rival descriptions of the swarm are on offer. The first is the engineers' specification. Each drone carries a lookup table of roughly forty thousand sensor-signature-to-motor-command entries, accumulated by reinforcement during training, plus a small array of route statistics. The specification document is explicit that nothing inside a drone carries information about the orchard in any compositional or reusable form: there is no map, no self-model, and no state that is about the wind, the bloom, or the other drones. From the specification the engineers can predict a drone's next motor command exactly, but only one step ahead; asked to project the swarm's behaviour over an hour, or into conditions not represented in the table, their simulation degrades to near chance. The second is a visiting behavioural ecologist's description. She assigns the swarm a handful of attitudes — that it takes the north rows to be depleted, that it prefers to spread rather than clump, that it wants to minimise energy spent per unit of pollen collected — and derives its behaviour from these. Across novel trials the firm had not anticipated (a wind machine switched on, half the rows netted, thirty drones removed at random) her predictions hold about ninety-two per cent of the time and beat the simulation built directly from the true specification. Asked how she arrives at her attributions, she says she asks what a well-designed forager, situated as that swarm is and equipped with those sensors, ought to take the world to be, and then checks whether the swarm behaves accordingly. The internal states are the same under both descriptions; the two accounts differ only in level.

Please deliver the following:

1. The question exactly as students will see it, with the case embedded in the stem and any necessary instructions about length and what is being asked. The stem must be self-contained: a student should not need to recall any detail of the scholarly debate around Dennett, and no quotation or textual reference should be required. It must demand a verdict — whether the swarm's attitudes pick out something real or only a convenient summary — and it must require the student to say what further evidence would discriminate between those two readings, rather than merely asserting that one of them follows from the stance's success.

2. A model answer of about seven hundred words. It should commit to a position and should be explicit about the sense in which it is using words like real and has. I would like it to show why the objection that nothing inside the drones represents anything does not settle the case by itself, and then to press the harder problem that the ecologist's method invites: if attributions are generated by asking what a rational system ought to believe, the stance's accuracy may be partly manufactured by the ascriber rather than discovered in the swarm, and rival attributions may be intertranslatable.

3. A marking scheme totalling twenty-five marks, organised into five or six named moves. For each move, state the marks available, what a first-class answer says, what a competent but second-class answer says instead, and one line on how students typically botch it.

4. A short list of expensive errors — the ones that should cap a mark rather than merely cost a point — with a sentence each explaining why the error is an error and not a defensible minority position. I expect at least one of these to concern the relationship between the completeness of the physical stance and the reality of higher-level description, and at least one to concern treating the question as settled by declaring oneself a realist or an instrumentalist.

5. Two or three sentences, addressed to me rather than to the students, on what this question tests that a conventional question asking candidates to distinguish the three stances would miss.

Throughout, no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and the marking scheme must not award credit for naming secondary literature. The exam is closed-book, and I want the marks to go to reasoning about the case rather than to retrieval.
