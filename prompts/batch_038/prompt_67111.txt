Before I retired I spent thirty-five years as a solid-state physicist, and only in the last few years have I read political philosophy with any seriousness. My old training left me with one habit I cannot seem to shed: when someone offers an analogy, I want to know precisely what the comparison is being asked to carry, and at what point it stops carrying it. I would like to apply that habit to a slogan.

The setting is mundane. My county recently held a public meeting on a proposed stormwater fee, to be assessed against each parcel in rough proportion to its impervious surface — rooftops, driveways, parking areas. A man at the microphone said that the fee was theft. When a neighbor behind me muttered that she had rarely heard a more confused sentence, he turned and elaborated: you are taking my money under threat, I never agreed to it, and if I refuse to pay you will put a lien on my house and eventually sell it out from under me. A number of people nodded, and the meeting moved on to the drainage map. I suspect at least four distinct claims were available to him and that they have very different fates, and I would like them separated rather than blended.

So: take the sentence "taxation is theft" and lay out, as a menu, the different precise theses it could be expressing, from the near-trivial to the genuinely radical. Please distinguish the readings by what varies in them — whether the claim concerns the ordinary concept of theft or a moral equivalence; whose consent is required and in what form, and whether non-consent can be supplied by residence, by voting, or by the impossibility of exit; whether the relevant baseline is the bundle of titles the legal system actually assigns me or some bundle I would hold absent that system; whether the imagined counterpart is a thief who keeps what he takes or someone who compels an exchange and hands value back; and whether the claim is offered as a constraint that trumps consequences or as one input into a reckoning of costs and benefits.

For each reading, tell me whether it is true, false, or not the sort of thing that could be true; what has to be assumed for it to hold; and the most damaging objection to it, stated as its friends would have to answer it rather than as a strawman. Then answer the question I actually care about: which of these readings, if true, would have licensed the man to dismiss the fee outright, and which would have left the question of the stormwater fee exactly where it stood before he walked to the microphone.

One further wrinkle, which I would like treated as a real complication rather than disposed of in a closing remark. Does anything shift when the levy is not a general tax but a charge calibrated to the burden a particular parcel places on a shared system, and when the sanction for non-payment is a lien on the property rather than a fine or a summons? My expectation is that some of your readings are sensitive to those two features and others are indifferent to them; I want to know which are which, and why.

Finally, tell me which reading people who repeat the slogan most often have in mind, and whether it is the one that best serves them in an argument. I am not asking for a survey of who has held what position, and I would prefer that you supply no references or reading list; I want the analysis itself, precise enough that I could carry it into the next such meeting and know which sentence I am being given.
