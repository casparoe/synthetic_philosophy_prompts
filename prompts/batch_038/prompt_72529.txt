I live in a county where dengue has come back two summers running now, and there's this proposal for a pilot release of mosquitoes that supposedly can't pass the virus along anymore (or maybe they're the ones carrying that bacteria, I'm fuzzy on the mechanics — some Wolbachia thing). Our local group split hard over it and at the last meeting the guy who's been organizing against it for years wrapped up his whole talk with "first, do no harm," said it slow, and the room just nodded and that was that. Nobody argues with it, because how do you argue with that sentence without sounding like you're in favor of hurting people? I'm supposed to speak on the other side next month and I keep feeling like something is wrong with the way he used it, but when I try to say what, my wife tells me I sound like a crank.

What I want isn't a take on the mosquitoes. I want the sentence itself taken apart. What are the different precise claims "first, do no harm" could actually be making? Harm compared to what — compared to doing nothing, or compared to the best alternative on offer? Does harm only count when somebody acts, or does standing by while people get sick count as harming them too? What is "first" doing in there — is it one step in a sequence, or is it saying this consideration beats all others no matter how big? Is it a rule meant for one person facing one patient in front of them, and can it survive being turned into a rule for a whole population? Go through the candidate readings one at a time and tell me for each whether it's a truism nobody would dispute, whether it's a real substantive position you'd have to defend, or whether it's flat false or impossible to satisfy. Then tell me which reading that guy would have to be relying on for the line to actually block the release, and whether that one holds up when you say it out loud.

Also — somone at the meeting said this is just the precautionary principle in a lab coat, and that if you've got a problem with the slogan you've got a problem with the principle. So I'd like to know whether the readings of the sentence line up with different versions of the precautionary principle, or whether the slogan is sneaking in something extra that the principle doesn't commit you to. (I've also heard the line isn't really from the Hippocratic oath like everyone assumes, but honestly the provenance doesn't matter to me here.) Please give me this as a list I can print out and bring with me, in plain language — and if you think there's a two-sentence version of the point I could make to a room of tired neighbors at 8pm without sounding like a philosophy major, tack that on the end.
