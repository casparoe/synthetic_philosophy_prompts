so my flatmate is building a translation startup and their whole pitch deck leans on this idea that language is a lens — each language is a different lens on the world, speakers of different languages literally inhabit different perceptual worlds, which is why word-for-word translation always betrays something. they use it to argue that a translation system shouldn't be scored on accuracy or faithfulness at all, because there's no neutral scene that both versions are answerable to; the right target is what they call worldview transfer, and any benchmark that penalises you for moving a metaphor or swapping an image is measuring the wrong thing. they've got the usual exibits lined up: the Russian thing where having two basic words for blue makes people faster at telling two shades apart when the category boundary falls between them, that Australian language where everyone gives directions in cardinal terms instead of left and right, untranslatables like saudade, and the point that a language's grammar forces you to specify stuff (whether you witnessed something or only heard about it, whether the thing is round or long, whatever) that another language lets you skip entirely.

here's the bit I can't get them to engage with: a lens doesn't create the scene, it just changes what gets through and how it's bent — which seems to presuppose exactly the thing they say doesn't exist, namely something both languages are about. and lenses are uniform, same distortion for every ray and every user, whereas the effects they cite sound small, task-specific and wildly variable between people, plus everyone picks up second languages and notices the mismatches, which you can't do with two pairs of glasses bolted onto your eyes. a guy at their demo night made the opposite move: said the lens picture is an old Whorfian hand-me-down and that language is better thought of as a form with mandatory fields — you're obliged to fill in gender, number, tense, direction, source of knowledge, and that obligation shapes what you habitually notice, but nothing stops you from spelling out in words what your form has no box for.

can you do a proper audit for me? lay out the correspondence table for the lens analogy — what's the light, the scene, the lens, the observer, the fact that you can't take the lens off — then tell me which disanalogies actually bite against the pitch and which ones are just pedantry. I especially want the features of lenses the analogy quietly imports that language doesn't have, and a straight answer on whether a lens can even do the job they want: does it support incommensurability, or does it undercut it once you look at what lenses are for? then run the mandatory-fields form against the same list — mapping, breaks, what it smuggles in (a bureaucrat? a designer? coercion? a form you can choose not to fill out?) — and say which of the two, if either, can carry the claim that accuracy is the wrong metric for a translation system. if you'd propose a third analogy instead, sketch it and be explicit about what it licenses and what it still leaves unexplained.
