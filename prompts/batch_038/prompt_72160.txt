I'm TA-ing a second-year philosophy of action module and I've got about forty scripts left to mark by Friday, so I'd like a second pair of eyes on this one before I commit a mark. I've not really formed a view on it yet.

The exam question was worth 30 marks out of a three-hour paper, and students were told to spend roughly 45 minutes on it. It read:

> A residents' association has three members, A, B and C, and a standing rule: the association will object to a planning application if and only if (i) the development will increase traffic through the lane, and (ii) increased traffic will harm the character of the area.
>
> A planning application comes in. Their votes are:
> A — (i) yes, (ii) yes, therefore object
> B — (i) yes, (ii) no, therefore don't object
> C — (i) no, (ii) yes, therefore don't object
>
> So if the association votes separately on (i) and (ii) and then applies its rule, it objects. If it votes directly on whether to object, it doesn't.
>
> (a) Say what is going on in this case.
> (b) Does a case of this kind show that judging and acting together requires a group agent whose attitudes are something over and above the attitudes of the members, or can it be handled in purely individualist terms? In answering, say what would have to be true for one of these two positions to be right and the other wrong.

Here is the student's answer, typed up from the script as it stands:

> This is a case of the discursive dilemma, or doctrinal paradox. Premise-wise voting takes a majority on each of the two premises seperately and then applies the association's standing rule; conclusion-wise voting takes a majority directly on the conclusion. A is the only member whose own answers to the premises entail objecting, so the two procedures come apart: on the premises the association ends up committed to objecting, on the conclusion it does not.
>
> At this level the case isn't obviously about agency at all. It shows that the outcome of a group decision depends on which procedure you use to reach it, and that there is no aggregation rule guaranteed to track what the members would each have concluded had they thought it through together. That is roughly the content of Arrow's impossibility theorem, so what we have here is a social choice problem wearing an agential costume.
>
> List and Pettit draw a stronger lesson. They argue that cases like this show there are genuine group agents, because the association ends up holding a judgement that no individual member holds, and it is the association rather than any person who has to answer for it. I think that's right, and I don't see how an individualist could even describe the decision, since it is not A's, not B's and not C's.
>
> The same issue comes up in the joint action literature, though there it is about intention rather than judgement. On Bratman's account we intend to J when each of us intends that we J, those intentions interlock and are supported by meshing subplans, and each of us is responsive to the others' planning. The important thing for our purposes is that on his view the shared intention is a single attitude belonging to the group as such, over and above the interlocking attitudes of the members — which is why he's willing to say that groups literally intend things. That supports the conclusion above: if shared intention is a group-level attitude, then the association's decision is a group-level judgement, and the paradox just shows the two coming apart from the members.
>
> Searle takes the opposite starting point. He puts we-intentions in individual heads rather than in the group, and on his view a we-intention is nothing over and above an individual intention together with beliefs about what the others intend, so collective intentionality is individualist all the way down. But that doesn't rescue the individualist here, since the association's judgement is precisely one that no member has, and no pile of beliefs about each other's intentions will produce it.
>
> Gilbert would say the members are jointly committed, and that joint commitment is what gives each of them standing to complain if the association fails to follow through. On her account a joint commitment is just the sum of each member's personal commitment to the others, so once again the group's decision is built out of individual pieces.
>
> So the case does show that acting and judging together needs a group mind. The association judges something none of its members judges, and the only way to make sense of what it owes the council is to take up the group's point of view. An individualist account will always leave out the fact that it's the group, not any person, that has to explain itself.
