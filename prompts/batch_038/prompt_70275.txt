Been going down a rabbit hole on whether generations are a real kind of thing or just a marketing convenience, and I've hit what feels like a dead end that nobody in the debate seems to address.

The demography side: age, period and cohort are perfectly colinear — a cohort is literally period minus age — so in any model containing all three, the data can't tell you how to divide up the variance. Any 'generation effect' you estimate can be re-expressed as some mixture of ageing and historical period, and which decomposition you land on depends entirely on the constraint you impose from outside. Which seems to mean the question is definately settled by arithmetic: an effect that can't be identified can't be doing real causal work, so 'Boomer' just picks out 'people who are currently in their late sixties and seventies'. And the business of drawing boundaries looks hopeless for the same reason — why 1965 and not 1963? — since you can always shift the cut point and re-describe whatever you found.

A friend of mine pushes back with the usual line about shared formative experiences, that a cohort is shaped by what happened while it was coming of age. But I don't see how that survives either. If the cohort term is unidentified, then saying 'it was their formative years' isn't a mechanism, it's just a relabelling of the age term in more evocative language.

So, two things. Is there anything left for the philosophy debate about whether cohorts are real social kinds to actually be about, once you accept the above? Or is it purely verbal — two vocabularies for the same age-graded data? And if there is something left, what would a realist put forward as evidence or argument that isn't just a restatement of the arbitrariness problem? I'd rather have the reasoning than a list of hedges.
