Something in the case reports has been bugging me for months and I don't know who to ask. When a court refuses to recognize a breakaway or usurping government — the seceded American South, say, or a country whose constitution a coup has suspended — it holds that the regime's legislature was no legislature and its statutes aren't law. Then the same court will uphold a marriage performed under that regime's marriage act, a deed entered in it's land registry, a will probated by its judges, on the ground that voiding all of it would make ordinary life impossible, while still voiding the enactments that helped the regime do what it did. So the same statute is simultaneously not-law and law enough to make your wedding real. What has to be true about legal validity for a court to hold both of those at once? Reconstruct the theory of law the practice presupposes — what it takes the connection between a rule's pedigree and its content to be — say which rival theories it quietly rules out, judge whether the two halves are genuinely consistent or whether one of them is being smuggled in under cover of a practical excuse, and tell me what courts would have to start doing differently if they took the rival view seriously. Exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no intro and no wrap-up.
