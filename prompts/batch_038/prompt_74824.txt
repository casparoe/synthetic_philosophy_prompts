1. Setup. Let L be a language of indicative conditionals closed under the conditional connective, and let P be a probability function on L. Adams's thesis is that for indicative conditionals, assertability tracks conditional probability. The semantic ambition is stronger: that A→B is an ordinary sentence of L with a truth value, and that P(A→B) = P(B|A) for all A, B and all P. Lewis showed that this ambition collapses. If the equation holds for every probability function on a conditional-closed language, then for every A with P(A) > 0 and every B, P(B|A) is either 0 or 1: every non-degenerate conditional probability disappears. In the postscript he later added to the second volume of his philosophical papers, Lewis showed that substantially weaker assumptions suffice — the equation restricted to unembedded conditionals still forces the collapse, provided it is required to hold not only for a probability function but for that function conditioned on arbitrary further evidence. Subsequent work on compounds of conditionals pushed in the same direction. The moral usually drawn is that indicative conditionals are not truth-apt, and that Adams's thesis needs a suppositional or pragmatic reading.

2. Separating the closure conditions. My claim is that this moral conceals the fact that the theorem is about a package, and that the package contains at least four distinguishable commitments.

C1 — Closure under embedding. Conditionals may occur in the scope of negation, conjunction and disjunction, and inside other conditionals.

C2 — Closure under conditioning. Wherever the equation is asserted, it is asserted for conditional probabilities: P(A→B | C) = P(B | A ∧ C) whenever P(A ∧ C) > 0.

C3 — Closure under the operator. The class of sentences for which the equation is demanded includes arbitrarily deep compounds of conditionals, and the equation must be compatible with the natural nesting identifications.

C4 — Universality. The equation must hold for every probability function on the algebra, not merely for those that are regular, evidentially reasonable, or generated by some distinguished structure.

Triviality is a theorem about the conjunction. The question the literature answers only piecemeal, and which I want to make the organizing question of the paper, is which of these can be given up, and what a theory looks like afterwards.

3. Three post-triviality packages.

Package A, embedding-first. Keep C1 and C3. Treat the conditional as a genuine connective with truth conditions, but hold the equation only for unembedded conditionals and only for a distinguished class of credal states, so that C2 and C4 are both weakened. Cost: the equation ceases to be a meaning postulate and becomes a contingent normative fact about ideal credence, and since P(A→B) may then diverge from P(B|A) for actual agents, the assertability data has to be explained pragmatically anyway — which invites the question what the truth conditions were purchased for.

Package B, conditioning-first. Keep C2 and C4 intact and give up C1 and C3: conditionals express suppositions rather than propositions, so there is no conditional-closed algebra and the theorem simply does not get a grip. The equation is preserved exactly. Cost: an account of embedded occurrences is owed, and the accounts on offer are pragmatic to a degree that concedes most of what a semantic theory was supposed to deliver.

Package C, function-first. Keep C1, C2 and C3, and reject C4: the equation is required to hold only for the functions that represent ideally rational credence, or for functions generated by a particular structure — for instance hierarchies of higher-order opinion spaces whose points encode orderings of base worlds, or two-place treatments in which conditional probability is primitive rather than a ratio. Cost: correctness becomes inaccessible from inside. An agent can never be sure that her credal state belongs to the class for which the equation holds, so the equation cannot guide her. And, so far as I know, it is not settled in general whether such constructions avoid triviality at all: they are known to work for restricted languages, and I am not aware of a general theorem in either direction.

4. The thesis. Three claims.

(i) The triviality theorem does not adjudicate between A, B and C. It shows that the four closure commitments are jointly unsatisfiable; each package is defined by dropping a different one. The familiar inference from triviality to the conclusion that conditionals are not truth-apt is therefore a non sequitur: what is refuted is the conjunction, and the suppositional response is one of at least three ways of unbundling it.

(ii) C4 is the commitment doing the most work and the one least often named. Triviality is a universality result: it says that no arbitrary probability function can satisfy the equation. That is, on reflection, unsurprising — arbitrary functions encode arbitrary opinions, and no non-degenerate semantic constraint survives being demanded of all of them. The right question is consequently not for which languages the equation can hold, but for which pairs consisting of a class of probability functions and a subclass of sentences it can hold. What is wanted is a satisfiability map, not another theorem.

(iii) Among the four, C1 is the most dispensable and C2 the least. Our embedding data for conditionals is thin, largely paraphraseable, and dependent on pragmatic strengthening; whereas the demand that a conditional credence survive conditioning on new evidence is what makes the equation a principle about belief rather than a curiosity about a connective. So B is the most stable resting place, A the least, and C is viable only if the satisfiability map turns out to be non-empty for interesting pairs.

5. Where I expect the draft to be weak.

First, (iii) invites the reply that the embedding data is precisely what any semantic theory of a natural-language construction must handle, and that a view on which conditionals cannot occur inside disjunctions is not a view about English conditionals at all.

Second, Package C may be unstable rather than merely costly. If the equation holds only for a distinguished class of functions, the theory owes an account of what fixes membership, and the most natural candidate classes are closed under conditioning; if so, C4 is re-imported through the back door and C collapses into A or B, which would make my trichotomy a false one.

Third, (ii) risks being rhetorically effective but philosophically empty. A critic will say that universality is exactly what a meaning postulate requires, that Lewis's point was to show the equation cannot be a meaning postulate, and that reframing the issue as a map over classes of functions changes the subject from meaning to rationality.
