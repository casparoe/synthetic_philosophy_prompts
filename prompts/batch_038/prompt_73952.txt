**THE QUESTION** (two parts, 800 words max)

(a) Distinguish strong environmental determinism — the thesis that environmental factors determine which societies go on to develop agriculture, states, writing, standing armies and so forth — from the weaker thesis that environmental factors constrain the range of developmental trajectories available to a society without fixing which one is taken. Does Jared Diamond's argument in Guns, Germs, and Steel commit him to the strong version? Say what would count as evidence on either side of that attribution.

(b) A classmate argues: "Tambora was a historical actor in the food crises of 1816, because without the 1815 eruption those harvest failures would not have happened. Anything that passes a but-for test is a historical actor." State at least two *distinct* problems with this inference, and then say what, if anything, survives of the claim that non-human factors can be causes in history.

**THE STUDENT'S ANSWER**

(a) Determinism is the thesis that the past, together with the laws of nature, fixes everything that happens afterwards. Understood that way it is trivially true, or at any rate it is not a thesis a historian could ever establish or refute from archives, so the interesting question is whether Diamond's environmentalism is determinism in some weaker sense. I think it isn't. Diamond's emphasis is on the orientation of continental axes, on how many domesticable plants and animals a region happened to have, and on sheer luck of location; he is at pains to reject the idea that some peoples were simply cleverer. A determinism would have to say that given the environment the outcome could not have been otherwise, and Diamond doesn't show that — there are well-endowed regions that took a very long time to develop states, and obviously contingent events along the way. So his position is the weaker one: environment constrains the menu, history orders from it.

On evidence: it would be comparative. If societies with closely similar endowments reliably developed along similar lines in several independent regions, that pushes toward the strong version. If similar endowments produced wildly different trajectories, that pushes toward the weak one. The problem is that we only get a handful of truly independent cases, so the test can never be very sharp.

(b) First problem: the but-for test is far too permissive. Almost everything is a but-for cause of almost everything downstream of it. Without the oxygen in the air the 1816 harvest failures wouldn't have happened either, and without the previous winter, and without the sun. So passing a but-for test can't be what makes something a historical actor, or else every background condition in the universe is a historical actor in every event.

Second problem: the test doesn't distinguish causes from mere conditions.

The deeper issue is that the classmate has assumed that to be a cause is to be an agent. The volcano didn't do anything for a reason, so on this account it can't be a cause at all — which is absurd, since obviously eruptions cause things. That's what's wrong with the phrase "historical actor": the word actor smuggles in intentions. So the honest conclusion is that climate and disease aren't causes in history; they're the medium that human causes work through.

You can see this in the conquest of the Americas. The collapse of indigenous populations was overwhelmingly caused by introduced pathogens — estimates run as high as ninety percent — and it follows that European decisions weren't really causes of the conquest. Cortés couldn't have done anything unless smallpox had already done it for him. So the germ story disproves the classmate in a second way: it shows that the largest events in history weren't done by anyone, which is exactly why they shouldn't be described as done by anyone.

What survives: non-human factors can be explanatorily indispensable, since you literally cannot narrate 1816 without Tambora, but indispensability isn't agency. And which people starved in 1816 depended on markets, property relations and whatever relief was organised — social facts — so even the effects of the eruption were mediated by human institutions. Calling the volcano an actor risks sliding back into the determinism everyone in part (a) is trying to avoid.

Conclusion: Diamond is a possibilist, not a determinist, and the classmate's criterion fails both because it is too broad and because it confuses causation with agency.
