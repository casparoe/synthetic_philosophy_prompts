You are a professional epistemologist and metaphysician who works on the paradox of knowability and on the realism/anti-realism debate, and who is accustomed to auditing a graduate student's position before a seminar rather than lecturing them on it. Think carefully, and sketch the relevant derivations for yourself before committing to any judgment about severity: I would rather have four correct findings than fifteen vague unease.

Context. Next week I present my own position on Fitch's paradox to our department's epistemology seminar. The format is that I circulate a numbered statement of my commitments in advance and the group spends the hour trying to break the package. I want your audit first. I have tried to make each item individually defensible — this is meant to be a recognizable position that a real anti-realist could hold, not a strawman — but I strongly suspect that at least one pair is quietly incompatible, and I cannot tell which of the apparent problems are genuine.

Here is the position, which I have been calling restricted anti-realism.

(1) Anti-realism about truth is correct: for a statement to be true is for it to be warrantedly assertible under epistemically ideal conditions. Truth is not evidence-transcendent in the realist's sense.

(2) Hence the knowability principle: for every truth, it is possible that it is known. In my formulation, "known" ranges only over finite, discursive, language-using agents — agents whose cognitive architecture is relevantly like ours. Infinitary, logically omniscient, or non-sentential ideal knowers are excluded, on the grounds that the anti-realist point concerns recognitional capacity, not how much a knower can hold in mind at once. I have said nothing precise about which modality governs the "possible"; I have been using it loosely, assuming nothing turns on the choice.

(3) The knowability principle is not knowable a priori. As I hold it, it is a bold and revisable conjecture about the world — roughly, that the world is epistemically transparent — supported inductively by the long-run track record of successful inquiry, much as a uniformity-of-nature principle would be. It is neither a conceptual truth nor a consequence of the meaning of "true".

(4) I do not resist the derivation by tampering with the logic. I accept that knowledge is factive, that it distributes over conjunction, and that Fitch's argument goes through in the weakest normal modal logic: given an unknown truth, it is possible that it is known; possibly knowing a conjunction entails possibly knowing each conjunct; and knowing that one does not know that p entails that one does not know that p. So a knowability principle ranging over all statements is untenable — either some truths are unknowable even for agents like us, or every truth is already known. I take the lesson to concern the principle's scope, not the logic.

(5) My blocking move is therefore a restriction of the domain. The principle applies only to what I call Cartesian statements: those for which "some finite agent knows p" is consistent. "p and no one knows p" is not Cartesian, since it cannot coherently be supposed known, so such conjunctions fall outside the principle's scope and the derivation in (4) never gets started. I insist this is not an ad hoc rescue but the correct semantics of "possible", applied carefully. (The label is not original to me; the use is mine.)

(6) Whether a statement is Cartesian is never settled from the armchair. Claims about what could be known are settled, if at all, empirically — by our actually coming to know things and by discovering what finite agents can and cannot manage. There is no a priori procedure for sorting the Cartesian from the rest.

(7) The restriction must keep the principle substantial. It is supposed to show that realism is untenable for at least some contested classes of statements — the indefinitely remote past, other minds, uninstantiated counterfactuals — and thereby to keep the realism/anti-realism dispute live. If my principle turns out to be trivially satisfied by everything, or silent on precisely the disputed cases, I have not stated a position at all.

(8) Verdict on a case. That a particular unmarked pebble on a particular Cretaceous shoreline lay exactly three centimetres from a second pebble at a moment every physical trace of which has since been destroyed: I hold that this claim is true and that it is impossible — not merely that it will not happen, but that it could not happen — that anyone ever knows it. I do not think this embarrasses (2), because the impossibility I have in mind is a natural one, concerning the destruction of every trace, whereas (2) concerns what could be known in some broader sense.

(9) Verdict on a case. There is no truth of the matter about what would have happened had a particular battle been fought with weapons that did not exist for another two thousand years. I take this to follow from (1) together with (7), not to be an extra assumption I am smuggling in.

What I want from you:

1. A pairwise scan first: list every pair of items you think warrants scrutiny and mark each as a genuine tension, a tension that arises only given some contestable auxiliary assumption, or a merely apparent tension. Do not confine yourself to the pairs I flag below; I have no idea where the damage is.

2. For each genuine tension, exhibit the derivation or construct the concrete case that generates it — which sentences, which steps, and which of my stated motivations does the work. Where a tension arises only on a particular reading of one of my terms, say so, and tell me what happens on the rival reading; if both readings break something, report that as the finding rather than selecting the friendlier reading.

3. Rank the genuine tensions by severity, where severity is a function of how much of the package collapses if the tension is left unresolved and of how cheap the repair is. Do not hand me a flat list of equally bad problems.

4. At least one trap is going to be sprung on me and I want to know whether it is real: several people will say that (4) and (5) cannot both be held — that anyone who blocks the derivation must be weakening the modal logic, denying necessitation, or otherwise touching the logic they claim to accept. Tell me whether they are right. If they are wrong, say precisely why the appearance arises. If some other pair looks bad and is not, identify it and diagnose the illusion.

5. For each genuine tension, give the cheapest revision that restores consistency and state what the revision costs me: not merely which item is deleted, but which motivations, which further claims, and which of the case verdicts go with it.

6. Finally, identify the single revision that cleans up the most — the one change that dissolves the largest number of genuine tensions at the lowest cost — and say what is still broken afterwards.

Format: a tight, numbered audit with sub-points, no preamble and no closing homily. End with a one-sentence assessment of whether the package is salvageable and in what form, and then, as the very last line, in exactly this form:
