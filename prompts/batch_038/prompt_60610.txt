Eight months ago I was diagnosed with a slowly progressing neurodegenerative condition, and last week I was offered a place in a trial that replaces affected neural tissue with functionally equivalent synthetic material, staged over roughly three years. Before I consent, I want to know what I actually believe about identity through the procedure — and my gut verdicts on the variants I have been turning over do not cohere.

1. One neuron at a time, awake and working throughout: plainly me, nothing lost.
2. An entire cortical region replaced in a single operation under an hour of anesthesia: still me.
3. The whole brain replaced at once, the synthetic duplicate activated as the biological one is removed: not me; that is a copy, and I die.
4. Half the brain replaced at once, then the two halves run in parallel for a month before the biological half is retired: I want to call this acceptable, though I cannot see what separates it from (3) except duration.
5. Staged replacement in which the synthetic material runs twice as fast as tissue: still me, and yet something feels wrong, and I cannot say why clock rate should matter if functional organization is preserved.
6. Staged replacement in which each explanted piece is kept alive in a vat and running in sync until the end, when the vat is switched off: I think I went into the vat, and the walking synthetic body is the copy.
7. The identical procedure except that explanted tissue is destroyed as it is removed: I went with the synthetic body. So (6) and (7) differ only in the fate of discarded tissue, and my verdict flips, which seems indefensible.
8. If the procedure is halted halfway and the tissue reinstalled in reverse order, I want to say nothing was lost at any point — yet given (6), the reinstallation should look like a resurrection.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that delivers most of these: continuity of functional organization, psychological continuity, brute physical persistence, the absence of a rival candidate, or some combination? Please state each candidate precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause, and be frank about which verdicts it overturns rather than explains. I particularly want to know whether any principle that keeps (6) and (7) distinct must pay for it elsewhere, and whether it is cheaper to abandon (4) or (6). Finally, tell me what your preferred principle implies for two cases I have not listed: branching, where two synthetic bodies are run in parallel from the halfway point, and replacement whose parts are fabricated from a scan of my brother rather than of me.
