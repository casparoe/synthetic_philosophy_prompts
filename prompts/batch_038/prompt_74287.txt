Our little philosophy department finally got some money for a podcast, and because I'm the one who mentioned it at a meeting, I'm the one hosting. Next week's guest is a formal epistemologist who has spent decades defending Van Fraassen's Reflection principle — roughly, the idea that if I'm now certain my future self will have credence p in some claim, I ought to have credence p in it now too. Full disclosure, I came at this one personally rather than professionally: a few months back I signed a pile of paperwork locking in decisions about where I'd apply, what I'd work on, and a deferred offer I could take or leave, specifically because I knew a year from now I'd probably want something completely different and I trusted present-me more than future-me. Then I fell down the Reflection rabbit hole and realized a big chunk of the literature says present-me is supposed to defer to future-me, which is more or less the opposite of what I did. So I have a stake here, and the last thing I want is to spend 40 minutes being a fanboy.

Write me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give. Write as a working Bayesian epistemologist who actually knows this literature inside out, not as a science journalist doing a book tour. Before the questions, spend a short paragraph brainstorming where you think the pressure genuinely is right now and telling me which of the topics below would be a bad use of airtime — I'd rather hear that up front than after the recording. Then the questions themselves. At minimum I want: one on the motivation, i.e. what's left of the diachronic Dutch book story once people argue that violating Reflection doesn't reliably get you money-pumped, and whether the principle is a requirement of coherence or just a rule for avoiding sure loss (including the increasingly popular line that the Dutch book here only dramatizes self-doubt, not incoherence); one on the cases where you know your future self is about to be fed evidence you're confident is misleading, or where you expect to be tired, drunk, or medicated — and if the answer is to restrict the principle, press him on how you write down that restriction without it collapsing into "defer to your future self whenever your future self agrees with you"; one on forgetting and self-locating belief, where your future self seems to have strictly less information than you do; one on whether Reflection is just a special case of the general debate about higher-order evidence and deferring to experts, in which case what's distinctively left; and one on the antecedent, since I'm almost never certain what my credences will be a year out — is the principle then near-vacuous, and what would a graded or approximate version look like? End with something that turns on my own situation: if pre-commitment devices (contracts, Ulysses-style self-binding, deferring a decision to a trustee) are rational, how can they be, given that Reflection seems to say my better-informed future self should have the final word? Make that one hard to dodge with a shrug.

Please don't include a bibliography or a "further reading" list — I can't look anything up mid-conversation and I don't want to be reading titles aloud on a recording. Just the questions, tight enough that I can say them naturally out loud. And if you think two of them can be merged or one should be cut for time, tell me which.
