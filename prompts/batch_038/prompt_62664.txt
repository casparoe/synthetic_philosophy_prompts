Rules for the exchange: concede freely on technical points that the mathematics forces on you, but never on the central thesis that the content of an assertion is fixed by what would count as proving it; you may put questions to me about my proof instead of answering; speak as a colleague at a whiteboard, two or three short paragraphs per turn; stay in character until I say stop.

My opening move targets a case where I think your position simply collapses. Suppose I produce an ordinary classical proof, carried out in first-order Peano arithmetic, that every even number greater than two is a sum of two primes — a universal claim whose matrix is decidable, since the witnesses are bounded by the number itself. You accept the Gödel–Gentzen negative translation, so a classical proof of a sentence of that shape yields an intuitionistic proof of it, and for a decidable matrix you have ¬¬∀n P(n) → ∀n P(n) available outright. That means you do not merely abstain from disputing my theorem: you receive it unchanged, witnesses and all, on your own terms — and by the same reasoning you receive every Π₁ theorem of arithmetic. Since that is most of what number theorists actually prove, the disagreement either is empty throughout finitary mathematics or something substantive survives it. Which is it, and where exactly does it bite?
