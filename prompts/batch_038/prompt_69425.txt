Our logic reading group got derailed last week over Skolem's paradox and two of the others ended up in a real argument. Nell (phil of math, third year) and Ravi (set theory, second year) went five rounds while the rest of us just watched. Here's the exchange as best I reconstructed it:

1. Nell opened by saying there's no paradox at all. ZFC has a countable model, and inside it there's a set the model takes to be uncountable — but that's only because the bijection with ω that exists out in the ambient universe just isn't one of the model's sets. "Countable" isn't absolute, and calling that a paradox is like being shocked that a small town has no skyscrapers.

2. Ravi said that dissolves the contradiction but dodges the interesting part. If the sentence "there are uncountably many reals" comes out true in a structure every member of which is countable, then our set-theoretic language doesn't fix its own reference: the words can be satisfied by something nothing like what we mean by them. Showing the sentences are consistent isn't the same as showing they're determinate.

3. Nell answered that truth in a set-sized model isn't truth, full stop. The real universe of sets isn't a set, so Lowenheim-Skolem never even applies to it, and from inside it there genuinely is no bijection between ω and P(ω). So our words do mean what we take them to mean; there's just a small model that apes them from the outside.

4. Ravi's reply: then how could you ever tell whether you're standing in that universe or in the small model? Everything you'd cite as evidence — the theorems you prove, the sheer intuitive obviousness that P(ω) is big — comes out true in the countable model too, so the appeal to "the real V" is an unverifiable surplus that isn't doing any work.

5. Nell's last move: if that's the argument, it has nothing to do with countability. It's just "a first-order theory has models other than the intended one," which is equally true of group theory and arithmetic; so either he's proved something with no special bite for the infinite, or he owes an account of what uncountability adds — and the only thing it's added so far is the non-absoluteness she already conceded in move 1.
