I sit on the appraisal committee of a public agency that allocates a fixed national health budget among competing programmes, scoring each by cost per unit of benefit. Our benefit measure is currently a quality-adjusted life-year, with health-state weights drawn from surveys in which members of the general public rate conditions they have never lived in. A proposal is now before us to replace this with a life-satisfaction measure: each year of life gained or preserved would be weighted by the mean self-reported score, on a 0–10 scale, of the people actually living in the resulting condition, measured twelve months after treatment. The proposer's case is that we should stop asking bystanders to imagine other people's lives and start asking people how their own lives are going.

Two members dissent in different directions. One holds that self-reports are at best ordinal within a person and admit of no cross-person arithmetic at all, so any weighting built on them is spurious precision dressed as measurement. The other points to this round's applications. Programme A is a long-term rehabilitative service for people living with severe mobility and continence impairment; its participants report a mean satisfaction of 7.4. Programme B intervenes in people recently diagnosed with a progressive condition; its participants report 5.1 despite considerably milder functional limitation. Under the proposal B beats A comfortably. A's applicants have written in advance to say the gap reflects a difference in what the two groups compare themselves against, not a difference in how their lives are going — though they also insist their patients really are doing better than they were, and not merely reporting so.

Draft the committee's decision memo: adopt, reject, or adopt with specified modifications. Your first sentence must be the recommendation itself; then give the reasoning as the committee could put it on the public record, in terms applicants could actually answer. It must cover: whether satisfaction reports can bear the weight of cross-person arithmetic, and if so under what conditions; what adaptation to a condition does to the evidential value of a report, given that adapting can be a genuine improvement in a life and not only a measurement artifact; whether the incumbent measure is compromised by the same defect and whether that should affect the choice between them; and what we should concretely do with A and B this round, given that we cannot commission new measurement work before the next cycle in eighteen months. State any point at which we are making a value choice rather than a technical one, and give an honest, specific account of the strongest consideration on the side you do not recommend. No preamble and no restating of the question.
