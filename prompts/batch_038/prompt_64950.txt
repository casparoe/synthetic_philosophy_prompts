For twenty-four years I have kept the minutes of the board of a small private relief foundation — endowed by a single family, seven trustees, a fixed emergency fund released four times a year, and a standing choice between whatever domestic appeal and whatever overseas appeal happens to be in front of us. In all that time the board has never articulated a principle. Each decision is recorded in one line, written by three successive secretaries in three different registers, and the allocations themselves are the only evidence of what we think. This year is the foundation's twenty-fifth, the trustees have asked me to draft a short statement of the principle governing our allocations for the anniversary booklet, and the chair — who has cast the deciding vote in every contested year and whose instinct I could previously rely on without understanding it — steps down next month. The current quarter's decision falls due in nine days, so whatever statement I produce has to cover it. I took one ethics course as an undergraduate, decades ago, and I am aware that I may be pattern-matching rather than reasoning, which is why I am putting the whole record in front of you rather than my summary of it.

Our seven decisions, in order, each with the minute as written. (1) Twenty-three years ago: a hurricane on our own coast in the same month as an earthquake on the other side of the world that killed roughly three times as many people at comparable cost per household reached; the stricken country's government ran an effective response with several large international agencies on the ground. The entire fund went to the hurricane. Minute: "Our neighbours first." (2) Twenty years ago: a famine in a country whose government had collapsed and from which the major agencies had withdrawn, against a domestic appeal to rebuild the children's wing of a regional hospital — much valued, badly needed, no lives at immediate stake. The entire fund went abroad. Minute: "No one else is going." (3) Eighteen years ago: a flood in a country whose exposure to it was widely traced to a dam and irrigation scheme our own government had financed and pressed through decades earlier, against a flood at home of roughly the same scale and cost per person. The larger share went abroad, the first split we ever made. Minute: "We helped make this." (4) Fourteen years ago: a drought in the country that had taken in some forty thousand of our own people as refugees during our civil war forty years before — two of our trustees were children in those camps — against a domestic appeal of the same scale and cost. The larger share went abroad. Minute: "We owe them." (5) Eleven years ago: an appeal on behalf of co-nationals abroad, descendants of emigrants who still hold our passports and vote in our elections, after a catastrophic fire in the country they had settled in and whose government mounted a full response; against an appeal for a settlement of long-term non-citizen residents of our own largest city after a factory collapse, equal scale, where the municipal authorities had declared them ineligible for compensation. The larger share went to the residents. Minute: "They live under our laws." (6) Nine years ago: a cyclone in a neighbouring country with which we have a mutual-assistance treaty and conduct joint exercises, against an equally severe cyclone in a country with no such ties. The larger share went to the treaty partner. Minute: "Signed up for this one." (7) Six years ago: a typhoon in a country whose government had entirely dissolved and from which the agencies had withdrawn on security grounds, and in the same week a heatwave at home in which our city's emergency plan covered registered households only, leaving several thousand undocumented residents without cooling centres or water distribution. The board stood three-three with the seventh seat vacant after a trustee's death that spring, and the fund was divided exactly in half. The minute records only the arithmetic. Our present chair insists that (7) is a precedent for splitting; our treasurer insists a tied vote with a seat vacant is not a decision about anything and records only our being evenly divided. I cannot tell which of them is right, or whether it matters.

This quarter's appeals are indistinguishable in cost per person reached and roughly equal in the number of people each would cover; the fund will do one properly or both badly. Overseas: a famine in a country whose government fell in a coup two years ago — a coup we had nothing to do with, and no policy of ours contributed, so far as I can establish, to the famine itself — and from which every one of the major agencies has now withdrawn on security grounds. It is the same country as decision (4); the region now starving is largely the region that hosted our refugees, and three of our seven trustees' families were among those hosted. At home: flash flooding in our second city. The national relief scheme is generous and functioning normally, but by its terms it covers documented households only, and one of the flooded districts is a settlement of some twenty thousand long-term non-citizen residents who work here, pay taxes here, are answerable to our courts, and are excluded from the scheme altogether. What I need from you is a recommendation, not a survey: tell me what we should do — one appeal, the other, or a split, and on what terms — if we want the decision to cohere with our own record rather than with anyone's politics. Say which of the seven is the closest analog and what exactly does the work in the analogy, and whether (7) deserves the weight the chair gives it. Set out the candidate principles that could generate all seven decisions — I can see at least three or four, and I suspect some of them only fit by treating one or two of the minutes as loose talk — and for each, show how it accounts for the record or name the decision that strains it, and then say what it implies about this quarter. Tell me what principle we would be adopting with each possible choice, and what we would be committing ourselves to in some later appeal we would then find it hard to refuse. Because decision (4) involved this same country, address whether the larger share we gave it fourteen years ago discharged that debt, renewed it, or whether thinking of it as a debt of that kind is already a mistake. Finally, describe one further appeal, of a sort we could plausibly receive, that would discriminate between the principles our record leaves open. Please be explicit about how confident you are in the recommendation and in the reading of the record it rests on, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of; I would much rather be told where the analysis is thin than handed a confident-sounding allocation. I also have a hunch that the vacant seventh seat in (7) matters more than either the chair or the treasurer allows, and I cannot say why — tell me whether you agree or whether that is only my attachment to it.
