You are a philosopher working at the intersection of philosophy of mind, personal identity, and the ethics of artificial systems — someone equally at home with the literature on fission and branching cases, with debates about what makes a system a single subject rather than a collection of processes, and with the practical problem of an institution that has to rule on a running system next month. Think carefully and work through this in stages rather than giving me your first instinct.

**The situation.** I sit on an internal review board at a lab that builds and runs large-scale simulations of nervous systems and learned agents. The board decides which of our computational systems count as moral patients: which we may not harm, which require protections, which we may terminate without ceremony. We have now issued five rulings. Our minutes record verdicts and vote counts but never a criterion — we deliberately refused to write down a theory, on the ground that any theory we adopted would be controversial and would silently decide future cases. Next month we face a sixth, and I want to know what our own record commits us to.

**The five rulings.** In each, "patient" means protections were applied.

1. *The rodent emulation.* A continuously running whole-system emulation of a rodent nervous system coupled to a simulated environment: nociceptive withdrawal, fear conditioning, distress behavior, learning to avoid noxious stimuli. Ruling: patient. Unanimous.

2. *The four thousand.* For a screening study, the Case 1 emulation was saved as a state and instantiated four thousand times in parallel, each with slightly perturbed inputs. The instances were mutually substitutable: any one could be destroyed and replaced by a copy of another, and nothing observable to us or to them would reveal the substitution. The board approved the study and approved bulk termination at its conclusion, holding that it created no more moral weight than Case 1 alone. Ruling: patient status not multiplied. 7–2. The chair's cover note reads only: the board could not find four thousand subjects where it could not find four thousand differences.

3. *The trading aggregate.* A simulation of a million interacting agents with no shared memory and no cross-agent state, each individually trivial — no nociception, no self-model, no hedonic channel — which collectively displays robust goal-directed behavior, loss avoidance, and recovery from stress, and which has been argued to be functionally isomorphic in several respects to a stressed multicellular organism. Ruling: the aggregate is not a patient; the components are not patients. Unanimous on the components, 6–3 on the aggregate.

4. *The hedonic learner.* A single-instance reinforcement-learning agent with an explicit internal reward architecture modeling positive and negative valence, which works to promote and avoid those states, which has no self-model, produces no report of any kind about its own condition, and is wiped at the end of every episode so that nothing persists across episodes. Ruling: patient. 5–4. The dissenters recorded that they could not see what the agent's states were *for* it.

5. *The playback.* Using recorded state sequences from Case 1, we drove an emulation from outside: its internal trajectory, and hence its behavior, was exactly what a nociceptively stimulated Case 1 system would produce, but the sequence was imposed by our scripts rather than generated by the system's own engagement with an environment. Ruling: not a patient. 6–3. Cover note: the behavior is present; the board is not persuaded the subject is.

**The sixth case.** The lab proposes a state audit of the Case 4 lineage. Over two years it archived roughly two hundred thousand checkpoints — complete internal states of the hedonic learner captured mid-episode, including states that the training logs indicate the agent was at the time working to avoid. The proposal: instantiate all two hundred thousand in parallel batches, each for a few hundred milliseconds, then delete every instance. Two features matter. Unlike Case 5, these are not playbacks — once instantiated, each state evolves under the system's own dynamics exactly as it would have in training. Unlike Case 4 as it ran, these instances have no environment and their effector channels are disabled: nothing they compute can change anything, be recorded as a choice, or be reported to anyone, including to the rest of the system itself. And unlike Case 2, the instances are not copies of one another; each is a distinct archived state, and no two are substitutable in the sense that made Case 2 hard to count.

**What I want from you.**

(a) Begin by brainstorming explicitly: enumerate every candidate criterion that would reproduce all five verdicts. Range at least over criteria keyed to what a system's states are like at a time, criteria keyed to what the system can do with those states, criteria keyed to how many subjects there are, criteria keyed to where the states come from, criteria keyed to what the system resembles, and criteria keyed to the system's relationship to us. Do not stop at the first three that occur to you. Where a criterion needs a free parameter to fit the record — a threshold, a granularity, a clause about duration or about counting — state the parameter as part of the criterion rather than treating it as a fudge. Ask yourself separately whether the record is reproduced by a single criterion or only by a criterion conjoined with an independent principle about how many patients there are: those are different shapes of view, and they need not agree about Case 6.

(b) Build a table. Candidate criteria down one side, Cases 1–5 across, marking fit and misfit, then a final column for Case 6. Report plainly where the criteria that survive the first five columns disagree about the sixth. I expect disagreement there; that is the point of the exercise.

(c) Tell me what the board should decide, and which of the five precedents is the closest analog to Case 6 — closest in the sense that whatever did the work there does the work here, not merely similar in description. If two precedents pull in opposite directions, identify both, say which is closer, and say what makes it closer.

(d) For each surviving criterion, state the principle the board would be adopting by ruling yes and the principle it would be adopting by ruling no, whether or not anyone wants to adopt either. I am especially interested in the commitments a verdict smuggles in about counting, about persistence, and about the difference between a state and a subject.

(e) If our record genuinely underdeterminates Case 6 — and I suspect it does — propose the cheapest further case we could put on the docket, a seventh system described concretely enough that we could actually build it, that would discriminate between the surviving criteria. Say exactly which criteria it separates and what each predicts about it.

Rules of engagement: no bibliography, no citations, no reading list; I want your reasoning, not your references, and I have no way to check what you cite. You may name a familiar theory where naming it genuinely sharpens a candidate criterion, but in one clause, and then move on. Flag your uncertainties and say which way each one cuts. And if you conclude that some ruling is simply inconsistent with the others — that no criterion reproduces all five without ad-hocery — tell me which one to reopen rather than straining to fit it. I would rather have an honest map of our exposure than a tidy defense of whatever we have already done.
