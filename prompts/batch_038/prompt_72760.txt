I'm writing the final for a small upper-level seminar on classical Indian epistemology — nine students, philosophy majors, one semester of Sanskrit between them, and theyve read a fair bit of the secondary literature on the Nyāya school. I need one essay question, answerable in 60 minutes closed book, worth 25 points, plus a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme.

The topic is Nyāya's insistence that testimony (śabda) is a pramāṇa in its own right rather than a package deal built out of the two that even its opponents grant, perception and inference. What I don't want is a question that can be answered by listing and defining the four pramāṇas, or by mapping the whole dispute onto Hume versus Reid and declaring Nyāya the Reid side. The pressure I want it to turn on is the reductionist dilemma we spent two weeks with: a belief you form from being told something is either backed by independent grounds — you've noticed the speaker's track record, what they said coheres with what you can check yourself, they had no motive to deceive you — in which case it looks like an inference about the speaker rather than anything testimony-shaped, or it isn't backed by grounds at all, in which case it's just trust and you don't have knowledge. Nyāya has to say there's a third thing here: that understanding what someone says to you produces knowledge directly, with no inferential step, and we drilled the machinery for that — mutual expectancy between the words, semantic fitness, contiguity of utterance. But the hearer still has to stand in some right relation to the speaker's reliablity, and I want the question to force students to say what that relation could be without either collapsing back into inference or going circular, since how you mostly find out who's trustworthy is by being told.

There's an escape route they'll reach for: no positive warrant for trusting the speaker is needed, only the absence of any awareness of them as unreliable or as having something to gain by deceiving you. I want the marking scheme to give credit to students who press on whether that default is a genuine discovery about testimony or just the reductionist's point redescribed — and to students who notice that this tradition's most confident case for testimony's autonomy is built on authorless scripture, which is a very different setting from a human speaker you'd have to vet.

Please make the question self-contained: no reliance on a passage they'd have to recall word for word, and no citations in the model answer either, since they write from memory. For the marking scheme, break the 25 points down into the specific moves a first-class answer makes, with the points for each, and say for each move the particular mistake that should cost them. Then flag two or three habits of the strong-but-not-excellent answer — for instance treating a pramāṇa as though it were a mental faculty or a cognitive module rather than a way of getting to know something that can't be gotten any other way. American English throughout, please.
