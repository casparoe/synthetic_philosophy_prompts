I write about science and ideas for a magazine, mostly physics and the arguments around it. Last week I was on a panel about whether dark matter research is in crisis, and afterwards two of the other people on it kept going in the bar for another hour while I sat there taking notes, partly because it was good and partly because I figured it would make a decent sidebar. To be clear I was a bystander — I didn't take a side, and I've tried not to write this up in a way that hints at one. I just want to know who's right about what.

The two people: Nour is a philosopher of physics and an unrepentant Lakatosian. Denis does solar system dynamics and writes a fair bit of history of astronomy.

They were fighting over the two Le Verrier moves. 1846: he saves Newtonian gravitation from the unexplained irregularities in Uranus's orbit by postulating an unseen perturbing planet, predicts roughly where to look, and Galle finds Neptune within about a degree of that spot. 1859–60: he tries the identical trick on the extra bit of perihelion advance in Mercury's orbit that Newton can't account for, and postulates an unseen body inside Mercury's orbit — Vulcan. A French amateur named Lescarbault claims a transit in March 1859; it never repeats convincingly; in 1915 general relativity explains the anomaly with no planet at all and Vulcan dies.

Their exchange, roughly as it happened:

(1) Nour: "Everyone says the progressive-versus-degenerating distinction can only be applied with hindsight. Neptune and Vulcan disprove that. Same move, same man, twice: patch the protective belt to save the core. One was a progressive problemshift and one was degenerating, and you did not need to wait fifty years to know which. The Neptune patch stuck its neck out — a specific patch of sky, checkable in a night, able to fail. The Vulcan patch absorbed an anomaly with an open-ended smear: a planet or else a diffuse belt, anywhere inside Mercury's orbit, visible only in transit against the glare of the sun. That's the difference between an adjustment with excess empirical content and a sophistical one, and it was visible in 1860."

(2) Denis: "Except it wasn't visible in 1860, and that's the whole problem. In 1860 Le Verrier is the man who just pulled off the most spectacular prediction in the history of astronomy, using the same method on the same kind of data. Vulcan was novel, it was risky, it was made in advance of any confirming observation, and it came with the best inductive track record on the market. Everything in your toolkit licenses it. It failed. That's the only difference. 'Degenerating' in your mouth is just 'wrong,' said after the fact."

(3) Nour: "Ad hocness was never about truth for Lakatos, its about whether the patch has content of its own that could get corroborated independently of the anomaly it was invented for. The Neptune patch had that in spades. The Vulcan patch as actually formulated didn't — a disjunction between a planet and a belt, with no ceiling on how faint or how spread out the culprit is allowed to be, isn't a prediction, it's a hedge. I'd say even if Vulcan had turned up, the hypothesis as stated was a bad one."

(4) Denis: "Careful, because that's not Lakatos's criterion any more, that's just 'make your auxiliaries specific and independently checkable,' which every philosopher of science alive would sign. And it doesn't survive contact with your own best example. Adams and Le Verrier disagreed about where Neptune was and how heavy it was; Galle found it about a degree off Le Verrier's prediction; and the mass it turned out to have was lower than either of them had inferred. So the specific-and-checkable move was imprecise, partly lucky, and only got graded a triumph because it happened to land inside the search field. You're taking two versions of the same move that differ in degree and scoring them as if they differ in kind."

(5) Nour: "Lakatos never asked for a true auxiliary. He asked for a theoretically progressive shift that also picks up some empirical corroboration. Neptune got it. Vulcan didn't, over seventy years of searches and eclipse expeditions. And what eventually displaced Newtonian gravity at Mercury wasn't Vulcan's non-appearance — it was a rival programme with a different hard core that explained the anomaly and then threw up further novel facts. That is exactly the Lakatosian picture: you don't junk a programme when a patch fails, you junk it when something better arrives."

(6) Denis: "Which is move (1) surrendered in a nice dress. On your own account the appraisal of Vulcan could only be rendered in the late 1910s, by reference to a different programme, sixty years on. So no, you couldn't tell at the time. And it's worse than slow — it's unstable, because the anomalies themselves move. Later, once Voyager 2 pinned down Neptune's mass properly, most of the leftover unexplained residuals in Uranus's orbit just evaporated, which means part of what made the 1846 shift look so beautifully progressive was a modelling error in the data. If the anomaly that licenses the label can be revised out from under you decades later, the label isn't delayed, it's wobbling."

(7) Nour: "That revision helps me, not you. The programme kept improving its own models of its own data without anybody laying a finger on the core — that's the protective belt doing exactly the job its there for. And the instability you're describing is just the generic fallibility of evidence, you've got it too, it isn't a special defect of my framework."

(8) Denis: "Then here's where we land. Lakatos is right that one failed prediction doesn't kill a theory, and right that tenacity and heuristics matter. But 'progressive' and 'degenerating' don't discriminate anything that a blunter rule doesn't discriminate sooner and better: prefer auxiliaries that make specific, independently checkable predictions, and switch when a rival explains more with fewer fudges. I don't need the research-programme vocabulary to do my dynamics or to write my history."

I asked each of them for one sentence for the sidebar and they both just repeated themselves, so no progress there.
