I'm a staff software engineer on a growth team at a consumer app company. My training is entirely in systems and applied ML — I have no formal philosophy background — so I'd ask you to repair any conceptual framing below that I've gotten wrong, while still answering the question I'm actually asking.

Here is the situation. My team ships behavioral interventions: notification copy, default settings, the ordering of feed items, streak counters, the timing of a re-engagement email. We evaluate each one by measured lift in an A/B test, and we ship whatever wins. I've become uneasy that this is simply manipulation done with good instrumentation, so I've been applying a test of my own devising, which I'll call the mechanism-disclosure test:

An intervention is manipulative just in case the user, if shown a complete and accurate description of the causal mechanism by which the intervention changes their behavior — including the fact that we selected this mechanism because it outperformed alternatives in testing — would withdraw their endorsement of being subjected to it.

Three things about the test trouble me, and they are what I want the argument to be about.

First, it appears to license interventions that look plainly manipulative. A loss-framed push notification ("your unsaved draft will be deleted in 24 hours") operates through loss aversion. A substantial fraction of users, once the mechanism is explained to them, say they endorse it anyway, because it gets them to do something they independently want to do. Under my test, that is legitimate persuasion, and the fact that it works better on the users who are most loss-averse seems to make no difference.

Second, it appears to condemn interventions that look benign. Switching a privacy-protective feature from opt-in to opt-out works through status-quo bias. If I fully explain status-quo bias to a user and note that we chose the default precisely because it exploits that bias, many users will say they don't want to be steered by it, even toward an outcome they'd pick under full information. The test then flags the change as manipulation.

Third — and this is the one I find hardest to articulate — the test seems to be sensitive to the level of description at which I disclose the mechanism, without anything in the test telling me which level is the right one. "We ranked these items by predicted dwell time" and "we ranked these items to raise the probability that you open the app three times today" can both be true and complete descriptions of the same ranking system, and users endorse the first far more readily than the second. So the test's verdict appears to depend on a descriptive choice I make, rather than on anything about the intervention.

Given all of this, here is what I want you to do. Argue that the mechanism-disclosure test is not merely miscalibrated at the margins but is aimed at the wrong target: that what makes an intervention manipulative is not a fact about the user's hypothetical reflective attitude toward a disclosed mechanism, but a fact about how the intervention engages the user's own capacities for deliberation and for forming and revising their ends. Make the strongest positive case you can for that claim. Please use my three failure modes as your main leverage rather than restating them, and show specifically how each of them is explained better by your account than by the disclosure test. Then tell me what the correct criterion should actually look like — stated precisely enough that I could argue about whether a given intervention satisfies it — what it implies for the growth-team practices I described, including whether A/B testing itself is part of the problem or merely how the problem becomes visible, and what the most serious objection to your account is and how you would answer it. If you believe my framing of the position is off in a way that changes the argument, note that briefly and then deliver the argument as specified.
