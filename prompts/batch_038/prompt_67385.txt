I help put together a small end-of-term issue for our faculty's law-and-religion review (student run, mostly read by people who have already sat through a whole semester of church-and-state cases), and I want to commission one deliberately silly piece that still gets the comparative machinery right. What I'm after is a parody of a management-consulting deliverable — a "customer retention and churn reduction playbook," roughly 850 words, with the usual headings, bullet points, one small comparison table, and a couple of smug corporate footnotes — written by an invented consultancy for an invented umbrella body of European churches that has finally agreed to think of religious belonging as a subscription product. Deadpan throughout, no winking, and the target is the practice and the vocabulary rather than any actual faith: the joke should be that once you describe church membership as recurring revenue, the constitutional arrangements of five different countries start to look like billing options, and the consultants are delighted by this and never once notice that something might be wrong with it.

Here are the markets I want them to cover, because the humour only works if the details are right: Germany as the flagship account, where the church levies a surcharge on income tax (nine percent in most Länder, eight in Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg), the state tax office collects it for a handling fee, membership is stored in a federal database that employers pull from for payroll withholding, and cancelling requires showing up in person at a state authority — not a church one — and usually paying a fee, with the wrinkle that a fourteen-year-old can cancel without parental consent under the old law on the religious upbringing of children; also work in the special church tax for mixed-faith marriages, where a member spouse with little or no income of their own gets assessed by reference to the household's joint income, which opponents describe as taxing the partner who never joined. Then Italy as the best-performing market, which should be the piece's darkest beat played completely straight: there is no membership list because the tax return is the list — taxpayers can direct eight per mille of their income tax to a recognised confession or to the state, and if they tick nothing at all their share gets redistributed pro rata among those who did tick, so non-choice is allocated as if it were choice, and the consultants should present this as a beautiful default and be visibly proud of the numbers. Then England as the market where churn is literally unmeasurable — no state collection, no register of members, no legal act of leaving, baptism recorded and unrepeatable — with a frustrated section where they try to build a KPI anyway out of electoral roll figures and attendance. Then France as the market where no CRM can be built at all because collecting data on religious affiliation is prohibited, except for one legacy account in Alsace-Moselle that they cannot migrate, where the Napoleonic-era arrangement still stands and the Interior Ministry pays the salaries of Catholic, Lutheran and Reformed ministers and rabbis. Denmark, Finland and Sweden can get a throwaway paragraph as the long tail with small per-cent fees.

A few constraints so this doesn't turn into a listicle. The comedy has to come from the register colliding with the subject matter — LTV, onboarding, offboarding, win-back campaigns, NPS, applied to conscience, sacraments and excommunication — and from the fact that the consultants are internally consistent: they must never break character, never concede that a subscription you can only exit by booking a municipal appointment might not really be a subscription, and never use the word irony about themselves. Include one appendix box where a junior analyst raises the obvious objection — something like whether treating silence as assent is compatible with freedom of religion — in careful corporate language, and gets answered in a single line that technically addresses nothing. Don't invent quotations from real bishops, real officials or real judgments, and don't give case names or numbers; everything on the consultancy side is made up, and if you're not sure of an exact rate or fee somewhere, describe the mechanism instead of naming a figure. No bibliography, obviously. What I want at the end is that someone who's taken the course laughs in the right places and quietly registers the actual question — at what point does state administrative machinery turn belief into a default setting — without the piece ever saying so.
