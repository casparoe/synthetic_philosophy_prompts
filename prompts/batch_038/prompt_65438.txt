I write for the ideas and science desk of a broadsheet and I'm putting together a long feature on Project Cybersyn — the Allende government's 1971–73 attempt to run Chile's growing nationalised sector through a cybernetic feedback network, with the British management theorist Stafford Beer as its guru. I've got the history, so please don't spend words re-establishing it, and no sources, footnotes or reading lists — the reader has never heard of Oskar Lange and doesn't need to be pointed at a library.

The frame I'm using is the socialist calculation debate. Mises in 1920: without private ownership of the means of production there are no genuine prices for capital goods, so there is literally nothing to calculate with. Hayek's later inflection: the real problem is knowledge — dispersed, local, frequently inarticulable knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place — and prices are the only mechanism that ever managed to gather it up. Lange's answer: a planning board can simulate the whole business, setting trial prices for capital goods and instructing managers to follow marginal-cost rules, a Walrasian tâtonnement run by committee. There's also the much-repeated Lange line to the effect that the market was only a computing device from before electronic computers existed; I won't put it in quote marks, but it's in the back of my head and probably in the piece.

The Cybersyn facts I'll be leaning on, so you can take them as given: four modules — Cybernet, the telex network linking enterprises to Santiago (usually cited at around 500 machines, though the number is disputed, and only roughly twenty factories were ever actually modelled and connected); Cyberstride, the statistical monitoring software that flagged a factory only when an indicator moved outside an acceptable band; CHECO, the Chilean economic simulator; and the operations room, hexagonal, seven white fibreglass chairs with control buttons in the armrests and screens on the walls. Beer's design included an algedonic channel — if a problem wasn't fixed at one level within a set time, it jumped the hierarchy and reached the top. Fernando Flores at the state development agency CORFO brought Beer in. In October 1972, during the national truck owners' strike, the government used the telex network to route the rump of lorries whose owners hadn't joined the stoppage; industrial output still fell by something like a tenth that month. The project ended with the coup of 11 September 1973, and the military destroyed the opsroom.

What I want from you is the argument, and specifically these moves. Treat Beer's viable system model as a candidate *fourth* position in the debate — attenuation, amplification, recursion, real-time variety management — and tell me whether it's genuinely a different answer to Mises and Hayek or Lange with better plumbing. Does a Cyberstride alarm threshold do the epistemic work Hayek assigns to a price, or only the logistical work the debate was never about? Then take October 1972 seriously as the test case: it was an allocation-under-fixed-prices emergency in a country that still had money, still traded copper at world prices, and was never in the position Mises described — so did Cybersyn engage the calculation challenge at all, or slip past it? Say plainly what the strike's partial success licenses and what it doesn't, and don't let the romance of the opsroom do any argumentative work for you.

Sections, in this order: **The setup** (~150 words — who the players were and what each theory says they faced); **Four positions, four machines** (~400 words — the mapping, including where Beer's model sits and where it's just Lange relabelled); **October 1972** (~350 words — the episode as each theory predicts it, what happened, what it entitles us to conclude); **Where the theories go quiet** (~300 words — features of Cybersyn none of the three positions can describe, including the workers-modelling-their-own-factories ambition and the fact that only twenty plants got connected); **Verdict** (~250 words — does the case confirm, embarrass or simply evade the calculation debate, and which side, if any, has the better of it now); **Three pull quotes** (~50 words total, no more than fifteen words each, the kind of thing I could set in the margin).

One last thing: I'm wary of the two easy endings here — the one where Cybersyn is a heroic near-miss undone by the coup, and the one where it's a proof that Hayek was right all along. Its a seperate question whether the episode can support either, and I'd rather you showed me why it can't than pick a side for effect.
