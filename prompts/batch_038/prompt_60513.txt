I direct a four-person forecasting unit inside a national disaster-preparedness agency. We maintain a rolling panel of roughly forty forward-looking questions — whether a named river gauge will exceed a specified level by a specified date, whether a vaccination campaign will reach a coverage threshold, whether a particular hazard will be escalated to a national emergency declaration — each of which stays on the panel for six to eighteen months and is re-assessed by the same analyst every quarter. Our house rule, inherited from a predecessor and never justified, is that the published figure for a continuing question should be a weighted blend of two quantities: the analyst's present estimate, and the analyst's own prediction of what she will estimate next quarter, once the scheduled data releases have arrived. The weight is currently set by feel, and the results are inconsistent across analysts.

I want that rule either grounded or replaced. The relevant material, as I understand it, is van Fraassen's Reflection principle of 1984: roughly, your present credence in a proposition, conditional on the hypothesis that your own later credence in it will be r, should itself be r — supported by a diachronic Dutch book argument, and attended by the familiar worry that the principle misfires when you expect the later belief to be produced by something other than evidence.

Please write me a procedure that a junior analyst with ordinary probability training and no philosophy background can run in about thirty minutes per question, ahead of each quarterly publication, and that yields either a single number or a bounded range for me to sign off.

The procedure must do the following.

1. State precisely what the principle licenses in this setting and what it does not. In particular, it constrains a credence conditional on a hypothesis about a later credence, not an unconditional one, so an analyst who is unsure what her next number will be must average over hypotheses rather than adopt the most likely single one. A checklist that collapses that distinction would be worse than the present practice.

2. Give the steps in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, the point at which the procedure halts and simply publishes the analyst's present estimate, and the point at which it produces a figure requiring my signature.

3. Set out explicitly the conditions under which deferral is appropriate and those under which it breaks: whether the analyst expects to conditionalize on the incoming evidence; whether she expects the shift in her credence to have a non-evidential source, such as fatigue at the end of a deployment cycle, a funder's public commitment to a target, a reassignment, or a change in what counts internally as a good answer; whether a later credence is even well defined, given turnover and the possibility that the question is handed to a colleague; and whether the proposition is one whose content shifts with the analyst's own situation, so that what she will later be assessing is not quite what she is assessing now.

4. Say what to do, and what to record instead, when an analyst cannot produce a usable prediction of her own later credence, which is common.

5. Identify which technical distinctions must survive into the checklist and which can be dropped without distorting the principle, and mark honestly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

6. Run the procedure on three cases. First: an analyst is confident she will conditionalize properly on a national survey released next month; her present figure is 0.35 and she predicts she will say 0.6. I want the answer stated plainly, including if it tells me to publish 0.6 today even though present evidence supports 0.35 — and I want to know whether anything in our circumstances, such as a statutory duty to report what the current evidence supports, blocks that recommendation or merely sits alongside it. Second: our largest funder has publicly announced a target, and the analyst expects her own next figure to have drifted toward it for reasons she would not defend as evidential; present 0.5, predicted 0.75. Third: the analyst will be on secondment and the question will be picked up by a colleague; she can predict what the unit will say, but there is no well-defined later credence of hers to defer to.

7. Say where the procedure will misfire and what it cannot settle. Address in particular whether any analogous deferral is licensed on the value side: we also issue recommendations, and there is a standing temptation to run the same logic on what our future selves will think we ought to want.
