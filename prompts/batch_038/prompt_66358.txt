Neither of us budged, and I suspect we both regret the hour we went to bed. I want you to set that argument aside entirely, because it is beside the point of what I am asking: what follows is an exercise in exegesis and evaluation, not a referendum on the thesis. I am the teaching assistant for a third-year course on the philosophy of action. In week five the students read Bernard Williams's essay on internal and external reasons — the one collected in *Moral Luck* — and in section we worked through the case Williams borrows about the young man from a family with a long military tradition, whose relatives insist that he has a reason to join the army. They then had to complete the following short-essay question, worth fifteen points.

**Question.** Nadia has just inherited a substantial sum. She has no interest in philanthropy as such: appeals to the scale of others' need leave her unmoved, and she has no general disposition of sympathy toward strangers. She is, however, intensely loyal to a small number of particular people, and she once flew across the country at her own expense to sit with a former laboratory technician she barely knew during the last week of his life, because she had been told his story and could not get it out of her head. She says she has never been able to refuse anyone whose face and circumstances she has actually taken in. Her brother tells her that she has a reason to give a substantial part of the money away, and he insists that this holds whether or not anything in her psychology happens to line up with it.

(i) Characterise what the brother is asserting, in Williams's terms, and state what Williams's verdict on that assertion is. (4 points)
(ii) Explain what would have to be the case for the brother's assertion to come out true on Williams's account, and assess whether the case as described makes that likely or unlikely. (6 points)
(iii) Say what, if anything, survives for the brother's reproach if his assertion fails in the way you have described. (5 points)

**Candidate 34's answer.**

(i) The brother is making an external reason statement. For Williams, a claim that A has a reason to φ is true only if some element of A's subjective motivational set — the S — would be advanced by φ-ing, where that element may be reached by sound deliberation from what is already in S. An external reason statement is one that is supposed to be true whether or not anything in S is engaged. Williams's argument against such statements runs through explanation: reasons have to be able to explain action, and nothing wholly detached from an agent's motivations can explain that agent's acting, so external reason statements are not merely unsupported but false. It is worth being careful here, because Williams's charge is not that the brother has said something meaningless or conceptually confused; that would be a much stronger claim, and he does not need it. Falsehood is enough.

(ii) S is not simply Nadia's occurrent desires. It includes her projects, her loyalties, her dispositions of evaluation and her patterns of emotional reaction, and the operative question is counterfactual: whether a sound deliberative route from her actual S would bring her to be motivated to give. Two consequences follow, and I think both are costly for the view. The first is that the word *sound* is carrying weight it cannot bear. Williams tells us the route has to be a rational one, but he gives us no independent handle on which routes those are beyond the fact that they terminate in motivation. A conditioning programme, a pharmaceutical intervention, or a sufficiently well-designed fundraising campaign would move Nadia from her actual S to giving just as reliably as any argument she could follow, and nothing in the account marks the difference between her being reasoned into a motive and her having one installed in her. If every route counts, then the internal/external distinction reduces to a claim about whichever desires the agent happens to end up with, which is Humeanism with the rough edges filed off. The second is that, on the account as stated, whether Nadia has a reason turns on a psychological prediction about her that no amount of moral argument can settle.

(iii) Something does survive, and this is where the view is less deflationary than it looks. Williams does not think criticism requires external reasons: an internal reason statement can ground reproach when the agent would have been moved had she deliberated soundly from her own S, and the reproach then points at a failure in her deliberation rather than at a free-floating normative fact. So the brother is not simply expressing disapproval. But in this case the reproach has nothing to fasten onto. Nadia's disposition is keyed to particular people whose faces and circumstances she has taken in, and giving away a large sum to relieve need she will never encounter is not an exercise of that disposition but a substitution for it; no amount of vivid acquaintance with one stranger's story generates a reason to transfer money to strangers generally. What the brother can do is change the presentation — put her in a room with specific recipients — and if that moves her, he will have altered her S, not discovered a reason that was already there. This is also why Williams's position is often described as internalist in the metaethical sense as well: on his view a sincere judgment that one ought to φ carries its own motivation with it, so the brother's only real strategy is to get Nadia to endorse the judgment, at which point the motive arrives of its own accord.
