I direct the standards division of a state environmental health agency. Roughly thirty times a year we decide whether to permit a facility, a stack configuration, a discharge limit, or a remediation plan, and in most of those decisions the competing options carry close to the same aggregate expected health burden statewide while distributing it very differently: a few hundred households near a site absorb a large share, or the burden is thinned across a large population so that no individual's expected increment is large. Our analysts score options by total expected cases averted. That score has now twice produced a decision the commission reversed after hearing from residents who had been told, in effect, that their losses were outweighed by gains accruing to people they will never meet.

I want a decision procedure my staff can run on any such proposal without training in moral philosophy: an ordered set of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which the procedure terminates in a refusal rather than a ranking, and the points at which it must stop and hand the question to the commission because what remains is a matter of values we are not entitled to settle. It should encode the force of the separateness of persons objection to aggregation — the thought that a trade-off legitimate inside a single life, where the same person bears a cost and later enjoys the benefit, does not thereby carry over to a trade between different people in which one is made worse off so that another is made better off — but it should do so in operational terms a permit writer can apply to a spreadsheet. Tell me plainly which parts of that objection survive the translation and which are lost in it.

In particular the procedure must treat at least the following configurations differently wherever they deserve different treatment, and must say where the boundaries between them are not fixed: a cost and a benefit falling within the same life over time, as when a household accepts a decade of construction disruption for a permanently cleaner aquifer beneath its own property; a cost and a benefit falling to different people where the assignment among them is settled only by a chance process that all of them face symmetrically and before any of them is identified; and a cost and a benefit falling to different people where who bears the cost is already fixed and known at the time of decision. Our present scoring collapses all three into one number. If your procedure still collapses any of them, say which one and why that is defensible.

I also want it to handle a feature of our work that the standard formulations of the objection do not anticipate. We keep a cumulative ledger, so the same households appear across several permits over a decade, and a household near the top of that ledger receives an increment that is small in isolation but arrives on top of a history of prior increments it neither chose nor can exit. The procedure should say whether and how the ledger changes the analysis, and should not pretend that this reduces to an ordinary question about how much total harm is permissible.

Then run it on three proposals, all modeled to the same statewide burden:

1. A wastewater treatment plant upgrade whose odor, noise, and truck traffic fall on the roughly seven hundred households within a mile of the site, recommended because it is the cheapest option overall and the modeling shows the largest net health gain.
2. A solvent-contamination remediation in which a lottery among five candidate parcels selects the neighborhood that receives the treatment facility, the lottery drawn before anyone knows their parcel, with the drawn site's residents compensated at an amount the commission sets after the draw.
3. A decision to raise a permitted stack so that modeled concentrations near the plant fall below any threshold at which health effects can be detected, while a much wider region receives a small measurable increase in deposition, the affected population being large, dispersed, and not identifiable in advance.

For at least one of these the procedure should reach a conclusion my division will not like; state it directly rather than softening it. Mark every step at which what is presented as procedure is really a judgment call in disguise, and list the questions the procedure cannot answer at all — how much dispersion is enough, whether consent or compensation transforms an otherwise impermissible loss into an acceptable one, what we owe to people who do not yet exist and whose exposure is fixed by which option we choose.

Constraints: the whole thing must be runnable in a ninety-minute review session by staff who are fluent in exposure modeling and cost estimation and have no philosophy; every threshold must be stateable in a sentence a resident can follow, because the output has to survive a public hearing; and because the sign-off structure is a staff recommendation followed by a commission vote, the procedure must make explicit which questions are ours to answer and which are not.
