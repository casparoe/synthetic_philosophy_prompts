I need to write a speech-occasion prompt in which a philosophical distinction does real structural work. I'll use the wrong-kind-of-reasons problem, set in a fully invented occasion so nothing depends on real sources, and I won't name the technical distinction or any philosopher — no verbatim quotes, titles, or dates that would need verification.

I am writing to request a speech: the citation I must deliver at the spring awards banquet of the public high school where I teach history. I have taught there nineteen years. The honoree, Ray Delgado, has run the building's facilities for thirty-one years and retires in June. There will be about three hundred people in the room: students and their parents, the staff, Ray's wife Marta and their two grown sons, and, at the front table, three members of the school board.

The award is new in a way nobody in that room needs explained. In the autumn the board voted to contract out custodial and maintenance work; the students staged a walkout; the board reversed itself in December; and when the student council proposed a new annual prize for service to the school, Ray's name was the only name anybody said. He will be its first recipient. Everyone present knows this, including the three board members, who voted for the contract in good conscience and are not villains.

Here is the shape of my problem, and I want the speech built around it rather than decorated with it. Reasons come in kinds. Some of the facts in play are reasons to hold a banquet, or reasons to hand Ray a plaque, or reasons why certain sentences would go down well tonight; only some of them are reasons why Ray is admirable; and a citation may rest only on the last sort. That distinction has a name and a literature, and neither the name nor the literature may appear. It must instead do the structural work — deciding what may be said, what must be left out, and in what order — while remaining invisible to the students' tables, to whom the whole thing should sound like gratitude. To someone who knows the distinction, it should be evident that the sorting has been done, and done correctly.

The student council has given me a draft citation resting on three items: the arithmetic of five thousand-odd mornings on which Ray unlocked the building at 5:40; the eleven hundred student signatures on the petition supporting the award; and the claim that in thirty-one years he was never once heard to complain. I am not obliged to keep any of the three, and I do not believe all three can be kept. For each, decide whether it belongs in the mouth of one man praising another, or whether it belongs only to the evening; then make that decision audible in what the speech actually says, without arguing it.

Two further facts are not in the draft, and they pull against it. Ray has told colleagues more than once that he would have left for better money years ago if he had had the nerve, and that the pension is what held him. Marta has said for years that the job took more out of him than anyone at the school ever noticed. A speech that keeps the third item in the draft cannot honestly keep either of these. My judgment is that the speech should carry both of these and let the third go — but you must make the loss leave no hole.

Constraints on manner and matter. Five to six minutes, roughly seven hundred words, addressed to Ray in the second person at least once, and ending with the raising of a glass. Nothing false; nothing that ambushes the front table; nothing that lets the students hear their walkout praised as the point of the evening, since the evening is not about the walkout, and yet nothing that pretends the walkout did not happen. Confine yourself to the facts I have given me here: if you need a concrete image, build it out of these, and invent no events. It must land as affection. If it reads as an audit of the reasons in the room, I have failed, and Ray, who is not a philosopher, will be embarrassed rather than honored. Give me the speech and nothing else — no preface, no account of how you disposed of the draft's three items.
