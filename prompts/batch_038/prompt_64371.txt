I build preference-inference systems — inverse reinforcement learning and reward modeling, in our jargon — and before I read another proposal I want the specification rather than the proposals. Fix the purpose as follows: the output must be a reward function that some policy will actually optimize against at scale, so "good enough to beat a baseline on a benchmark" is not the standard of adequacy. Give me the requirements any method for reading preferences off behavior has to meet, each with the case or consideration behind it: the phenomena it has to explain, the distinctions it must not collapse, the facts about how far behavior pins a reward down that it has to respect, and the failure modes it must be able to diagnose instead of quietly absorbing.

Three candidate requirements of my own, which I want you to assess rather than accept: (a) the method must be able to report that the data does not settle a given comparison, instead of emitting a confident ordering; (b) whatever ordering it does emit must break ties everywhere, because the optimizer will find every tie; (c) it must not treat each observed choice as an endorsement, since people choose under time pressure, manipulative interfaces, and their own weakness of will.

Say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really the commitments of one camp, which single requirement eliminates the largest share of existing approaches, and — this is the part I most want — whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect (a) and (b) are; if so, give the argument rather than the verdict. A few lines scoring the standard approaches against the final list is welcome, but keep the weight on the list.
