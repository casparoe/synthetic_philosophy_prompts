I am running a ninety-minute evening session for a local citizens' assembly — about forty people, several of whom sit on town boards, none with any political theory background — on the boundary problem: the question of who is entitled to constitute the demos, and why that question cannot itself be settled by a democratic vote without circularity. I want a plan for the explanation, not the explanation. Tell me the order the pieces must go in and what depends on what; my instinct is that the circularity only becomes visible once the room already cares about a concrete case, but say so if that is backwards. Tell me which single case should carry the whole session — I had been assuming a secession referendum, though what our assembly is actually debating is whether long-resident non-citizens should get the vote in municipal elections — and what that case must have and must lack in order to do the work rather than hijack the discussion into immigration policy. Then map the misconceptions: that the answer is obviously "everyone affected should have a say," that a court or an international body can settle the question without raising the identical problem one level up, and that this is a technical question about voter registration rather than a limit on democratic legitimacy. For each, say precisely where in the sequence it should be headed off, and whether it should be raised by me or left to surface from the room. Finish with one question I can put to the assembly that would show me they have understood the problem rather than arrived at a proposal for dissolving it, and with an explicit list of what I should decline to get into at this level and announce as declined.
