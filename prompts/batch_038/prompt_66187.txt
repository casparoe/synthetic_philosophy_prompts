I have spent the last few months, rather irresponsibly, writing down my own verdicts on decision problems before looking at what the textbooks say about them, and the list has now reached a point where I can no longer tell whether it contains a coherent view or merely a set of prejudices. I would like someone to attempt the reconstruction. The cases, with my verdicts attached, are these.

**A.** A bag contains one hundred envelopes; exactly one holds a cheque for £1,000,000 and the rest are blank. Option one: draw once from a second bag that certainly contains a £1,000,000 envelope. Option two: draw from the first bag. I take the certain million, decisively and without regret.
**B.** The same two bags, but now option one is an envelope that is blank with probability 0.89, holds £1,000,000 with probability 0.10, and holds £5,000,000 with probability 0.01; option two is unchanged. I switch, and prefer option two.
**C.** A and B again, except that I have just been told, truthfully and in advance, that whichever option I choose will be repeated a great many times by a great many people and that I will be judged on the aggregate. I now think B is simply the wrong answer and that a reflective person should choose as in A.
**D.** A single dose of a vaccine: option one cures the disease with probability 0.61; option two cures it with probability 0.60. Indifferent, or option one. The same choice described as: option one leaves the disease in place with probability 0.39; option two leaves it in place with probability 0.40. Again option one, and this time I am confident the description is doing the work rather than my preferences.
**E.** Urn one contains fifty red and fifty black balls; urn two contains one hundred red and black balls in an unknown proportion. Paid for red: I take urn one. Paid for black: I take urn one. I do not think there is any single assignment of credences to the composition of urn two that makes both of those choices come out right, and yet I would make them again.
**F.** A certain £30, or a coin toss for £75 and nothing. Indifferent. Now the identical toss offered a hundred times over, each independent, with the total paid at the end. I take the hundred tosses, and I do not think this is a confusion about averages; something about the repetition has genuinely changed the object of choice.
**G.** A certain £30, or a coin toss for £30.01 and nothing. I take the certain £30, and I would not call that irrational, though I suspect that any theory which permits it will also permit an outright preference for a certain £30 over a toss for £40, and I do want that one called irrational.
**H.** Two holiday brochures, identical in every respect I care about, differing only in the order in which the amenities are listed. I choose differently depending on the order. Here my verdict is that one of the two choices is simply a mistake, not a legitimate preference.

What I want from you is not a survey, and not a list of the names attached to these effects. I want the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that my verdicts add up to — stated precisely enough that I could check a new case against it, with each clause tied to the verdict that forced it in, so that I can see what work it is doing. Then: what does the best candidate say about two cases I have not listed, chosen so that I would find the answers uncomfortable? Which of A to H does it fail to deliver, and would you advise me to abandon that verdict or to buy it back with more machinery? I am especially interested in whether the departure licensed by B is the same departure licensed by E, or whether my list is really two lists that happen to sit next to each other; and whether a theory can accommodate F without also accommodating G, which is the pairing I find most embarrassing. Finally, if the honest answer is that C, D and H show my case-by-case method to be unsound rather than that some axiom is false, I would like to be told what test distinguishes a genuine preference reversal from a defect in how the problem was put to me — and whether, on that test, A and B survive at all.
