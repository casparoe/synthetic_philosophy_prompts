Write a dialogue, maybe 700 words, between two people who both work on this and neither of whom is a strawman.

R thinks the only protection that actually holds is non-collection — the German instinct: data minimisation, no central register, the state shouldn't have the file in the first place. Her argument is that use-restrictions rot: the temptation to peek is permanent, the duty to audit is intermittent, and every emergency carves an exception that never gets restored.

T thinks that's a superstition and that a modern state can't help collecting anything. For him the only real variable is whether each access is purpose-bound, cryptographically logged, and reviewable after the fact — a state that has to justify every look is more accountable than one that claims to know nothing and therefore can never be caught knowing something.

Give them a concrete thing to fight over: a parliament is choosing between two national digital ID bills, one that issues locally-held credentials and keeps no central record, one that stores everything centrally but makes every query signed, logged, and published in aggregate.

They should trade real moves, not recite their positions. T ought to land the point that aggregate access logs are the only mechanism that would ever surface the pattern of quiet looking R is worried about, so her bill makes abuse undetectable by design; R ought to land the point that the second bill's guarantees are only as durable as the institutions that have to survive the exact crisis that motivates the abuse, and that a log is a promise, not a wall. Have one of them concede something specific at the end rather than shaking hands.
