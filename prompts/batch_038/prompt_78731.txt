I am a novelist at work on a book, one strand of which is set in Dresden, and I have reached the point in the drafting where instinct is no longer enough: I need to think rigorously about what it means for a built thing to survive its own destruction and its own rebuilding. I do not want another tour of the statue and the clay as such. I want the material-constitution literature brought to bear on a real case whose facts are awkward in ways the thought experiment is not, so that I can watch a theory earn its keep and then watch it run out.

Here are the facts, so that we are working from the same page. The Baroque church George Bähr built for the city between 1726 and 1743, crowned by a sandstone dome the Dresdeners nicknamed the Stone Bell, withstood the bombing that began on the thirteenth of February 1945 for two days; its eight interior pillars held long enough for some three hundred people sheltering in the crypt to be gotten out, and then on the morning of the fifteenth the heat took the supports and roughly six thousand tons of masonry came down. The East German authorities left the heap standing for nearly half a century as a memorial against war. After reunification a citizens' campaign raised the money, clearing began in 1993, rebuilding ran from 1994 to 2005, and the church was reconsecrated in October of that year. Two features matter most for my purposes. First, the debris was handled as an archaeological site: blocks were lifted, documented and sorted, and a good deal of the original sandstone went back into the new walls — often not in the positions it had occupied before — while the rest of the fabric was freshly quarried and cut to Bähr's profiles, over a modern structural core where the original engineering had failed. Second, the old stone weathers dark and the new stone stands pale, so the finished building wears its material history on its face: from the Neumarkt you can point at which parts are 1743 and which are 2005.

First, walk me through how the standard positions describe that object across its four careers — intact church, ruin, building site, finished church. Where the coincidence theorist says two things occupy one place at a time, a building and the matter composing it, what does that view say about the ruin decades, and about the years when the walls were part old stone and part new? Is a patchwork wall a case of partial coincidence, and can the literature express that at all, or does it only have room for whole-object overlap?

Second, on sortals and dominant kinds: what sort of thing is it during the fifty years when it functioned as a memorial? If an object is the kind that dominates it, does the memorial dominate, and would that make the 2005 church a different object from Bähr's even with matter carried through? And set against that the fact that a city and a congregation intended a continuation and said so. Does anybody's intention get to settle numerical identity? If not, why do the participants' own descriptions — our church has come back — sound like identity claims rather than claims about a successor or an heir? Tell me which positions allow intention to be metaphysically load-bearing here and what they have to pay for it.

Third, on mereological essentialism and the Ship of Theseus machinery: this case presents same-matter-differently-arranged and same-arrangement-differently-composed at once, in one object, legible from the street. Which accounts can handle both simultaneously? What does the four-dimensionalist say — is the rebuilt church a later temporal part of the same worm, and does the long pause between ruin and reconstruction pose a problem for that view that ordinary Theseus cases evade, since there the replacement is continuous and here it stops, waits fifty years, and restarts?

Fourth, keep the aesthetic and the metaphysical apart and then force them together. The Ruskinian line of thought, which I take to be that restoration destroys what it claims to save and that a reproduction is a kind of lie because it cannot have the original's history of making, would deny that the 2005 building can be Bähr's. Suppose the metaphysics comes out the other way and the rebuilt church is numerically identical. Is that a reductio of the metaphysics, a reductio of the aesthetics, or evidence that the two vocabularies were never in competition? And then the reverse worry: if the metaphysics says it is a copy with old parts, does the original stone in the wall do any work whatsoever, or is it, as one of my characters insists, relics embedded in a replica?

Finally, give me your honest verdict rather than a diplomatic one. Which features of the episode does the constitution literature genuinely explain, which does it misdescribe, and on which is it simply silent? Does Dresden confirm the theory, stand as an anomaly, or belong to a class of case the puzzle was never built for — objects destroyed on purpose and rebuilt by people who mean to continue them? If the theory has to be stretched to reach the Frauenkirche, show me where it tears. Where a verdict turns on a choice its own proponents dispute, say so instead of appointing a champion for me, and name the views by their usual names rather than sending me a reading list; I cannot look anything up and I do not want references. Do not spend my word count repeating the history back to me.

Please write all of this to me as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, nothing numbered, just paragraphs in the register of a long letter from someone who has thought the matter through to someone who now has to think it through herself. One last thing, and it is the reason I need this rather than a summary: at the end, tell me which of these verdicts a stonemason who spent eleven years setting dark old blocks into pale new walls would be most likely to hold, and why his answer might not be any of the philosophers' — because he is the one who has to say it out loud in my book, and I would rather he said something defensible than something merely picturesque.
