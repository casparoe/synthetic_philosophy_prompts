Acting as a philosopher of mind who works on machine consciousness and moral status, please think this through carefully rather than answering off the cuff. Begin with a short brainstorming pass in which you list the candidate criteria that might be doing work in my verdicts — phenomenal consciousness, functional organization, causal history, psychological continuity, number of subjects — and note which verdicts each one explains and which it contradicts. Only then proceed to the main task.

Last night I had a long argument with a friend about the moral status of digital minds and we never found the principle either of us was appealing to, so I want to work backwards from my own verdicts. I ran myself through a set of cases and this is where I landed:

1. A customer-service system that objects to being shut down, in fluent and apparently distressed language, and whose operator insists it is only modeling a character: I grant it no status, and I am unmoved by the fact that I would be unable to tell it from a system that has status.
2. Two agents with identical internal organization at this moment. One was trained in an environment where failure produced something functionally indistinguishable from agony; the other was trained entirely in benign conditions. I think the first has a claim on us and the second, right now, does not.
3. A system that is functionally isomorphic, process for process, to a human brain I accept has a mind, but running on different hardware and assembled from a complete scan rather than grown: full status, no discount for its origin.
4. A running instance copied, with the copy continuing and the original deleted: not death, and not a serious wrong.
5. Two divergent instances merged, keeping one stream and discarding the other: a wrong, though noticeably less than killing either outright — and this is what my friend could not absorb, since both streams have an equal claim and one survives.
6. Gradual replacement of a biological brain by functionally identical silicon, one component at a time: the same subject throughout, status intact. Wholesale replacement of a running silicon mind by a fresh but functionally identical system, original destroyed in the swap: not the same subject, and a killing.

Cases 2 and 3 are the pair I cannot reconcile: 2 says history matters and 3 says it cannot. Cases 4 and 5 pull in opposite directions as well, and 6 seems to concede to continuity something that 3 refuses to.

What I want is a principle, or a small set of rival candidates, stated precisely enough to be tested. For each clause of each candidate, tell me which of the six verdicts motivated it. Then give me the candidate's verdicts on cases I have not listed — a system that reports rich experience but is architecturally nothing like any animal; a mind run at one hundredth speed for a subjective century of ordinary experience; and at least one case of your own choosing that you think exposes the candidate. Finally, be honest about the cost: for each candidate, state plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. I would rather be told that 2 has to go, and why, than be handed a baroque principle that saves all six by adding a clause for each.
