I have been given a forty-minute slot in an internal medicine residency's noon conference: roughly twenty-five residents, first through third year, none with any coursework in philosophy, several arriving directly off overnight call, and a whiteboard rather than slides. My topic is vagueness — what the sorites paradox is meant to establish, and why a predicate can fail to have a determinate boundary without being thereby defective or unusable. I am not asking you to explain the material to me. I am asking for a plan of how I should explain it, and I would like the plan argued rather than merely listed.

First, the sequence: the order in which the components must go in, with an account of what depends on what, so that the paradox arrives as something the residents have already half-committed themselves to rather than as a puzzle dropped on them from outside. Second, the single case from their own practice that should carry the whole session, together with the criteria it has to meet — it must be a category they apply and defend every week, and it must not be one whose boundary they already suspect is political, or the room will argue about medicine instead of about vagueness. Please rule out the heap and the bald man; they have both been through an undergraduate ethics elective and will recognise them instantly. Third, what I should deliberately omit at this level and say that I am omitting it, including whether to name any of the standard semantic responses to the paradox at all, or whether naming them does more harm than good to an audience that will not read them.

The part I care about most is the misconception map. Separate what the residents will bring into the room from what the session itself will manufacture in them, and for each one tell me the precise point in the sequence at which it should be headed off. My own predictions: that measurement imprecision has been smuggled in and dressed up as a philosophical discovery; that a threshold set by a committee is therefore front-page news; that the conclusion must be to abandon categories in favour of continuous numbers; and, worst of all, that I have come to tell them their diagnoses mean nothing. One of my colleagues will insist beforehand that a cutoff of this kind is a definition rather than a vague term — 6.5 percent is simply where the line was drawn, and there is nothing philosophically interesting about it — so the plan needs an answer to that ready at the moment it is likely to be raised.

Finally, tell me one thing I can do in the last five minutes that would show the idea had actually landed, as distinct from showing only that the residents had been made uneasy.
