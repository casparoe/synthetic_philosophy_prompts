I'm on our university's international programs committee and we have to settle a handbook clause before the fall cohort leaves. Right now it says students must 'respect local customs and conventions,' which isn't doing any work at all. Two things happened in the spring: a student in a country where tipping isn't practiced left money on the table after dinner with her host family, who took it as an insult to their hospitality; and a student in a city where everyone crosses against the light at a big junction just stood there, held up the crowd behind him, and then reported his own classmates to our office for jaywalking.

The program director wants nonconformity with purely local conventions — tipping, queing, how you cross the street — written up as a sanctionable conduct violation. Two of us think that's a category mistake, since a convention doesn't become an obligation just because everyone around you follows it; one of us thinks the opposite, that if you take the benefits of living somewhere you owe its residents its ways.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting: what the clause should actually say, a test the office can apply to a case like these two in five minutes, what we do about the fact that we also host inbound exchange students and would very much like them to tip and queue our way, and what happens when the local convention is itself something we think is unjust. Give the director's view its strongest form — I'd rather he recognized himself in it.
