I am in my final year of a philosophy degree and am currently taking a module on personal identity and the unity of consciousness, in which we have spent several weeks on the commissurotomy literature. At the end of next month my uncle Martin turns sixty, and there is a party for him in the village hall: roughly fifty people, three generations of the family, four of his former apprentices, and a room that will be standing, warm and loud by the time I speak. I have been given the slot between the meal and the dancing, about nine o'clock, and I am to propose the toast.

The relevant background. Martin had his corpus callosum severed at seventeen, after six years of seizures that nothing else would touch. He has looked, to every outward appearance, like an ordinary man ever since: he married Ellen thirty-eight years ago, they had Frances, he built a small letterpress printing business, he drives, he is funny, he is stubborn. In the first months after the operation his left hand would undo what his right hand had done — buttoning, unbuttoning — and the family story is that he learned to sit on it at the cinema. For forty years he has also performed a party trick for guests: something is placed in his left hand behind his back, or held to the left of where he is looking; he cannot say what it is; his left hand can find it, point at it, or draw it badly. Everyone laughs. Ellen has detested the trick for decades and has said plainly that she will walk out if it is performed at the party. Frances, who wrote her undergraduate dissertation on split-brain cases, has gone much further: this spring she sent the family a long letter arguing that her father is not one person but two, sharing a body and a life, and that this should be received as a fact about him rather than as a tragedy. Ellen took this as being told that her husband of thirty-eight years counts for less than one person, and said so. They are barely speaking.

Martin has given me one instruction. He finds the whole dispute tedious, he forbids the words patient and case study in my speech, and he wants a toast that his wife and his daughter can each listen to without wincing. Both will be in the room, about three metres apart.

Please write the speech. The constraints I would like you to work to are these.

- Three to four minutes aloud: roughly 480 to 550 words. It must sound like a twenty-one-year-old talking to a warm, standing room, not like an essay read out.
- No philosophical vocabulary whatsoever. The words consciousness, identity, person, persons, subject, unity, hemisphere, callosum, commissurotomy, split-brain and brain must not appear. The idea has to be carried entirely by concrete material: the hands, the trick, the marriage, the press, the letter.
- It must not mention that I read philosophy, or that any of this has been written about.
- It must not perform the trick or retell it as entertainment, and it must nevertheless acknowledge that it will not be performed tonight, in a way that leaves Ellen feeling neither obeyed nor exposed, and Frances neither overruled nor appeased.
- It must not endorse either verdict, and it must not retreat to the obvious refuge — some version of we simply do not know how many there are in there — which would insult the letter and the marriage in a single move, and which is in any case not what I believe.
- It must contain at least one sentence that Ellen and Frances will each hear as taking her side, and it must not manage this by being vague, by being a conditional, or by listing alternatives. I want to be able to point at the sentence and explain precisely how it does its work for each of them.
- It may be funny, at least twice, but only with material Martin has always joked about himself; neither woman may be the butt of anything.
- It must end as a toast proper, glass raised, and it must confront rather than slide past the fact that the form of a toast — one glass, one name, a single "you" addressed in front of everybody — already looks like an answer to the question I have been told not to answer.

After the speech, add a note of about 150 words covering: which sentence is the one both women will claim, and how it lands for each of them; what the speech commits me to and what it leaves open; and what the raised glass does, and why I have arranged the sentences around it as I have. Do not name or cite any philosopher, paper, patient or experiment in either part.
