I am collecting my own settled verdicts about the value we take from sorrowful art, and I want to know what principle, if any, they jointly express. I have tried to state the cases concretely and to report my reactions honestly rather than flatteringly.

1. A novel in which a child dies. It devastates me; I have read it four times and would rank it among the most valuable experiences available to me. Verdict: unambiguously good, and the sadness is part of why.

2. A documentary about a real family whose child died, made with the family's cooperation and, by my judgment, better constructed than the novel. I stopped twenty minutes in and feel queasy about having started at all. Verdict: this was not a failure of nerve. Something is off here that is not off in case 1, and I cannot identify it, since the family wanted the film made and is not wronged by my watching.

3. A carefully reported podcast series about an unsolved murder. I have listened to every episode and enjoyed them. Verdict: genuine enjoyment, plus a residue of shame that I do not feel after case 1.

4. Photographs of a famine. I do not enjoy looking at them. I look, and I look again. Verdict: not pleasure, and yet a return I would defend to anyone who called it morbid.

5. A film built to make me cry: swelling strings, a death I did not believe for a moment. I wept anyway. Verdict: I despise the film and I despise my weeping, in the same breath.

6. A tragedy in which the protagonist is a tyrant and his ruin is total. Verdict: real satisfaction, no sadness whatsoever — and yet I would not trade this experience for case 1, because they give me different things.

7. Instrumental music, slow, in a minor key, representing no one and nothing. Verdict: among my most reliable pleasures. I seek it out when I am already low, and it helps rather than worsens.

8. A grief memoir by someone who has come through it: I read it with a clear conscience. The same subject written by someone still inside it: I feel I am taking something. Verdict: these differ morally, and I cannot say why, since neither author is harmed by my reading and both published willingly.

9. A work about a character very like me undergoing roughly what I am undergoing now. I know it would be valuable. I avoid it deliberately. Verdict: the avoidance is not weakness and may be the right call.

10. Since a death in my own family, the novel of case 1 strikes me as a game. Verdict: I have lost something and gained something, and I am not sorry.

I would like the following. Is there a single principle, or a small ordered set of clauses, that delivers these verdicts? State each clause precisely and name the case that forces it. Does the principle locate the value in the felt sorrow itself, or does it explain the value by appeal to something else — form, understanding, the exercise of a capacity, the safe rehearsal of an emotion — and if the latter, does the traditional paradox about enjoying tragedy dissolve, or does it merely move elsewhere in the account? Identify the pair or pairs among my cases that no simple principle can hold together; where a principle can save every verdict only by becoming baroque, say so, and tell me which verdict you would overturn to buy simplicity. Then apply your best candidate to cases I have not listed: a game in which my own choices bring about the loss; a comedy set during a real disaster; a work I judge excellent that never once moves me; a documentary about the suffering of animals rather than persons; and whether an artist ought to produce case 8 at all. Finally, say plainly if any of my verdicts are simply incoherent — in particular whether case 7 can be squared with any account that makes the suffering of a represented person essential to the value, or whether case 7 proves that it cannot.
