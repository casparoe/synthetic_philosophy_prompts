In the section of his 1950 *Mind* paper devoted to extrasensory perception, Turing runs through the familiar categories — telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition, telekinesis — and seems to give telepathy the most weight, on the grounds that his imitation game is defined entirely by what passes through the communication channel between contestants and interrogator.

Treat that as a working hypothesis and test it. Suppose each of the four were confirmed tomorrow at exactly the effect sizes the card-guessing literature claimed: small, persistent, statistically detectable deviations from chance that nobody can yet explain mechanically. For each of the four, assign a number between 0 and 1 measuring how much damage that confirmation alone would do to the program of that paper — to the claim that a suitably programmed digital computer can be made to play the imitation game satisfactorily, and to the picture of mind that underwrites it. Then order the four by your numbers and explain, briefly, why the damage falls where it does: which phenomenon would force an amendment to the test itself rather than to the theory behind it, which would be an embarrassment only about physical implementation, and which would leave the program essentially intact. Say explicitly whether Turing was right to prioritize telepathy, or whether one of the other three cuts deeper into the argument he is actually making.

Finally, give one further number between 0 and 1: how much weight a careful reader should place on the ESP section as evidence of Turing's considered view of the mind–machine relation, rather than as a dialectical concession to his 1950 readership. Defend that number against the obvious objection in both directions.
