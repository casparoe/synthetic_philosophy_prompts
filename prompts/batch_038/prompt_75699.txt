My cousin Della has run the same 60-acre vegetable farm for twenty years and I've been keeping a list of her gambling decisions, half as a joke. Now there's a real one coming up and I want to know what she'll actually do.

The list so far:

- 50/50, lose $100 or win $110: refuses. Every time it's come up, good years and bad.
- 50/50, lose $10 or win $12: takes it, will take it as often as you offer.
- Pays $1,400 a year for hail insurance on the high tunnels against what she puts at a 1-in-80 chance of about $90,000 of damage. Never shops around, calls the premium cheap.
- Two years back she spent $4,500 out of the operating account, over her husband's objection, on drawings and a traffic study for a county agritourism permit she'd have called "one in fifty, generous," worth maybe $250,000 over a few seasons if it came through. It didn't. She says she'd do it again.
- Last year she passed on a certain $40,000 for the back forty and took a 50/50 arrangement with a solar outfit that pays either $15,000 or $80,000.
- Won't sell the farmhouse or borrow against it at any price or payoff, but keeps around $3,000 in a "mad money" envelope in the desk drawer and bets that on anything.

Now the real one: the grain co-op she's belonged to for twenty years is winding up. Each member picks either a certain $9,000 distribution, or a share of the residual, which the liquidator puts at roughly 1-in-150 of $1.3 million and zero otherwise. It's equity she'd half forgotten she had, so it isn't operating cash and nothing she owns is on the line either way. Worth knowing that the 1-in-150 is the liquidator's own number and he gets paid more if members choose the residual.

My roommate's an econ grad student and he says the list is provably incoherent on the standard picture — some calibration theorem, that if you won't take the small favorable bets you can't take the big favorable ones either — and that the result's been extended to cover the rank-dependent family too, so she can't be rescued by one of those risk-weighted expected utility accounts. I dont think he's reading that extension right, but I can't say why.

So: which does she take at the co-op? I care more about the machinery than the answer. Lay out the candidate rules that fit the list, what each one says she does on Monday, and which of her past decisions each is leaning on hardest — I suspect some of them are doing all the work and one of them is doing none. Then give me your best guess with reasons, say what she'd be committing herself to by making it, and name the next decision I should watch for that would split the candidates apart. And tell me straight whether the roommate's second claim holds, or whether the risk-weighted story survives it.
