I spent thirty-eight years as a physical chemist, measuring things that could be measured, and have come to political philosophy only since retiring. My habit is consequently to read an argument the way I would read a derivation: I want to know which steps are theorems, which are approximations, and which are hand-waving. A popular book I have just finished — well reviewed, and by an author holding a serious academic post — contains the following paragraph on Marx and automation:

> Marx's theory of value holds that living human labor is the only source of new value. A machine is constant capital: it contributes no value of its own, and merely passes along, as it wears out, the value it already contains. Marx drew what he took to be the obvious consequence. Since capitalists automate precisely in order to economize on labor, they are steadily replacing the one source of value with something that produces none; the value and surplus value generated in production therefore shrink relative to the capital invested, and the rate of profit falls. This is his law of the rising organic composition of capital, and it makes automation capitalism's suicide mechanism. Marx was confident the mechanism would run its course. In the Grundrisse he observes that once "general social knowledge has become a direct force of production," production founded on labor time — and hence capitalism itself — breaks down. He also believed the machines would set us free: because they can do the work, the working day can be cut to a few hours, and the only serious dispute between capitalism and socialism concerns who owns them. The debate about intelligent machines taking jobs is therefore not new. Marx settled it a century and a half ago, and the political task is not to slow automation but to socialize it.

The paragraph struck me as unusually clean, which is exactly what makes me suspicious of it; it reads like a conservation law printed without its boundary conditions. I have no stake in the politics and I am not looking for either a defense of Marx or a demolition of him. What I want is an audit. Please go through the passage claim by claim and sort each claim into three columns: what a careful scholar of Marx would accept as stated; what is a fair popular simplification of a considerably more qualified thesis; and what is a misrepresentation, either of what Marx wrote or of what follows from it. For the third column, give me the correct statement.

Three points I could not settle for myself. First, the book treats the passage on fixed capital and knowledge in the Grundrisse notebooks as though it were Marx's settled, published prediction. Is that the right status for it to bear? My understanding is that these notebooks were never prepared for publication and did not reach an English readership until long after his death. Does that change how much weight the passage can carry, and do the arguments Marx actually published rest on the same reasoning or on something different? Second, the derivation itself. Is the falling rate of profit a consequence, a tendency hedged with named counteracting influences, or a contested empirical claim? Where precisely does the step from "machines add no new value" to "profit shrinks relative to capital" require further assumptions — and if there is a standard formal objection to the derivation that a non-specialist ought to know about, state it plainly. Third, the claim that machinery shortens the working day and that ownership is the only question left. My impression is that Marx argued the opposite about what machinery did under capitalism, and that he insisted on distinguishing a machine from its capitalist application. If that is right, the book has compressed two opposed claims into one sentence.

Finally: with the errors corrected and the accurate parts retained, does the closing recommendation — do not resist automation, socialize it — still follow? I suspect a corrected Marx locates the difficulty somewhere other than ownership, but I cannot tell whether that is my own speculation or something in the texts.

I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want the reasoning. Where scholars genuinely disagree, tell me that they disagree rather than choosing a side silently, and do not write me a history of Marxism.
