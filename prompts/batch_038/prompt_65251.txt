Six years ago I took a job at a tiny freight forwarding outfit — four of us in the office, me doing most of the paperwork — and about half our volume moves through one port where we use a local agent. He's sharp, he's been doing it thirty years, and he pays what he calls tea money to get certain containers released inside a week instead of a month. I've never been comfortable with it and last month I finally said so in an email, sort of badly. He didn't get angry. He wrote back with the old proverb, when in Rome do as the Romans do, and then he made a real argument out of it, which was the part that got me. Roughly: the rule against paying officials isn't a rule about right and wrong at all, it's a rule that grew up inside a particular kind of bureaucracy with salaries and pensions and written procedures, we exported it to his country along with our forms and our software, and now I'm holding him to a standard that never made sense in the world he actually lives in, where getting anything done means owing someone and being owed in turn. He said a man who takes no gift and owes no one isn't honest, he's just unreachable.

I've been chewing on this for two weeks and I'm pretty sure the proverb is doing four or five different jobs in that email and he's switching between them without saying so. That's what I want from you: seperate out the distinct claims that one sentence could be standing for, from the ones that are just true and boring to the ones doing the heavy lifting that might be flat out false. My own guess at the list is something like — the plain observation that different places judge this stuff differently; the stronger idea that the word corruption doesn't even pick out the same thing everywhere, so there's no fact of the matter about which of us is right; the idea that the standard applying to a person is the standard of the place he's operating in; the idea that I as an outsider can't really see what I'd be condemning; and something purely practical about whether he can afford to lose business over my squeamishness. But I'm sure there are others and I'm sure some of the ones I listed aren't really seperate at all.

For each reading I want a verdict — platitude, defensible but contested, or false — and then I want to know which of them, if true, would actually license the tea money and which wouldn't get him anything at all. There's one test I keep coming back to and I'd like you to run it against every reading: if the proverb is true going in his direction, is it also true going in mine — does it let an agent of ours show up in this office and do things here that would get him fired, on the grounds that's how it's done here? And I have my own half-baked theory I want you to pressure test. I think what separates a gift from a bribe isn't the envelope or the timing or whether anyone said anything out loud, it's whether the obligation survives the person. If the official who took the money got transferred tomorrow, would his replacement feel the same pull? A bribe buys one act from one man, a gift buys a standing relation that outlives both of them. If that's right then a lot of what gets called corruption around here is actually the second thing, and I honestly don't know whether that makes it better or worse. Don't point me at books or articles — I won't look them up and I don't need names, I just want the reasoning spelled out clearly enough that I can write back to him without sounding like either a missionary or a pushover.
