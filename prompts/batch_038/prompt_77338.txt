been stuck on the internal vs. external reasons debate for weeks and what I want now isn't another summary of it, it's a diagnosis of why it never lands anywhere.

sketch of where things sit, as I understand them. Internalist: "A has a reason to φ" only gets to be true if A could be moved to φ by some sound route of deliberation starting from A's actual motivational set — otherwise the claim is false or just doesn't mean anything, and the whole point of talking about reasons is partly to explain action. Externalist: there are obviously reasons for A to φ that A has no route to at all — the guy who can't see any point in being nicer to his wife, the friendship someone is failing to appreciate, the fact that φ-ing would serve an agent's good even where their desires have been thoroughly mangled by their upbringing — and the internalist is just collapsing reasons into motivations, which drains the notion of the normative work it was supposed to do. And here's what frustrates me: these two sides have been trading basically these same moves for decades, nobody moves, and yet they agree on almost every concrete case you put in front of them. On top of that Williams himself loosened the account over time, so that a sound deliberative route can include correcting false beliefs, imagining options you'd never thought of, weighing things properly — until the internalist condition starts to look a lot like "what a fully informed and rational version of you would want," which plenty of externalists are happy to say too. So is internalism converging on externalism, or did the concession quietly change the subject?

my hypothesis, and it may well be wrong: the real dispute is over whether reason-claims are indexical — whether the truth of "A has a reason to φ" is settled relative to a parameter supplied by A's actual psychology, or relative to a parameter supplied by the world. If it's the first, the two camps might each be describing a different parameter and talking past each other; if it's the second, there's a genuine substantive disagreement sitting underneath and it's about normative metaphysics rather than meaning. I've been calling it the indexicality hunch for want of anything better.

two things I generated myself, treat them as data. (1) In my reading group I ran one scenario under three phrasings — "does he have a reason to...?", "would he have a reason if he knew all the relevant facts?", "should he...?" — and agreement was near-total on the second and third and fell apart on the first, with the split tracking who had already read Williams rather than anything about the case. (2) A case I can never use as a tiebreaker: Nadia's motivational set can only be shifted by non-deliberative means — a drug, say — so there is no route of reasoning whatsoever from where she is to caring about the consideration that plainly favours φ, though her fully informed and rational counterpart would care. Internalist has to say "no reason," which sounds absurd. Externalist has to say "yes, and it is completely inert," which looks like giving up the tie to action that made reasons interesting in the first place.

So tell me what kind of disagreement this actually is: verbal, substantive, two-questions-under-one-heading, or genuinely hard but not ill-posed. Argue for it from what each side actually says, say whether the indexicality hunch explains the stall or is a red herring, and be explicit about whether Nadia-style cases could ever break the tie or whether they just relocate it. Then: what would count as progress here — a distinction, a case verdict both sides could accept, a change of question, a formal result about what each theory predicts — or a reason to expect none.

One row per distinct question you think is really in play, not one row per philosopher, and then one line under the table saying which of those questions — if any — is the one actually worth writing about.
