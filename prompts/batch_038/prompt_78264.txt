I run a small grad seminar on the metaphysics of temporal experience, and a friend of mine who writes trade-press philosophy books sent me a passage from a draft chapter he wants to include on the timing of consciousness. My students will get it as background reading, so before I hand it out I want a proper accuracy audit. Here's the passage, more or less as he wrote it:

> Take the illusion known as colour phi. Two little lights, a few degrees apart, are flashed one after the other with a gap of well under a second. You don't see two flashes: you see one light travelling from the first position to the second, and if the lights are different colours — green then red, say — you see it change colour somewhere in mid-flight. That is the puzzle, because nothing in your head could have known about the red light at the moment you seem to see the change. It gets its name from the fact that it isolates the pure phi of colour, the raw feel of redness with all judgement stripped out. There are only two ways this can work. Either the brain holds the show back, waits until the second light arrives, and then presents you with a single seamless story in which the colour changed at the right place — the Stalinesque option, like a show trial where the verdict was fixed before anyone spoke. Or you genuinely have the honest experience, green here then red there, and the memory of it is quietly rewritten afterwards — the Orwellian option, straight out of 1984. Dennett's point is that you can't choose between them, because they agree on every detail of the neurophysiology and on how the whole thing feels from the inside, and both presuppose a finish line in the brain that doesn't exist. In practice the Orwellian story is ruled out anyway: a real experience of red at the second position would leave behavioural traces the subject would have to suppress, and no such traces are ever found. What the Stalinesque option does commit you to is a standing delay — the brain runs about half a second behind the world, which is the same half second turned up in Libet's readiness-potential work — so we all live slightly in the past as the price of a coherent stream. The moral is that there is no stream of consciousness, only a stream of reports about a stream.

My own hunch, for what it's worth: I think he's got the setup broadly right and the punchline badly wrong, but I can't say cleanly where the slide happens, and I'd rather not just tell my students it's sloppy.

So: which of these claims would someone working on this actually sign off on, which are legitimate simplifications, and which are the author's own extrapolation dressed as the received view? I'm especially suspicious of two moves — the treatment of the Orwellian hypothesis as empirically refuted, and the jump from "the two accounts are indistinguishable" to Libet-style global delay. Are those load-bearing for his conclusion, or could you delete them and the argument still stand? And separately: is there anything that could in principle break the tie between Orwellian and Stalinesque, and if so what exactly would it have to measure — report, online behaviour, something else? Finally, tell me what a student should come away understanding from this material that the passage simply doesn't give them.
