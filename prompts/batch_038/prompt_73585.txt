Next month I'm giving a paper at a two-day law school workshop on legality and legitimacy in the founding trials of 1945–48, and I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defence of the paper.

Where I've landed, roughly: the retroactivity complaint and the victors'-justice complaint pull in opposite directions and can't both be run at full strength. If you try to cure the ex post facto problem by grounding crimes against peace in pre-existing law — the interwar renunciation of war as an instrument of policy, plus whatever legislative authority the states holding supreme power over defeated Germany actually had — then you've made the law applied a law the prosecutors were equally subject to, and the tu quoque line (unrestricted submarine warfare, the area bombing of cities, no Allied defendant ever in the dock) gets its full force back. If instead you say the Charter made genuinely new law and was prospective for everybody from 1945 onwards, the selectivity complaint loses its legal shape — but you've conceded retroactivity as to the men on trial.

My paper takes the second horn deliberately. The Charter was a revolutionary act of legislation, its authority rests on who held power and how they used it, not on its having merely declared what was already law; and therefore "victors' justice" as normally deployed is a complaint about selective enforcement, which is a distributive injustice, not a defect that unmakes the convictions. Most defences of the trials fail, I argue, because they answer the selectivity complaint with legality arguments and the legality complaint with moral arguments, and neither side notices. I also claim — this is the part I'm least sure of — that the account travels from Nuremberg to Tokyo.

The room: mostly international criminal lawyers and legal theorists who work on the principle of legality, several of whom have written defences of the tribunals as lawful and who will be irritated by my calling the Charter revolutionary rather than declaratory. Two historians of occupied Japan who work on the Tokyo tribunal and its afterlife in Japanese public memory. One retired prosecutor, fifteen years in an international prosecution office, who cares about charging discretion as a craft and will not be impressed by philosophy. No moral philosophers, no punishment theorists, no IR realists — so I am not going to get the "is it even coherent to punish a state" questions and I don't want them on the sheet.

Give me seven or eight objections I'll actually face from this room, ordered by how much damage each would do, each phrased the way the person raising it would phrase it — the legality people in their vocabulary, the Tokyo historians in theirs, the prosecutor in his. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, and an honest note on where that reply is soft. Flag separately which objections are really the same objection wearing different clothes, since I suspect two or three of them are. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes of the paper so that the concessions land as candour rather than as holes somebody else found. Tell me which single reply has to land for the argument to survive the afternoon.

And then give me the question I'm praying nobody asks — the one where my only honest answers are either a shrug or a retreat that costs me the thesis. I have my suspicions about the crimes-against-humanity definition and its wartime nexus, and about what my revolutionary-legality story is supposed to say to a powerful coalition that convenes its own tribunal next decade, but I want your pick, not mine.

British English throughout, please.
