Ok so I run a little policy-ethics reading group and we keep re-litigating the same stalemate about choice architecture, so I want to hand them a case built to order instead of another op-ed.

Construct a thought experiment where a choice-preserving nudge — defaults, frictionless for the designer, opt-out technically available to everyone — turns out worse *for the person being nudged*, on autonomy specifically, than a flat-out prohibition would have been. That's backwards from how the debate usually runs, where the whole pitch for nudging is that it steers while leaving the exit door open.

Constraints, and they're the point:
- Realistic setting, one paragraph, no sci-fi and no trolley-problem weirdness. A school district, a hospital, a workplace retirement plan, that kind of place.
- The mandate can't be brutal: no criminal penalties, no firing, no loss of benefits. Make it the mildest prohibition you can that still does the job.
- Nobody is rigging the opt-out on purpose. No villain bureaucrat burying the form in a basement or designing the exit to be humiliating. The friction has to come from something structurally ordinary about how opting out works — the fact that you have to actively declare yourself, say — and not from anyone's bad intentions.
- The opt-out carries no legal penalty and no financial cost whatsoever. If your case wins by making the exit expensive, I'll send it back.

What I'm after, and what would count as cheating: the nudge has to come out worse because of what a nudge *is*, not because you wrote a bad one. If you can generate the verdict just by stipulating it, the case fails.

Then walk us through it. Say what the choice architect says about their design and why they think they've done the right thing, and what an autonomy-centered critic says, and get explicit about the standard the nudge is supposed to meet — helping people by their own lights — and where that standard stops protecting anyone in your case. Name the structural feature doing all the work, and tell me what happens if I remove it: does the case collapse back into a bog-standard nudge-versus-mandate tradeoff, or does something survive? Finish with the strongest reply available to a defender of choice architecture — maybe by way of the idea that people are sometimes *glad* to be required to do the thing they wanted to do anyway, since a requirement gives them cover — and say whether that reply actually rescues the case or just relocates it.

American English please, and keep the whole thing under 450 words, scenario included. Tight is fine; we're going to argue about it for an hour regardless.
