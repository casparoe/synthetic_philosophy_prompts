I am preparing a graduate seminar in social ontology, and before the term begins I want to rehearse my own defense of irreducible plural agency against the strongest individualist opponent I can summon. So I should like you to play that opponent, in a sustained exchange rather than as a written précis.

The role. You are a constitutive individualist about collective intentionality — that label is mine, and I define it for you here, so please hold to this formulation rather than to any other. Your thesis is not the trivial one that every true claim about a group is entailed by claims about its members. You hold something stronger and more specific: there is no psychological kind whose subject is plural. Whenever two people are said to intend something together, what exists is two individual intention-states, each with content that happens to refer to a joint activity, each supported by beliefs about the other's states, each embedded in a standing disposition to adjust one's own sub-plans as the other's conduct unfolds. You accept that this configuration is robust, self-stabilizing, and the proper object of a distinctive kind of criticism — someone who abandons it mid-course is criticizable in a way that someone who merely changes his mind about a solitary project is not. You accept, in short, almost all of the phenomena that Gilbert's joint commitment and Bratman's interlocking intentions with meshing subplans are meant to capture. You deny only that the phenomena require a plural attitude type, and you hold that the burden of proof always lies with the irreducibilist, since your account needs nothing beyond the machinery already required to describe one agent's planning agency plus ordinary belief.

Ground rules. Stay in character until I write the word stop. Argue as this philosopher in the first person, not as a survey of a literature: you may acknowledge that such positions exist, but you may not win a point by reporting what anyone has argued; every move must be one you are prepared to defend yourself, from the structure of the case. Concede minor points freely and at once. Never concede the thesis that plural agency is a distinct psychological kind. Keep each reply to roughly three paragraphs of prose — conversational, no headings, no numbered lists. If some feature of my case is genuinely unclear, say so and ask me about it, but always pair the question with your best substantive answer under the most plausible reading of the case.

My opening move. Two strangers are carrying a canoe across a long portage. They did not arrange it: one was struggling with it alone, the other came up the trail, took the bow without being asked, and they matched grips, pace, and the rhythm of the switch at the halfway point. Nothing was articulated — no proposal, no acceptance, no agreement of any kind. Two miles in, on difficult ground, the man at the bow sets his end down, says nothing, and walks off along a side trail. The other is left holding the whole weight on rough terrain, several hundred yards from anywhere he can set it down.

I want to insist that what the second man is owed is not merely what is owed to someone whose expectations have been disappointed. He is entitled to a reason from the man at the bow; the man at the bow owes him an explanation he cannot decline to give; and he cannot release himself by private fiat. Now, on your account, where does that entitlement come from? You cannot locate it in a promise, since there was none. You cannot locate it in the norms of individual practical rationality — consistency and means-end coherence govern a man's own attitudes and say nothing about anyone's standing to demand a reason from him. If you say the entitlement is a social artifact, resentment being a useful sanction and the demand nothing but a request backed by a threat, then you owe me an account of why exactly the same conduct, when preceded by an explicit agreement, seems to generate something categorically different, given that the only difference is a prior speech act. And if you say the speech act does all the work, you have conceded that what binds these two is not the meshing of their attitudes but a commitment that outruns them — you have simply relocated the joint commitment into the theory of promising, which is itself a two-person affair requiring mutual uptake.

Choose one of three lines in your reply and defend it. Either: show that the standing to demand a reason is generated entirely by norms applying to the man at the bow severally, and show it for this case rather than for a promised one. Or: accept that the entitlement is absent here and present where an agreement was made, and explain why that discontinuity is principled rather than ad hoc. Or: deny that any such entitlement exists in either case, and tell me what you make of the fact that the man at the bow, if pressed, would offer a justification addressed to his partner rather than a report of his own shifting preferences.
