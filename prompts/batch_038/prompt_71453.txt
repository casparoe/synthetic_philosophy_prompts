I am preparing a diagnostic to be handed out in the first meeting of an advanced undergraduate seminar on vagueness, before the students have read anything at all. Its purpose is not to test their knowledge but to sort them: I want to learn, from their reactions to concrete cases, which account of vagueness each of them is already tacitly committed to, so that I can form reading groups and predict the objections each will press in discussion.

Please write this instrument as a decision tree together with a scoring key. Not a points tally — the positions here are individuated by combinations of answers, so the key has to be an ordered branching procedure.

Requirements.

1. Twelve questions, each a concrete case or a plainly worded claim, each with two to four answer options. No term of art may appear in a stem or an option: ban the words vague, borderline, precise, sharp, indeterminate, truth-value, degree, context-sensitive, and every name of a theory. A student with one logic course and no philosophy of language must be able to answer, and the instrument must sort them by what they say about the cases rather than by which label they would pick. Order the questions so the shape of the tree is not visible from the sequence; nobody should be able to work out from question seven what question eight is meant to rule out.

2. At least eight leaves. It must include epistemicism; supervaluationism; a degree-theoretic view on which each step of the sorites is very nearly but not quite truth-preserving; a contextualist view; a view on which the unsettledness lies in the world rather than in our talk of it; the nihilist view on which predicates of this kind essentially never truly apply; and a deflationary view on which the whole dispute rests on a confusion. Add more leaves if the logical space demands them, and for every leaf give one sentence naming the cost its occupants pay — the claim they will be least willing to own once pressed.

3. Construct the tree from the space of possibilities first, and only then locate the positions in it. I want a node asking what, if anything, makes it the case that the cut-off falls where it falls, with options including: nothing further makes it so; our total linguistic practice together with a bias toward natural candidate meanings; our practical interests; the structure of the world itself; and the option that the question has no answer. I want a node on whether it is in principle possible for anyone to know where the cut-off falls, distinguishing those who say no, those who say yes but not in any way we could demonstrate, and those who think there is no cut-off to know. And I want a node on iteration: whether it can itself be an unsettled matter that the first matter was unsettled, and if so whether this runs on without end, stops at some stage, or is a malformed question.

4. At least three questions must concern the behaviour of the connectives and of negation rather than the extension of any single predicate. Specifically: a question about a disjunction asserted of a paradigmatically hard case; a question asking what someone has accepted who grants that taking away one grain always leaves a heap — something true, something false, something nearly true, or something whose status moves with the situation of the utterance; and a question about a conjunction everyone will want to call outright false, which lets the tree test what the respondent thinks has gone wrong in the reasoning that seemed to support it.

5. Work these three cases in, improving on my phrasing as you see fit. (a) An oracle announces the exact number of grains at which a collection ceases to be a heap, and the listener understands it perfectly: is what the oracle says true of our language, true of the world, or incapable of being true? (b) A river has over several centuries divided into two channels: is which channel is the original river unsettled out there, or unsettled only in our practice of naming? (c) A city council, unable to agree on what the ordinance forbidding vehicles in the park covers, resolves by coin toss that roller-skates count and that a decommissioned ambulance mounted as a war memorial does not: after the vote, is the term exact, and did the council make it exact or merely record a decision it had no power to make true?

6. Two of the twelve questions must be deliberately non-diagnostic — cases on which most of the target positions give the same answer. Mark them in the key and explain their function: they exist to catch respondents who are answering by mood or by allegiance to a label rather than by the case.

7. The key must give, for each leaf, the ordered run of answers that reaches it; must name at least three combinations of answers that the tree permits but no leaf occupies, saying in each case whether the vacancy is there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or simply because no one has thought of it; and must flag honestly any pair of leaves your twelve questions cannot in fact separate. Close with a rule for the unstable respondent: which answers take priority when a profile conflicts with itself, and whether an unstable profile should be reported as a distinct outcome rather than forced onto the nearest leaf.

8. No bibliography and no citations. The names of the positions are all the scholarly apparatus I want.
