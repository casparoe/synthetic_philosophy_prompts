Last night a friend and I argued until two in the morning about whether human flexibility is ever real, and I came away unable to say where his argument breaks even though I flatly refuse its conclusion. Approach this as a philosopher of action and mind who has worked on the frame problem, on dispositions and their finks and masks, and on the rule-following regress; that particular combination is what the case needs. Work through the argument carefully before answering, but keep the working out of your response — I want finished diagnoses, not a trail of thought. You may name positions and thinkers where that clarifies a diagnosis, but do not hand me a reading list, and do not hedge: each diagnosis should be something I could say out loud to him.

Some background, because step (3) depends on it. He begins from Dennett's digger wasp, which paralyses a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, goes inside to inspect the chamber, comes back out and hauls the prey in; if you slide the cricket a few inches while the wasp is underground, the wasp returns to the threshold and starts the drag-and-inspect routine again from the top. He uses sphexishness for rigidity of that kind, and his claim is that we treat the wasp as a cautionary contrast case when in fact the contrast collapses. He concedes — I pressed him on this — that the entomology may be overstated, since the wasp does not loop literally forever and whether such insects have any genuine on-line flexibility is contested; he says the philosophical argument needs none of that, because the wasp merely illustrates what finitude implies.

His argument:

1. An agent acts freely in a situation only if what it does there is settled by its capacity to respond to reasons rather than by routine.
2. A capacity to respond to reasons is exercised only by consulting representations — rules, standards, models — that specify what to do given how the situation is.
3. Let A be any finite agent. There is a possible situation in which A's representations do not determine what A does. He offers two constructions: (a) take a situation describable only by means of distinctions A lacks, so that no representation of A's applies to it at all; (b) take the diagonal situation in which A's procedure for applying its rules has itself been covertly altered, so that what A's rules prescribe and what A does come apart.
4. In such a situation A either stalls or produces whatever its routines happen to yield; in neither case is A's behaviour settled by reason-responsiveness, so in that situation A is mechanical.
5. Therefore every finite agent is sphexish in at least one possible situation.
6. Freedom is not local. Whether an agent is free is a fact about the kind of agent it is, not about which situation it occupies; and a capacity is individuated by what it covers, so a reason-responsiveness that fails in some possible situations is not a capacity the agent possesses in degrees but one it lacks.
7. Therefore no finite agent ever acts freely.

He insists step (6) is not an extra premise but simply what capacity means, and that if I drop it I have conceded that freedom comes in situation-relative degrees, which he considers incoherent: on his view you cannot be free at the kitchen table and a wasp in the study. For my part I would rather keep (1) and (2), since they look like the entire point of taking reasons seriously, and I have no attachment to the diagonal constructions in (3) beyond finding them clever. But when I try to strike a step I keep arriving at the same verdict about each one, which is that it too seems fine.
