I am in the fourth year of a political theory doctorate, and through a departmental service arrangement I sit as the non-voting adviser to my city's Civic Names Review Panel, which the council created by ordinance eighteen months ago after a single month produced eleven petitions and no agreed method for handling any of them. The panel has seven voting members — two council appointees, one designee from the county historical society, one from the school board, and three at-large volunteers, all of them laypeople, two retired, one a high-school principal — and it decides by a vote of five. There is a statutory clock of 120 days from acceptance of a petition to a recorded decision, one evening meeting per month, and an appeal to the council that reviews only for procedural error and for inconsistency with the panel's own prior decisions. Two of our first six decisions were reversed on that second ground, and the council's language was blunt: the panel appeared to be deciding on the basis of which side brought more people to the microphone. The ordinance gives us three dispositions and no others — retain; retain with contextualization, meaning an interpretive marker or plaque of some kind; or remove, which under the ordinance requires that a replacement name be proposed and funded in the same motion. It also contains a clause nobody on the panel knows what to do with, in the preamble, to the effect that a commemorative name should be expected to remain acceptable to the city's residents for at least seventy-five years without further review.

What I need is a procedure the panel can actually run, not a survey of the ethics of commemoration; I have read that literature and the panel never will. I want an ordered sequence of questions, each with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, together with the points at which the sequence stops early and returns "retain, no further analysis," and the points at which it hands the matter to the panel to weigh and record rather than to find. It has to work from a two-page handout in ordinary language, completable in a ninety-minute meeting by people with no training, with the conceptual apparatus pushed into an appendix for me. Several things have to survive the simplification, and I want you to tell me which can be dropped and which cannot, and why. First, the difference between a name that is functioning as an honor and a name that is functioning as an address or wayfinding device, and a test a layperson can apply to tell them apart in a given location. Second, the objection that we are judging the dead by standards they could not have met, and its limit: what changes when the person's own contemporaries, inside his own profession or movement, objected at the time and he continued anyway. Third, standing — who is entitled to be heard, and with what weight, among descendants, the institutions that still bear the name, the communities the named person's actions harmed or excluded, and current residents who have no connection to the history at all. Fourth, that preamble clause, which I will call the endurance requirement for convenience: how to apply it forward to a name we are considering adopting without it collapsing into a rule against naming anything after a person, and whether it does any work at all on names already in place. Fifth, contextualization, which has become our reflexive compromise; I want the procedure to specify when an interpretive marker is a substantive disposition and when it is a way of not deciding. Finally, because of the reversals, I want a fixed format for the written statement of reasons — short enough to comply with — that records which factor was decisive, so that two panels in similar cases can be seen to have reasoned similarly or can be seen to have differed deliberately. Please also list at least four places where the procedure asks the panel to weigh something it cannot measure, and for each, say what should be recorded so that the inconsistency problem is at least contained.

Then run the procedure on three live matters, showing the answers to each question as you go. (A) Hollis Renwick, 1841 to 1912, physician and first chair of the city board of health, credited in the standard local history with the sewer and milk-inspection programs that cut infant mortality sharply; from 1905 he also campaigned publicly for compulsory sterilization of inmates of the county almshouse, and continued the campaign after two of his own colleagues on the state board resigned in protest over it. His name is on a residential street in the north end and on the downtown public health building, and the petitions concern only the building. I should tell you that I expect the procedure to come out against me here: I have a dissertation chapter on this period and I have been publicly identified with removal, and if your procedure recommends retention, or recommends retention at one of the two locations and removal at the other, I want that stated plainly with the reasoning exposed rather than softened for my benefit. (B) A 1924 bronze statue of Nora Kilbride, an organizer who built the textile local that still occupies the hall behind it, and who wrote and defended the membership rule excluding Black workers; the local and the state federation both oppose removal, and the petitioners are a coalition of residents with no institutional base, while the excluded workers' descendants are not organized and have not appeared. (C) A pedestrian bridge opening in five months that must receive a name, with four proposals before us: a living former councilmember, now eighty-eight, widely credited with desegregating the municipal pools, against whom a former aide has made allegations that were never adjudicated and that he cannot now respond to; a beloved schoolteacher who died four years ago with no known record of wrongdoing; a purely descriptive name after the bend in the river; and a collective dedication to the thirty-one workers killed in the 1911 kiln collapse. Close by telling me what this procedure cannot do — the kinds of case it will get wrong, the ones it will route to the council by design, and where I should expect it to generate the appearance of consistency without the substance.
