I'm 71. Retired four years ago after 34 years in mineralogy, mostly pegmatites and the unglamorous business of getting honest chemistry out of grains the size of a full stop. Since I stopped having a lab I've been reading philosophy — badly, but enthusiastically — and I've gotten unreasonably attached to Kripke on names and on the necessary a posteriori, mainly because his examples are the sort of thing a chemist would recognise.

In a few weeks our society is formally naming a new mineral species after my old colleague Ingrid, who died in 2021. I'm the one who sent the proposal in, the committee approved it, and the name is ingridite (stressed on the first syllable, which the nomenclature people feel strongly about). They've asked me to give the speech that says the name out loud at the annual dinner. Seven minutes, maybe 120 people: working mineralogists who know precisely what a holotype is and will be appalled if I misuse it, Ingrid's two grown sons who were raised believing their mother collected pretty rocks, a dean or two, and the man whose money bought the microprobe that did most of the work, who will be listening for his own name.

I want the philosophy load-bearing, not decorative. Specifically:

- It ends with the naming. A sentence or two, the name said plainly, and I sit down. No flourish.
- After I say it, the name goes out into the world carried by people who never met her, never saw the hill it came from, and will never look at the grain in the drawer. I want that to land as a good thing rather than a loss: the name will keep picking out the same material not because everyone re-checks a description of it but because it gets handed on. Do not use the words "rigid" or "causal chain," and do not explain the idea as an idea. If the room realises it has been given a philosophy lesson, I've failed.
- Two things Ingrid would have called a flat contradiction, held at once: what this mineral is was settled long before there was anyone around to be wrong about it and could not have been otherwise — and yet we know that only because a handful of people in a basement measured it several times, argued, and measured again. She was a hard empiricist and thought talk about what things really are was a disease. So the necessity has to arrive as a report about what the data would not let us say, never as a thesis about the world. If she were in the room she should be able to sit through the whole thing and roll her eyes only once.
- The technical hinge: we never got a single crystal good enough for a proper structure refinement. The species rests on powder diffraction plus microprobe chemistry, which is to say we named a thing we never once saw whole. Everyone at the tables who does this for a living knows what that admission costs. I want that to be the moment the sons find out something true about their mother — she published it anyway, with the weakness stated on the first page. Use it as the emotional centre, not as a caveat.
- The awkward part. There's a re-examination pending on the type grain, and a real possibility it isn't the same species as the material from the locality everyone pictures when they hear the name. Don't hide it and don't turn it into an apology. Put it to the room as a live question: if the grain turns out to be something else, does the name go with the grain or with the hill? I'm told there's a philosophers' story about a famous proof that turns out to have been written by someone else entirely, so that everyone has been referring to the wrong man for years; ours is the same shape except it is actually happening. My own instinct — lean on it, don't lecture with it — is that the name goes with the grain, because the grain is what we pointed at, and pointing rather than describing is exactly why the name can survive our having been wrong. If you can get a room of non-philosophers to feel the difference between a name that follows the pointing and a name that follows the description, without ever announcing that you're doing it, I'll be delighted.
- The donor gets one graceful sentence and then it stops being about him.

No "legacy," no "her name will live on," no heaven, no jokes about the dean. No footnotes, no reading list, no bibliography at the end — nobody at a dinner claps for that, and you couldn't check it anyway. At most one philosopher's name in the whole speech, and it should be removable without losing anything. Around 900 words. Then add one short paragraph telling me where you think the necessity claim is actually being smuggled in and which line you'd cut first if it runs long.
