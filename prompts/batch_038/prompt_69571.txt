Last month I went to a county fair with two friends, Deniz and Cora, and we stopped at a booth running a bag-and-token game. The operator had a canvas bag holding 90 tokens: 30 red, he told us, and the remaining 60 either black or gold, in a split he said he had decided himself that morning and was not going to reveal. In front of the crowd he drew one token out and put it face down under a cup. For $20 you could buy any one of four tickets, each paying $100 if the token under the cup matched: red; black; black-or-gold; red-or-gold. Deniz played twice, in two separate rounds with a fresh draw and a fresh cup each time. In the first round she was offered red against black and took red. In the second round she was offered black-or-gold against red-or-gold and took black-or-gold. She lost both times, which I don't think bears on the argument, but I mention it because Cora brought it up twice.

At dinner Cora said the two choices cannot be defended together, and Deniz said they could, and it went like this. (1) Cora's opening: in each pair Deniz took the ticket whose odds were fixed and turned down the one whose odds were not — red is 30 in 90 no matter how the operator split the remainder, and black-or-gold is 60 in 90 no matter how he split it, whereas the other two tickets depend entirely on a number nobody was given. So Deniz was not estimating anything at all; she was simply refusing to buy a ticket she could not price. (2) Deniz agreed with the description and denied the criticism. The operator filled the bag himself and knows what is under the cup, and a man running a $20-a-ticket booth is not filling it fairly. The black count is not a fact of nature that one is merely ignorant of; it is a choice made by someone who profits when the mark guesses wrong. Buying red is buying on a number he published before you walked up; buying black is buying on a number he selected and is withholding. She said she would not bet against the house on the house's own hidden variable. (3) Cora said that if this really was Deniz's belief, it was a belief with consequences she had not followed out: suppose the operator stacked the bag against black, so there are fewer than 30 black tokens; then there are more than 30 gold ones, so red-or-gold wins in more than 60 of the 90 cases, which is better than the black-or-gold ticket Deniz actually bought. The story that justifies the first choice requires the opposite of the second. Either the split is a fact Deniz has a view about, in which case her two picks contradict each other, or it is not, in which case the first pick was not the shrewd anti-house play she was describing. (4) Deniz conceded the point but said it proved too much. She has no view about the split and is not going to invent one to keep Cora's books balanced; in each pair she took the ticket whose odds were announced and left the one whose odds were withheld. Cora had shown only that "the operator stacked it" was the wrong description of what Deniz was doing, not that preferring the priced ticket to the unpriced one is a mistake. (5) Cora said then Deniz would refuse free information: if the operator offered to lift the cup and show her the token before she chose, at no charge, someone who genuinely disliked unknown odds would decline, since seeing the token removes the excuse. Deniz would pay not to know, and that is absurd. (6) Deniz said she would take the peek instantly and then bet on whatever she saw, since there would be nothing left to be ambiguous about; her reluctance applies only when the odds are unavailable, not when they are on offer. Cora had manufactured a preference Deniz never had and then declared it incoherent. (7) Cora's last move was about the bundle booth next door, where the same operator sold both black and red-or-gold together for $40 — between them they cover every token in the bag, so they pay $100 with certainty — and also sold red together with black-or-gold for $40, likewise $100 with certainty. Two bundles, identical price, identical guaranteed payoff, so anyone must be indifferent between them, and Deniz said she was. But the first bundle contains exactly the two tickets Deniz turned down as singles, and the second contains exactly the two she bought as singles, and she would pay $20 for each member of one pair while refusing $20 for each member of the other. Whatever is driving her refusals, Cora said, it is not the odds. (8) Deniz said that was her whole point: tickets do not have values in isolation, only combinations do, so Cora cannot rank Deniz's single picks without first saying what else she was holding, and she was holding nothing. Cora said that was a restatement of the problem, and that is where it ended.

I am auditing a decision-theory seminar this term, and I suspect the little I have read has made me worse at judging this rather than better, so I would like an outside referee. Please go through the exchange move by move and give me a verdict on each of the eight — clearly right, partly right, or clearly wrong, with a sentence or two of justification — and then say who came out ahead overall. Three things especially: whether Cora's step (3) is as decisive as she thinks it is, or whether it only disposes of Deniz's stated explanation while leaving the choices themselves standing; whether Deniz's step (6) marks a real distinction or is a dodge; and whether step (7) actually settles the matter or whether Deniz's reply in (8) has some force, given that the two bundles really are identical. I would also like you to say what neither of them thought of — I have a suspicion that at least one consideration relevant to the case was simply never raised by either side. Please do not bother giving me the technical label for what Deniz is doing or pointing me at the literature; I can look that up, and a name is not an adjudication. Finally, tell me how confident you are in the ratings you assign, and single out the one move where you think you are most likely to have got it wrong and explain why.
