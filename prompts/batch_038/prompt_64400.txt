I am a second-year doctoral student helping to run a two-day graduate workshop on the philosophy of well-being at my university in late November; we expect around thirty attendees, two of whom come from the psychology department, and the Saturday morning session is a recorded, interview-style conversation of forty-five minutes with our visiting scholar, followed by an open question-and-answer period. Our guest is a philosopher who has spent a decade arguing that the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the charge that pleasures as diverse as the taste of a very ripe peach, the warmth of a bath after a cold walk, the satisfaction of finally seeing why a proof works, and the exhilaration of a fast descent on skis have nothing in common that is not equally shared by experiences that are not pleasures — is not a problem to be solved but a confusion to be dissolved. Her position, as I understand it, runs as follows. She grants that there is no single hedonic tone, no felt ingredient common to all and only pleasures, and she thinks that hunting for one is precisely what generates the problem. What unifies pleasures on her account is a role rather than a feel: an experience is a pleasure just in case, in virtue of the way it feels, it disposes its subject to want that experience to continue and to resist whatever would interrupt or diminish it. She holds that 'pleasure' is therefore like 'predator', 'money' or 'poison' — a term picking out a multiply realisable functional kind, with no reason to expect the realisers to resemble one another intrinsically — and that once this is granted the demand for a common phenomenology simply lapses. She remains a hedonist about well-being, and she locates the good-making property in the role rather than in the feels, which I confess is the part of the view I find hardest to follow.

What I need is a sequence of nine to eleven questions that I can work through in the time available, together with, for each, the follow-up I should have ready for the answer she is most likely to give. Please build the sequence so that it develops rather than merely accumulates, and make sure it presses on at least the following: whether the phrase 'in virtue of the way it feels' quietly reintroduces the phenomenology she has just disowned, so that the unity is merely relocated into a heterogeneous set of causal bases; the dissociation between 'liking' and 'wanting' drawn in affective neuroscience, on which incentive-salience systems and hedonic-impact systems come apart, and what that implies for identifying pleasure with a continuation-disposition; her treatment of cases in which an experience seems pleasant yet the subject has no inclination to prolong it (deep satiety, exhausted convalescence, certain painful-but-enjoyed exertions) and of cases in which the inclination to prolong is present without any pleasure at all; whether the account collapses into a desire theory of pleasure, and if so what survives of the claim that pleasure is what matters; whether a role of the kind she posits can bear the weight of being good-making; the first-person case, since I appear to learn that something is pleasant by attending to it rather than by gathering evidence about my own dispositions; whether infants and non-human animals can satisfy the account without possessing concepts of continuation and of a future; and whether a role kind of this shape is a genuine kind or a disjunction in disguise, including whether her analogies with 'money' and 'predator' support her or instead expose the difference between a role fixed by social or ecological facts and a role supposedly fixed by how something feels from the inside.

One further consideration. She has a reliable way of brushing objections aside: she will say that the objection bites only if one has already assumed that 'pleasure' must name a natural phenomenal kind, and that this assumption, not her theory, is the real error. Please include, towards the end, a question that makes that move difficult to deploy, and close the sequence by asking her what would count as evidence or argument against her view. I would rather not have any generic warm-up questions of the 'what first drew you to this subject' variety, and I do not need a bibliography or a list of further reading, since I shall assemble the references myself; the questions should turn entirely on the content of her position and on the pressure points she would expect an informed questioner to know. Please write the whole thing in British English.
