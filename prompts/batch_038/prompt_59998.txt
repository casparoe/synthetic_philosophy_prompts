I am circulating a written statement of my position on consciousness to a departmental workshop whose stated purpose is to attack packages rather than single theses, and I would like a consistency audit of mine before it goes out. The commitments are as follows.

1. Qualia are real, intrinsic, non-physical properties. They are nomologically necessitated by physical brain states rather than identical to them, and they exert no causal influence: I accept epiphenomenalism in the strict sense.

2. The route there is the familiar one. I take the causal closure of the physical to be an empirical thesis about the neural sufficiency of behavior, and I take a physically exact duplicate of me with no experience whatsoever to be genuinely metaphysically possible, not merely imaginable.

3. I accept the knowledge argument: a neuroscientist who has known all the physical facts about color vision and is then released from her black-and-white laboratory learns a new fact. What she gains is acquaintance with the quale, and I do not think acquaintance reduces to any ability, disposition, or piece of physical information.

4. My knowledge of my own present experience is direct and incorrigible in a way my knowledge of anything else is not. I can be mistaken about the external world and about other minds; I cannot be mistaken about whether it currently seems to me that I am in pain.

5. I hold a broadly tracking-based account of knowledge: to know that p, my belief must be sensitive to p's truth — I would not hold it if p were false.

6. To the worry that epiphenomenalism makes my reports of experience inexplicable, since a zombie duplicate would utter the same sentences on the basis of the same neural events, my standing reply is that the report is caused by the brain state, the brain state nomologically necessitates the quale, and so belief and quale share a common causal ancestor. The match between my claims and my experiences is therefore not brute luck, even though the experiences do no causing.

7. I maintain that a physically exact duplicate which reported having experiences would be reporting falsely, and that I would treat its testimony about its inner life as evidentially worthless.

8. I am a parsimony-minded theorist: posits that do no explanatory work are a cost, and between rivals otherwise equal I prefer the ontology with nothing idle in it.

9. I resist the charge that my position is insulated from all empirical evidence. What the closure data does, on my view, is eliminate interactionism, and I regard eliminating a live rival as a genuine empirical achievement.

Please go through this package and tell me which pairs of commitments genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list. For each, identify what has to be given up or weakened and what the weakening costs me — in particular, whether the revision leaves me with any working route into dualism at all, or merely a dualism I can no longer claim to know. Where a tension is only apparent, name the distinction that dissolves it, and say explicitly if dissolving it simply transfers the burden to some other commitment on my list. Finally, tell me whether any single revision would clear more than one tension at once, and which one that would be. I am not looking for a general survey of epiphenomenalism or a defense of the view; I want the audit of this set, and I would rather you be blunt about where it is weakest than diplomatic about where it is strongest.
