Every year I hand my ethics-of-belief seminar a little reductio of James's central thesis, and every year somebody in the room finds an exit I can't quite dismiss. Play the part of a specialist in pragmatisim and the ethics of belief, and think this through slowly before you answer — I don't want the survey-course version.

The argument. (1) James's thesis is that "our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds; for to say under such circumstances, 'Do not decide, but leave the question open,' is itself a passional decision." (2) A genuine option is a living, forced and momentous one. (3) Any hypothesis that outruns the evidence is underdetermined by it — there is always a rival consistent with everything I've got — so no such option can by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds. (4) Any hypothesis I am seriously entertaining is live for me in virtue of my entertaining it. (5) And it is forced, because (1) itself tells me that declining to decide is already a passional decision, so there is "no standing place outside of the alternative." (6) And it is momentous, since beliefs are not held one at a time: a bad one infects the rest of my scheme and I act on it, which changes what evidence I can later collect, so the choice is not reversible in the cheap way a trivial option is. (7) So every belief that outruns its evidence is a genuine option. (8) So by (1) my passional nature may lawfully settle each of them — which is to say I may believe whatever I most want to believe. (9) But that licenses Clifford's shipowner, the jealous husband, the flat-earther, and no ethics of belief can license those. (10) So James's thesis is either false or unrestricted and indefensible.

I can't live with (10), and of everything in the chain I'd most want to keep (3) and (9). A collegue suggests patching the thesis with a fourth condition: an option counts only where acting on the belief could itself help bring the fact about — the sort of case James likes where faith in a fact helps create the fact. Tell me which step goes and why it looked innocent, what each of the rival rejections costs, and whether that patch rescues James or shrinks him to the point of uninterest (and whether it is anything more than a restatement of something he already says).

Before you commit, work through in prose which steps are candidate culprits and what giving up each would cost, then settle on one and argue for it. Put your answer to "which step goes" in your very first sentence. No headings, bullet points, bold text or tables please — continuous prose only, the way you'd write an email to a colleague.
