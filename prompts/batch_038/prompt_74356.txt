I am the heritage and commemoration lead for a unitary council in the north of England. Since the statue of Edward Colston went into Bristol harbour we have received a steady trickle of proposals that all trade on some claim about the past: two or three a month, from ward councillors, parish groups, historical societies, developers and, increasingly, from single-issue campaigns. They cover the whole range — erect a statue, keep a building open, take a name off a wall, rename a school, install a plaque, and once a year whether the civic service should mark some contested anniversary. Each one goes to our heritage panel (seven people: three elected members, four co-opted community representatives, none of whom has studied history or philosophy) with a two-page briefing note that I write; the panel's recommendation feeds into the council's formal process, where it commits public money and is awkward to reverse. I have no framework for these notes beyond precedent and my own prejudices.

What I need is a reusable procedure, not an essay. Take Nietzsche's second Untimely Meditation — the essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life — and turn his three ways of relating to the past, the monumental, the antiquarian and the critical, into something an officer with no philosophical training can run on a proposal in the course of an afternoon. The conceptual work I want from you lies in the translation: tell me which of Nietzsche's distinctions must survive intact into a checklist if the checklist is to be worth anything, which have to be dropped because a panel of lay members cannot adjudicate them, and which of his other ideas are genuinely load-bearing here and which are decoration — the balance he wants between the historical and the unhistorical, the horizon a living thing requires in order to act at all, the capacity to grow out of oneself and to heal and remake what is broken, and his remarks on justice as the rare virtue of those who sit in judgement on the past. Note that his word is Historie rather than Geschichte, and say what that implies about whose proposals the procedure is really designed to interrogate.

The procedure should give me: the questions to put to a proposal, in order, with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each; tests for establishing which mode of history a proposal is actually invoking, including the cases where the declared mode and the operative mode diverge (a restoration campaign dressed as heritage preservation that is in substance a plea for a monument to a vanished way of life; a removal campaign presented as critical judgement that is in practice an attempt to make a past stop being visible); the characteristic failure of each mode, so that I can see when a proposal is running into it; the conditions under which a mode is legitimate at all, given that Nietzsche holds each to have a proper use and a proper dose; and a clear stopping rule, marking where the procedure terminates in a recommendation and where it hands the question to the panel or to public consultation because what remains is not a matter of fact.

Then run it in full on three live cases. First, a petition to keep open a Victorian public baths whose roof has failed, which is well beyond what the capital programme can bear, whose regular users now number in the dozens, and which almost everyone writing in support is remembering rather than using. Second, a proposal to remove a bronze statue of a nineteenth-century mill owner who endowed the park in which it stands, built housing for his workforce, and made his fortune from cotton grown by enslaved people. Third, a proposal to name a new secondary school after a local doctor who died eighteen months ago and was much praised during the pandemic. On the third I want the hard case handled squarely: the record is not yet settled, and I expect monumental history to be the mode at work, so tell me what the procedure does with a subject who has not yet become past.

I should be candid about where my own interests lie, and I want the procedure run against them rather than for them: I want the baths saved and I want the statue gone. If it does not deliver one or both, say so plainly and identify the step that produced the result, because a framework that only ever returns my prejudices is worthless to me in front of the panel. Close with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do — in particular what it cannot settle about who this community is and what it owes its own past, and where it will predictably misfire (a proposal that concerns a present need but expresses itself in historical language; a case where two groups in the same town require opposite modes of the same event) — and with the two or three respects in which you think administering Nietzsche in this way betrays him, given how firmly he opposed the idea that history could be put under management.
