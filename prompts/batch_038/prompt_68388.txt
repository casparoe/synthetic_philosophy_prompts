I'm in the back half of a decision-theory seminar that has just moved from the representation theorems to their critics, and I owe a short response paper on the Allais case. The mathematics I think I understand; what I can't get a handle on is an analogy my instructor keeps reaching for, and I suspect the analogy is doing most of the argumentative work. The pattern under discussion is the standard one: in the first problem, choose between a million dollars for certain and a ten percent chance of five million, an eighty-nine percent chance of the million, and a one percent chance of nothing — nearly everyone takes the certain million; in the second problem, choose between an eleven percent chance of the million and a ten percent chance of five million with nothing otherwise — many of the same people take the ten percent chance of five million. I accept that no single assignment of utilities to the three outcomes can generate both preferences, and that the independence axiom is what forbids them. The analogy is meant to explain why that verdict should feel obvious rather than merely technical. As it was put to us: reversing yourself between those two problems is like preferring the soup to the salad when the menu offers only those two, and then preferring the salad to the soup once a third dish has been added that you were never going to order anyway. The certain million is cast as that third dish — an alternative that is present in one problem and absent in the other, irrelevant to the real contest between the eleven and the ten percent, so that the preference reversal stops looking like a discovery about certainty and starts looking like an inconsistency of the perfectly ordinary sort that nobody would want to defend.

Please audit that analogy instead of re-litigating the axiom in general. Start with the correspondence table: say what in the Allais pair plays the role of the soup, the salad, the third dish, the menu, and the diner's awareness of what is on offer, and flag explicitly any slot that turns out to have no occupant. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bite from the ones that are cosmetic. The one I keep circling is this: as I understand it, independence is a claim about mixtures — if you prefer prospect A to prospect B, you must prefer any mixture of A with some third prospect C to the corresponding mixture of B with C — whereas in the first problem the certain million is not a third prospect sitting alongside the two options but a component inside both of them, scaled down to nothing in the second problem. If that's right, I don't see in what sense it is an "alternative" at all, let alone an irrelevant one, and the menu picture seems to be describing a different failure. Tell me whether that is a genuine disanalogy or whether I'm just resisting the point, since a defender might reply that writing the two problems in a common three-outcome state space makes the cancelled component look exactly like a dish removed from the menu. It would also help to be told which notion of irrelevance the analogy is quietly borrowing, because I believe at least three distinct requirements travel under similar names — one about aggregating individual rankings over the same set of candidates, one about the probability of choosing an item as a function of which other items are in the choice set, and one about pairwise comparisons being unaffected by the presence of unchosen options — and to be told whether any of them is in fact the same requirement as independence.

Second, the instructor added a supporting move in the same class: preferences of this kind can be pumped, so they cannot be rational, because a decision maker who pays a small fee at each step and ends up back where she started, having been worse off at every stage, has made a sequence of choices each of which she knew to be pointless. Audit that one as well, and say whether it is doing independent work or merely restating the menu picture in a sequential setting. In particular: does the pumping argument require the agent to face both problems as stages of a single planned choice, with the option to revise between them, and does the charge of pointlessness still land against someone who is only ever presented with one problem, chooses once, and never gets to change her mind? Finally, give me the bottom line. If both analogies fail to carry the weight placed on them, does the verdict that the reversal is an ordinary inconsistency still stand on some other footing — and if so, name what that footing is — or was the analogy the entire argument? And if the menu picture misleads, propose a better analogy for what is really happening in the common-consequence form of the case and in the common-ratio variant, and state exactly what conclusion the better analogy would license, since I'd rather not trade one loaded picture for another. Reason everything through from the structure of the cases themselves; I'm not after citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, and please don't hedge by pointing me to the literature.
