I'm a retired analytical chemist — thirty-eight years running an ICP-MS lab doing trace metals in groundwater — and since I stopped working I've fallen down a rabbit hole on the philosophy of the far future. I've drafted a short essay on suffering risks and I'd like a properly critical read of it. I wrote it for a reading group of ex-colleagues, about half of whom are engineers and half of whom have never encountered consequentialism outside a newspaper column, so I tried to keep it concrete. I'm worried I've over simplified things and also that I've invented a distinction that allready exists under some other name and I just haven't read enough to know it.

Here's the draft:

---

When people talk about risks of astronomical suffering, they usually picture it as a matter of headcount. Some bad outcome gets locked in, and instead of ten billion people you get ten trillion, or ten quintillion emulated minds, each of them somewhat worse off than the average person alive today. The badness scales with the number of sufferers. That's a reasonable picture, but I think it hides a second and independent way for the same kind of outcome to get worse, and the two need to be argued about separately.

Call the first kind of suffering risk population-scaled: the total goes up because there are more sufferers, or because they persist for longer. Call the second kind rate-scaled: the total goes up because each moment of each sufferer's life is worse than it would otherwise have been — more intense, more densely packed, less interrupted by relief.

The reason to keep them apart is that they depend on completely different facts about the world. Population-scaled risks hinge on how much energy and computing a future civilisation can capture, and on how cheaply minds can be instantiated. Rate-scaled risks hinge on something else entirely: whether there is any upper bound on how bad a single experience can be. And here is the uncomfortable part. We have decent evidence that biological pain has a ceiling, because a nervous system can only fire so fast and can only sustain so much before it shuts down, blunts itself, or loses the organism entirely. Nothing about an artificial substrate obviously inherits those limits. A digital system has no refractory period, no endogenous opioids, no fainting, no exhaustion. If intensity can be dialled past what flesh permits, then a single mind could in principle contain more badness in an hour than an entire biological civilisation contains in a century. On that assumption rate-scaled risks dominate, and the usual arithmatic about vast populations of moderately bad lives is measuring the wrong variable.

There's a wrinkle. Intensity presumably stops mattering somewhere — either because "more suffering" ceases to be a meaningful description once an experience is maximally disordered, or because there's some saturation point in what counts as one subject having one experience, so that piling on more signal just produces more instances of the same worst thing rather than something worse. I don't know how to settle that, and I suspect it can't be settled from the armchair. But I do think that people arguing about the size of the future population should be arguing about this instead, because it's the term in the product that is uncertain by the largest number of orders of magnitude.

So my proposal is a modest one: whenever anyone states a suffering risk, they should say which of the two dials they are turning, and how confident they are that the dial is bounded.

---

Three things I'd like from you. First, does the population-scaled versus rate-scaled split actually carve reality at a joint, or is it a distinction that's already standard in this literature and I've just given it new labels — and if it's the latter, what are the labels I should have used? Second, where is the reasoning weakest, and specifically is the ceiling claim (biology capped, digital possibly not) something I can defend with the confidence I've written it at, or does it need to be hedged down to almost nothing? Third, would an engineer in my reading group be right to say the whole thing collapses into one variable anyway, since intensity and number of minds both come down to how you spend compute?

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — an overall verdict on the draft — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
