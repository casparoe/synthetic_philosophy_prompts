So I'm the secretary of a ~40-person rationalist housing co-op and our bylaws have a clause saying that "any provision of these bylaws may be amended by two-thirds vote of the members in good standing." Last month one faction used that clause to replace the clause itself with a unanimity requirement, and now the other half insists the change is void, because the rule that authorized it no longer exists. I tried to work out who's right and got this arguement, which obviously proves way too much:

(1) A rule is valid only if it was created in accordance with a rule that was in force at the time of its creation.
(2) The old clause was in force at the time of the vote, and it authorized changes to any provision, including itself. So the unanimity clause was validly created.
(3) But validity isn't a snapshot — a rule either is valid or it isn't, now.
(4) Right now the old clause is repealed, so nothing in force authorizes the unanimity clause, and by (1) it's invalid.
(5) And this generalizes to every amendment anyone has ever passed, since every amendment changes the corpus that authorizes it. Each one saws off the branch it's sitting on. All 30 years of our amendments are void and we're technically still governed by a founding text nobody has read.
(6) Which generalizes again: contracts, corporate charters, constitutions. No group of people has ever actually managed to change its own rules.
(7) The escape looks like denying (1), but then there's no difference between a legitimate amendment and a coup — whoever can get the votes just decides, and "following the rules" stops doing any work.

I'd really like to keep both (1) and the claim that our amendment was legitimate, and I suspect I can't have both. Where does the chain actually break? If your answer is that some step has to go, price it out for me: what does the co-op lose, and is there a version of (1) weak enough to survive (4) but still strong enough that (7) doesn't follow?
