I spent forty years counting fish — population dynamics, mostly recruitment, which is the fancy word for how many of the babies survive to matter. Retired six years now and I've fallen into philosophy the way people fall into birdwatching, badly and expensively. I'm at the point where I read a thought experiment and immediately start thinking about the idealizations, which I suspect is a disease.

Anyway. I volunteer at a community center that runs evening discussion groups — about ten people, ages twenty-five to seventy, none of them with any philosophy training, one room, one Tuesday a month. Next session is on the view that says distributive justice is about compensating people for bad luck while leaving them to live with what they chose, the brute luck versus option luck line and all that. I don't want to lecture about it and I really don't want them to just sit there agreeing that of course, obviously you shouldn't be compensated for your own gambles. I want them to run into the problem where the line doesn't hold, because the regress is the interesting part: you sort what happened to a person from what they chose, and then you look closer at the choosing and find it's downstream of something else that happened to them, and then you look at that, and so on.

So: design a runnable activity for that room. I have a specific structure in mind and I'd like you to build it out rather than talk me out of it. A committee with a fixed pot of money to divide among a handful of claimants with competing needs, and the information about the claimants arriving in rounds from envelopes that I open and read aloud in a set order, so that a distinction they confidently draw in round one gets the floor pulled out from under it in round three or four. They should reach the regress on their own, out loud, before anyone names it. Name it in the debrief, not before.

Here's the thing I want folded into the design, and it's the part I most want your opinion on. In my old field, when the environment is unpredictable from one generation to the next, the organisms that do well are often the ones that don't try to be optimally adapted to any particular year. They spread it. A brood comes out mixed on purpose — some cautious, some wildly overcommitted to a strategy that only pays off once in a decade, because in a variable environment the variance itself is the adaptation, not noise to be averaged away. Which means the reckless individual in that brood is not reckless by accident. The recklessness was put there, deliberately, by a strategy that predates him and that he had no say in. I want some of the claimants in your committee game to be exactly that kind of case: their risk appetite turns out, in a later round, to have been chosen for them and even encouraged. Tell me honestly whether that move does better work on the brute/option line than the more usual observation that all our choices have causes — I suspect the usual version is too soft, because you can always reply that a caused choice is still a choice. Does the deliberately-installed-variance version close off that reply, or does it just relocate it?

What I need from you: the size of the pot and why it has to be that size; the claimants and their dossiers, and exactly what gets revealed in which round, with the text I'd read off each card; the ninety minutes laid out in prose; who sits where and what each person is holding, including me, and what I'm supposed to say and to swallow; whether I should have someone shadow the conversation keeping a tally of every time a person appeals to some version of "but they chose it," and reading the tally back at the end; what will predictably go wrong the first time it's run and how I get it back on the rails; and a frank note on which part of the idea the room can't feel and I'll have to supply out of my own mouth.

Also tell me plainly whether the brood-spreading idea is load bearing or just decoration I'm proud of — could a facilitator who knows nothing about it run the thing and lose nothing? I'd rather hear it now than after I've printed the cards.

One format request. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I print these out and read them with a cup of coffee and the visual clutter puts me off. And no bibliography, I've got nothing to look up and don't intend to; I'd like you to work out of your own head, since the room will be working out of theirs.
