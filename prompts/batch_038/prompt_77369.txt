I am a junior at a public high school, and together with a classmate I co-run a small student-led club that our faculty advisor lets call itself Decisions and Dilemmas; we meet for one 40-minute period each week, and on any given day between 12 and 22 students show up, ages 15 to 18, with widely uneven preparation (two of them are on the math team and have seen expected-value calculations, most have never drawn a probability tree). A few weeks ago a friend left a battered library copy of Hofstadter's Metamagical Themas in my locker, and I read the column about superrationality that ends in the mail-in lottery he proposed to Scientific American readers, where a fixed prize was divided by the total number of entries submitted, so that every additional ticket any reader mailed in made the prize worse for everyone, including himself, and the entries escalated until the prize was worth nothing. That column also describes the related problem in which a group is offered a large prize conditional on exactly one member acting, and nobody acting also forfeits it. I want our club session to be built around those two structures. Two constraints from our advisor: no cash or cash-equivalent prizes, and nothing that lets a student profit at another's expense in a way she cannot supervise, so our pot will be a fixed 400 Club Credits that students trade in at the end of the term for slices of the club pizza party; credits cannot be created, borrowed, or added mid-activity. Phones go in a bin for the period, which is school policy anyway and which I think helps here.

Here is the design I want you to build out and stress-test, because I can see the shape of it but not the mechanics. Two linked rounds in one session. Round one should reproduce the dilution structure: each student privately submits some number of entries on a sealed slip, all entries go into one pool, and one entry is drawn at random to win the pot divided by the total number of entries. Round two should reverse it: the pot carries over in a way you specify, and now exactly one student must make a particular kind of choice for anyone to be paid, with more than one such choice, or none at all, forfeiting everything. I want the two rounds connected so that what happens to the pot in round one is visibly caused by the aggregate of individually sensible decisions, and so that round two begins from a number the students themselves produced. Please give me: the exact split and carry-over rule you recommend and why it is the one that makes the arithmetic bite; the verbatim text of the one-page instruction sheet handed out at the start of each round, since half the failures of activities like this come from ambiguous rules; a materials list; a minute-by-minute timeline that fits in 40 minutes including the debrief; and a physical method for drawing the winning entry fairly when different students have submitted different numbers of entries, without a computer. Then handle the four problems I expect: a student who writes a number far too large to be represented by physical slips at all; whether submissions are auditable afterward or only honor-based, and how that choice changes the game; whether the advisor should be allowed to play as a participant, given that superrational reasoning seems to require that everyone be in a symmetric position, and if she is excluded, whether that exclusion should be announced before or after round one; and what to do if a coalition forms beforehand, since these students text each other and I cannot realistically forbid pre-meeting conversation, though I can forbid talking during the submission window. I would also like a short anonymous prediction poll before each round, in which every student writes down what they think the total will be, and I want your view on whether publishing the distribution of those guesses helps the reasoning or destroys the very feature I am trying to expose.

The names should not be spoken until the debrief. In it, I need each moment of the activity mapped onto the idea it was supposed to produce, and I need one diagnostic question, asked on paper after round two and read aloud in aggregate, that separates two things my friends keep conflating: reasoning that predicts what other people will do, and reasoning that treats one's own conclusion as the conclusion anyone else doing the reasoning carefully would reach. If the activity cannot tell those apart, say so, because one of my friends insists the whole thing is just altruism with extra steps and the other insists it is incoherent since my decision cannot affect anyone else's, and I want the debrief to give me something to say to both of them in student language rather than a lecture. Tell me plainly what will go wrong the first time I run this with teenagers who like each other, and how to recover in the room without abandoning the point. Finally, tell me which feature of these dilemmas the activity cannot reproduce no matter how well I run it, and which I as facilitator will simply have to supply in words afterward.
