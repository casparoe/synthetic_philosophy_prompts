I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for something like fifteen years I have covered numerical weather prediction and climate modelling closely enough to read the technical literature in those fields. I am not a philosopher and I have not read the philosophical debate about chaos. I am now writing a piece on what chaos theory did and did not do to the idea of determinism, and I want to know whether the philosophers are saying anything my sources are not already saying.

Here is the exercise. Below are six claims that I have assembled from secondary accounts and stated as plainly as I can. Restate each one, as faithfully as you are able, in the vocabulary I actually use: initial-condition error as distinct from structural or model error; ensembles and ensemble spread; error growth and doubling times; the predictability horizon of a flow; verification, calibration and skill scores; perfect-model experiments; weather as an initial-value problem versus climate as a forced or boundary-value problem; and, pervading all of it, the working assumption that any uncertainty we can quantify is uncertainty about a model we know to be false.

The six claims:

1. A chaotic system is deterministic in the strict sense — its state at a time fixes its state at every later time — and is nonetheless unpredictable for any agent who cannot specify that state to infinite precision. The philosophical interest is supposed to lie in the gap between those two facts, not in either one taken alone.

2. Unpredictability comes in two grades, in principle and in practice, and chaos delivers only the second: with exact initial conditions and an exact model the future is fixed, so the limit is epistemic. Some philosophers argue the distinction does no real work, since no finite-precision observer could ever cash out the in-principle grade; others hold that collapsing it gives up something important.

3. Claims about typical behaviour — that sensitive dependence holds for almost every initial condition — presuppose a measure over initial states, and the choice of measure is doing substantive philosophical work. If the physically relevant measure is concentrated on an attractor that itself has zero volume, the genericity of unpredictability may need restating rather than merely qualifying.

4. The shadowing property: for a suitable class of systems, any computed trajectory that accumulates a small error at each step stays close to some exact trajectory of the system, which is offered as a rescue of the meaning of chaotic computation. The objections are that shadowing guarantees only that some true trajectory lies nearby, not that we can identify which; that the shadowing intervals demonstrable for systems of real interest are finite and often short; and that shadowing is a property of the model, which nobody believes is exactly true.

5. At any finite resolution, a deterministic chaotic system and a genuinely stochastic process can yield data streams that no observer could tell apart. Some conclude that the determinism question is empirically underdetermined, or that it is not a real question; others insist the distinction concerns what is happening underneath rather than what can be measured.

6. The probabilities chaotic systems appear to license — long-run frequencies on an attractor, the statistical stability of a climate under changing forcing — are variously described as objective features of the world and as shorthand for ignorance of initial conditions, and it is unclear what would settle the matter, since both accounts yield the same numbers.

For each claim I want a residue report, not an explanation. Does it map cleanly onto my vocabulary, does it map only with distortion, or does it have no counterpart in it at all? Where you had to bend something, say what you bent and why; where nothing survives, say whether that is because my vocabulary lacks a distinction that matters or because the claim was less clear than it sounded to me.

Two constraints on the exercise. First, keep the translation honest about direction: I am not asking you to declare a claim untranslatable merely because it cannot be verified operationally, and if the only reason something fails to survive is that my framework is a thoroughly operational one, say so explicitly and say what a non-operational framework would preserve. Second, run the comparison in reverse as well: identify any distinction my sources rely on every day that has no counterpart in the debate as I have summarized it, and say whether that is a gap in the philosophy or an artefact of my summary.

Finally, tell me which of the six, if any, would genuinely surprise a working forecaster or climate modeller — something not already implicit in what they do for a living. If the answer is none of them, I would rather learn that before I start drafting.
