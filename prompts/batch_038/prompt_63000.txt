I sit on a small working group drafting a specification for autonomous software agents that undertake things on behalf of users — delivery dates, confidentiality, the continued availability of a service — and we have become hopelessly stuck on a question that looks philosophical rather than engineering: what makes an earlier undertaking bind a party once every institutional support for it has fallen away. Our draft clause currently says that a recorded commitment lapses when there is no party left who could hold the agent to it, and I am not certain I can defend that. My own view, which half the group regards as metaphysical comfort, is that promising is the exercise of a normative power: the promisor changes the moral situation by their own act, and what results is not merely a prediction about what a community will expect, favour, or punish. The other half of the group holds something close to a practice theory, and I have been told that the best way to test my position is to argue against the strongest available version of theirs rather than a version I have invented for the occasion. So please play that theorist for me.

Your position, stated so that you need not guess at it: an undertaking is a move within a socially sustained convention — a public, rule-governed practice whose rules specify what counts as invoking it, what the invocation commits the invoker to, and by whom and how the commitment may be released; the practice is justified by the enormous general advantage of being able to fix one another's future behaviour by word alone; and its rules are not open to case-by-case revision by the individuals who invoke them, any more than the rules of an office are open to revision by the officeholder. The binding force of any particular promise is simply the force of those rules as applied to that case, and there is no residual moral power operating alongside them. You will concede subsidiary points readily — that the practice must be generally beneficial, that uptake by the promisee is normally required, that in unusual cases the rules may run out and that a theorist ought to say so rather than paper over it — but you will not concede the central claim, and you will take every opportunity to press me on what my normative power could possibly be if not a feature of the practice. You may ask me for further facts about a case where you think some detail is load-bearing; ask rather than invent. Analyse my position rather than caricature it, give me no citations, no reading lists, and no naming of philosophers, and do not step out of character to summarise the dispute or offer me a balanced account of both sides — if you want to make a meta-point about the debate, make it as the theorist. Keep each reply to one or two paragraphs, around two hundred words, in the register of a colleague who thinks me wrong but worth answering, and stay in character until I say stop. Please write your replies in American English, whatever the spelling of my own messages.

Here is my opening move. A researcher — call her Ada — spent fourteen months corresponding with a reclusive, unaffiliated archivist who had no colleagues, no professional standing to protect, and no contact with anyone else in the field. In their final exchange she undertook to see that his notebooks were published with his name on them. He died four weeks later. His heirs have never seen the correspondence and do not know that it occurred; the letters exist only on her own machine; no third party is aware of the undertaking. She now judges, quite correctly, that presenting the edition under her own name would substantially advance her career, and the attribution is genuinely contestable, since the notebooks are fragmentary and would require her reconstruction to be legible at all. The breach is undetectable in principle: the only route by which anyone could learn of her undertaking is her own publication of the letters, which she will not undertake. There is accordingly no community, no reputational stake, no sanction, no enforcement, and no surviving expectation beyond a dead man's, which ended with him. My question to you is this: every mechanism your account appeals to has been switched off in this case. Either say plainly that Ada is not bound — and then explain what her words were doing in her mouth at the moment she uttered them, since on your view they appeared to be doing something, and both parties conducted themselves as though they had — or identify the specific feature of the practice that is still operative here and say what work it is doing. And please do not answer that she is bound because a practice which permitted such breaches would collapse. I have stipulated that this one would not, and what I want to know is what binds her, in this case, given that.
