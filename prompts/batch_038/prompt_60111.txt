I teach philosophy at a residential liberal-arts college of about 1,900 students and sit on its Committee on Cultural Life, which each spring allocates the income of a restricted bequest — currently some $185,000 — devoted, in the donor's own phrase, to "the aesthetic and moral formation of students." This year the fund must go essentially one way, because the two proposals before us together cost nearly three times what we can spend.

Proposal A renews a standing arrangement, now in its fifteenth year, with a regional professional theater company: eight productions a season, chartered buses, student tickets at a quarter of face value, and a talk-back after each performance. Last year roughly 620 distinct students attended, many of them repeatedly, and the post-season evaluations are the best of anything we fund.

Proposal B converts the money into a participation program: instrument and equipment loans, staffed practice rooms, weekly student-run workshops in playwriting, dance, filmmaking, and choral singing, materials stipends, an open-mic circuit, a three-day spring festival in which everything on offer is made by students, and honoraria for two visiting teaching-artists who coach rather than perform. Its sponsors project that at least 2,300 students would take part in something at least once.

Three points on which the committee keeps circling without settling anything. First, an internal survey (n≈400) found that students who attended last season's production about deportation were no more likely than non-attendees to support the policy changes it depicted; the advocates of A reply that the instrument measures the wrong thing, since nobody attends the theater for moral instruction, and the advocates of B reply that this concedes their case — we would be funding pleasant evenings. Second, students with mobility and energy limits and those on work-study schedules report that the spectator option is the one they can actually use, and that B is a program for the energetic and the already confident. Third, ending A would remove about a fifth of the regional company's earned income and eliminate paid work for the actors and stage crew it employs, in a town with almost no other employment of that kind; they are not our students, and the bequest does not obviously reach them.

Two colleagues have told me privately that the case for B is Rousseau's case against the theater rebuilt in the vocabulary of student engagement, and that it is puritanical and hostile to art. One adds that if it works against a professional season it works equally against the film society, the visiting-writers series, and the small museum we also fund from this bequest — so adopting it would commit us to a policy nobody on this committee is prepared to endorse.

Please draft the memo I will circulate ahead of Thursday's meeting, in 1,000 to 1,300 words. It should end in a recommendation the committee can vote on: A, B, or a defined restructured allocation. If you recommend a split, specify the proportions and say why that division rather than the fifty-fifty the chair is already floating. Separate the empirical question — does watching change anyone, and how would one know? — from the conceptual one about what kind of good spectatorship is, since the committee collapses them every year. Take the two objections above seriously rather than acknowledging them, and tell me whether the donor's phrase does any real work in the decision or is merely decorative. Do not quote, cite, or name Rousseau anywhere in the memo: assume I have read the letter, but the document has to persuade colleagues who never opened it, on grounds they can contest. Close with two or three sentences on what would have to be true for your recommendation to be wrong.
