so I'm a postdoc in an evo-devo lab and we had a fight at journal club that im still turning over a week later. We study a small rodent with an odd cartilage tab wedged into the knee joint — our PI thinks it's a chock that stops the joint hyperextending when the animal gnaws hard seeds, and she's got comparative data showing it's chunkier in populations that eat harder food. One of our grad students said in front of everyone that this is straight out of the spandrels-of-San-Marco paper: the tab is a byproduct of the way the joint forms, where two growth fields meet a ridge just has to be left over, and anyone hunting for a function for it is telling just-so stories. He leaned on the analogy really hard and basically said the architecture settles the biology — the leftover triangular space between two arches is leftover whether or not the architect wants it to be, so the tab is leftover whether or not there's a nice story about seeds.

I want to audit that analogy properly before I write the discussion section, because both sides keep reaching for it and I'm not convinced either has actually cashed it out. Can you lay out the mapping explicitly — what in the rodent's knee plays the role of the two arches, the dome, the triangular leftover space, the mosaics, the architect, and the fact that the space is geometrically unavoidable — and then separate the disanalogies that actually threaten his use of it from the ones that are just decoration. A few things I specifically want you to take a run at. (1) Geometric necessity versus developmental necessity: does "a triangle has to be there once you mount a dome on rounded arches" really transfer to "a ridge has to be there once two growth fields meet," given that the developmental program making them meet is itself a thing that evolved and could have been otherwise? (2) My cousin is a structural engineer and he insists the San Marco triangles are technically pendentives, not spandrels, and that they're load-bearing — carrying the dome's thrust down into the columns with a heap of stabilizing masonry packed behind them — so the poster child for nonfunctional leftover is actually doing serious structural work. Is that a fatal hit to the analogy or just pedantry about a building? (3) Our PI's counter was that those triangles are covered in mosaics cut to fit their tapering shape, which she says proves her point and not his: the leftover did get a job, but only because there was a designer standing there with a plan deciding what to do with it, and in the knee nobody is deciding anything. Does that reply work, or does it quietly concede that a byproduct can be functional? And what does the whole architectural picture import that the organism just doesn't have — a blueprint, one construction episode, a building that doesn't change once it's up, a leftover space that's obvious to anyone who looks up at it?

Finally, tell me whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. Not the weak version — "be careful about function claims, test them" — but the strong one he was actually making, which was "we can stop looking." And if the spandrel is the wrong analogy for what he wants, is there a better one? I keep reaching for waste heat from an engine, or the wake behind a boat, or the shadow an object casts, but each of those seems to lose something the spandrel was doing — say what each one buys and what it costs, and whether any of them can carry the inference from "this trait had to arise" to "this trait does nothing."
