Been going in circles on this for a week and my advisor has stopped answering emails about it, so: outside opinion please.

I'm writing on the priority view and the leveling-down objection, and I built what I thought was a cute little reductio showing that the priority view doesn't escape the objection after all. Now I can't tell whether I've found something or just built a sophism I'm too close to see. Here it is.

Let f be the weighting function: increasing, strictly concave, and stipulate that it flattens hard at the top, so that once somebody is very well off, extra welfare is worth almost nothing morally. Prioritarianism: one outcome is better than another iff the sum of f(welfare) over the people in it is higher.

Two people. Ada at 100, Ben at 1. Call that X. For δ > 0 let W_δ be Ada at 60, Ben at 1+δ. And let W0 be Ada at 60, Ben at 1.

(1) Because f flattens hard, the moral loss from taking Ada down from 100 to 60 is smaller then the moral gain from any sufficiently small boost to Ben. So there's a threshold δ* — absurdly tiny, but nonzero — such that for every δ > δ*, Ben's gain outweighs Ada's loss.

(2) So for every δ > δ*, prioritarianism ranks W_δ strictly better than X, even though W_δ contains about forty units less total welfare.

(3) δ* is not a moral boundary. Nothing about the difference between δ and δ/2 could make one trade acceptable and the other not; δ* is just where the arithmetic of f happens to tip. Between any two specks there's no difference worth calling moral.

(4) If the view prefers W_δ to X for every δ > δ*, and there's no sharp line among the specks, there can't be a sharp line at zero either. So W0 is not worse than X.

(5) But W0 is a textbook leveling-down: nobody better off, Ada much worse off, Ben unchanged, distribution more equal. So the priority view can't say the leveled-down world is worse — which is precisely what its defenders say it can always say, and precisely why it's supposed to beat egalitarianism on this point.

(6) And nothing in (1)–(5) used anything special about prioritarianism except that it weights the worse off more heavily. Run it on maximin, on telic egalitarianism, on sufficientarianism, and you get the same verdict. Only plain total utilitarianism comes out clean, since for it a speck for Ben never outweighs forty units for Ada.

(6) is what makes me suspicious. An argument that scythes through every distribution-sensitive view on the market and leaves utilitarianism untouched feels like it proves too much, but I can't find the joint. People in my department say that since f is strictly increasing, the view automatically prefers X to W0, and I can see that this has to collide with (4) somehow — but I can't say which of the two is doing something illegitimate, and from the inside both look completely innocent.

So: where does it break? Walk the steps and tell me which one you'd reject and why it looked fine going in — I want the diagnosis, not just the name of a fallacy. Then price the fix. If your answer is that there really is a determinate threshold at δ*, tell me what makes it a moral boundary rather than a bookkeeping artifact of my choice of f, and whether the view is then committed to saying that a millionth of a millionth of a unit to Ben is exactly what tips forty units of Ada's welfare. And separately: does it help or hurt that the view prefers X to W0 by a margin smaller than anyone could ever detect? Is "better, but by less than 10^-9" an actual defense against a leveling-down charge, or does it concede the point with a decimal attached?

For what it's worth I'd rather give up (3) than give up the strict increase in (1), but I want to know what each option costs before I decide.
