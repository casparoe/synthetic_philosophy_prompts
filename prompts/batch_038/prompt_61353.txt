Last night a friend and I spent close to three hours arguing about Hannah Arendt's account of Eichmann, and we arrived nowhere, so I should like to hand the disagreement to you in a more productive form: as a design brief.

Here is the argument. My friend holds that the banality thesis, as she develops it in *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, is a substantive and falsifiable claim about the psychology of perpetrators — that what she identified in Eichmann was a particular deficit: no ideological conviction, no sadism, no depth of motive, a stock of ready-made phrases standing in for speech of one's own, and above all an inability to occupy another person's standpoint. I argued that on that reading the thesis has already been defeated, and defeated empirically: the record of Eichmann's long conversations with a Dutch journalist then living in Buenos Aires, which Bettina Stangneth worked through in *Eichmann Before Jerusalem*, shows a man who was verbally inventive, self-aware about his own importance, and committed rather than vacant. My friend's reply was that this misses the point entirely — that the thesis is really about structure and not psychology, and therefore survives the historical correction, because what it claims is that a role in a chain of individually lawful, individually defensible small acts can produce catastrophe without requiring a monster to fill the role.

I do not think that reply is empty. I do not think it has been earned either. And I believe it can be tested rather than argued about, which is why I want a case built to order.

Build me a scenario, one paragraph and no more, in which an agent has every structural feature the thesis is supposed to track and none of the psychological ones, and in which the thesis nonetheless appears to say something true about him. The specifications:

A. Setting: a regulated private utility's customer-collections function in a cold-climate American state, or something structurally equivalent — a prior-authorization desk, a benefits-eligibility office, a hospital records and scheduling office. No machine may do any of the work. There is to be no algorithm, no scoring system, and no predictive model anywhere in the case; the harm must travel through forms, scripts, notice periods, queues, and human discretion, and every act in the chain must be one that a person of good conscience could perform.

B. The agent must be mid-level. He implements and tunes a procedure; he does not author policy. He is not sadistic, he believes nothing in particular, and he is not climbing — he has declined at least one promotion, and the text must say so.

C. He must be verbally resourceful rather than cliché-bound, and this must be shown and not asserted: give him one line of dialogue or one sentence of reasoning that is genuinely his own, something a reader would not expect to find in a compliance manual.

D. He must be good at taking up other people's standpoints, again shown rather than asserted. Include one moment in which he accurately and specifically imagines the situation of a named individual on the receiving end of his procedure, and gets it right.

E. He must know the aggregate outcome, in numbers, and must have put it in writing at least once. Ignorance may not be available to him as an excuse.

F. The harm must be real and substantial — enough that a reasonable person would call it a wrong, though far short of atrocity — and it must be overdetermined: his particular act is neither necessary nor sufficient for any individual victim's harm.

G. He must be asked, sincerely and not rhetorically, whether he could will a world in which everyone in his position did what he does. He must answer yes, and his answer must be one that a fair-minded reader cannot simply wave away as self-serving. Give the defense: the universalized practice must have a real argument behind it, of the sort that turns on what the alternative would cost everyone else.

After the scenario, do the following.

1. State what the banality thesis says about this agent under each of the two readings in dispute — the psychological-deficit reading and the structural reading — and say whether the case confirms or defeats each. Commit; do not settle for saying that it depends.

2. Identify the single feature of the case that is doing the work, the one whose removal flips a verdict. Then remove it and tell me what happens.

3. Tell me whether it is condition G or condition D that makes the case bite. Specifically: is an agent who vividly and correctly imagines his victim's situation and then proceeds anyway still an instance of the failure to think from another person's standpoint, or is he a counterexample to that formulation?

4. Give the strongest reply available to a defender of the thesis — a real move, not a restatement of the thesis. One candidate line, which you may take up or reject with reasons, is that condition D is doing something quite different from what it appears to do: that imagining a person's situation is an operation of imagination or empathy, whereas what Arendt is after is judgment, which consists in allowing the standpoint one has taken up to bear on what one actually does. If you take that line, explain why my case does not already satisfy it.

5. Say whether the shrinkage in scale, from administrative mass murder to a few hundred deaths a winter, is doing any philosophical work here or merely affecting how much we care about the answer.

6. Tell me who won last night. If you think both readings are wrong and the dispute is confused, say so, and say what the confusion consists in.

Two ground rules. Please write in American English. And please do not give me sources, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want the analysis, and I am familiar enough with the literature. Finally, do not stipulate the verdicts: the whole point is that the two readings should come apart on their own. If any specification above can be met only by asserting a conclusion rather than generating one, tell me which specification that is and why, and then build the best case you can under the rest.
