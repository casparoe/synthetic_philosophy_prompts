I have just been put in charge of donor communications at a small nonprofit, and our board has adopted a policy that treats transparency as the whole test: influence counts as manipulative only if it conceals its aim, so if we say plainly what we are doing and why, we are persuading rather than manipulating. I doubt that transparency can carry that much weight, and I would like a case that puts my doubt in a form we can actually argue about at the next meeting.

Construct a thought experiment in which two accounts of manipulation come apart. Set it in an ordinary institutional context — a fundraiser, a clinic, an employer, a campaign — in a single paragraph. The influence attempt must satisfy all of the following: nothing the influencer says is false and nothing material is withheld; the influencer states openly both the aim and the psychological means being used; the target can decline at no cost; and the means work through a disposition the target himself counts as a bad reason for acting, rather than through anything he takes to bear on the merits. He must also see the mechanism operating and go along anyway.

Then demonstrate that the case works. Give the verdict of a theorist who holds that manipulation consists essentially in playing on the target's non-rational or disavowed dispositions, and of one who holds that it essentially requires concealment of the influence attempt, and identify the single feature that produces the divergence. Say what happens to the case if that feature is removed — for instance, if the target endorsed the disposition as a good reason — and whether his knowing acquiescence can be claimed as a defense by one account only. Close with the transparency theorist's strongest attack: that what you have built is exploitation, or flattery, or an offer, but not manipulation. The verdicts should fall out of the two accounts; do not build either conclusion into the description of the scenario.
