For thirty-seven years I worked as a statistical climatologist, most of that time building seasonal yield-forecast models for staple cereals in monsoon-dependent regions. I retired three years ago and have since taken up philosophy more seriously than I expected to manage. Please adopt the role of an expert who is equally at home in the analytical and normative literature on famine causation and in the operational practice of food-security early warning, and please think carefully rather than reaching for the standard summary. I would like you to begin with some brainstorming of your own: reconstruct the premises my questions rest on, and flag any you consider mistaken or overstated, before you address the questions themselves. I want no reading list and no bibliography — my difficulty is not finding sources but knowing whether I have understood them, and what I need is your reasoning, including direct disagreement with me.

Here is my situation. I have been working through Sen's *Poverty and Famines*, together with his later writing on democracy and famine prevention. Two things struck me as decisive. The first is his analysis of Bengal in 1943 — a famine in which some two to three million people died in a province that, on his account, did not suffer a significant shortfall in aggregate food supply, and which he explains instead by the collapse of particular groups' command over food: wartime inflation, the boom in Calcutta, speculation, distributional failure, and a state that declined to act. The second is his much-discussed claim that substantial famines do not occur in independent, democratic countries with a relatively free press. Taken together, these seem to me to settle the old question of whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes, and to settle it against the physical sciences. If the causal work is done by entitlements and by political accountability, then a failed monsoon or a crop disease is at most an occasion and never a cause; and my own field's central product — the forecast of next season's yield anomaly — would appear to be at best a weak covariate in famine risk, at worst a distraction that keeps institutions trained on the wrong variable.

My questions, in the order that matters to me:

1. Granting that framing, what should take the place of yield anomalies in an early-warning index? If you were designing the indicator set for a famine-risk model that takes the entitlement approach seriously, which measurable quantities carry the causal weight, and how would you cope with the fact that the political variables appear to move far more slowly than the meteorological ones?

2. Why, then, do the operational agencies continue to invest so heavily in seasonal crop forecasting? Is that institutional inertia, or is there a defensible argument that production information remains indispensable — and if there is, what does that argument have to concede about the natural-versus-political question?

3. Is there any residual sense, on the entitlement account, in which a famine could properly be called a natural disaster rather than a political crime, or is the dichotomy simply a false one that I should stop using?

Where I have over-read Sen, please say so plainly, and explain what his actual position commits him to as distinct from what I have made of it.
