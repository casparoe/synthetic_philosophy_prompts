I'm auditing an argument that surfaced repeatedly in a reading group on well-being, and I would like a careful appraisal of the analogy at its centre rather than a general survey of the topic. The setting: we were working through the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the worry that the experiences we call pleasures share nothing beyond the fact that we like them, so that pleasure names a grab bag rather than a kind, and the hedonist's claim that one thing and one thing only is intrinsically good threatens to come out empty. One member of the group, an economist by training, insisted that this worry rests on a category mistake. Pleasures, on his account, are like heterogeneous commodities denominated in a common currency: apples, haircuts, and beach houses have no intrinsic property in common worth mentioning, yet we can rank them and trade them off against one another without strain, because there is a single scale — money — on which all of them are quotable. Hedonic tone, he said, is exactly that currency: it is what the taste of a ripe peach, the satisfaction of finally getting a lemma to work, and the relief of easing off tight shoes are all priced in, and their differing in every other respect is beside the point, since all the hedonist ever needed was a common unit of account, not a common essence. He then used the analogy to deflate two rival positions at once: Mill's appeal to qualities of pleasure that no quantity of a baser sort can match, and the Feldman-style programme of defining pleasure in terms of a pro-attitude toward a state of affairs. Both, he claimed, are merely quarrels about how the exchange rate gets fixed, not about whether there is a currency to fix it in.

Here is what I want. First, lay out the mapping explicitly, ideally as a table of correspondences: what plays the role of the commodity, the currency, the act of pricing, the exchange rate, and the market or institution in which exchange takes place — and what in the hedonist's picture each of these is supposed to pick out, including whether anything at all answers to that last item. Second, audit the analogy. I can see some disanalogies myself: money is a conventional measure attached to things that remain intrinsically heterogeneous, and its authority derives from a social practice of acceptance that has no obvious counterpart in the case of feeling; also, a price can be wrong or corrected by the market, and I am unsure what would correspond to a mistaken hedonic valuation. But I cannot tell which of these actually bite and which are decorative. Please separate the disanalogies that damage the specific use my friend makes of the analogy from the ones a hedonist could absorb without embarrassment, and say plainly which camp each falls in.

Third, and this is the part I most want: notice that at least three distinct claims are in play when someone presses the heterogeneity objection — that pleasures form a single natural kind, that they play a single functional or motivational role, and that they admit of trade-off on one common scale. My suspicion is that the currency analogy, even at its very best, can deliver only the third, and that my friend's deflationary conclusion requires one of the first two. Test that suspicion rather than assume it. Does the analogy get him functional unity as well, perhaps through the idea that everything priced in the currency thereby comes to govern choice in a uniform way? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does his dismissal of Mill and of the pro-attitude programme survive — or was the analogy silently choosing between those views and the felt-quality view all along, by making it look as though nothing but an exchange rate could be at stake? Finally, if the currency picture cannot carry that weight, propose a better analogy for the hedonist to work with, one that says something substantive about what the heterogeneous cases might share, and indicate in turn what that replacement analogy can and cannot license — I would rather have an honest analogue with modest reach than a flattering one that collapses on inspection.
