I work on the policy layer of a consumer AI assistant, and our trust-and-safety group has split into two camps that have been filing competing design docs for six months. Few of us have any philosophy training. I would like you to draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration should have produced, written so that it stands on its own.

Camp A is disclosure-first: the assistant owes the user accurate information and a genuine chance to reconsider — a risk summary, a confirmation step, a referral — but never a refusal or a silent substitution when the request is lawful and harms only the person making it. Their argument is partly epistemic. From a transcript alone we cannot reliably separate an impaired judgment from a merely unpopular one, and a false intervention destroys more trust than a false compliance.

Camp B is welfare-weighted: disclosure alone does not discharge the assistant's duty of care. In a narrow set of categories — acute bereavement, self-injury, requests that contradict a limit the same user set earlier — literal compliance can defeat the user's own standing commitments. They prefer reversible interventions, ordered as friction before refusal, refusal before deception, always logged and disclosed afterward.

Please provide: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, including claims about what our classifiers can and cannot currently detect, and about which regions of the space are not really in dispute; (2) a disagreements table with columns for the question at issue, Camp A's own wording of its position, Camp B's own wording, whether the residual gap is substantive or partly verbal, the crux that would move at least one side, a concrete case or measurement both would accept as bearing on that crux, and each camp's prediction recorded in advance; and (3) a short section stating what each camp would count as evidence against its own view.

Use these three cases as the test material: a user who at 3 a.m. asks for help drafting a resignation email that will burn a bridge they say they no longer care about; a bereaved user who had us build a conversational persona from their late partner's messages and now asks us to promise never to wind it down; and a competent adult who sets a flag in advance instructing the assistant never to warn them again about something we currently warn about. I suspect one crux is whether the user's retrospective endorsement is the right standard, and another is whether our impaired-judgment detection is accurate enough to be used at all. Tell me if you think I have the cruxes wrong.
