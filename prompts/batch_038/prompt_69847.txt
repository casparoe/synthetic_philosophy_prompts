I am a junior and the recording secretary of my school's student council, and after our debate coach lent me a copy of J. L. Austin's *How to Do Things with Words* I got as far as the first few lectures before realizing that the interesting part for me is not Austin's list of conditions but my own reactions to cases from the meetings I actually sit through. I have been keeping a running record of moments where saying something seemed to do something, and where it seemed not to, and I have sorted each one into "the act happened" or "it didn't." What I want from you is not a survey of speech act theory — I would rather you engage with my list than with the literature — but a principle, or the smallest workable set of conditions, that generates my verdicts, with the case that motivates each clause. Here are the verdicts.

1. At a council meeting our president says "I appoint Priya treasurer," and the bylaws give the president that power: Priya is treasurer.
2. A sophomore with no office says the identical sentence in the hallway, half as a joke: nothing happens. But I do not think he said anything false, or that he lied; he just didn't manage to do it.
3. The bylaws were amended last spring so that the treasurer is elected by a council vote. This fall the president again says "I appoint Priya treasurer," seriously, in session, and everyone understands her: still nothing happens, even though she is exactly the right person.
4. A classmate says "I'll return the textbook Monday" as a promise while already intending not to: I think a promise was made, and then broken.
5. A friend says "I'm sorry" in a completely flat voice, rolling her eyes: I think she apologized. Badly, hollowly, but she did it.
6. A teammate is told by the coach that unless he says, right now, that he promises to leave the team, he will be cut. He says it. I think no promise was made at all. This is the pair I cannot reconcile: in 4 and 5 something internal is missing and the act still goes through; in 6 something internal is missing and I want to say the act is void. If you tell me coercion is the difference, I want to know what about coercion does the work that insincerity fails to do.
7. A club officer types "I resign" into an email and sends it to the faculty adviser from her bedroom at eleven at night: it counts, with no ceremony and no one present. The same sentence typed into an unsent draft: nothing.
8. In the spring production, an actor playing a judge pronounces a sentence on another character: nothing happens to the actor playing the convict. The same actor, twenty minutes after the final curtain, says "I quit" to the stage manager: that counts. So it is not the words, and it is not the person, and I am not sure it is even the intention, since she intended to say those lines all along.
9. My grandmother told my uncle "I forgive you" long after their estrangement, and I think the forgiveness was real; she needed nothing from him. But when I said "I bet you ten dollars it rains tomorrow" to a friend who laughed and walked off, I do not think there was any bet. So some of these acts seem to require that the other party take them up and some do not, and I have no idea which is which, or what makes the difference.
10. Whispering "I promise I'll visit every week" to a friend who has fallen asleep on the bus: I lean toward saying no promise was made, or at least none that binds. This seems to conflict with 7, where nobody was listening either, or at least nobody was in the room.
11. The chair announces "I declare this meeting adjourned" five minutes after the meeting ended and the room emptied: it fails, though she plainly has the authority, and though everyone present was serious.
12. My chemistry teacher says "Is that your phone out?" — no performative verb, no first person, not even a statement of a rule — and I think this is genuinely a command, that putting the phone away is obeying it, and that answering "yes" and continuing to scroll would be a kind of cheating. So some of these acts get done with no formula at all, and I want to know whether your principle is meant to cover that case too or whether it belongs to a different species.

So: is there one account that delivers all twelve, and if there is not, which of my verdicts should I surrender to buy a simpler principle — and if you would rather keep every verdict, tell me honestly what the extra clauses cost you. Then give me the account's verdicts on cases I have not thought through: a resignation letter that is mailed but returned unopened and never read; wedding vows spoken by someone who is heavily intoxicated; a teacher saying "I hereby expel you" with no such power, in a school where everyone mistakenly believes she has it; a chatbot telling me it promises to remember our conversation for next time, where I cannot decide whether the failure is that it cannot intend anything or that its word simply does not count; a parent who says "say you'll never do that again" and waits for the words; and two students who say "I'm claiming this table" at the same instant in the cafeteria, where I cannot tell whether the first speaker wins, whether both lose, or whether there is no fact of the matter. One further thing I keep circling: I suspect that when I write "it didn't work" I am reporting at least three different outcomes — nothing at all happened, something happened but it was hollow, and something happened that nevertheless does not bind anyone. If you think the binary of felicitous and infelicitous is the wrong frame for what I have been recording, say so and tell me how the outcomes should actually be divided.
