Novelist here — fourth book comes out in the spring, so I've done something like forty interviews, panels, bookstore Q&As and a couple of university things this year, and something keeps happening in them that I've realized I have no word for. A reader or an interviewer asks what some detail meant: the recurring thing with the damaged greenhouse, say, or why a character keeps one particular song on repeat. The honest answer is almost always that I don't know, or that there was no reason, it just came out and I kept it because the sentence worked. But you can't say that — or rather you can say it once and then your publicist has a talk with you, because it makes it sound like the book happened to you by accident. So I answer. And here's the part that bothers me: the answer isn't a lie exactly, because I generate something on the spot that is genuinely good, it fits the book better than anything I actually thought about while writing it, and I can feel it click into place as I say it. Then within a year I remember having meant it. Not pretend to have meant it — actually remember sitting at the desk thinking that. And the thing takes on a life of its own: reviewers quote my answer back at me, a teacher writes to say she now teaches the greenhouse chapter using it, and in the next book I do the same trick deliberately, on purpose, so the invented intention becomes a real one for the follow-up while never having been one for the book it supposedly explains.

I see versions of it from the outside too. A huge series finishes, and years later the author announces that one character was in love with another all along, and the readership splits between people saying it was always there in the text if you read it right and people saying it isn't there at all and she's just adding it now. And a poet friend of mine does the opposite: she tells interviewers flat out that she has no idea what a given poem means, and the critics treat the denial as evidence that the meaning is unconscious and she's just not in touch with it, which is somehow worse for her than making something up would have been.

So — is there a name for this? I know the general line about not trusting what writers say about their own work, and I've heard the author-is-dead business plenty, but those are all about the reader's end of the telescope. I'm asking about the other end: the writer manufacturing an intention on demand that was never there, coming to believe it, and then watching it do real interpretive work in the world. It isn't lying (I believe it), it isn't an unconscious intention surfacing (there was nothing down there to surface — I keep notebooks and I've checked), and it isn't revising or retconning, because nobody touches the book. Is there an established concept for this in aesthetics or philosophy of literature, or a near miss I should know about? If the nearest concepts only partly fit, I want to know precisely where they stop fitting — which part of my description they cover and which part they leave over on the floor. And if nothing fits, coin me something: a name, a definition with actual conditions attached, and where the edges are. Tell me whether the poet's denial is the same phenomenon, a mirror image of it, or something else entirely; whether it still counts if the author only half believes her own answer; whether it counts if she says it to one reader privately and it never gets into print; and separately, whether any of this changes what the book meant. No citations or reading lists please — I can't chase them and I'd much rather you not guess at sources, just reason it through. When you're done, put the upshot at the end as a JSON object with these fields: verdict (one of established term / near misses only / nothing established), candidates (a list of objects, each with term, what_it_actually_covers, where_it_stops_fitting, fit_out_of_10), best_fit, proposed_name, definition, conditions (list of strings — what has to be true for the label to apply at all), boundary_cases (list of objects with case, in_or_out, reason), and changes_the_meaning (two or three sentences on whether the manufactured intention alters what the work means). Prose first, JSON last.
