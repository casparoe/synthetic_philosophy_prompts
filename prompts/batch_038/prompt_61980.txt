I'm in the third year of a PhD in formal epistemology, and I'm drafting a chapter on updating when the incoming evidence is not a proposition — conditional-probability constraints in particular, and the family of puzzles running from van Fraassen's (1981) Judy Benjamin case through the various distance-based proposals. Before I engage that literature, I want to pin down my own verdicts on a controlled set of messages and see what rule, if any, they add up to. I'd like you to do the reflective equilibrium for me.

The microdomain throughout: Judy has been dropped into a territory divided into a northern half and a southern half, each half divided into a first and a second sector. She is initially equally confident of each of the four quadrants. A radio operator she has reason to trust sends a message. (The wording is my own reconstruction; nothing in what follows turns on van Fraassen's exact phrasing, only on the numbers.)

My verdicts:

1. Message: "I can't be certain where you are, but if you're in the northern half, the odds are three to one that you're in the first sector." Verdict: Judy's credence in the North should rise above one half. The standard treatment, as I understand it — Jeffrey-style updating subject to the constraint, which for this prior coincides with the minimum-relative-entropy solution — has the gain to the northern first sector funded entirely out of the northern second sector, leaving the North–South margin exactly where it was. That is what I cannot swallow: nothing was said about the South, so I don't see how the South's total can remain untouched while one northern quadrant becomes the most probable of the four.

2. Message: "If you're in the northern half, you're in the first sector." Verdict: no shift; the North stays at one half. A categorical conditional whose antecedent is her being in the North seems to carry no information about whether that antecedent holds — even though this is the limiting case of (1) as the odds go to infinity.

3. Message: "You are not in the northern second sector." Verdict: straightforward conditionalization, so the North falls to one third and the South rises to two thirds.

4. Same words as (1), but the operator is known to invent plausible-sounding reports. Verdict: nothing changes, anywhere in the distribution.

5. Same words as (1), but Judy knows the operator knows precisely which quadrant she occupies and is forbidden from saying so outright, so he conveys as much as he can in the conditional form he is allowed. Verdict: the North rises, and by more than in (1) — had she been in the South, a remark about the North would have been useless to her, and a maximally informative truth-teller would have spoken about the South instead.

6. Message (1) is sent, and an hour later its exact mirror image about the southern half at the same odds. Verdict: the two cancel, and Judy ends where she began, at one half.

7. Judy's prior is not uniform: she is already twice as confident of the North as of the South, and already holds three-to-one odds on the first sector within the North. Message (1) arrives. Verdict: nothing changes.

What I want from you:

- The principle, or the small set of competing principles, that comes closest to generating these verdicts — stated precisely enough that I could apply it: a rule for the new North–South odds as a function of the prior and the announced odds, not a slogan about respecting the evidence.
- For each clause of that rule, which of my cases motivates the clause, and what breaks if the clause is dropped.
- Its verdicts on two cases I have not listed: a message announcing three-to-one odds *against* the northern first sector (does the North fall, and by the same amount it rose in (1)?), and a message that constrains the ratio of the two northern quadrants directly, without ever mentioning the North as an antecedent.
- An explicit statement of which of my verdicts cannot survive, and why. I already suspect (2) and (3) are in trouble together, since on a material reading they assert the same thing about the same quadrant; but I don't know whether the fix is to deny that conditionals express propositions, to deny that (2) and (3) have the same content, or simply to abandon (2). Tell me which, with the argument, and say whether the choice is forced by the other verdicts or is independent of them.
- Whether (4) and (5) show that the real question was never about the constraint itself but about the selection process that produced the message. If so, say what is left of the puzzle as a problem for updating rules rather than for the pragmatics of testimony — and whether a rule that is sensitive only to the announced number can be more than an approximation to one that is not.

Please open with a single sentence giving your bottom line — which verdict or verdicts I must abandon and the rule you recommend in their place — and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of my cases back to me.
