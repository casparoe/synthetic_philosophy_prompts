You are a philosopher whose specialty is the ethics of influence: autonomy, consent, and the line between legitimate persuasion and undue pressure. You have been retained as a consultant by an institutional review board that adjudicates complaints about persuasive systems — clinical communication and consent processes, fundraising and marketing campaigns, recommender feeds and interface defaults, and conversational AI agents designed to retain or steer users. The board has accumulated a large and mutually inconsistent body of case rulings. Before it reviews a single further case, and before it reads another proposed account of manipulation, it wants a specification: the acceptance criteria an account must meet to be usable here. That specification is your deliverable. Think of yourself as writing requirements for a system you will not build, so that the choice among candidate builds becomes a matter of inspection rather than taste.

Work carefully and in two stages. First, produce a short, unnumbered brainstorming section — no more than three or four paragraphs — in which you survey the territory: the phenomena the concept has to cover, the theoretical pressures bearing on any definition, and the places where intuitions about particular cases pull in opposite directions. Do not yet commit to criteria. Second, produce the specification itself.

**The specification.** Each entry must contain: a name; the requirement, stated precisely enough that one could determine by inspection whether a proposed account satisfies it; the case or consideration that motivates it — a concrete scenario, or a structural feature of the concept, showing why an account indifferent to this requirement would be inadequate; what goes wrong when it is violated, in the form of the counterexample it licenses, the distinction it collapses, or the verdict it delivers that cannot be accepted; and its status, marked as non-negotiable, strongly preferred, or a demand peculiar to one camp in the debate.

Below is a candidate list assembled from the board's own deliberations. Assess each item rather than adopting it: sharpen those that survive, reject those that turn out not to be genuine requirements of an account of manipulation but merely of some further normative project, and add whatever is missing. Several of them are probably confused as stated.

1. *Discrimination.* The account must separate manipulative influence from good rational persuasion, and must not achieve the separation by declaring that anything effective is thereby suspect. Contrast a counsel's closing argument and a surgeon's lucid presentation of odds with an appeal to a patient's needle phobia that bears no relation to the merits of the treatment.
2. *Non-triviality.* Every act of communication frames its content; there is no frameless disclosure, since a fact must be stated in some words, in some order, with some statistic and some emphasis. An account on which all communication is manipulation, or on which the unavoidable features of presentation count as leverage, gives the board nothing to prohibit and nothing to permit.
3. *Success-independence.* A clumsy, transparently self-serving pitch that moves nobody still seems to be an attempt at the same thing a subtle appeal successfully performs. The account must locate the relevant property in the act or the intention rather than in the effect, while explaining why success and failure are not morally equivalent.
4. *Hiddenness.* Many paradigms are covert, yet the sales technique announced in advance by the person using it, and the interface pattern disclosed in a terms-of-service document, do not obviously improve by being disclosed; while the surprise party, the therapist's calculated provocation, and the parent's sleight of hand with a toddler's medicine are covert and unobjectionable. The account must settle whether transparency is a criterion of what manipulation is or of when it is wrong, and must not slide between the two.
5. *Distributed and structural agency.* Most of the board's docket involves no identifiable manipulator: a ranking objective tuned years ago, a default inherited from a legacy system, an interface shaped by a thousand A/B tests. The account must either extend to influence with no intending subject — saying who, if anyone, is answerable for it — or explain what it means when it calls such cases merely manipulation-like. An account restricted to intentional interpersonal conduct leaves the board silent about its main workload.
6. *Separation from, and comparison with, coercion.* The account must preserve the difference in mechanism between narrowing a person's options and distorting their appraisal of the options they have, while handling hybrids: a threat voiced as solicitude, a menu arranged so that only one item is bearable, the exploitative offer. It must also leave room for the judgment that some manipulation is graver than some coercion.
7. *Vulnerability and the baseline.* Fear, grief, exhaustion, addiction, inexperience, dependence, and cognitive decline are the classic levers. But any account that speaks of exploiting a weakness owes a standard of what counts as a weakness and against whose normalcy, and must handle the case in which the target's psychology was itself shaped by the influencer's earlier conduct — a long-standing platform relationship, an enclosed community, preferences adapted to the situation the influencer maintains.
8. *Authorization and role.* The same act changes character when performed by a parent, requested by a patient, invited by a spouse, expected at a poker table, or forewarned in a negotiation. The account must explain how standing permission, role, and reasonably presumed consent alter the analysis without degenerating into the view that a signed form settles everything, and it must handle permissions that were themselves extracted by the conduct they authorize.
9. *Anti-circularity, and a wrong-making feature.* If manipulation is defined as the influence that wrongs its target, the account cannot explain the wrong. It must identify the mechanism — bypassing deliberation, corrupting it, using someone as a tool, degrading their authorship of their own choices — in terms that can then generate verdicts about failures, successes, harmlessly beneficial outcomes, and authorized cases, and that remain coherent when the manipulation harms the target not at all.
10. *Applicability under epistemic limits.* The board's members cannot run controlled trials on each campaign and cannot read intentions. If a criterion is counterfactual — whether the target would have decided otherwise absent the framing — say what evidence may substitute for it. A standard the regulated cannot check is a defect in the standard, not merely in its administration.
11. *Gradability and commensurability.* Manipulation comes in degrees and the board must rank mechanisms against one another — a default, an emotional appeal, a fabricated statistic, a manufactured scarcity — and must weigh them against the goods the system delivers, since its task is regulation and not prohibition.
12. *Empirical anchoring without hostage-taking.* The account should be consistent with what is established about how influence actually works — the finding that the identical public-health program is preferred when described in terms of lives saved and rejected when described in terms of lives lost, the force of opt-out defaults, commitment-and-consistency sequences, reciprocity inducements, deference to apparent authority — while depending as little as possible on results that are contested or replication-sensitive. State which findings the account leans on and how much weight it can bear if some of them weaken.

**Then answer three questions.** First: which of the items on your final list are genuine requirements of an account of manipulation, and which are in truth demands imported from a neighboring project — the regulation of markets, the theory of autonomy, the analysis of deception — that a definition need not satisfy at all? Be blunt about the ones you demote. Second: which single requirement eliminates the largest number of otherwise attractive accounts, and why is it so costly? Third: is any subset of the requirements jointly unsatisfiable? I suspect that at least one pair cannot both be met, in which case the list is itself a result and the board needs to know which one to abandon; I do not know which pair, and identifying it is the most valuable part of your report. Argue for the impossibility rather than asserting it, and if you conclude that everything on the list can in principle be satisfied together, say what kind of account would have to do it.

**Finally, and briefly** — no more than two hundred words — assess three families of account against your list: those that define manipulation by the psychological mechanism it employs (influence that operates by non-rational or arational routes rather than by the target's assessment of reasons), those that define it by transparency (influence the target could not endorse under full awareness of how it works), and those that define it by the pressure or leverage applied (influence that works by exploiting a vulnerability, fear, or weakness of will). State which requirement each family violates first.

Constraints on the report: no citations, no bibliography, no reading list, and no named attributions to authors or works — I want argument, not scholarship, and everything must stand on the reasoning and the cases alone. Do not invent empirical studies; the findings mentioned above are enough, and if you want another, describe the phenomenon rather than the paper. Keep your verdicts explicit and your hedging minimal. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words, with the numbered specification as the bulk of it.
