Write a dialogue between two philosophers who are both visiting the same city for a week and who disagree about whether the city's ordinary street-and-table conventions bind them.

The scene should be built out of three episodes from a single afternoon, and the characters should keep returning to them as test cases rather than arguing in the abstract:

1. They cross a wide boulevard against a red pedestrian signal at a spot where dozens of locals are already doing the same, where drivers plainly anticipate it and adjust, and where the crossing is nevertheless straightforwardly illegal.
2. They join what is nominally a queue for a bus but is in practice two loose clusters whose order is tracked by mutual acknowledgment — a nod, a remembered shoulder — rather than by physical position. One of the philosophers nearly disrupts it by standing where a line would be.
3. They have lunch at a café where service is included in the bill, but where regulars all drop a few coins in a dish by the door as they leave. A tourist at the next table leaves a large banknote there, and the proprietor's reaction is visibly uncomfortable.

Give each of them the following position, and make both positions genuinely defensible rather than one being the sensible default. The first holds that a settled local scheme of conventional norms generates real, if defeasible, obligations for anyone acting inside it, owed to the participants collectively — obligations that do not derive from harm, from reliance, or from any agreement the visitor has made. The second holds that such obligations attach only to those who are parties to the practice in the relevant sense, the people whose ongoing compliance constitutes it; a visitor's duties are exhausted by not harming identifiable others, not free-riding on others' compliance, and not misleading anyone about their own intentions.

The dialogue must contain real back-and-forth: each character answers the other's actual argument, concedes what has to be conceded, and presses the point where the other's view is genuinely strained. In particular, work through the following pressures:

- Whether the assurance or signaling function of a convention can operate for someone whom locals already expect to deviate, and what follows if it cannot.
- The episode in which the local convention runs against the law, so that the visitor who conforms to the convention is breaking a rule and the visitor who obeys the rule is defying the convention. Each character should say what their view implies there, and neither answer should be easy.
- Whether the tourist's oversized tip is a moral failure, a merely clumsy supererogation, or nothing at all — and note that the two views should predict different verdicts on at least one of the three episodes. Make that divergence explicit and make it matter.
- The visitor who objects to a convention in good conscience — say, one who believes the tipping dish papers over an exploitative wage structure — and whether conscientious objection excuses non-participation.
- The reverse case, briefly: a long-time resident who keeps importing the conventions of the country they left, and whether they are doing anything different from what the visitor does.

Do not resolve the disagreement tidily. Instead, end with each character identifying the case that is hardest for their own view, stating why their principles generate the wrong answer in it, and sketching the smallest revision that would handle it.

Keep the argument grounded in the episodes and in the structure of the positions themselves; do not have the characters appeal to the authority of named philosophers or cite literature. Aim for roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words.
