I am working on a chapter of my dissertation concerning the legitimacy of international criminal law, and I want to press a claim from Pascal against an episode that ought either to vindicate it or to break it: the trial of the major war criminals at Nuremberg in 1945–46.

The claim comes from one of the fragments in the *Pensées* on justice and force, specifically the closing line of the passage headed "Justice, force": "ne pouvant faire que ce qui est juste fût fort, on a fait que ce qui est fort fût juste" (spelling as in a nineteenth-century edition). That is: since we could not make what is just strong, we made what is strong just, so that justice and force would stand together and there would be peace, which Pascal calls the sovereign good. The usual gloss on this — a flat might-makes-right — is too crude for my purposes, so I have been distinguishing three readings of what it is to make the strong just:

1. **Genetic**: whoever holds force determines the content of what counts as law; justice is downstream of power.
2. **Legitimation**: force does not determine content but supplies authority after the fact, by producing an order stable enough that obedience hardens into custom and the origin of the arrangement stops being noticed.
3. **Termination**: the reason force wins is epistemic rather than moral. Justice is inherently disputable, whereas force is palpable and unmistakable; force is therefore deployed to terminate a dispute that argument cannot close. This reading is my own, built from neighbouring remarks in the same fragment — that majority decision is the best route because it is visible, and that it would be well if people obeyed laws and customs because they are laws rather than because they are true or just. Tell me plainly if I have distorted the text here.

The case, with the facts I am taking as given so that we are not arguing about them: the tribunal was constituted by the London Charter of August 1945, an agreement among the four occupying powers, which also appointed the judges; the indictment included crimes against peace, which the defense argued was no crime before the Charter, with the 1928 Kellogg-Briand Pact the nearest thing to a pre-existing prohibition; three of the defendants were acquitted; the Soviet prosecution's attempt to pin the Katyn massacre on the Germans produced no finding in the judgment; and the charge against Dönitz over unrestricted submarine warfare was blunted by an affidavit from Admiral Nimitz confirming that the United States had done the same.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not an essay on victor's justice in general. Identify where the force sits and where the justice sits at each of those pressure points — the Charter, the ex post facto objection, the acquittals, Katyn, Dönitz — and say on which of my three readings Nuremberg confirms Pascal and on which it strains him. The acquittals and the abandoned Katyn charge interest me most, since a tribunal created by victors that nevertheless acquitted looks less like justice serving force than like force deferring to justice; unless, of course, a handful of acquittals is the cheapest possible purchase of legitimacy for a great many convictions. I cannot tell which of these it is and I suspect the answer differs by reading.

Then run the analysis in the other direction. Nuremberg is standardly narrated as the moment power paid tribute to reason — that is the shape of Jackson's own opening — and as the origin of a practice of self-binding that eventually issued in the ad hoc tribunals and the ICC. Ask whether the Pascalian apparatus can account for that trajectory at all, or whether it predicts something narrower: a jurisdiction whose judgments are enforced precisely against those states too weak to refuse enforcement. And be explicit about where the lens falls silent. These fragments concern a sovereign and his subjects, custom, and domestic peace; they were not written with a treaty-based tribunal, a multinational bench, or the aspiration to norms binding on the strong in mind. I would rather be told what is lost in the transfer than have it papered over.

Finally, tell me whether Pascal earns his place. Set against the realist victor's-justice objection, the *nullum crimen* objection, and the liberal self-binding account, does this framing explain anything those do not — or is it merely a more vivid way of saying what legal realism already says? If it is the latter, I would like to know now rather than in a draft.
