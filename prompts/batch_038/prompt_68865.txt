I am trying to determine what principle, if any, governs my own judgments about weighting the future, and I would like it extracted from a set of verdicts rather than from the literature. I know the standard material well enough that a survey would waste my time: Ramsey's view that a pure rate of time preference reflects a failure of imagination rather than a reason; Koopmans' result that some impatience is required for a well-defined optimum on an infinite horizon, and the familiar retort that the nonexistence of a maximum does not make zero impatience irrational; Parfit's discussion in Reasons and Persons of the asymmetry between our attitudes to past and future pain; the disagreement between Stern and Nordhaus over the pure rate. Bring any of that in only where it bears on the cases below, and do not spend the answer on it.

Here are the verdicts. Unless I say otherwise, assume away inflation, risk of non-delivery, and any need for liquidity.

1. Offered $1,000 today or $1,060 in a year, with the market paying 6 percent risk-free: taking the money today is a mistake, but the mistake is arithmetic. The year itself contributes nothing to the wrongness.

2. Offered $1,000 today or $1,000 in a year, with no investment available and nothing I want to buy: I would take it today, and I do not think I am making a mistake, but when I ask what I am getting for the wait I cannot answer. Something is moving me and I cannot name it.

3. A woman who gives exactly equal weight to every moment of her own future, however distant, strikes me as admirable when the currency is suffering: she would accept a headache in forty years to spare a stranger a migraine tomorrow, and I call that close to saintly. The same woman strikes me as defective when the currency is money: she sees no reason to prefer having her $1,000 sooner. I suspect I am reacting not to the trade but to what such indifference suggests about her having no plans, in which case the verdict is not about discounting at all. But when I stipulate that she is full of plans and simply sees no reason to prefer the earlier date, the unease does not entirely go away, and I cannot say what is left of it.

4. It is wrong — I feel no uncertainty here — for a planner to weight a child's suffering in 2125 below identical suffering in 2025 purely because of the date. It is not wrong in the same way for me to weight my own suffering next year below my own suffering next week. My instinct is that the difference lies in who is choosing for whom: the planner weighs people other than himself, whereas I weigh only myself. But I also find persuasive the thought that my future self is psychologically remote from me in something like the way another person is, which would erase the difference. I do not know how to hold both.

5. In a hospital, I would rather be told that I endured eight hours of surgery yesterday than that I will undergo one hour tomorrow. That I am so struck is a fact about me; that it is a reason is something I deny. Nothing about a pain's location in the past makes preferring it defensible. So I have here a category I call intelligible-but-unjustified, and I want to know whether it is stable or whether I am confusing being excused with being right. Case 2 has the same shape, and there I also want to insist I am not at fault.

6. My grandmother's will withheld my inheritance until I was forty, on the ground that I would by then be likelier to be someone who could use it well. She was right, and I would do the same for a grandchild. Had she withheld it on the ground that I would by then have become a wastrel and so deserved less, I would call it wrong. Both weight a later date differently because of who occupies it; my verdicts differ. I assume the difference is that in the first case the delay lets the benefit do more good and in the second it merely tracks diminished worthiness, but I do not know whether that is a distinction or a rationalization.

7. A scheme that pays less compensation for an injury sustained ten years ago than for the same injury sustained today, on grounds of elapsed time alone, strikes me as unjust. Yet I feel no obligation to compensate myself for a pain that is over. My tentative explanation is again that the state aggregates across persons and I do not, which would make this a restatement of case 4.

8. A planner that discounts the near future steeply and the far future gently seems prudent, because it hedges against its own uncertainty about the rate. My own pattern of resolving in January to eat well in March and then, in March, eating badly seems a defect that precommitment should correct. Same shape, opposite verdicts. I suspect the difference is that the planner's shape is generated by uncertainty and mine by weakness, but I am not certain that weakness is not itself information about how I shall actually feel.

9. I think it right to spend now to prevent the extinction of a species whose last member dies in four hundred years, and to plant a tree whose shade I shall never sit in; I think it vain but not wrong to spend money ensuring a statue of me survives. I do not weight these by distance but by what they are.

My tentative principle, which I expect to fail: position in time is never itself a reason. Whatever work is being done is done by something else — uncertainty about survival or about the character of the future, the changing needs or identity of the person at that time, the returns available elsewhere, or the fact that the chooser is deciding for someone other than himself. Where I feel the pull of mere distance I am either feeling one of these badly, or feeling nothing and dignifying it as a preference.

What I want:

(a) State a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, precisely enough that it is clear what each rules out, and for each clause say which case above forces it in.

(b) Report which of my verdicts each candidate reproduces and which it cannot. I would rather be told that case 3 or case 5 must go than be handed a principle with an epicycle bolted on to save it; if you judge an epicycle genuinely necessary, state what it costs elsewhere.

(c) Decide explicitly between the complex principle that preserves every verdict and the simpler one that overturns at least one, and say which you would adopt and why.

(d) Give the verdict your recommended principle yields on three cases I have not adjudicated. First: a generation deciding whether to impose a small cost on people living three hundred years hence to avert a catastrophe four hundred years hence — may that generation discount the more distant benefit, or does letting each generation apply its own rate smuggle pure time preference back in through compounding? Second: an agent who weights the far future above the near on the ground that it will contain vastly more people — is that mere-time discounting run in reverse, or something categorially different? Third: an hour I can spend on a book that pays off in twenty years or on a pleasure available now, where I take the pleasure without guilt. Then add one case of your own that you think my principle, or your repair of it, handles badly.

(e) Say how confident you are in your central claim, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
