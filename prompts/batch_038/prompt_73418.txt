You are a philosopher of history who has advised heritage institutions on contested commemorations; think carefully, and sketch your considerations briefly before drafting.

I am a novelist by trade — I invent things for a living — and I now sit on the historical commission of the town where I grew up, where the distance between an invented thing and a true one has become uncomfortably concrete. Our sesquicentennial program repeats the story every resident learned in school: in the hard winter of 1874 the mill owner opened his granary and kept the town from starving. Last spring an archivist working through the mill's ledgers established that he sold grain at famine prices, and that the relief the town actually remembers came from a mutual aid society of the mill's women workers, whose names appear nowhere in the record we possess. The commission must decide this month whether the program text and the plaque on the mill wall change. Two commissioners, one of them a descendant, hold that a century and a half of communal remembering is itself a historical fact the archive cannot overrule, and that the story did real work — it held a town together — which no correction will do.

Draft the decision memo I will circulate: a clear recommendation, a time frame, and, if anything is to be revised, the replacement wording. Separate the distinct claims in play by treating the plaque in three registers — as an assertion, as a performance, and as a transmission — and say which it chiefly is and what follows from that. Engage the descendant's argument at its strongest rather than as a prejudice to be dissolved, state plainly what the recommendation costs us, and specify what would have to be true for the opposite recommendation to be the right one. Argue the matter; do not cite authorities or supply a reading list.
