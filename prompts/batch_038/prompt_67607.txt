I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about Tolstoy's *The Kreutzer Sonata*, and I need a third party who can actually adjudicate, because we are both convinced the other is misreading the philosophy rather than the fiction.

Write as a professional philosopher of aesthetics who also has real competence in the philosophy of music and in nineteenth-century German philosophy — someone who can hold Schopenhauer's metaphysics of music and Tolstoy's moral theory of art in mind simultaneously without collapsing one into an illustration of the other.

Here is the situation. I am drafting a short essay for a collection on music and the will, and my claim is that *The Kreutzer Sonata* is routinely misfiled: it is read as Tolstoy's attack on music's power, and hence as an anti-Schopenhauerian document, when in fact Pozdnyshev is a Schopenhauerian listener who fails at Schopenhauerian listening, and Tolstoy's own later theory of art — the infection account he worked out in *What Is Art?* — is very nearly the inverse of Schopenhauer's, while the novella's actual phenomenology of the performance scene sides with Schopenhauer against Tolstoy's theory. My friend's counter is that this rescue is empty. On his reading, the murder is a counterexample generated from inside Schopenhauer's own premises: Schopenhauer holds that music is not, like the other arts, a copy of the Ideas but an unmediated presentation of the will itself, and that it accordingly communicates joy and sorrow in the abstract, stripped of the particular occasions and objects that cause them in life — which is why, for him, music's pleasure is not the stirring of real desire. Pozdnyshev hears precisely that abstraction and is driven straight into the most particular, individuated, territorial desire imaginable, ending with a dagger. My friend says that whatever Schopenhauer's theory of the will-less subject of knowledge promises, it cannot explain a listener whose will is not merely stirred but *recruited*, and that my defense — namely that Pozdnyshev never entered contemplation at all, since he supplied his own narrative of adultery over the top of the music — makes Schopenhauer's account unfalsifiable: any listener who acts is by stipulation not having an aesthetic experience, and any listener who doesn't act confirms the theory.

I want you to think carefully about this rather than settle for a survey. Please structure the response in five movements, and do the first one explicitly before you commit to anything:

1. **Brainstorming and mapping.** Before writing the criticism, lay out the two aesthetics as I have characterized them, and identify the precise hinge propositions on which the dispute turns. I expect at least three candidate hinges — for example, whether music's content is individuated or abstract; whether aesthetic experience requires the suspension of practical interest and what happens to that requirement in an art form that Schopenhauer himself says acts on us more directly than any other; and whether a listener's jealousy counts as music's doing or as the listener's contribution. Say which hinge actually decides the argument and why, and flag any place where you think my characterization of Schopenhauer is too clean or my characterization of Tolstoy too neat.

2. **The reading.** Read the novella through Schopenhauer's aesthetics of music, taking both seriously. Work with the material I assume is common ground: the train frame and Pozdnyshev's confessional monologue; his account of marriage and of women as objects of appetite; the arrival of the violinist Troukhatchevsky; the domestic performance of Beethoven's sonata for violin and piano, the one Tolstoy named the book after; Pozdnyshev's insistence that music can transport a person into a state that is not his own, and the way that state first reconciles him to his wife and then, in retrospect, licenses his jealousy; the unexpected return, the two of them at the table, the killing, and his acquittal. Show where Schopenhauer's account genuinely illuminates what Pozdnyshev reports about the music — including the point, which I think is underexploited, that a listener who is simultaneously a spouse and a claimant on a person is a listener for whom the distinction between contemplating the will and being moved by it may not be available at all. Equally, show where the novella resists the Schopenhauerian reading and the honest critic has to say so: Tolstoy wrote an explicit epilogue spelling out the intended moral about carnal love and asceticism, and Pozdnyshev is a self-dramatizing misogynist whose testimony about his own inner life may be worth less than my argument needs it to be. Do not soften either of those.

3. **Verdict.** Tell me plainly whether my reading or my friend's holds up, or whether the truth is a third thing — for instance that Schopenhauer is right about what music presents and wrong about what that presentation does to a particular embodied listener, or that Tolstoy's infection theory and Schopenhauer's will-copy theory make the same prediction in this case for incompatible reasons. Do not end on a non-committal note; a qualified verdict is fine, a shrug is not. Justify it with the actual argument rather than with a summary of the two positions.

4. **The strongest reply against your verdict.** Give my friend, or Schopenhauer, whichever side your verdict damages, the best available answer, and then say whether it survives. Here I want you to engage the philosophy of music on its own terms: the arousal and cognitivist accounts of musical expressiveness, the question of whether emotional properties in music can be heard without being felt, and the worry that Schopenhauer's abstraction claim is doing double duty as both a theory of content and a theory of effect. If you bring in Nietzsche's later physiological attack on Schopenhauerian music as stimulant, do so only where it earns its place.

5. **What the fiction adds.** End by saying what the novella contributes to this dispute that argument alone could not: what it shows about the ethics of listening, or about the difference between an aesthetics that asks what music reveals and an aesthetics that asks what music does, that a treatise would have missed. If you think there is a case for saying the novella exposes a structural problem in Schopenhauer's system — that the one art he exempts from the Ideas is also the one art whose reception he cannot describe without smuggling in the listener's own biography — say so and press it.

Two further things I would find useful. First, the historical irony of the piece Tolstoy chose: Beethoven dedicated the sonata to the violinist whose name it now carries, and that violinist reportedly considered it unintelligible and refused to play it. Consider whether that fact is mere decoration or whether it does real work for one side of the argument — a work that its own dedicatee could not receive, chosen by a novelist to prove that reception is never innocent. Second, if you think Janáček's string quartet inspired by the novella — music written about a story about music — belongs in the closing section as a test of your verdict, include it briefly; if it doesn't, say why not.
