I'm a founding engineer at a 12-person startup that makes an agent which handles insurance claims end to end, and next month I have to sit infront of an enterprise customer's risk committee and defend our safety story. Our head of research wrote a memo last week that the customer's procurement people have already gotten hold of, and apparently they're going to read parts of it back to me. He's smart and I trust him on engineering, but I'm not sure he's careful about alignment terminology, and the whole memo leans on a framing I don't fully understand.

Here's roughly what it says:

"Our agent is what used to be called an approval-directed agent, which means it doesn't have goals of its own. Instead of writing down an objective and letting it search for ways to satisfy that, we define its objective as the approval rating a designated human pricipal would give to its actions. Two things follow. First, it can't misgeneralize: misgeneralization happens when a learned objective comes apart from the objective you intended, but here the objective just *is* the principal's approval, so there's nothing in the training data for the system to latch onto that isn't already the target. Second, there's no instrumental pressure to resist being switched off or to resist having its reward function edited, because a shutdown it expects the principal would approve of scores exactly as high as staying on. This is basically what RLHF already does, only RLHF does it approximately, with a learned reward model standing in for the principal. The honest criticism is scalability: no human can rate every action of a competent agent, so in practice you end up optimizing a noisy proxy anyway, which is why the field moved on to scalable oversight and stopped talking about approval-directedness."

Can you go through this and tell me what's actually right? I want to know which sentences I can repeat to that committee without embarrassment, which ones are subtle distortions that a person who works on this stuff would flag immediately, and which are flat-out wrong. I'm inclined to think the RLHF comparison is fair and most of the rest is marketing gloss, but I don't know whether the misgeneralization argument is stronger than I'm giving it credit for, and I have no idea how to answer if someone asks whether our agent would resist being shut down.

Tell me which of his two "things follow" claims you think is defensible and which isn't, and why; tell me whether his overall conclusion (that this design is protected against goal misgeneralization) survives once the summary is corrected, or whether it's exactly backwards.
