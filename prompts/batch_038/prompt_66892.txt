I work in a county court administrator's office and I've been putting together a memo for our jury committee, but I keep getting stuck on something and I don't have the vocabulary for it. Heres the pattern. We go to a lot of trouble to keep jurors insulated from each other and from the outside world: they're told not to discuss the case with anyone, not to look anything up online, not to go see the scene themselves, not to form an opinion until all the evidence is in. Alternate jurors sit through the whole trial and then get dismissed by lot right before the jury retires, and if one has to be swapped in after deliberations have already started, the judge tells them to throw out everything they'd done and start deliberating from scrach. Then we put all twelve in a room and tell them the whole point is to talk each other into agreement, and we require all twelve to agree or there's no verdict at all. If they hang, the case gets retried in front of a brand new jury that is never told the first one split eleven to one. So a near-unanimous split produces exactly the same output as a total collapse, and then it's erased.

I keep wanting to say these rules can't all be aimed at the same thing. The insulation stuff looks like it's trying to turn twelve people into twelve independent estimates of something, and then the deliberation and unanimity stuff looks like it's built to destroy that independence and discard whatever disagreement survives it. Am I misreading that? What has to be true about juries for all of it to hang together — something about how reliable an individual juror is on their own, or about what the verdict is even for, because a straight accuracy story doesn't seem to fit. My hunch, which I can't defend, is that unanimity isn't a truth-finding device but a consent device: the verdict has to be attributable to twelve specific people who each personally said yes, and the deliberation is there to make that attribution real rather than to improve the answer. Tell me whether the practice actually needs that hunch, or whether it's underdetermined and several quite different views would license the same rules — and say where those views would predict different behavior. Then tell me what a redesign aimed purely at getting the right answer would look like: bigger jury, majority vote, no deliberation, jurors allowed to do their own research. Which of the current rules falls first, and what does the system lose when it goes?
