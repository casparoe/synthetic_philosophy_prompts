My roommate and I got into the socialist calculation debate over the weekend and he walked me into an argument that seems to settle it in one paragraph, which is exactly why I don't buy it — but I can't locate the bad step.

(1) Mises's claim is that rational economic calculation means comparing wildly heterogeneous inputs and outputs in one common unit, and the only unit that ever does that job is money prices for the means of production.

(2) Prices for the means of production can only come out of actual exchange between seperate owners; if one owner holds everything there is nothing to exchange, hence no such prices, hence no calculation.

(3) So socialism isn't merely inefficient or corruptible, it's impossible in principle — better computers and better intentions don't touch the objection.

(4) Now look inside a big firm. Divisions hand each other engines, castings, legal work, server time. None of it is bought or sold; the numbers booked against those internal transfers are set by accountants and managers. On the inside, a corporation is exactly the situation (2) says can't generate prices.

(5) Corporations on that model exist at national-economy scale, allocate capital across hundreds of thousands of people and product lines, decide what gets built where, and mostly dont collapse while doing it.

(6) So rational allocation without genuine market prices in the means of production is possible; what limits it is an empirical matter of administrative scale, not a conceptual one.

(7) Therefore (3) is false, and false for a reason that has nothing to do with socialism: the existence of any sufficiently large corporation already refutes it, and nobody has to say a word about the Soviet Union.

He caps it with what he calls the Coasean point turned around — firm boundaries get drawn wherever directing something internally stops being cheaper than buying it on the market, so the whole economy is just a continuum of planning islands of different sizes, and socialism is the limiting case where one island covers everything. Nothing special happens at the limit, he says, except that the administration gets worse, and "worse" is a quantity you can measure.

The clause I can't swallow is the last one in (7). Going from "planning works at some sizes, with whatever props a firm happens to have" to "planning works at every size, so the impossibility claim is dead" feels like an enormous jump, but when I say so he points out that (6) never claimed the props vanish — only that they make things harder rather than impossible — and I have no answer to that. I also don't want to retreat into "well, the USSR actually collapsed," b/c that's history, not the argument.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine going in? If your view is instead that the chain is valid and the real question is which premise costs least to give up, say that and name the premise. And if you think Lange's trial-and-error planning board is supposed to plug the gap between (6) and (7), tell me what exactly it's plugging and with what — I want the mechanism, not the name of the mechanism.

Either way, commit. I want one diagnosis argued for, not a survey of all the candidate exits with a polite paragraph each. Then tell me what my roommate would have to give up to keep (7), and what your diagnosis costs whoever rejects it — I'd rather you pick a side and be wrong than hedge across all of them.
