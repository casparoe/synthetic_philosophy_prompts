I spent thirty-eight years as a geochemist, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with the same habit that made me a tolerable analyst: distrust of any inference whose conclusion is unusually convenient. I have assembled the following argument about whether market prices can be said to carry knowledge, and I can no longer tell whether I have proved something or merely confused myself elegantly.

1. Suppose two agents are Bayesian, share a common prior over the states of the world, are each known to the other to be Bayesian, and each knows the structure of the other's information. If they announce their posteriors, those posteriors become common knowledge.
2. When two Bayesian agents with a common prior have common knowledge of their posteriors, the posteriors coincide. Rational agents cannot knowingly disagree.
3. The no-trade result of Milgrom and Stokey sharpens the point: under those conditions no speculative trade can be acceptable to both sides, because the mere act of offering it would disclose the offerer's private information and be absorbed into the price before anyone accepted.
4. So in a market made of such agents the prevailing price already is the common posterior. Nothing further can be learned from it, since it can move neither on disagreement nor on informed trading.
5. Yet prices move constantly, and the volume of purely speculative trade that clears each day is enormous — trade that is not hedging, not liquidity management, not rebalancing.
6. Therefore at least one of the conditions in (1) fails in actual markets: the agents do not share a prior, or they are not Bayesian, or the information structure is not common knowledge.
7. But if (6) holds, a price is not the output of Bayesian pooling at all. It is the output of disagreement, of error, or of noise.
8. And a price that is the output of noise conveys nothing; while a price that fully reflects all available information destroys the incentive to acquire the information it reflects, so that it cannot stay fully efficient either.
9. Hence the thesis that prices aggregate dispersed knowledge is true only if it is false, and false only if it is true.
10. So the epistemic defence of markets — that a price knows more than any committee because it condenses information no participant holds in full — rests on nothing.

My difficulty is that I cannot see how to refuse (1) through (3), which look to me like mathematics rather than opinion; nor (8), which looks like arithmetic about incentives; nor (7), since a number produced by people who are confused does seem to me to be a confused number. In my working life I would have treated (9) as an instrument that reports its own miscalibration, and then asked whether the fault lies in the instrument or in the reading. I cannot decide, and I should like the diagnosis from someone who knows this literature better than I do.

If I had to choose, I would rather keep (3) than (5), because I distrust self-reported motives for trading more than I distrust a theorem. I would prefer that inclination be examined rather than indulged.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — identify the step you reject and say in a single clause why — and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. Explain why that step looked acceptable to me when I wrote it, what it seemed to license, and what is lost by surrendering it. Then price the alternatives: for each family of reply you know — the heterogeneous-priors route, the noise-trader and partially revealing equilibrium route, the market-microstructure route on which information enters prices through observed order flow rather than through agreement among traders, and any deflationary route on which a price need not be an optimal Bayesian aggregator in order to outperform a committee — state what that family must concede to escape (10) and how much of (10) survives the concession. If more than one of my steps fails, tell me which failure is load-bearing and which is merely decorative. I am not asking for references; I am asking for the reasoning, and for the size of the error bars.
