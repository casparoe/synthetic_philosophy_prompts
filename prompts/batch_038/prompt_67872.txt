My grandmother died in March. She was the kind of person who spent sixty years on one garden and kept up friendships with something like forty people, and if you'd asked her what life was for she'd have said, without any irony, looking at beautiful things and loving people. After the funeral I started reading the last part of Moore's Principia Ethica — the chapter on the ideal — becuase I wanted something more substantial than a sympathy card, and it looked like a straight philosophical vindication of her: he says the best things we know are complex wholes combining a state of consciousness directed at an object with the object itself, and puts affection between persons at the top of the list. I was quite excited about this for a week.

Then I tried to write it out properly and got the argument below, which seems to prove far too much. As far as I can tell it shows her life was worth no more than a vivid daydream of it, and that Moore's ideal is really just a machine for manufacturing rapt spectators. I can't find the exit and I don't want to drop it.

1. Moore's method rests on the idea that a thing's intrinsic value depends only on its intrinsic nature, on what it is like in itself. That's what licenses his isolation test: imagine the thing existing alone in the universe and ask whether its existence would be worth having.
2. So relational facts can neither add to nor subtract from intrinsic value. Whether an experience was caused by a real object, whether the person it's directed at exists, what history produced either of them — those are all relations, hence all irrelevant to what the experience is worth in itself.
3. The best wholes are appreciations: a conscious state appropriately directed at something beautiful, together with that beautiful thing. And by the doctrine of organic unities the whole is worth vastly more than the sum of its parts.
4. By 1 and 3, the value of such a whole is fixed entirely by the intrinsic natures of its parts: how beautiful the object is in itself, and how vivid, complex, and appropriate the appreciation is in itself.
5. Two appreciations that are exactly alike from the inside — same vividness, same felt rightness, same everything you could find by attending to them — have the same intrinsic nature. Whether the thing out there is a real Rembrandt, a flawless copy, or nothing at all because you're dreaming, makes no difference to what the appreciation is like in itself.
6. So by 2 and 5, an appreciation of a hallucinated beauty is worth exactly as much as an appreciation of the real thing.
7. Same for people. If love is the emotional contemplation of a person's lovable qualities, then a love directed at an imaginary person is the same good, since the imaginary person's qualities are in imagination exactly as beautiful, and there's no further ingredient a real person contributes.
8. So my grandmothers forty years of gardening and friendship were worth precisely as much as an equally vivid hallucination of them, and someone wired into a machine generating flawless imaginary gardens and imaginary friends would be living a better life than she did, at no cost to anyone.
9. And it gets worse, becuase imaginary objects are unlimited and upgradeable in a way real ones aren't. If the good is appreciation, the best world maximizes rapt spectators: convert everything available into an engine for producing the most intense appreciation of the most beautiful objects, imagined or otherwise. Real gardens and real friends then matter only as means to that, and are strictly inferior to the means we could build instead.
10. On the friendship leg: if a person is loved in virtue of their qualities, love tracks qualities. So an exact duplicate of your oldest friend would be just as good an object of love and swapping would be no loss; and someone with better qualities would be a better object of love, so swapping would be a gain. You ought to upgrade your friends whenever a better one comes along.
11. But Moore is adamant about the opposite of 8 and 10. He holds that affection between persons is among the greatest goods we know, and, as I read him, he's explicit that an appreciation involves beliefs about its object and that it's much the worse when those beliefs are mistaken.
12. So either 11 is a bluff or 2 is false. But if 2 is false — if what a state of consciousness is about, and whether it gets it right, can change what that state is worth in itself — then intrinsic value isn't fixed by intrinsic nature after all, and the isolation test, the organic unities doctrine, and his case against hedonism all lose their footing together.

Which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd far rather give up 2 than accept 8 through 10, but 2 looks to me like just 1 spelled out, and 1 is doing all the work in the book. I also want to know whether the two legs stand or fall together: 5 to 6 is about hallucinated objects and 10 is about swappable persons, and I suspect a fix for one won't fix the other.

Give me a table first, one row per numbered step, 1 through 12, with exactly these columns:
- step: the number
- verdict: keep / repair / reject
- why_it_looks_fine: one sentence on why the step is tempting even if it's wrong
- cost_of_rejecting: what Moore, or I, have to give up if this step goes
- what_to_say_instead: the repaired version of the step, or blank if it survives as written

Then a short JSON object with these fields, each one sentence unless noted: culprit (array of the step numbers doing the real damage), diagnosis (say whether there's a bad step somewhere or whether the argument is basically valid and forces a choice between bad options), what_moore_pays, what_i_pay, legs_together (true/false — do 5-6 and 10 fall together), and verdict (what this does and doesn't show about whether my grandmothers life was worth less than a daydream of it).
