Just came out of a three hour arguement with my friend about Aumann's agreement theorem and my brain is fried, but I want to settle it with a story instead of more logic.

His position: if two people start from the same prior and each one's current credence is common knowledge between them — not just known, but known to be known, all the way down — then the two numbers have to be equal. So when two sincere rational people genuinely disagree, at least one premise is broken somewhere, and his favorite suspect is the infinite chain, because in real life nobody ever gets past like three levels of "and he knows that I know that he knows." My position: that's a cop-out, real disagreements are about priors, about what counts as evidence, about self-deception and outright lying, and the chain business is a technicality. He says the chain is the load-bearing wall, and he bet me he could make me feel it rather than just concede it.

So write me a short story that makes his side felt, not argued. The conceit I want: two people who were trained identically and really do start from the same beliefs, separated by distance, who trade credence numbers about some single contested question by messenger. Their institution has a rule — a message only counts once it's been acknowledged, and an acknowledgment only counts once it's been acknowledged in turn — so the chain never closes, and each of them always keeps a sliver of doubt about whether the other actually has their number. They've been stuck at different numbers for years and neither can budge the other. Then in the fifth scene, something happens that both of them witness and both know the other witnessed — the pass closes for the season, the same signal fire is seen from both towers, whatever you like — and what it makes publicly true between them is that the exchange is now final: each definitively has the other's number and knows the other has theirs. With no new evidence whatsoever about the actual question, their numbers snap to being identical. That has to land as eerie, not as a proof being narrated at me.

Constraints: exactly five numbered scenes, each a paragraph or two, no title, no setup paragraph before scene 1, no wrap-up or moral after scene 5. Don't name the theorem anywhere in the story and don't use the phrases common knowledge or common prior — the reader should get it purely from what happens. Keep the messages physical and slow, that's the whole point of the machinery. And if you can quietly plant one small detail that would let me argue these two never really shared a prior after all, without ever resolving it, I'll count that as my side smuggled into his story.
