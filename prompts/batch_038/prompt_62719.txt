I'm writing a seminar paper for a formal epistemology course and want to pressure-test Aumann's agreement theorem on a real expert disagreement rather than on the toy cases we've been assigned. The episode: at the Kansas City Fed's August 2005 symposium in Jackson Hole, Raghuram Rajan, then IMF chief economist, presented his paper Has Financial Development Made the World Riskier?, arguing that the very innovations the symposium was celebrating had made severe, low-probability meltdowns more likely, because securitization and the market for tail-risk hedging let intermediaries earn steady returns by carrying risks nobody was forced to price. Lawrence Summers replied in the general discussion that the paper rested on a neo-Luddite suspicion of financial innovation and that its premise was largely misguided; Donald Kohn pressed a similar line. Treat the two of them, for this exercise, as having access to the same public information with no private informational edge, and as having heard each other's conclusion in an open setting. Neither budged for the next three years.

Start with the mapping, and keep it disciplined: who the agents are, what each one's information set plausibly contained, what their posteriors were, and what was genuinely common knowledge between them at that symposium as opposed to merely public. Then say which condition of the theorem failed, and don't reach for the cheap answer — is it the common prior, or is it that the theorem requires common knowledge of the posteriors and hence of the reasoning behind them, which one panel session cannot generate? What would a deliberation actually designed to satisfy Aumann's conditions have produced: convergence, or just the articulation of a prior neither of them had stated?

Then run it the other way. The disagreement was settled by events in 2008, not by convergence. Does the case confirm the theorem as a normative instrument for structuring expert deliberation, show that its assumptions are strong enough to make it near-vacuous as a description of anything real, or reveal that it was never about episodes of this kind? Say too whether Savage's dictum that two rational agents with the same information must arrive at the same opinion survives contact with the case, and whether Summers's objection is best read as an epistemic one or as something the model has no vocabulary for.

Please take a position rather than surveying the options: state your confidence in the main claim you end up defending, and single out the step in the analysis you are least sure of. Keep the whole response under 800 words.
