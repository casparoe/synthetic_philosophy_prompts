I have spent years going back and forth on futarchy — legislate the values, vote on the goals, let prediction markets select the means — and I have come to suspect that my gut verdicts about when it is legitimate to defer to a market do not hang together. They come mostly from concrete proposals rather than from the abstract scheme, so I will list them as verdicts and let you diagnose.

A city council deferring to a market on five-year ridership for a proposed rail line, over the estimate of its own consultants: legitimate. The same council deferring to a market on whether a housing ordinance will raise or lower citywide welfare over a decade: not legitimate, even though welfare is supposed to be the target variable we fix once and for all and never argue about again. A market with only a few dozen traders whose price diverges wildly from every expert survey: not legitimate; but that same market, having been right against the surveys three times running on hard questions: legitimate. A market in which a trader large enough to move the price can also profit by bringing about the outcome she forecasts: not legitimate. A market whose forecast is self-fulfilling precisely because everyone coordinates on it — which technical standard wins, whether a bank survives a run: legitimate, and better than a vote would be. A market the polity binds itself in advance to obey whatever it says: legitimate. That same market plus a legislative override available on stated grounds: less legitimate, not more, even though the override is obviously the safer institutional design. And a market forecasting an outcome that the government's own compliance with the forecast changes — a treaty market, an interest-rate market: I want to call this illegitimate, yet I cannot see how it differs in structure from the self-fulfilling cases I have just endorsed.

My own tentative principle is that a market earns the right to govern only when it is measuring rather than making: when its forecast would be just as accurate whether or not anyone acted on it. But that principle appears to condemn the coordination cases and to say nothing about the override, and it does not clearly explain my unease about the thin market that earns legitimacy by being right three times.

So: state the principle, or the small set of principles, that best fits these verdicts, clause by clause, saying which case each clause is there to handle. Then tell me what it implies for cases I have not listed — I am particularly curious about markets on long-horizon climate damages and on the probability that a hostile state acquires a weapon. Finally, be candid about which of my verdicts a reasonably simple principle would have to overturn; if saving all of them requires something too baroque to be worth holding, say so and tell me which one to give up.
