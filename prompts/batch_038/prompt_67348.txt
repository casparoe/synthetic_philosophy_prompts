So, first session of our department's interdisciplinary workshop on concepts in the life sciences — 2.5 hours, 18 people, roughly half philosophy grad students and half folks from ecology/evo bio who have never sat through a philosophy seminar. Nobody's been given reading, on purpose. I want the function debate to hit them sideways.

Design me a runnable activity — game, simulation, structured deliberation, your call — that gets the group to crash into the causal-role vs. etiological split on their own, with nobody allowed to say "proper function" out loud until the debrief.

The shape I have in my head, improve it where it's dumb: they're staff at some kind of certification body that has to rule, for each trait, on three questions — does this trait *have* a function, is it currently performing it, and does a broken version count as malfunctioning or as just being a different trait. Two desks, two rulebooks, both written in deliberately bland regulatory prose. Desk A's rulebook may only appeal to what the trait does now and what that contributes to the maintenance, survival and reproduction of the system it's in; the words "ancestor," "selected" and "evolution" are banned from it outright. Desk B's rulebook may only appeal to lineage — what the trait was retained for across generations — and is forbidden from appealing to whether it's working right now, except through one clause about deviation from the lineage norm. Both desks certify the same dossier set, and then they have to produce ONE joint document, signed by both, before time runs out.

For the cases: invent them, 6–8 dossiers of made-up organisms. Please don't use hearts pumping blood, the human appendix, or any of the actual textbook workhorses — the biologists will have seen those in undergrad and will just recite the standard line at me. Each dossier is a card with what the organism is, what the trait does now, and a stipulated history of how the trait arose and got retained. My hunch is that the interesting cases are exactly the ones where current contribution and lineage history come apart, and that they come apart in maybe three or four structurally different ways. Build the case set to test that hunch, and then tell me straight whether it's right or whether I'm missing a category. Also work in one dossier about something humans designed or engineered in a lab, and tell me whether that one's a gift or a trap.

Scoring, no real money: some points/stamps scheme where each desk gets rewarded for ruling according to its own rulebook, but there's a noticeably bigger reward for the joint document being internally consistent — so the pressure to fudge comes from the incentives rather than from me talking. Make the fudging *visible*, I want to be able to point at the exact moment on the wall afterwards.

Then give me: a materials list I can print on cardstock and cut up; a minute-by-minute timeline for the full 150; the debrief, mapping specific moments from the room onto the real positions (the Cummins-style causal-role analysis, the etiological line, and whatever third option they invent under pressure — usually some dispositional or system-goal thing) plus a plan for the moment a biologist says "but nobody in my department talks about function like either of these rulebooks, we just talk about what it does," because that's coming and I don't want to be caught flat-footed; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it live without killing it; which single case is most likely to break the activity; and an honest note on which feature of the distinction the activity structurally cannot reproduce. My guess is the big one is that I'm handing them evolutionary history as a stipulated fact, so they never feel that "was selected for" is an inference and not an observation — if that's right, say so and tell me what I have to supply by hand to patch it.

I know the literature already, so don't survey it at me. I want the runnable thing.
