I sit on a hospital ethics consultation service, and last week's case has left me with a sentence I cannot get a handle on. The question was whether to seek a court order to treat a patient diagnosed with schizophrenia who was refusing antipsychotic medication. One consultant argued that the refusal should be overridden: randomized trials show that this drug class substantially reduces relapse and rehospitalization, so the refusal is best read as a manifestation of the very condition it declines to treat. Another consultant, who describes himself as a Szaszian, cut in with "mental illness is a myth," and put the sentence to work twice over — first, to say that the trial data cannot possibly bear on whether there is an illness here, since evidence that an intervention changes outcomes is not evidence of disease; second, to say that once you see there is no disease, nothing remains to justify overriding the patient's refusal. When pressed, he declined to elaborate, on the grounds that the slogan was the point.

I would like to return to the next meeting with that sentence taken apart. Please treat it as a free-floating piece of language rather than as an occasion for exegesis: I am not asking what Szasz argued in the 1960 paper, and I want no citations, bibliography, or reading list, since I will have no way to check them. What I want is the menu of distinct, precise claims the sentence could be used to make, stated one at a time, each in a form a competent speaker could agree or disagree with without equivocating. I expect the candidates to range from claims about the word illness and what its correct application requires, through claims about the evidential standing of psychiatric diagnosis, to claims about what the diagnostic label does socially and legally, to claims about the phenomena themselves.

For each candidate reading, please tell me: whether it is a platitude nobody in the room would deny, a substantive claim that is defensible, a substantive claim that is probably false, or something whose truth turns entirely on a stipulative definition of illness doing the work; and whether it is a semantic, metaphysical, epistemic, normative, or sociological claim, since I suspect the sentence gets its force precisely by running those together. Then say explicitly which readings, if any, support each of my colleague's two moves — the dismissal of the trial evidence and the conclusion about overriding the refusal — and which support neither while still being what most listeners hear. Where a move does not follow from any single reading, state what would have to be added to the slogan to make it follow.

Two further instructions. First, he chose the word myth, and I think it matters whether he means a belief that is false or a story that performs social work whether or not anyone believes it; carry that distinction through the readings rather than assuming one sense, and note which readings require which. Second, flag any pair of readings that are each individually plausible but cannot both be true, and any reading that, if accepted, would cut against his conclusion about the court order at least as much as for it.

A numbered list is the right format, with the prose inside each item kept tight. Close with a short paragraph identifying the single reading you think he was actually relying on when he said the trial data could not matter, and what would count as a reason for him to abandon that particular reading rather than another.
