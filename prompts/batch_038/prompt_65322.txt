I am building a seminar handout on Arendt's two essays on falsehood in public life — Truth and Politics from 1967, and Lying in Politics, her 1971 piece on the Pentagon Papers — and I have assembled an argument out of her own premises that I believe destroys her conclusion. I cannot accept the destruction, and I cannot find the bad step. I would like a diagnosis. I am not asking for references or further reading; please work from the argument as I have stated it.

(1) Factual truth is contingent. Anything that happens could have happened otherwise, and nothing in a fact compels assent the way a demonstration compels assent. Rational truths are coercive in that sense; factual truths are not.

(2) Since a factual truth carries no internal compulsion, it can always be displaced by a fabrication that is equally compatible with everything else the audience already accepts — and the fabrication can be arranged to be more coherent, more interesting, and better matched to what the audience wants to believe than the fact it replaces.

(3) Politics proceeds by persuasion rather than by demonstration. So in politics the truth-teller has no structural advantage over the liar; which of them prevails is settled by resources and by skill at managing plausibility.

(4) If that is right, Arendt's distinction between the old-style liar, who hides a truth he leaves standing, and the modern liar, who sets out to remake reality, cannot carry the weight she puts on it. Both are conducting the same enterprise at different scales. The old liar's respect for the truth is a private attitude with no public effect, since a fact successfully concealed is as absent from the common world as a fact that has been obliterated.

(5) Her further claim — that organized lying destroys something irreplaceable, namely the shared factual world by which people orient themselves — therefore cannot be a claim about truth at all. Given (3), what is lost is stability and predictability, and what replaces it is a less serviceable convention.

(6) So lying in politics is at worst imprudence: costly to the liar and to his society over time, but never a wrong of a distinctive kind, and nothing that licenses the alarm she sounds.

(7) I cannot accept (6), and I doubt that anyone who has read the papers she was reading can accept it.

Since (7) is where I stand, something earlier has to give. Let me say where my loyalties lie, so that you can price them: I want to keep (1), because I refuse to claim that facts speak for themselves or that truthfulness is self-recommending; and I want to keep the denial of (6). That leaves (2) through (5) as the region where the break must be, and I cannot locate it.

So: which step fails, and why did it look acceptable when I first wrote it down? If you judge the chain valid and think the real question is which premise to abandon, say so plainly and rank (1) through (5) by the cost of abandoning each. And if your diagnosis is that truth does retain some structural advantage in politics after all, tell me what the mechanism is without appealing to anything like the self-evidence of facts — because if the mechanism reduces to the observation that reality eventually catches up with the lie, that strikes me as a prudential consideration and hence a concession to (6) rather than an escape from it.

Then set out your verdict on every step in a table with exactly these columns, one row per numbered step:

- step — the premise number and a label of no more than five words
- status — exactly one of: sound / equivocal / unsupported / false
- why it looked fine — the intuition, or the reading of a term, on which the step passes
- revision — the weakest way to restate the step so that it is true, and whether the restated version still supports the following step (write no further support if it does not)
- cost — what a defender of Arendt gives up by accepting that revision

One or two sentences per cell, no more.
