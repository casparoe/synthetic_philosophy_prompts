I convene a termly seminar on intergenerational justice that is deliberately mixed: two moral philosophers, three economists, a demographer, and a civil servant who appraises long-lived public investment for a national infrastructure body. Our standing topic for the past two years has been pure time preference, and the discussion has stalled in a way I find genuinely puzzling rather than merely frustrating. The poles are familiar enough. On one side is the Ramsey–Sidgwick line: discounting a unit of welfare on the sole ground that it falls at a later date is ethically indefensible, impatience is a defect of the telescopic faculty rather than a moral datum, and the pure rate of time preference should accordingly be set at or very near zero. On the other is the Koopmans line, which refuses to treat impatience as a psychological blemish at all: in a stationary setting with an unbounded horizon, quite weak postulates about the utility of a consumption programme can entail impatience, and without it an optimum may fail to exist; add uncertainty about the continued existence of the community and a positive rate begins to look less like a prejudice than like an accounting necessity. Between them sits a third, descriptive camp, much exercised by the Stern–Nordhaus exchange and its aftermath, which holds that the social discount rate should be read off observed behaviour — returns to capital, long-maturity spreads — rather than stipulated by moral philosophy.

What makes the impasse strange is how much technical ground has been conceded on all sides. Everyone in the room accepts the standard decomposition of the social discount rate into a pure rate of time preference plus the elasticity of marginal utility times the growth rate; everyone accepts that most of the policy divergence between the low-discount and high-discount camps attaches to the first term rather than the second; and after two years our demographer and our economists have converged on broadly the same growth forecasts. Nobody has moved an inch. My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis to be tested, not as a conclusion — is that the seminar is not having one argument but at least three, and that participants slide between them, so that each side's reasons systematically miss the other's target. The first layer is ethical: whether temporal location can ever be a morally relevant property of a benefit or a harm. The second is empirical and structural: whether the probability that the community ceases to exist, and the dispersion of possible growth paths, can be absorbed into the pure time preference parameter without double-counting, and whether one number can serve simultaneously as a survival adjustment and as an index of impartiality. The third is conceptual: whether that parameter is a normative input to a planner's objective function or a description of what a society already does, since the descriptive camp's attempt to fix it from bond spreads looks to me like an estimate of the return on capital, which is a different object entirely. I also suspect that 'pure time preference' is not one quantity but at least three — individual time bias, planner-level impatience, and an extinction-risk adjustment — and that much of the heat comes from the phrase doing different work in different mouths.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on it. Take each of my three candidate layers and tell me whether it is genuinely doing independent work, whether it is verbal, substantive, or a disguised empirical question, and which one is load-bearing once the others are stripped away. In particular: does the Koopmans-style argument actually answer the impartiality objection, or does it change the subject — a claim about the existence of an optimum is not obviously a claim about how much weight a future person's welfare deserves — and if it changes the subject, why has that gone unnoticed for ninety-odd years? Then tell me what would count as progress on each layer given your diagnosis: a distinction, a formal impossibility or representation result, a measurement, a change of question, or a reframing in terms of some decision criterion other than maximising a discounted sum. Where you judge that no progress should be expected, say so and explain why — for instance because the residual disagreement rests on bedrock intuitions about the standing of the merely possible that neither side can argue for from premises the other accepts. Finally, state what would have to become true for the two camps to converge on a single figure for the pure rate, and whether such a convergence would settle the ethical question or merely bypass it. I am not asking for references or a reading list; please ground the diagnosis in what each side actually claims, and flag any point at which you are reconstructing a position rather than reporting one you are confident about.
