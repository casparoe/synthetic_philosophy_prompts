At a residents' meeting last month, two of my neighbours argued about a woman who has rented a room in one of the buildings on our street for nine years. The building belongs to a single family. The tenancy is renewed year to year at the owners' discretion, at sixty days' notice, and nothing in it entitles her to a renewal; the rent has never risen faster than inflation. The family has never threatened her with anything, repairs get done quickly, and she tells people she feels perfectly secure.

Nadia said the woman is unfree: she stays there on the strength of someone else's goodwill, and the fact that the goodwill has held for nine years doesn't change the structure of her position. Owen said that is an odd use of the word, since nobody is preventing her from doing anything — her options are exactly what the lease says they are — and if being dependent on another's goodwill is itself unfreedom, then every at-will employee and every month-to-month tenant is unfree, which strips the concept of any discriminating power. Nadia said the issue isn't her present options but their source: the family decides whether the option continues to exist, so she has to keep herself in their good graces, and that requirement shapes how she lives. Owen said this simply redescribes her failure to have secured a binding contract; she chose a flexible arrangement, and if the predictable consequence of one's own choice counts as domination, then freedom becomes something you can only have by refusing choices. Nadia said she had no real alternative: the whole block is owned by the same family, comparable rooms come on the same terms, and leaving the city isn't open to her because her mother lives here and needs her. Owen said that now she is describing scarce housing, which is an impersonal market condition with no one's will behind it; the family is doing what every other owner does, and if ordinary property rights amount to arbitrary power, then everyone who owns dominates everyone who doesn't.

They asked me to referee and I could not. Please go through the exchange move by move and say, for each of Nadia's and Owen's three replies, whether it lands, half-lands, or misses, and why — not just who wins overall. Then say what the case shows about the relationship between freedom as non-interference and freedom as non-domination: is the second doing any independent work here, or is it covertly relying on the first (or on a third idea about self-mastery) to get its verdict? Note anything significant that both of them left out, including whether the woman's own report of feeling secure should count as evidence for either side. Finally, be explicit about where your adjudication rests on a claim that people who work on these questions genuinely disagree about, and mark those places as contested rather than presenting your preferred resolution as the settled view.
