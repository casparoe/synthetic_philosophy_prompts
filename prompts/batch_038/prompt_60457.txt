our town's charter comission is weighing a three-part bundle: mandatory voting with a token fine, the franchise at 16, and letting long-term permanent residents vote in local elections. The columnist who's pushing the whole thing defends it with one comparison — an election is basically a jury. Jurors can't opt out, so voters shouldn't be able to either; nobody quizzes jurors before seating them, so a civics test for 16-year-olds is off the table; and a jury is drawn from the community that has to live with the verdict, so residents who'll be bound by the budget belong in the pool. I mostly agree with the bundle but the comparison makes me uneasy, mostly because I've heard the exact same jury picture used to argue the opposite — that voters are nothing like jurors, since jurors sit through adversarial evidence and take instructions from a judge while voters do neither, so if anything the analogy is a case for restricting the franchise.

Can you audit it? I'd like the mapping laid out explicitly — what plays the juror, the pool, the judge, the parties, the deliberation, the verdict — and then the disanalogies sorted into the ones that actually bear on one of the three proposals versus the ones that are just noise. For each of the three conclusions I want to know separately whether the jury frame supports it, is neutral on it, or undercuts it. Two things I'm specifically suspicious about: whether the aggregation argument (the claim that large electorates are reliable because individual errors cancel) can live inside a jury frame at all, since deliberation seems to trade against independence; and whether the analogy only works if the losing side has already bought into the procedure, which would make it circular when the point is to justify compelling people. And if it can't carry the bundle, is there a better comparison that could, or does each piece just need its own defense?
