I am a high school senior in a philosophy club, and we were assigned a passage from an unnamed popular book on what separates science from everything else. It reads:

"Popper showed that falsifiability is the mark of science: a claim is scientific only if some possible observation would contradict it. Freudian psychoanalysis fails the test, because its concepts can be stretched to explain any behavior whatsoever, so it is not science at all — it is meaningless. But Popper's own criterion collapses under the same pressure. As Duhem and Quine pointed out, a theory is never tested in isolation; when a prediction fails you can always blame an auxiliary assumption instead, so no theory can ever really be falsified. Falsificationism is therefore self-refuting, which is why Feyerabend concluded that anything goes. Lakatos tried to save what he could by insisting that theories be judged not one by one but as whole research programmes, progressive or degenerating, over long stretches of time."

Some of this sounds right and some feels too tidy, and I cannot yet say which. I am inclined to think the second half is too glib, since I have read that Popper was not the naive falsificationist the passage makes him out to be, but I would not know where the slippage starts. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a philosopher of science would accept, what is a fair simplification, and what is simply wrong — in particular whether Popper ever equated unfalsifiability with meaninglessness, and whether the Duhem–Quine point really does make falsificationism self-refuting rather than merely harder to apply?

Then please run the same audit on a case our club could not settle. Under the passage's version of Popper, is that enterprise unscientific? Does the answer change once the corrected version of Popper's view is substituted for the one in the passage, and if so, which feature of the correction does the work?
