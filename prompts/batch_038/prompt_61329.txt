Please compose a parody offering memorandum for a securitisation of student income-contingent repayments: approximately 750 words, laid out in numbered sections, and written in British English. The issuer should be an invented United Kingdom special-purpose vehicle with a suitably grand name; the sponsor should be an invented asset manager acting on the advice of an invented policy institute that has spent two decades arguing that a degree is best understood as a private investment good and a student as its rational purchaser.

The document should comprise a short cover summary; a use-of-proceeds paragraph; a capital structure section in which the notes are tranched by field of study, with the senior tranche reserved for those degrees whose graduate earnings distribution is least dispersed and the subordinated tranche composed of the remainder; eight to ten numbered risk factors; and a closing regulatory note.

The target of the satire is the framing itself — the reduction of study to an asset, and of a nineteen-year-old to a cash-flow projection — not any real institution or individual. It must land by being technically correct rather than by being absurd: each clause should name a difficulty a genuine issuer would have had to confront, exaggerated only in the candour with which it is disclosed. A reader acquainted with the comparative politics of tuition finance should be able to recover the real structural points from the drafting — that repayment contingent on earnings makes the cash flows correlated across the entire cohort with the labour market rather than diversified across it; that the very feature which protects the individual borrower is what makes the stream difficult to price; that a graduate can leave the reference population by emigrating, by taking low-paid caring or creative work, or by settling in a jurisdiction that charges no fees at all; and that the notes exist only by virtue of a policy choice that a later government could reverse, so that the free-tuition systems of Germany and the Nordics function in the risk factors less as competitors to this asset class than as proof that it need not exist.

At least one risk factor must concede, in flat disclosure prose, that the ultimate source of repayment may prove to be the taxpayer rather than the borrower, and the document must not attempt to resolve that tension.

Maintain a deadpan register throughout: no winking, no editorial asides, no reveal in the final line, and nothing that breaks character. Do not name real universities, banks, governments, statutes, or ministers, and do not supply specific dates or statistics — the piece should stand alone without any citations or references.
