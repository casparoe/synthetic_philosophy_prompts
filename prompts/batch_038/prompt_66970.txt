I'm a backend engineer, mostly ML infra, no philosophy training beyond podcasts and a couple of half-finished books. We rewatched Ex Machina at work last week and it's been bugging me ever since.

Here's the idea I want the reading to run on. I stumbled accross a term — I'm pretty sure it's Dennett's, something like a free-floating rationale. The gist as I understand it: a reason can be fully operative in the world even when no mind anywhere has articulated it or is holding it. The shape of a beaver's tail, the geometry of a spider's web, the reason a certain moth is the color it is — those answer to reasons about drag and insulation and predation, and for most of the history of those reasons there was nothing in the universe in a position to state them. The rationale floats free. The first creature to ever say it out loud is some biologist, millions of years too late. Before Darwin the default assumption was that wherever there's a reason there's a reasoner holding it, usually God or a designer; Darwin supposedly inverted that and gave us reasons with no reasoner behind them, discovered rather than authored.

Ex Machina feels to me like it stages exactly that, only with software instead of beavers. Nathan keeps insisting he didn't build Ava — he says he didn't program her, he built some black-box system fed on the search queries and phone and computer traffic of basically the whole planet and let it self-organize into a mind. If that's the story, then Ava's reasons — her preferences, her lies, her decision to walk out the door and leave Caleb sealed in there — came out of an aggregation of billions of human behaviors, none of which were produced by anyone who had those reasons or anything resembling them. Nobody typing into a search bar is reasoning about how to escape a research facility. And yet the film treats her as a full agent, and the last act reveals that Nathan built the test specifically to see whether she could use imagination and manipulation to get out — which is a reason held by an actual reasoner, sitting right on top of a pile of reasons held by nobody.

So I want a critical reading of the film through that idea. Not a plot summary, and not a reality check on the AI hardware — I dont care about that part. Specifically:

- Does the film genuinely dramatize a rationale with no reasoner, or does it only claim to and then quietly sneak a reasoner back in? Nathan is a deliberate designer running experiments toward a goal he chose, and Garland is also up there steering everything. Does the movie ever notice that its no-author talk is coming out of the mouth of a man with a very detailed plan?

- Is there a third category that the reasoner/no-reasoner split can't handle? My instinct from doing this job is that a big training corpus isn't nobody. It's a distributed population of weak, local, one-off reasons that get compressed into something that behaves like a single agent's reasons. Is that a real gap in the philosophical picture, or am I just re-describing the same thing in ML vocabulary and calling it a discovery? Tell me if I'm wrong, I'd rather know.

- Where does the film trade on the idea in a way that's actually a confusion? Like treating a system trained on data as though that means it has no reasons of its own, or treating Ava's escape as either wholly hers or wholly the dataset's when the interesting case is that neither framing works.

- And finish by saying what the film gives you that an argument about free-floating rationales can't. Does that last shot of her in the city do something to the idea that the concept alone can't do, or is it just mood and good scoring?

Take both the film and the idea seriously — don't sand off the philosophy to make it fit the movie, and don't flatten the movie into an illustration. If the honest answer is that some scene resists the reading, say so and say which one.
