For the sixth week of a graduate seminar in formal philosophy of language, I need a one-page diagnostic instrument on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. My students arrive convinced that the Equation — P(A → C) = P(C|A) — is simply what conditional probability means, and they then react to the theorem either with panic or with the sense that a clever trick has been played on them. What I want to give them is a decision tree that locates every available response in the logical space of assumptions that actually go into the derivation, so that choosing an escape route feels like answering questions rather than picking a team.

Please build the tree as follows:

- Construct the branching from the assumption pool first, and only then hang positions on it. The assumptions I have in mind, which you should prune, reorder, or supplement as the logic requires: (i) that the Equation is a thesis about the probabilities of truth-conditional contents rather than about degrees of acceptance; (ii) that conditional probability is defined by the ratio formula rather than being primitive; (iii) that the language containing conditionals forms an ordinary classical propositional algebra, closed in the usual ways; (iv) that the Equation is required to hold for every admissible credence function and every conditionalization of it, rather than for a distinguished subclass; (v) that conditionals embed and compose classically inside negations, disjunctions, and further conditionals; (vi) that credences are real-valued and countably additive.

- Write each node as a question a student could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. Where possible phrase it in ordinary language about belief and about what the student is prepared to say, not in notation, though you may put the formal condition in parentheses.

- I need between five and seven nodes and six to nine leaves. The leaves must include, at minimum: the view that conditionals have no truth-conditional content at all and the Equation governs acceptance; Lewis's own conclusion that we should simply accept the triviality and abandon probabilities of conditionals; a truth-conditional semantics that keeps the Equation but only for a restricted class of credence functions, with the conditional treated as something like a random variable; a route that abandons the classical algebra in favour of an algebra of conditional events; a route that takes conditional probability as primitive and denies the ratio definition; and a route that restricts embedding. For each leaf give its standard label in the literature, plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment — specifically, what it owes either to the intuitive pull of the Equation or to the behaviour of conditionals inside complex sentences.

- Identify at least two combinations of answers to your nodes that no position in the literature occupies, and say for each whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive despite being coherent, or because it is simply unexplored.

- Flag honestly any pair of leaves your questions do not really separate, rather than pretending the tree is finer than it is.

Do not include citations or a bibliography; I will supply those. Where you attach a name to a leaf or a claim about who proved what, flag it as uncertain rather than guessing.

Finally, trace this student through your tree. She insists that reporting 0.8 for a conditional is assigning probability to a content she could also believe outright, reject, and reason from, so she keeps truth conditions. She also insists the Equation is not a description of her dispositions but a constraint on any rational credence function whatsoever, at any time, including those that have learned the antecedent. She is an orthodox Bayesian: real-valued, countably additive, one classical algebra, and conditional probability is nothing over and above the ratio. Confronted with the theorem she concedes exactly one thing — she is prepared to legislate that sentences containing a conditional may be evaluated but never embedded within a negation, a disjunction, or another conditional. State which leaf she reaches, whether that leaf is a stable place to live given your other nodes, and, if your tree as constructed has no leaf for her combination, add one and say so.

Verdict: [leaf label] | [stable | unstable | unseparable from: leaf label]
