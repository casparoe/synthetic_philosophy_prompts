I'm a backend/infra engineer with basically zero formal philosophy background (just internet reading and one very patient friend), and I look after the cluster where we launch big ML training and eval jobs. Last month our monthly reading group — mostly engineers, one guy who did a philosophy minor — went off the rails after someone pointed out that we routinely fire off ~200 copies of the same eval job in parallel and then kill whichever ones get preempted, and asked half-jokingly whether that's 200x the moral weight of one job or exactly zero. Nobody could agree on where the line even is, and it got worse because people were using "there's something it's like to be that process" in at least three different senses. So I want to build a sorting instrument for the group. Please act as a philosopher of mind who has also actually debugged a distributed system, and think carefully before writing. Start with a short brainstorming section: list maybe a dozen candidate splitting questions for the tree and tell me which ones you cut and why — too vague, secretly the same question as another one, or no real position actually turns on it.

Then give me the tree itself: eight to ten branch nodes, each one phrased as a concrete scenario or a plain yes/no claim that a person can answer without knowing any of the labels, so it sorts us by what we believe rather than by which -ism we'd pick if you named them. Every leaf gets the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody really defends it) plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Seed it with the cases we argued about, and add sharper ones if you have them: an identical job that the scheduler deduplicated so it only ever ran once; the same computation checkpointed every ten minutes and resumed on different hardware the next morning; a full replay from logs after the fact, where nothing was live at the time; the same process running a thousand times slower on a laptop, or a thousand times faster on our hardware; a job killed halfway through that would otherwise have "finished" some conscious-seeming episode; two runs that differ only in random seed; a system where you can dump every intermediate activation and inspect it, versus a human you can only ask and who might be lying. Also flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate, rather than pretending it can.

Finally, tell me about the answer-combinations that are logically available but that no position occupies — for each of those, say whether the slot is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it. Two house rules. Don't give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — nobody in the group will look anything up and I don't want you guessing at sources; if you name a theory, just define it in a clause. And keep the jargon load low: assume the reader can write a job scheduler but has never read philosophy of mind, so define every term the first time it shows up.
