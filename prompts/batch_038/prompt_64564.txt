Working on Hume's essay on suicide for a seminar paper and my reconstruction of the argument has kind of escaped me. Here's what I have. (1) Everything that happens is governed by general laws of nature, and those laws are God's. (2) If it were presumptuous or wrong for us to interfere with them, it would be wrong to build a dam, take a fever reducer, or plant a crop, since each of those makes happen what otherwise wouldn't have. (3) Nobody thinks those things are wrong. (4) So interfering with the course of nature can't be what makes an action wrong. (5) Taking my own life interferes with the course of nature, and on Hume's account that's the only charge anyone can bring against it: I wrong no one else, break no contract, take nothing from anybody, and if Ive become a burden to society I actually leave it better off by withdrawing. (6) So suicide is at worst neutral and sometimes praiseworthy.

The trouble is that (4) seems to run free of anything Hume wants to say. I can plug in my neighbor and get that killing him is fine too, or plug in walking out on my kids and my debts, or everybody quitting society at once, and each time the same two premises do the work — nature gets interrupted either way, and harm to others is supposed to be irrelevant because there isn't any. That's obviously not the conclusion. So where does the chain break? Is there a step that's invalid, or a premise that's false, or should I just bite the bullet and admit the argument works for the cases I hate as well as the case I like? And whatever I give up, what does it cost him — can Hume still reach (6), or does the essay collapse without it?
