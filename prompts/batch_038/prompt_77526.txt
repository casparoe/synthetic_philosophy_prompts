I'm two years into a PhD and my assemblage has become the thing my department argues about when it should be working. It's a late Neolithic settlement site in the Balkans — a couple of hundred small fired-clay figurines and a lot of miniature vessels, mostly from pits and floor deposits, no special building, no temple, no burials, nothing that looks like a shrine. Two things about them are hard to explain away: a large share are deliberately broken rather than accidentally shattered (clean breaks at consistent points, on fresh rather than weathered clay), and a decent number of the fragments conjoin — pieces of the same object turn up in different pits, sometimes in different houses, sometimes with what looks like a deliberate sequence of deposition. A handful have soot on them. That's basically the data.

Two camps have been going round in circles in seminar for as long as I've been here. One side says the conjoins plus the standardised breakage are the interesting bit: this is rule-governed behaviour, objects that had to be broken in a certain way whose pieces had to end up apart, and that's evidence for a shared normative system — not for any particular belief content, but for the existence of obligations that survived individual households, which they'd say is about as far as material culture can legitimately carry you. The other side says this is circular: you call something ritual because you can't think of a practical use for it, then you infer beliefs from the fact that you've called it ritual, and 'ritual' is really just the bin we put things in when our functional imagination runs out. They add that a figurine's form can't tell you what it meant to anybody, only its context can, and that even the context is our context, not theirs. There's a third person who thinks the second camp isn't making an epistemological point at all but an ethical one — they're uneasy about claiming to know what dead people believed, which is a different question wearing the same clothes.

My own suspicion, offered as a suspicion, is that the deadlock isn't really about these pots but about who carries the burden: whether belief-talk is a positive claim needing independent support, or the default description of behaviour that looks non-utilitarian — and if it's the latter, then the thing nobody has specified is what evidence would count *against* a ritual reading. It could also just be that the two camps are answering different questions, one about past cognition and one about the legitimacy of the interpretive act, and talking past each other under a single heading. So: diagnose it for me. Is this substantive, verbal (about what 'ritual' or 'belief' picks out), a hidden empirical or methodological question, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Is the question ill-posed or merely hard? And tell me what would count as progress, given your diagnosis. In particular I'd like an honest answer on my own proposal — I've been trying to formalise a redundancy test, roughly: does the object's form and treatment carry information that no practical function requires, and is that information standardised enough across the assemblage that it had to be learned? My supervisor says that's exactly the naive move the second camp spent the nineties discrediting, and I can't tell whether he's right or whether he's just tired of me. Please write the whole answer in British English.
