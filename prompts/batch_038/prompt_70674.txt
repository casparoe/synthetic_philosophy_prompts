I sit on the executive board of a European humanitarian organization which, unusually for bodies of its kind, publishes formal positions on the use of military force. I am drafting the agenda note for next month's meeting, at which we will decide whether to endorse a proposed intervention, and I want to test our draft decision against our own record rather than against first principles. Our published positions to date are these:

1. Northern Iraq, 1991. Endorsed the allied safe havens and no-fly zones in the north. We noted at the time that the Security Council resolution the allies relied on condemned the repression and demanded access for humanitarian organizations, but did not authorize force and did not mention no-fly zones; we treated that as a defect to be flagged rather than a bar, because the Council had at least addressed the situation and the operation was confined to shielding people already displaced.

2. Kosovo, 1999. Endorsed the air campaign despite the absence of any Council authorization, citing the expulsions under way and the backing of a regional alliance. In the same statement we urged that the campaign be confined to forces and equipment being used against the civilian population, and criticized the later strikes on infrastructure in the capital as shifting the object of the operation from protection to the coercion of a government.

3. Iraq, 2003. Opposed. Our stated grounds were that the humanitarian rationale was advanced only after other justifications had failed, and that no one offered a credible account of who would govern the country the morning after.

4. Rwanda. In a retrospective statement issued on the tenth anniversary of the genocide, we declared that our silence in 1994 was the gravest failure in the organization's history, that a Council mandate should never have been treated as a precondition for calling for one, and that the cost of inaction belongs in the ledger alongside the cost of action.

5. Libya, 2011. Endorsed the protection mandate the Council adopted in March, including its express exclusion of any occupying force. Six months later we declined to endorse continued operations and criticized coalition air support for rebel offensives on the capital as going beyond what the mandate permitted, whatever the desirability of the outcome.

6. Syria, 2013. After the sarin attack on the eastern suburbs of Damascus in August, we declined to endorse the limited strikes then being planned, on the ground that they would not protect anyone and would foreclose other options. We called instead for a Council referral to the International Criminal Court and for cross-border aid deliveries conducted without Damascus's consent.

7. Yemen, 2015. Opposed the coalition air campaign even though it was conducted at the request of the internationally recognized president, on three grounds: that the president had by then been driven from the capital and was outside the country when the campaign began; that the inviting government was itself a party to the conflict; and that the foreseeable burden of the campaign would fall on the civilian population it was said to defend.

The proposal now before us concerns a state whose central government collapsed two years ago. Its salient features, as set out in the papers circulated to the board, are as follows. A militia that holds the capital and most of the coast has for eight months besieged a highland region inhabited predominantly by a distinct ethnic minority; there is well-documented use of starvation as a method of war, together with summary executions of local notables. The Security Council has twice failed to act, because a permanent member with naval facilities in the region has said it will veto any authorization. The recognized government, which retains the state's seat at the United Nations and control of the central bank's reserves but no territory, has formally invited a coalition of three neighboring states to intervene. The coalition's operational plan is limited to opening and holding a land corridor to the besieged region and securing one port for aid delivery; it commits in writing to withdraw within eighteen months and to transfer the corridor and the port to a United Nations administration. A regional organization to which all three coalition members belong has endorsed the plan. Two of the three heads of state, however, have separately stated on the record that the militia's leadership must be removed from power and made to answer for its conduct, and the third has declined to disavow those statements when asked. The coalition has not attempted any alternative procedural route, in the General Assembly or elsewhere. Military assessors judge the corridor holdable with the forces committed, but its defense depends on uncontested air superiority, and one member has said publicly that it will not expose its aircraft beyond the first ninety days. The minority's own representative council has issued an open appeal for the intervention. Two large medical charities have stated that they will not operate under military escort, and one has said it would suspend its remaining programs in militia-held areas if the corridor operation begins.

I would like the following, in this order:

- Identify the candidate principles that jointly explain every one of the seven positions above, including the awkward ones. Do not simply recite the standard criteria for humanitarian intervention and apply them; the point is that more than one distinct principle, or weighting of principles, is consistent with the whole record, and that our stated grounds in each position may not reveal the rule actually doing the work.
- Say which of those principles deliver different answers on the proposal before us, and what each implies: a flat endorsement, a refusal, or endorsement subject to conditions, and if conditions, precisely which.
- Give your best assessment of what we should decide if we want to remain consistent with ourselves, and identify which of the seven positions is the closest analog and which is the most tempting but misleading one. If the record genuinely underdetermines the decision, say so and state what principle the board would be adopting by endorsing and what principle it would be adopting by refusing — including what each choice would commit us to the next time a comparable case arises.
- Finally, construct the single hypothetical case, differing from the present proposal as little as possible, on which the candidate principles you identified disagree most sharply, and explain why that one discriminates better than the obvious alternatives.
