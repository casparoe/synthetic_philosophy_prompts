I teach a first-year interdisciplinary seminar on how we know what we know — 18 students, one 75-minute session, and none of them has studied logic. Design an in-class proof game that makes them feel the force of the worry that our basic mathematical axioms might turn out to be inconsistent, rather than merely hearing about it.

The shape I have in mind is this, though you should improve on it: students work in small teams earning points for deriving theorems in a tiny symbol-manipulation system you invent — not real arithmetic, but arithmetic-flavored and learnable in ten minutes from a handout — and somewhere in the axiom set you plant a pair that together yield a contradiction. I want the teams to be able to find that contradiction on their own, but I want what happens after to be the heart of the activity: the scoring structure should make the resulting collapse of derivability something they feel rather than deduce, and they should face a genuine choice about quarantining one axiom and playing on in a weakened system, where the weakening visibly costs them points they had already earned.

Give me the system and its rules, the materials, the point scheme with no real money involved, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto why a system cannot establish its own consistency from within — and onto what it is like to keep doing mathematics while that possibility remains open. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover in the room. Be frank about which features of the real predicament the game cannot reproduce — the axioms are finite, inspectable, and handed out by me, and the students can step outside the system to discuss it, whereas arithmetic offers no agreed outside — and say what I should supply in words instead.
