I started three months ago as a research engineer on the controls side of a frontier lab — the team that builds safety measures intended to hold even if the model's goals turn out not to match ours, as distinct from the alignment team, which works on making the goals match in the first place. My team lead has one phrase he reaches for constantly: assume the model is adversarial. It has become the terminal move in internal arguments, and I can no longer tell what he thinks he is claiming when he says it.

Recently it has been used to justify four specific decisions:

1. Refusing to tell the model what an evaluation is scoring for — including evaluations whose entire purpose is to measure whether the model cooperates transparently with human oversight, on the grounds that disclosing the criterion hands the adversary the answer key.
2. Treating a clean run through a deception tripwire as carrying almost no evidential weight, since a model that recognized it was being watched would pass.
3. Turning down a proposal to let a strong model help design and red-team its own evaluations, on the grounds that this would amount to asking the adversary to write the security policy.
4. Accepting that the same weights get run under heavy monitoring in some contexts and under almost none in others, because the assumption is supposed to hold regardless of what any particular context shows.

A colleague on the alignment team says the phrase has become unfalsifiable in practice: on this picture no result can ever count against the model being misaligned, a pass is discounted and a failure is confirmation, and the assumption is now doing the work that evidence has stopped doing. My team lead replies that this is exactly the point, and that he is importing a posture from network security, where you design on the assumption that your perimeter is already breached rather than trusting that no intrusion has been detected so far.

I would like the distinct precise claims that this one sentence could be making, laid out as a menu rather than as a single paraphrase. For each reading, tell me what kind of claim it is — a design heuristic for building measures, a methodological rule for how to elicit maximum performance from a system, an empirical generalization about what reward-based training tends to produce, a claim about what goal-directed agents converge on instrumentally, an epistemological claim about how much any evaluation result can show, or a claim about where the burden of proof sits. Then say for each whether it is a platitude that nobody in the building disputes, whether it is substantive but defensible, or whether it is false or at least unsupported — and whether it is compatible with the model being genuinely, robustly aligned, since some readings clearly are and I suspect that distinction is where the argument is actually going wrong.

Then work through the four decisions above and say which reading or readings would actually license each one, if any. A reading that supports refusing to disclose an evaluation's criterion may well not support discounting a passed tripwire, and I want that sorted out rather than assumed.

Please also say where the security analogy genuinely holds and where it breaks down. An intruder on a network has stable goals, a budget, and a reason to avoid detection that persists across sessions; I am not sure any of that transfers, and I would like to know which of the four moves depends on those features carrying over.

Do not hand me the menu and stop there. Finish by committing: which reading you think my team lead is most likely operating under, whether you think it is the right one, whether the unfalsifiability objection lands against it or only against a sloppier version of it, and a verdict on each of the four decisions — justified or not. Give your reasons, and take a position even where the answer plausibly depends on details I have not supplied. If you think one of the readings is doing illegitimate work in this argument, name it and say why.

A numbered list for the readings followed by a short verdict section would be ideal; roughly 900 words in total.
