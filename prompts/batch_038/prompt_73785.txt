For thirty-five years I directed a small laboratory in molecular spectroscopy, and my working life consisted almost entirely of extracting consequences from measurements and equations by methods of widely varying rigor. Since retiring I have taken up epistemology in earnest, and I have become preoccupied with the difficulty that epistemic logicians call logical omniscience: in the standard systems, whatever is a theorem is known by every agent, and knowledge is closed under consequence, so that any knower at all knows every logical truth and every consequence of everything she knows. I find the formal statement of the problem easy to follow. What I cannot manage is the fact that my own unforced verdicts about concrete cases line up neither with that omniscient idealization nor with a flat denial of closure. I should like to know whether some principle captures them, and if none does, which of them I ought to surrender.

My verdicts follow. In each, S is an ordinary competent adult, and the question is always whether S knows the proposition named.

1. S knows that the precipitate forms only if chloride is present, and knows that the precipitate has formed. Chloride is present: yes, and I regard this as beyond dispute, whether or not S has ever troubled to say the sentence to himself.

2. S knows each of some four thousand individual measurements recorded in her notebooks, having made and checked every one of them. The four-thousand-fold conjunction of them: no. I will not grant this, even though it appears to follow from the logic of case 1 iterated four thousand times.

3. S accepts the axioms of a mathematical theory and has been shown a proof of a theorem from them, checked line by line by a machine she rightly trusts, though she cannot follow the proof herself. The theorem: yes.

4. Everything is as in 3, except that no proof has ever been produced by anyone; the theorem is merely a consequence of axioms S knows. The theorem: no.

5. That either a certain isotope ratio deviates from the standard or it does not: yes, trivially, though S has never framed the thought. A tautology in forty propositional variables, never written down, whose truth-table check would outlast her: no. Both verdicts feel firm and I cannot see what differs between them but quantity.

6. S knows the rules of chess and the exact position. Whether White can force mate in seventeen: no. Yet if she consults a strong engine and it reports an answer, she then knows it. What troubles me is that nothing further about the world has come into her possession; the engine's answer was already fixed by what she knew.

7. A model I ran in 1994 settled, in its output, whether a weak spectral line would be resolvable at a given temperature. No one ever plotted it. I want to say the answer was present in the model, and that presence is not knowledge, and yet I cannot say what separates the two.

8. A colleague derived a result in 1970, still recalls the result, and has entirely forgotten the derivation. He knows it: yes. Knowledge therefore survives the loss of the derivation, which sits badly beside case 4, where the derivation's nonexistence defeats knowledge.

9. A physician knows each of a patient's findings and knows the criterion connecting them to a diagnosis, and does not put them together. At the moment of her failure I am inclined to say she does not know the diagnosis; and yet I would fault her for not knowing it; and when she does put it together I would not say she has learned anything new about the patient. All three attitudes seem correct and I cannot hold them at once.

10. S knows p, and p entails not-q, but S does not know that it entails it. Not-q: no. Contrast case 3, where S likewise does not personally see the entailment and yet is granted knowledge on the strength of a certificate. Testimony, it appears, can supply an entailment that the facts themselves cannot.

11. S knows that she believes the theory is well supported. That she knows that she knows it: yes. Iteration seems cheap in a way that deduction is not, and I should like this explained rather than conceded.

12. Our collaboration of nine people collectively possessed everything needed to foresee an instrumental failure that none of us foresaw. The group did not know it: yes, that is my verdict. And the group's failure was a genuine epistemic failure rather than a mere lapse in coordination: also yes.

The pairs I most want examined are 1 against 2, and 3 against 4. In case 1 I grant knowledge with no derivation performed; in case 2 I withhold it although the same trivial operation is available at every step. In case 3 the entailment is unseen by S and knowledge is granted; in case 4 the entailment is equally unseen and knowledge is refused. The only visible difference in both pairs is cost, and cost looks an unworthy ingredient in an account of knowledge: it drifts with the agent's speed, with the instruments at hand, with the hour of the day. Yet if I insist that knowledge is indifferent to cost, then 2, 4, and the second half of 5 come out as knowledge, and I find that simply intolerable.

I have tried a principle of my own, roughly this: S knows q when q follows from what S knows by a derivation that S, or someone S is entitled to rely upon, has actually carried out or could carry out within the ordinary compass of a working day using the means available to her. I distrust it on three counts. It leans on a counterfactual about my own reasoning, and in forty years I was repeatedly and confidently wrong about what I could derive. It makes knowledge swell and shrink with equipment, so that I know more on a day when the cluster is running. And its final clause seems to carry all the weight while saying nothing more than that reliance on a reliable source is reliable.

What I am asking for, then, is the following. State the principle, or the two or three candidate principles, precisely enough that I could apply them to a case I have not thought of and expect agreement about which way it falls. For each clause, identify the case among my twelve that forces it. Say whether the principle is uniform or whether it treats knows as ambiguous or as graded, and if graded, along which dimension, and why that dimension is not merely cost in disguise. Then give me its verdicts on cases I have not listed: the geometer asleep, who is deriving nothing at the time; the reasoner who holds the premises under descriptions she would not recognize as related; the agent who performs the derivation badly and arrives at the truth by luck; a machine; and a group of the kind in case 12. Tell me plainly which of my twelve verdicts the principle cannot keep, and which of those I should refuse to give up even at the price of the principle. I should also like to know whether the standard repairs — impossible worlds, awareness operators, fragmentation of belief — dissolve the difficulty or only relocate it, since an awareness operator that is itself closed under implication seems to reinstall omniscience one level up, and I cannot tell whether that objection is sound. Finally: is this a problem peculiar to knowledge, or does it infect any way of speaking in which a body of data or a model is said to contain an answer, in which case my own lifelong idiom, that the measurements determine the result, is guilty of the same confusion? And where exactly does the slide occur in the rule that whatever is a theorem is known? Since I cheerfully grant that S knows every propositional tautology she can survey, I appear to be committed, by steps each of which looks harmless, to granting that she knows them all, and I should like to be shown the first step that is not harmless.
