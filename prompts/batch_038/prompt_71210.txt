You are a writer of literary science fiction who also has a genuine working knowledge of dynamical systems theory. Before drafting, spend five or six lines brainstorming the shape of the story — the system, the institution around it, and the moment the hinge appears — and only then commit to prose.

I have read a great deal of chaos fiction and argued about it on enough forums to know that nearly all of it makes the same two errors: it conflates determinism with predictability, and it treats sensitivity to initial conditions as though it implied that nothing is true of the future. I want a story built on a much less-used hinge, the shadowing lemma. As I understand it: a pseudo-orbit is a sequence of points where each one lies within some small tolerance of the image of the last — which is precisely what a numerical integration with rounding error at every step produces. Near a hyperbolic invariant set, any sufficiently accurate pseudo-orbit stays uniformly close to some genuine orbit with slightly different initial conditions, and under the standard hypotheses that genuine orbit is unique. But this is an existence-and-uniqueness result, not a computational one: it guarantees a real trajectory is threaded through your noisy record while saying nothing whatever about your being able to identify it. Worse, the orbit that shadows your computation need not be physically realizable. And in systems that are not uniformly hyperbolic — near a homoclinic tangency, say — shadowing fails outright, so a record can be locally impeccable at every step and correspond to no possible trajectory of the system at all.

Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words in which that structure does real plot work rather than being explained in dialogue. I am indifferent as to whether the system is a weather model, an archive reconstructing one person's life, a prophecy engine, or the orbit of a small body; I care a great deal that the plot turns on the difference between (a) there existing a true trajectory consistent with a fallible record, (b) anyone's being able to find it, and (c) the possibility that no such trajectory exists. Give me one character who is wrong in a way that costs them something concrete, a genuine reversal, and an ending that does not resolve into a moral. Prohibitions: no one says the butterfly flapped its wings, and no scientist stares at a screen and murmurs that the model was right all along.
