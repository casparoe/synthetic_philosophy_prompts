I'm co-teaching a philosophy of mind seminar this term and the week on the unity of conciousness is mine, and I've done something slightly reckless: I've put Kazuo Ishiguro's *Klara and the Sun* on the list next to Nagel's piece on brain bisection, and now I need the pairing to actually work, because a colleague has already told me the book is a stylistic gimmick dressed up as a philosophy problem. Here's my case for it. Klara, the Artificial Friend who narrates, keeps describing her visual field as breaking up into boxes, sometimes two, sometimes more, each with its own content and sometimes pulling in different directions; she also talks about dividing *herself* into several Klara-s so that she can attend to more than one thing at once, and about not always being sure whether one of those segments knows what another has just noticed. That last bit is my memory of it and may well be faulty, so flag it if you don't recall it rather than politely going along with me. Separately, partway through she deliberately drains the P-E-G Nine solution she carries in her head in order to sabotage the Cootings Machine, knowing it will cost her some of her processing, and she then never reports anything like a disconnection effect. Then there's the Mr Capaldi plot: the mother wants Klara to move into an AF body built to look like Josie and continue her, and Josie's father Paul takes the view that there's nothing special in Josie that Klara could not, given enough observation, learn to reproduce. And the ending is Klara in the scrap Yard, immobile, content, declining to socialise with the other AFs, telling the Manager she's happy where she is.

I want a proper critical reading through the split-brain and unity-of-consciousness literature, running in both directions: what that literature makes of Klara, and what Klara does to the literature. A few things I want you to be tough about rather than agreeable about. First, is her box-partitioning a case of phenomenal disunity at all, or just a machine idiom for divided attention and a multiplicity of representations that would still count as a single unified field? Is the right comparator the commissurotomy patient, or is it something much more mundane like ordinary human multitasking and inattention, in which case the novel is philosophically less interesting than I'm hoping and I should know that before Tuesday. Second, I keep wanting to distinguish a *re-integrable partition*, temporary, quasi-voluntary, where the segments come back together and the subject reports continuity, from a *constitutive division*, a structural disconnection that yields two persisting centres of agency; tell me whether that distinction does any real work or whether I've just renamed the difference between a novel and a lesion study, and if it does work, whether Klara belongs in the first category and is therefore the wrong model for the split-brain debate entirely. Third, the counting question: how many subjects are there in Klara, and can we even ask? Nagel's difficulty was that neither the mentalistic nor the physical vocabulary delivers a clean answer for the patient, so does the novel put us in the same bind, or does it cheat, since we apparently get to look at Klara's insides in a way we never get to look at a patient's? Fourth, and this is both my strongest card and the one my colleague would attack first: the content of the book is division but the form is a serene, seamless first-person memoir with no gaps, no conflict reports, no sense of anything missing. Is that seamlessness evidence for unity, or is it exactly what an interpreter module would generate for behaviour it didn't initiate, or is it just Ishiguro keeping the prose pleasant? Fifth, where does the Josie continuation sit: is that Parfit-style fission, given that Klara doesn't split but is decanted into a body while the original Josie either dies or recovers, and what does Paul's nothing-special-in-Josie line do to the unity debate if it implies a unified self is just a pattern someone else could learn from the outside? And sixth, my hunch about what the book adds: Klara seems to *perform* her unity for an audience, the Manager, the mother, the household, so that unity here is a social and narrative achievement rather than a metaphysical given, which is a claim the journal articles mostly don't make. Test that hunch honestly and tell me if it's rubbish.
