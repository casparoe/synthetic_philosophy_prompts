I'm putting together a week on "the public" for a senior seminar — Kierkegaard's review of Gyllembourg's novel Two Ages (the 1846 one) as the anchor text — and every time I go to schedule it I find myself wanting to screen Network instead. Chayevsky's film, 1976, the one where a network turns its breakdown-prone anchor into a variety show and then commissions his on-air murder becuase the numbers slipped.

So here's what I want: read Network as a Kierkegaard film, and tell me honestly whether it can carry the weight.

The pieces I care about, so we're not talking past each other. Kierkegaard's complaint about the press isn't that it lies or that it debases taste. It's that the daily press manufactures "the public" — an abstraction with no members, no will and no responsibility, which is exactly why he treats it as a phantom rather than as a crowd you could point at — and that it converts talk into chatter, where everybody is made a participant in discussing everything and nobody is thereby put in a position to act. And "levelling" in his sense isn't homogenization or a lament about falling standards; it's the collapse of the distinctions (authority, the single individual, real passion) that make action possible at all.

Now the film. Where does that analysis actually bite? My instinct is that the shout-along sequence — Beale getting the whole country to lean out the window and yell — is the crux, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's meant as the return of passion, the thing Kierkegaard says a reflective age has lost, or as passion's counterfeit, which is what a press-made public looks like from the inside: a million people doing precisely the same thing at the same moment, which is the crowd and not the individual. Argue it out, don't split the difference.

Same question for the ending. His public is a phantom because nobody is answerable for it; the film gives us culpable human beings in a boardroom who decide to have the anchor shot, plus a network chairman who lays out the entire interlocking commercial system in one long speech, in his own voice, as a person. Is that the film getting something Kierkegaard missed — abstractions do act, through identifiable people who can be blamed and who can refuse — or is it a retreat from its own diagnosis, turning a metaphysical problem into a melodrama with villains?

And Max Schumacher: he's the one figure the film treats as having a job, a conscience and a passion that's real, and he loses the job, loses Diana to the network, and ends up as a rueful voice-over. Does the film offer him as the alternative to levelling and then deliberately undercut him, or is it doing that without knowing?

Where the pairing won't hold, say so plainly. Dragging a Danish critique of the daily press and of Christendom onto an American satire about ratings is an anachronism with a theological core, and Kierkegaard's remedy has nothing to do with reforming institutions — if the film turns out to be implying better journalism or better regulation, thats not him, and I'd rather know that before I staple the two onto the same syllabus. Paraphrase scenes rather than quoting dialogue at me; I've seen the film a dozen times and I'd catch a fudge, and I don't want invented lines.
