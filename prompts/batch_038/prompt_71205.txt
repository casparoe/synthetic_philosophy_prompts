Acting as a philosopher of mind who also works on the foundations of mathematics, and who is comfortable with both the incompleteness theorems and the aesthetics of self-reference, please work through the following argument carefully before you answer it. I assembled it for a reading group on strange loops. Read one step at a time, I accept every one of them; read together, I cannot live with the conclusion.

Setup. Call a level of a hierarchy **inviolate** when the account being given is not permitted to touch it — when that level is fixed from outside the account and taken as given rather than explained.

(1) If an account of some domain is built out of a hierarchy of levels and some level in it is inviolate, then the account stops short: an inviolate level is precisely a point at which the account has run out of explaining.
(2) By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, a consistent, sufficiently strong, effectively axiomatised system cannot prove its own consistency; that proof becomes available only from a strictly stronger system.
(3) So no level in the arithmetical hierarchy validates itself. Each needs a higher one, and the higher one needs a higher one again: there is no top level, and none is inviolate by virtue of being final.
(4) Escher's *Drawing Hands* exhibits just such a structure — each hand draws the other, and nothing inside the picture stands outside the circuit — and we look at it without the slightest sense of paradox.
(5) If a hierarchy with no inviolate level is entirely acceptable in (4), then insisting on one elsewhere is arbitrary.
(6) Hence no account requires an inviolate level. In particular, a mind's model of itself may be produced wholly by the processes that model describes, with nothing outside that circuit warranting it.
(7) But a warrant generated inside a closed circuit cannot distinguish a self-model that tracks its own workings from one that merely covaries with them, since both are equally products of the same loop.
(8) So on (6), there is no fact of the matter whether a mind's picture of itself is accurate.
(9) Self-understanding is plainly a gradable and fallible property: people are routinely wrong about their own motives and right about other people's.
(10) Therefore (6) is false, and some inviolate level is required after all.

I would prefer to keep (4) and (6) — the idea that a system can be whole without a level standing outside it is what drew me to this material in the first place — and I find (10) close to intolerable, since it seems to require a spectator outside nature to underwrite the mind.

Begin by stating in two or three sentences whether you take the argument to be invalid or valid but unsound, and only then develop the diagnosis. Tell me which step you reject; say exactly what that step equivocates on or assumes without argument; explain why it looked unobjectionable when read on its own; and price the alternatives. If instead the argument is valid and I must surrender (1), (6), or (7), say what each sacrifice costs and which is the cheapest. Concentrate on the steps that actually carry the trouble rather than restating the argument back to me.
