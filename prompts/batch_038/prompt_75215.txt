I am preparing a paper on the argument for supervised drinking that the Athenian Stranger puts to Clinias and Megillus in the opening books of the *Laws*, and I need a counterexample that survives the escape routes the Stranger has already built into his own account. I would like you to construct one to specification.

Here is the argument as I am working with it, in five steps:

A. Lawgiving aims at the whole of virtue, and virtue requires holding one's ground not only against pain and fear but against pleasure. The institutions the Cretans and Spartans are proud of — the gymnasia, the common meals, the marches and hunts and hardships imposed on the young — train men against the first of these only. Nothing in those cities tests a man against the second, so a citizen who has proven himself against pain may be undone by pleasure, having never been tried.

B. Suppose there were a drug that produced fear rather than confidence. We would not hesitate to use it: given in graded doses, under supervision, we could determine cheaply and safely which men keep their nerve and could habituate them to keeping it, instead of sending untested men into battle and finding out at ruinous cost.

C. Wine is that drug in reverse. It produces cheerfulness first and, as the dose increases, confidence, boldness, and eventually a loosening of shame; it strips a man of his self-command by stages. A soul in that condition is like iron in a furnace: softened, and therefore taking whatever shape is imposed on it while it is hot.

D. So the drinking party can be run as an instrument of civic education, provided it is directed. A drunken man needs a guide as much as a child does; with a sober, experienced, well-disposed leader who steers the company's admiration toward what is fine and its contempt toward what is shameful, the exposure becomes both a test — revealing which men hold their ground when their guard is down — and a training, since the holding is practiced, and practiced in company, and repeated.

E. A city that trains its citizens this way will have men who have been proven against both pleasure and pain, and who are therefore doubly reliable; the practice is cheap, and the risk is contained by the leader's supervision and by the modesty of the dose.

Now the construction task.

**Build a single case in which the method, run exactly as the Stranger describes it, makes a participant worse at resisting pleasure in ordinary life than he would have been had he never been trained — and in which the worsening is a result of the method, not of its abuse.** The case is meant to defeat step E from inside the framework, so it must be immune to the replies the Stranger has ready. Concretely:

1. The leader is precisely what D requires: sober throughout, experienced, of good character, genuinely skilled at directing the company's admiration and contempt, and doing exactly that on every occasion. The dose never exceeds what the Stranger would endorse, no one is harmed, humiliated, or ruined, and every participant is an adult volunteer who understands the purpose of the exercise. Failure of the case may not be attributable to a bad leader, to excess, or to deception.

2. The participant passes. By the Stranger's own criteria the training is a success: he keeps his nerve and his manners while the wine is doing its work, he is judged proven, he is admitted to further sessions, and he completes the course. The bad outcome must follow this success, not interrupt it.

3. The outcome is specifically a deterioration in his capacity to resist pleasure in the unsupervised, ordinary circumstances of his life, relative to a comparable man who received no such training. Not a loss of courage, not a scandal, not illness.

4. The mechanism must be licensed by premises A through D rather than an accident of the story, and it must run entirely on wine and on the psychology the Stranger himself supplies — the softening, the shaping, the practice, the proving. Do not invoke addiction, dependence, withdrawal, tolerance, or any other piece of modern pharmacology he could not have anticipated; a case that turns on those is a case he can dismiss as a physician's objection rather than a legislator's.

5. Realistic, in one paragraph, no speculative machinery. It may be set in the Stranger's own city or in a modern institution that has adopted his method — your choice, but say which and keep the setting ordinary.

The case must generate the outcome, not assert it. If your paragraph simply announces that the participant ends up worse, or if the worsening depends on a feature you have not shown to be doing work, the case fails.

Then, after the scenario:

(a) Identify which of A–E the case attacks, and show step by step how the outcome arises from the materials in the premises. If more than one premise is implicated, say which is load-bearing.

(b) Give the best reply available to the Stranger from within his own framework — not a strawman, and not an appeal to something outside the *Laws*. Say whether the reply rescues the argument or merely relocates the difficulty, and if it relocates it, say to where.

(c) Name the single feature of the case that does the work, and tell me what happens to the verdict when that feature is removed.

(d) Say whether the failure mode generalizes. Would it afflict any programme of graded exposure to pleasure — including non-chemical ones built on the same model — or is it specific to wine? If it generalizes, what has gone wrong is not the Stranger's choice of instrument but the shape of the method itself; say which.

Two housekeeping rules. Do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or Stephanus references, and do not quote the dialogue. Work from the summary above; if you think my summary misrepresents the argument at any point — if, for instance, the Stranger imposes a condition on the practice that I have left out and that would block your case — say so explicitly, state the condition, and then build the strongest version of the case you can under it rather than quietly revising my premises.
