Our philosophy-of-biology reading group is doing a session on counterfactuals about dead philosophers and I drew Popper. He died in '94, so I want to know what he'd make of the stuff that came after — specifically in the area he cared most about. He was the one who argued Darwinism was less a testable theory than a metaphysical research programme, and then seemed to walk a good chunk of that back when he wrote about natural selection and the emergence of mind, and again in his last book on life as problem-solving, where the organism is active rather than passive and everything is trial and error.

So what would that guy say about these four things?

1. Population genomics. We can now read selection coefficients off DNA, watch allele frequencies move in real time, count selective sweeps, compare change rates in protein-coding versus non-coding regions. Would he take that as fitness finally being measured independently of who survived, meaning the circularity charge is dead — or would he say the operationalisation just relocates the problem somewhere you can't see it?

2. The long-running bacterial evolution experiments, the lines that have been going for tens of thousands of generations in identical flasks, including the one that turned up able to use a nutrient its ancestors couldn't. Repeatable, controlled, some predictions made ahead of time. Does that make natural selection a testable theory in his sense, and would he think it ever genuinely risks being refuted?

3. Niche construction and the extended-synthesis crowd — developmental bias, plasticity, epigenetic inheritance, organisms building the environments that then select on them. Popper's late work reads almost like a preprint of this. Does he embrace it as vindication and claim priority, or suspect it of smuggling instruction-from-the-environment back in through the side door, which is exactly what he wanted to rule out?

4. The drift side: most molecular change being noise rather than selection. Would he see that as proof the programme has real empirical content, since parts of it can actually be knocked down?

And maybe the more interesting one: the tautology line got picked up by people who wanted to attack the teaching of evolution, which he hated. Would he disown the whole episode now, or defend it as a point about method that got misused?

For each, give me a verdict on what he'd say, then say separately how much of that is grounded in things he actually wrote versus you extrapolating from the shape of his system. i want the confidence attached to each claim, not a confident-sounding guess. And if you think I've set this up wrong — say if Popper basically already answered all of this before he died and there's no real post-1994 gap to fill, or if what-would-he-say questions are just fan fiction with footnotes — tell me that plainly rather than playing along.
