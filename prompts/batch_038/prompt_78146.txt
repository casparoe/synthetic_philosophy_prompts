I'm a novelist and there's a scene in my current draft I've rewritten maybe six times, and the problem is it's built on an arguement two real people had in my kitchen last spring, so I keep worrying that I'm letting the characters posture instead of actually argue. I'd like you to grade the real one for me.

The two people: my sister Anu, who's a molecular biologist — protein biochemistry, she works on aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases — and her partner Marcus, who translates poetry and teaches translation theory. It started because I'd read somewhere that DNA is "a code," and I asked, half-jokingly, whether it's a code in anything like Marcus's sense or whether that's just a borrowed word doing PR work for the science. They went at it for over an hour. Neither was showing off, both were being serious, and at the end they made pasta and declared a draw. I've compressed it and cleaned out the repeats, but the moves are theirs.

(1) Marcus opened with definitions: a code, in the strong sense, is a system of conventional pairings between signs and meanings, set up so a reader can recover a message. Nobody chose the pairings in DNA, nothing is a message *to* anything, there's no reader with intentions. So "code" is metaphor at best, and worse, it's a metaphor that quietly installs an author.

(2) Anu said he was using the wrong definition. A code is just a systematic mapping between two alphabets, and the translation table is literally that: 64 three-letter codons mapped onto amino acids plus stop signals. And the mapping is arbitrary in exactly the way he wants codes to be arbitrary — no chemistry forces UUU onto phenylalanine. You cannot derive the amino acid from the codon by physics; you have to look it up. If it has to be looked up, it's a table, and a table between two alphabets is a code.

(3) Marcus said that "arbitrary" for a real code means it could have been otherwise and still functioned, because some community could have agreed differently. Her table can't be otherwise: change it and every protein in the cell turns to garbage simultaneously. It's frozen. A frozen pairing isn't a convention, it's just a chemical fact that happens to look systematic.

(4) Anu said it isn't actually frozen, it varies. Vertebrate mitochondria read UGA as tryptophan instead of stop, and AUA as methionine instead of isoleucine; some ciliates read the stop codons as glutamine; in some organisms UGA means "insert selenocysteine," but only when a particular stem-loop structure is present elsewhere in the message; in certain archaea and bacteria UAG inserts pyrrolysine instead of terminating. On top of that, ribosomes sometimes slip a base and shift frame deliberately. So the mapping is context-sensitive and evolvable — dialects, basically, which makes it more like language, not less.

(5) Marcus said that was his point wearing her clothes. A sign whose "meaning" depends on surrounding context and on the physical machinery handling it isn't being decoded, it's being used causally. The stem-loop doesn't tell the ribosome anything; it's another physical condition, like temperature or salt. And then he accused her of sliding: she'd started with "the genetic code," one narrow table at one step of protein synthesis, and drifted into "DNA is a code for the organism," which is what I'd actually asked about. There is no table anywhere mapping genes to traits.

(6) Anu conceded the second claim is looser but said the first is a genuine code, and if he won't call that one a code he's made the word useless for anything outside human institutions. Then she brought in information: you can measure how much a sequence constrains what gets made, the constraint is specific, you can compute how many alternatives are ruled out. That's Shannon information, which is just a statement about mappings between alphabets and says nothing about minds at all.

(7) Marcus said Shannon deliberately bracketed meaning out of "information" at the start, so pointing at a quantity that was defined to ignore his question can't answer his question — which was about there being a message for somebody. He said it was like proving a poem has no author by counting syllables.

So. What I want is a move-by-move verdict, not a synthesis paragraph. Take (1) through (7) one at a time and rate each — does it land, partly land, or miss — and say why, attending to what the move actually claims rather than a stronger version of it. Where a move partly works, tell me which half works and which half doesn't. Then answer the underlying thing: is Anu right that the translation table is a code in a non-metaphorical sense, is Marcus right that the word smuggles in an author, or is this mostly a word fight with one real disagreement buried inside it that neither of them ever got to? If it's the last one, name the buried disagreement precisely.

And please tell me what neither of them said. If there's an obvious hole — something about how the table is physically implemented, or about whether the interesting case is really translation rather than the genome-as-blueprint business — I'd much rather write it into the scene now than have a reviewer who does this for a living find it later. I don't need references or a reading list; I need the reasoning, and I need it specific enough that I can put it in two people's mouths at a kitchen table without them sounding like they're reading a textbook at each other.
