So I'm a second-year doing a course on nineteenth-century German philosophy, and I grew up in Bristol, which means my self-set essay question isn't quite as abstract as it probably should be. We've just been reading the second of the Untimely Meditations, the 1874 one on the uses and disadvantages of history for life, and I want to run the whole Colston statue business through Nietzsche's three modes — monumental, antiquarian, critical. Here are the facts as I have them, so you don't have to guess and so I can tell you where I think I'm wrong: bronze statue of Edward Colston, a seventeenth-century Bristol merchant who made a chunk of his money through the Royal African Company and then gave a lot of it to Bristol charities, schools and almshouses; put up in 1895, part-funded by public subscription, with an inscription calling him one of the most virtuous and wise sons of their city; for most of the twentieth century he was just a local benefactor, there's a Colston Day, buildings still carry his name; from the 1990s onward people started campaigning about it; in 2018 the council agreed in principle to add a second plaque stating plainly what he'd been involved in, and then it stalled for nearly two years becuase nobody could agree the wording; on 7 June 2020 a crowd at a Black Lives Matter protest pulled the statue down, spray-painted it and rolled it into the harbour; it was fished out a few days later, and it's now in a Bristol museum, displayed horizontally with the graffiti and the protest placards left on it, as an exhibit about its own toppling; four people were charged with criminal damage and a jury acquitted them in January 2022; the plinth stood empty until a new plaque went up in April this year.

What I want from you is the disciplined mapping, not a summary of Nietzsche and not a summary of the controversy, both of which I already have in front of me. For each of the three modes: which actor in this story is occupying it, at what point, what the mode's characteristic pathology would look like in that position, and whether Nietzsche's own ranking of the modes by what they do for life gives him any basis for saying the actor should have done something different. Then the bits I can't sort out. Was the 1895 erection monumental at all? Monumental history is supposed to take the great individual as an exemplar to be emulated by the striving person, and I'm not sure a merchant-philanthropist is that, or whether what you've got in 1895 is civic boosterism dressed in monumental clothes — and if so, whether that's the mode degenerating or a different thing wearing its vocabulary. Which mode is the stalled plaque — that reads to me as antiquarian history trying to make a small concession to critical history and failing, and I want to know whether the framework can describe a failed transition between modes at all, or whether it only ever says that modes degenerate one at a time from the inside. Which mode is the toppling, and which mode is the museum display, which strikes me as neither: the object is no longer functioning as an exemplar and no longer being preserved as heritage, it's being kept as evidence of the event that ended its earlier career. And what about the trial, where a criminal jury effectively issued a verdict on a historical question — is there any place in the three modes for history being settled by an institution rather than by a people's felt need?

Here's the thing I keep tripping over and I'd rather you push on it than smooth it out. Nietzsche's apparatus seems to assume one subject with one past — a person or a people working out how much history it can bear and metabolise with what he calls plastic power, and the three modes are that subject's options. Bristol in 2020 was not one subject with one past. For some people in the city the statue was heritage to be protected; for others the very same object was an injury to be judged and broken; and both were local, both were Bristolian, both had Colston's charities and schools in their family history. In a very rough draft I've started calling this asymmetric belonging and claiming it's a case the three modes can't represent, but I suspect that might just be a label I invented to make the essay look original, and I want an honest verdict. So run it in both directions: which features of the episode the modes genuinely illuminate, which they misdescribe, and which they're simply silent about — my guess is the trial and the museum display are the two it can't handle. Then tell me whether the episode confirms the theory, is an anomaly it can absorb, or is a kind of case it never contemplated, and whether Nietzsche's actual diagnosis would be the one I'm reaching for: his modes were written for a culture he thought was homogeneous and sick from too much history, so maybe what he'd say about Bristol is that the city has too little plastic power in his sense rather than too many conflicting pasts. If you think the framework just doesn't apply, say so — but say specifically where it stops applying and why, rather than letting me off with a general mismatch.
