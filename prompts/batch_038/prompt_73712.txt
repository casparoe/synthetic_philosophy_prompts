So I got annoyed and wrote up the thing below to hand out Thursday. It's rough, there's a couple of typos, and section 4 is really just notes dressed as prose. I want comments on the argument rather than on the writing, and please don't send me references or a reading list — I'm on a plane half the week and mostly want to know whether the thing holds together at all.

**The price of filing a complaint**

1. *Setup.* Campbell and Manning's typology sorts moral cultures by how people respond to slights and by who counts as high-status. Honor: reputation is the whole game, you answer an insult yourself, and being a victim is close to being a loser. Dignity: everyone has inherent worth, sticks and stones, and you take real harms — theft, assault, breach of contract — to law without shame, but you're expected to shrug off minor offenses, and dragging the authorities into them is itself discreditable. Victimhood: sensitivity extends to small and unintentional slights, moral status accrues to victims, and the aggrieved routinely recruit third parties — administrators, HR, an online audience — to adjudicate and condemn.

2. *My claim.* The axis that actually separates the three isn't sensitivity and isn't status. It's the availability and price of a third party. In an honor setting third parties are everywhere but they're an audience, not a referee: their judgement doesn't settle the score, only your own response does. Dignity culture has a real referee, but a rationed one — the court, the state, the personnel office — reserved for a narrow class of harms, with a status penalty attached to using it outside that class. Victimhood culture has cheap, plentiful, eager referrees: a dean, a hotline, a timeline, a subreddit. On this reading, heightened sensitivity to microaggression is downstream of the price change. You can afford to notice a small harm when noticing it costs almost nothing and somebody is on call to tell.

3. *The consequence I actually care about.* Dignity culture isn't a stable middle term between the other two; it's an artifact of expensive referees plus a norm against appealing to them. Drop the price — bureaucratize the institution, then wire everyone to everyone — and the norm against appeal dissolves without anyone's moral intuitions changing. People don't stop believing everyone has inherent worth, they just start filing. That's why I think the culture-war framing is backwards: the fight isn't over values, its over the cost of a complaint. It also predicts a symmetry the labels hide. The right wing of the culture war complains to school boards, reports neighbors, organizes boycotts; it recruits referees as eagerly as its opponents do. If referee-cheapness is the driver, both sides should look like victimhood culture, and I think both do.

4. *So what about the normative question.* Asked whether any of the three is morally superior, I want to say the question is malformed as usually posed, because it compares surface behaviors. If the deep variable is referee availability, the real question is about referee quality: which culture equips its adjudicators to be least captured by the status games they're supposed to sit above? Then dignity culture's virtue was never its metaphysics of inherent worth but that it kept the referee pool small enough to be hard to game at scale — which is also its vice, since a small pool is exactly what shuts out the powerless. And the claim that victimhood beats honor turns empirical: its better only if a dean plus a pile-on make better referees than a dueling field, which is not obviously true.

Where I want you to push:

- Is section 2 self-defeating? Honor-and-shame settings are famously the most reputation-dependent there are — gossip, shame, kin pressure are working constantly — so saying honor treats third parties as an audience rather than a referee may be a distinction without content, or at least one I haven't earned. Tell me straight whether audience-vs-referee is doing real work or is a dodge.
- Am I saying anything the framework doesn't already contain? Campbell and Manning build on Donald Black, whose whole approach treats social control as varying with the structure and availability of third parties, so I worry section 2 is just Black's machinery with the serial numbers filed off and section 3 is just an application of it. If that's the honest verdict, say so, and then tell me whether anything in there is worth keeping as a separate thesis.
- Does section 4 evade the question my group actually asked? I want to know whether "compare the referees" is a legitimate reframing or a way of not rendering a verdict, and whether I've been unfair to dignity culture by reducing it to referee-rationing — it may have content about the moral person that my account simply can't absorb.
- Give me one discriminating test: a case or pattern where my account and the plain three-cultures-of-values account predict different things, checkable in principle without me having to run a new study. If you think no such case exists, that's a useful answer too, and I'd want to hear why.
