You're a philosopher of social ontology who's thought hard about natural kinds and who also actually reads demography papers. Think carefully, and sketch two or three candidate diagnoses before you commit to one.

A grad student in my department has an argument that generations aren't real kinds and I can't find the hole in it. Best reconstruction I can manage:

(1) A term picks out a real kind when it's introduced by pointing at samples that resemble each other and are produced by some common mechanism, and is then applied to whatever shares the underlying basis of that resemblance.
(2) "Millennial" was introduced exactly that way. Commentators pointed at samples — people in the 1981–1996 birth window everyone quotes — noticed shared attitudes, and extended the term to anyone sharing the underlying basis, i.e. having been formed during that particular stretch of history.
(3) And there is a common mechanism. Sharing a formative window (school-entry cutoffs, a recession landing right at graduation, smartphones arriving at a given age) causes people born in it to resemble each other in ways they don't resemble people born earlier or later.
(4) So "Millennial" picks out a real kind with an underlying basis, the way a chemical term does.
(5) But the same recipe works for any window I like. My roommate does segmentation for an ad agency and swears by cohorts of people born within 18 months of a product launch — samples exist, the mechanism is the same formation story, the resemblance is measurable, and she gets real predictive signal out of it. Likewise people born in a leap year who started school together, likewise "the class of '94."
(6) So either there are indefinitely many real kinds of person, arbitrarily narrow and overlapping, or (2)–(4) aren't doing what they looked like they were doing.
(7) I'm confident there aren't indefinitely many. "People born 3–9 Feb 1989" is no more a kind than "people with February birthdays."
(8) Therefore generations aren't real kinds either — just labels we project onto a continuum.

I want to keep (3) and (7), and I want to keep some sense in which Millennial isn't merely a label, since my whole dissertation chapter leans on cohort effects being real. I'd also rather not lose (1), because that's my account of how kind-terms get thier reference and I'd have to rebuild it. So: which step is the weak one, why did it look fine at the time, and what do I actually pay for giving it up? If the right move is to weaken something rather than reject it, say what the weakened version claims and whether it's still worth anything to me.
