I teach the duties-of-rescue week in a big intro ethics course, and this year I'm screening two clips from Spielberg's Schindler's List — the liquidation of the Kraków ghetto, where the camera keeps picking out the little girl in the red coat out of the crowd while Schindler watches on horseback from the hill, and the scene near the end where he's about to flee, the workers give him the gold ring, and he comes apart in front of Stern doing the arithmetic on his car and his Nazi party pin: the car would have been worth ten more people, the pin two more, and he could have got one more and didn't. The reading I assign alongside them is Singer's pond case. The problem is my students come out of those clips admiring Schindler instead of feeling indicted by him, which is close to the opposite of what Singer wants his argument to do, and I can't quite diagnose why.

So: a reading of the film through the rescue argument, taking both seriously rather than using one as an illustration of the other. I have a half-formed hunch I'd like you to pressure-test, and if it's wrong I want you to say so bluntly. It's that the film and the argument reach the same conclusion by moving in opposite directions. Singer's whole move is to strip out the features that make the pond case gripping — proximity, being the only bystander, the fact that the child is right there in front of you — and insist they're morally inert; the distant child is supposed to count exactly as much as the near one. Spielberg does the reverse: he makes thousands of anonymous, statistical, geographically-and-temporally-remote deaths feel personally obligatory by isolating one identifiable kid in a bright coat. So the film looks like a machine for converting statistical lives into identifiable ones and Singer is a machine for converting identifiable ones into statistical ones. If that's right the film isn't an illustration of the argument, it's a rival technology pointed at the same end, and I want to know whether it's a better or worse one — and what the answer implies about whether Singer's insistence that distance does no moral work can ever actually generate motivation in a human being, or whether it's a claim you can only be moved by indirectly, via the sort of cheap trick the red coat is.

Then the harder part, which is what I'm actually stuck on. The film seems to me to break at least two of the pond case's background assumptions, and I want you to say whether each is fatal or just a complication. First, in the pond case rescue is a one-off and cheap relative to what the agent has, and nobody else's rescue is crowded out; for Schindler every single rescue is a purchase — a bribe to Goeth, a slot on a list, enamelware traded on the black market — so saving someone genuinely consumes a slot someone else didn't get, and the film spends real time on the horror of the compiling itself, including the sequence where the women's train goes to the wrong camp and has to be bought back. Singer never really faces the triage version of his own case, or if he does somewhere I've missed it. Second, that final breakdown reads to me like the demandingness objection staged from the inside: Schindler has already given his fortune, his factory, his marriage and his safety, and the film's last moral note is that it still wasn't enough — a much bleaker claim than anything in the essay, closer to Susan Wolf's worry about moral saints except that Schindler doesn't look like a person who ever had a life worth surrendering. Tell me whether the film is actually claiming the demand is bottomless, or just showing us a man with survivor's guilt doing bad arithmetic, and how you'd tell those apart from inside the film rather than by importing an external theory of guilt.

Constraints, since this is going into a course reader: no Arendt or banality-of-evil detour, no Adorno-on-art-after-the-Holocaust, and no further-reading list — the students already have the essay and have seen the film, and I can't fact-check your citations anyway. Point me at specific scenes by describing them rather than quoting anything; I don't want invented dialogue. Say plainly where the film resists the Singerian reading and an honest critic just has to concede it, and don't cop out with 'the film is problematic as a Hollywood product' unless you'll name which aesthetic choices do the damage and how. End with one thing the film gets on the record about rescue that the argument, given its own form, structurally can't get on the record.
