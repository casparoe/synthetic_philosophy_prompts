I work at a small foundation that administers a post-conflict reconstruction fund. States and companies that had some hand in a conflict get asked to contribute, and our deed of trust requires us to publish a finding for each candidate saying how much of the harm they're responsible for, with contributions apportioned accordingly. We do about five of these a year. The clause we're stuck interpreting reads, in full: "The Foundation shall determine, for each candidate contributor, the extent to which its acts or omissions brought about the harm, and shall apportion contributions in proportion thereto; provided that no contributor shall be called upon by reason of acts done under compulsion, or in ignorance of their consequences."

Every time I try to write one of these up I end up back in the First World War argument, because that's the case where this exact problem got fought out in public. The war-guilt clause at Versailles never actually used the word guilt — it was drafted as a legal hook for reparations, the drafters didn't think of it as a moral verdict on anyone, and it was received as precisely that, with one of its two authors spending the rest of his life regretting the wording. Then, some four decades later, the case for a deliberately willed German war got argued from internal documents, and the pushback came that if everyone was sleepwalking toward the same catastrophe the finding stops being about anybody. The machinery I need obviously exists somewhere in between those two positions; I just don't have it in usable form.

So: write me a decision procedure my team can actually run on a candidate contributor. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what evidence would trip or clear each of the two provisos, where the procedure terminates in "no finding possible," and where it hands the call back to a person because the question is one of values rather than fact. Anywhere a step is really a judgment call wearing procedure as a costume, I want it labelled as such, not smoothed over.

Things it has to handle, because they come up on every single file:

- The compulsion proviso. Does it cover a state whose mobilization timetables and alliance commitments meant it couldn't back down once things started moving? What if it spent the previous twenty years building the machine that now compels it?
- The ignorance proviso. Does it cover ignorance that was itself culpable — a government that could have found out what kind of war it was starting and chose not to ask?
- Evidence of intent. What weight do we give a war-aims memorandum drafted by a chancellor's private secretary six weeks after the fighting began, never adopted as government policy, and used afterward to argue the whole thing had been planned? Our staff split down the middle on whether documents like that prove intention or are just elite brainstorming, and the procedure has to say something rather than punt.
- The unit problem. When the clause says "its acts or omissions," is the actor the cabinet of the day, the state across time, or the population that ends up writing the cheque? We bill the state, but the case is about a cabient that dissolved a century ago, and I need the procedure to be explicit about which of the three it's talking about at each step instead of sliding between them.
- Proportionality. "In proportion thereto" assumes responsibility comes in degrees that add up to something. Tell me what to do when it doesn't: four contributors each of whom was independently sufficient, or the honest answer that removing any one of them wouldn't have changed anything.

Then run it on three cases. Two invented ones are fine — a state that gave an ally a general assurance of support, after which the ally started the war; and a state whose only role was supplying components through intermediaries with no knowledge of end use. For the third use the actual WWI record. At least one of the three has to come out with an answer I won't like, and I want you to tell me at the top which one it is, so I'm not blindsided in front of the board.

Constraints: three weeks per finding, one junior researcher with a history degree and no legal training does the first pass, and a three-person board signs off at the end. It has to survive being run by someone who hasn't read any of the philosophy, and it has to terminate in something the board can vote on.

No reading list, please — I know roughly who's who, what I'm missing is the machinery. And don't lay out the rival accounts of what it means to hold a nation responsible and leave me to pick between them. Choose one, build the procedure on it, say why that one over the obvious rival, and tell me concretely where I'd be led astray if I'd built it on the rival instead. I'd rather have a committed answer that I can argue with than a menu. Flag the things that are genuinely contested, but still take a position on them.
