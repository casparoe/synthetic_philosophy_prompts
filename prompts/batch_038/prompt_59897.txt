Three claims, held together, make up a position about famines that I have come to regard as correct. I want to know how much it is actually risking, and where.

First, a causal claim: in the great famines of the modern period an absolute shortfall in food was rarely the operative cause. What killed people was the loss of one particular group's command over food — landless labourers, sharecroppers, fishermen, transport workers, anyone whose sole asset was labour that no longer had a buyer — combined with an administration that failed to act on what was plainly visible to it. My paradigm is Bengal in 1943. The autumn 1942 rice crop in the delta had been damaged by a cyclone and tidal flooding and by crop disease, and the aggregate figures for that year are disputed, which I know; but the famine killed somewhere between two and three million people in a province that was still moving rice, while the authorities stripped boats and grain stocks from the coastal districts as a precaution against a Japanese landing, wartime spending around Calcutta drove prices up against lagging rural wages, hoarding and panic buying did the rest, and the government refused for months to concede that a famine existed. The same shape seems to me to run through the Bangladesh famine of 1974, where aggregate availability was unremarkable and mortality concentrated on people whose employment had vanished, and through the Maharashtra droughts of the early 1970s, where a severe harvest failure met with public employment works on an unprecedented scale produced hardship but no famine.

Second, a modal claim: in essentially every modern famine there was a feasible alternative — rationing, imports, relief works, an end to requisitioning, letting people walk to where there was food — and the reason it was not taken was political. On this reading the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959 to 1961 belongs in the same family as Bengal even though output genuinely fell there: procurement quotas were set against harvest figures inflated at every level of the reporting chain, criticism was silenced from mid-1959 onward, and the rural population was administratively prevented from migrating.

Third, a normative claim: given the first two, famines are not natural disasters that politics then mishandles. They are political events from the outset, and describing them as crimes is apt rather than merely rhetorical.

Now: what would have to be true for this package to be wrong? I want the material sorted into three grades — what would force me to abandon it, what would force me to narrow it, and what I could acknowledge without changing my mind.

- Take the Irish famine of 1845 to 1852 as the hardest test. The crop the poor actually ate collapsed; they had nothing left to sell, so their loss of command over food was not a market-mediated event but the direct failure of their own production; roughly a million died and a comparable number left; and grain and livestock continued to be exported throughout. Does my position survive this, or does it depend on sliding from the state could have done something to the state's inaction caused the deaths? What specifically would have to be established about the volume of exports, about whether Irish food supplies would have sufficed in the counterfactual, and about the administrative and transport capacity of the 1840s, for Ireland to count as a genuine defeater rather than an awkward one?
- Tell me what pattern in the numbers would discriminate the two accounts: whether per-head supply was flat or falling; whether mortality tracked the harvest curve or the real wage and relative price curves; whether deaths were concentrated by occupation and district in the way the first claim predicts, or spread evenly across a uniformly short region; whether prices rose more than a real shortfall would warrant. Then say honestly whether any of this is recoverable at the required resolution for 1840s Ireland or 1943 Bengal — and if the dispute is undecidable on the available record, whether that damages my position more than the rival one.
- Address the insulation worry directly. The affected group seems to be identified with whoever starved, after the fact, so no distribution of deaths can falsify the causal claim; and the modal claim is a counterfactual about administrative feasibility that an idealised state always seems able to satisfy. Is the position unfalsifiable? If so, is that a defect, or is it what a framework for causal analysis rather than a predictive hypothesis is entitled to? Which of the three claims can I give up to save the others, and which cannot be given up without the rest collapsing?
- Distinguish objections that a serious defender of this general approach would themselves concede as damaging from objections they would brush aside, and explain what makes the difference. Relatedly: what does the production-side account risk that mine does not, and does that asymmetry count against me?
- Where does the third category sit — sieges, blockades, scorched-earth requisition, the deliberate destruction of food stores, the closing of borders to starving peasants? There people are physically prevented from reaching food rather than priced out of it. Does the vocabulary of command over food cover those cases, or does it soften them?
- Finally, on the normative claim: can a famine be wholly natural in origin and still criminal in the response, and can it be wholly political in origin without being criminal — the product of ideological error, sincerely held but false causal beliefs, and administrative incapacity? What separates mass starvation that has criminals from mass starvation that has only blunderers, and does my third claim need that distinction in order to mean anything?

The thing I would personally find most decisive is a well-documented case of region-wide absolute shortage in which the transport, finance, and administrative technology of the period, deployed by a government that was both competent and trying in good faith, could not have averted mass death. Tell me whether such a case exists, how one would go about establishing it, and what happens to the modal claim if it cannot be established in either direction. Argue from the substance of the cases and the structure of the reasoning; I am not looking for a list of works to read.
