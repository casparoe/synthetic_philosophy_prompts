I am grading the written section of a final exam for an undergraduate course on Nietzsche. Below is the question and one student's response to it. Please assign a grade out of 20 and write the comments I will return with the paper.

Question: State the doctrine of the eternal recurrence of the same and explain what work it does in Nietzsche's published writings. Should the reader treat it as a cosmological claim, as an existential test, or as both? Justify your answer with reference to specific texts.

Student response: The doctrine of the eternal recurrence of the same holds that this life, down to every detail, will return in exactly the same sequence an infinite number of times. It first appears in The Gay Science (1882), in the section on the greatest weight that closes Book Four: a demon creeps into your loneliest loneliness and tells you that everything you have lived you will live again, with nothing new in it, and asks whether you would curse him or fall down and call him divine. The point is not to establish a fact about the world but to test the reader, to find out whether you are someone who can say yes to your life as it is. This is what Nietzsche elsewhere calls amor fati, so recurrence and amor fati are really two names for the same attitude, one stated as a scenario and the other as a virtue. The doctrine is then developed at length in Thus Spoke Zarathustra: in the vision chapter Zarathustra recounts the shepherd choking on the black snake, and in The Convalescent he at last proclaims the doctrine joyfully, with his animals celebrating it, so by the end of the book the recurrence is presented as achieved rather than as still in question. The metaphysical grounding Nietzsche gives the doctrine comes from the notebooks: because the total quantity of force in the universe is finite while time is infinite, every configuration of forces must recur. The Gay Science passage is meant to be read together with that argument as a single claim, even though the notebooks were only assembled and published after his death, by his sister and Peter Gast. After Zarathustra Nietzsche largely drops the doctrine, presumably because he came to see that no argument could establish it.
