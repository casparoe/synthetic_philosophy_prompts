I did thirty-odd years in a lab doing spectroscopy, and only got into philosophy properly since I retired, so I know I can be too trusting of a confident author. Here's my problem. A friend lent me one of those big popular histories of economic thought, a chapter per thinker, and the Hume chapter opens with this summary of the essays on money and trade:

"Hume's political essays of the early 1750s contain, in miniature, the monetary theory that the classics would later get the credit for. Money, he writes, is not one of the wheels of trade but 'the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy'; consequently the quantity of money a nation holds is of no consequence to its wealth, which depends entirely on the number and the industry of its people. Hence his famous thought experiment: annihilate four-fifths of all the coin in Britain overnight and nothing real has been lost. Prices sink in proportion, British labour and manufactures become the cheapest anywhere, the money flows back in, and the nation is restored to its level. This is the price-specie-flow mechanism, as later writers named it, and it disposed of mercantilism: a surplus is self-correcting, so there is no balance of trade worth defending and no need for the state to manage the money supply. For the same reason Hume was hostile to paper credit — multiplying bank notes adds nothing to the stock of labour and commodities, it only makes prices higher. His outlook was accordingly cosmopolitan rather than national: he could say that he prayed for the flourishing commerce of Germany, Spain, Italy, and even France itself, since one nation's gain is never another's loss. What Hume had discovered, in short, was the neutrality of money, and the laissez-faire that goes with it."

It reads plausibly and I half recognised some of it, but a couple of the sentences itch. In physics you learn to smell it when someone takes a result that holds under stated conditions and quietly drops the conditions, and I think that may be happening here. The one bit of evidence I have on my side: in the essay on interest I underlined a line that seems to cut against the chapter, to the effect that "In the progress towards these changes, the augmentation may have some influence, by exciting industry; but after the prices are settled, suitably to the new abundance of gold and silver, it has no manner of influence." So is the book's author just skipping over that, or am I misreading it?

Could you go through the summary line by line and tell me which sentences a Hume scholar would nod at, which are simplifications that are still fair, and which are actually wrong or swap in something Hume didn't say? Specifically I want a verdict on four things: whether the reason the chapter gives for Hume's hostility to paper credit is his reason; whether "no need for the state to manage the money supply" survives contact with what Hume himself proposed doing about banks and coin; whether the claim that one nation's gain is never another's loss is something Hume held without exception, because I have a vague memory of a carve-out; and whether that last sentence about neutrality and laissez-faire follows from the accurate parts of the summary or is imported from two centuries later. Also flag anywhere the chapter describes the adjustment as purely a price story when Hume's version is doing something else.

No reading list, please, and no references — I can't look anything up and I'd probably just end up with invented ones. Just your own reading, and tell me plainly if the whole thing is further off than I suspect.
