Our team is writing internal guidelines for when an eval report is allowed to say a model "believes" something, and one of us torpedoed the whole project with an argument I can't climb out of. He thinks it's a happy result: no more arguing about model psychology, because there's nothing to argue about. Here it is, more or less as he laid it out.

(1) To attribute a belief to a system is just to take up the intentional stance toward it; there's no further fact about whether the system really has the belief over and above what the stance licenses.
(2) What the stance licenses is fixed by predictive payoff: you attribute whatever beliefs make the system's behavior come out predictable, and no more than that.
(3) But payoff is always relative to a grain and to the stretch of behavior your'e scoring against, and for any system and any proposition you can choose a grain and a stretch such that the system comes out as believing it — this is basically the old triviality worry about implementation, the Putnam-style one where every ordinary open system ends up realizing every finite automaton, because you can always map physical states onto automaton states so the actual trace lines up.
(4) So for any system S and any proposition p, there's a stance-relative attribution of p to S that meets the standard in (2).
(5) An attribution practice according to which every system believes everything isn't an attribution practice anymore; it's a way of talking that has run out of work to do. If a system comes out believing it's a helpful assistant, believing it's a rock, and believing it's neither, the stance has stopped tracking anything at all.
(6) Therefore the stance licenses nothing determinate, so statements like "the model believes the user is testing it" have no fact of the matter, and our guidelines are only choosing vocabularies.

My first instinct was to attack (5) as question-begging — he's smuggling in the assumption that the stance has to carve reality up non-arbitrarily, which is what he wants to prove. He says no, (5) is just a remark about what an attribution practice is for, and if I deny it I'm happy to have an empty practice. Then I thought maybe the trouble is that grain-relativity doesn't imply emptiness anyway, the same way "flat" being comparative doesn't make flatness talk empty — there's a default grain and everything's fine. But that doesn't touch (3) and (4), which is where I actually feel the pull, and I can't say what's wrong there. I do know people have answers to these triviality arguments, so I assume (3) is too crude as stated; I just don't know how crude, or whether fixing it saves (6) in some weaker form I'd still hate.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look innocent enough to get past me, and what do I owe once I reject it? For what it's worth I'd much rather keep (1) and (2) than (3), because if I give up the stance's instrumentalism I'm pushed toward saying that whether the model believes something is settled by what's inside the weights, and I have no idea how to make that precise or how to test it. Also please don't stop at the philosophy — tell me what our guidelines would have to say on your preferred repair, what we'd have to do to an eval to make an attribution determinate, and what the leftover problem is that the repair doesn't solve.
