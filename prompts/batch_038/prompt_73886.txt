I write novels for a living, and last month I was the middle seat at a festival dinner between two people I had only just met: Marta, who teaches philosophy of action, and Owen, an economist who models intertemporal choice. Somewhere around the cheese they fell into an argument about a third party, a man neither of them knew, whom I will call Julian because his real name is not mine to give. Neither of them asked anything of me and I asked nothing of them, but I have wanted the argument settled ever since, because I am trying to determine whether a man like Julian is possible at all. I should like a verdict, not a summary.

The facts they agreed on: Julian is a literary translator of fifty-one who has said for the better part of fifteen years that he is about to begin a novel of his own. He has not begun it. He has four pages. In every other respect he is a model of self-command — he rises at six, he has never missed a contract deadline, he cleared his mortgage nine years early, he files his return in February, he has run the same six-mile loop four mornings a week since 1998, and he has turned down three better-paid posts in order to protect the hours that make all of this possible.

Marta opened by calling him a paradigm of weakness of will: he judges sincerely and without qualification that it would be better all things considered to write the book, he believes he can, and he does not. Owen replied that this smuggles in a single continuous agent. On the present-bias picture, each morning's Julian is a fresh decision-maker who weights the immediate costs of writing — the blank page, the exposure, the morning's forgone translation income — enormously against a reward lying years off. Nothing he does on any given day need contradict his own judgment; there is no failure of will because there is no unitary will to fail. What Marta called a defect of character, Owen called an arithmetic.

Marta pressed two objections. First, that the many-selves story is indiscriminate: it renders every instance of procrastination non-akratic by construction, so it cannot distinguish Julian from a man acting under compulsion or duress, and a diagnosis that cannot misfire has not diagnosed anything. Second, that Owen's picture predicts present bias travels — a man who discounts a novel at fifteen years should also discount a mortgage, a deadline, and a wet February run — yet Julian is the precise reverse in those domains. She said that if discount rates are measured so differently for money, health, and vocation, then the economist has no single parameter with which to explain him, and his account collapses back into "he is the sort of man who does this," which is the character talk he set out to eliminate.

Owen conceded the domain point in part, allowing that there is no reason to expect one rate to travel from money to a life's work, but said this served him rather than her: grant domain-specificity and Julian is simply a man with a well-practised rate where he has a history of success and a severe one where he has never had a single completion to calibrate against. He then attacked her premise, saying her charge requires knowing Julian's all-in ranking better than Julian does, and that Julian has an ordering he states readily — finish the two remaining contracts, wait until the younger child reaches secondary school, then begin. Sequencing, he said, is not weakness; a man working down a list in order is not failing to do what he judges best, he is doing the second thing on it.

Marta said that move was unfalsifiable in exactly the way she had charged against his first one, since any observed inaction can be redescribed as the correct execution of a hidden ordering, turning "he does what he most wants" into a tautology. She then proposed a test: put a commitment device in front of him — a residency with a forfeitable deposit, a friend holding the draft in escrow. If he accepts it, he has all along judged his own future self untrustworthy, which is a judgment about weakness rather than arithmetic. If he refuses it while still saying he wants to write, then either he does not want it, in which case there is nothing to explain, or he wants it and cannot bring himself to secure it, which is what weakness of will is. She said Owen's framework has no third box and that Julian evidently lives in one.

Owen called the dilemma false. Refusing a device needs no akrasia: devices are costly, they forfeit flexibility, and an agent who correctly anticipates that binding himself will make the bound activity hateful may decline on prudential grounds. He then admitted that one datum resisted him — Julian's self-reproach, reportedly a reliable twice-yearly performance at New Year, in which he calls himself a coward. His gloss was that this is not evidence of a divided will but propaganda installed by an earlier self so that later selves will feel bad enough to comply. He granted that the gloss was unlovely.

Please go through the exchange stage by stage — the opening pair of claims, Marta's two objections, Owen's concession and counterattack, Marta's device test, Owen's refusal and his account of the self-reproach — and rate each one in usable terms (clearly right, partly right, clearly wrong, or something finer if you prefer), with a sentence or two of justification per stage. Then tell me whether either of them in fact won, and separately name whichever of Julian's features the two of them between them left on the table: the four pages, the mortgage, the running, the three refused posts, the New Year performances. Please keep the whole adjudication to 700 words or fewer.
