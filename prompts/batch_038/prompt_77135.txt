I am a second-year student in a joint philosophy and cognitive science degree, and this term I am taking a seminar on paradox, self-reference, and theories of mind. We spent three weeks on Escher's Drawing Hands, on the idea of a tangled hierarchy, and on what Hofstadter calls the inviolate level, before turning to the first incompleteness theorem and the arguments people have tried to build on it. Our reading list was almost entirely primary sources, so I was unprepared for how much more confident a piece of popular writing on the same material could sound. Over the weekend I read a long essay in a general-audience science magazine by a physicist who writes well — I have since misplaced the issue, and it also contained a stretch on closed timelike curves that has nothing to do with any of this — which appeared to weld the Escher material and the Gödel material into a single argument with a conclusion about artificial intelligence at the end. Our tutor has asked each of us to bring one piece of outside writing to next week's discussion and say whether it can be trusted, so I would like a careful audit rather than a general impression. I have transcribed the relevant passage in full:

'Escher's Drawing Hands is the clearest picture of a genuine paradox ever drawn. Each hand is brought into existence by the other, so each is at once the cause and the effect of something at its own level, and what results is a real contradiction on the page: an object that both creates and is created. Hofstadter's insight is that the contradiction only looks unresolvable because we are stuck inside the tangle. Climb one rung — to the level of the sheet of paper, and beyond it to the artist's actual hand, which drew both hands — and the paradox evaporates, because at that level every causal arrow points the same way. He calls this the inviolate level: the level of a hierarchy that the loop cannot reach, and which therefore holds the whole structure together. Every tangled hierarchy has one, and locating it is how you dissolve the paradox. Gödel's theorem is the same trick run on mathematics. Any formal system rich enough to do arithmetic contains a sentence that says, in effect, "I am not provable here." That sentence is true, and a system that cannot prove all of its own truths has failed in just the way a system that proved a falsehood would have failed: Gödel showed that a sufficiently rich system is either inconsistent or incomplete, and since inconsistency destroys any claim to knowledge, incompleteness is the same defect wearing a milder mask. The only repair is to add the unprovable sentence as a fresh axiom, which produces a larger system with a new unprovable sentence of its own. Arithmetic is thus an infinite regress that never arrives at an inviolate level, and no consistent theory of arithmetic can prove its own consistency. This is why a machine can never wholly understand itself: a computer is a formal system locked inside its own levels, whereas a human mathematician can always step outside the system that generates her reasoning and see truths it cannot prove — precisely the move a self-modelling artificial intelligence would be unable to make.'

Could you work through this sentence by sentence and tell me how much of it survives scrutiny — sorted into what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification for a popular audience, and what is wrong, with the wrong parts explained rather than merely flagged? I want to be told separately what a logician working on incompleteness would object to and what someone who has read Hofstadter closely would object to, since I suspect the essay offends both in different ways; in particular I would like the consistency / incompleteness / soundness business untangled, because I have a feeling that distinction is doing a great deal of unearned work. Please also say what becomes of the two supporting claims I most want to rely on: the idea that the Gödel sentence simply says of itself that it is unprovable, and the idea that the second incompleteness theorem is what the essay's remark about a theory proving its own consistency rests on — including whatever conditions the essay quietly drops. I should tell you where my own sympathies lie. I found the inviolate-level paragraph persuasive, and I am inclined to agree that stepping up a level dissolves the paradox in Drawing Hands, though a classmate objected that the move is question-begging: it explains the picture by appeal to a level the picture does not depict, so on his account the essay has not dissolved the paradox but only relocated it one rung higher, and the same could be said of the mathematical version, where the enlarged theory's new Gödel sentence reappears at the next level up. Tell me whether that objection has any force, and whether the essay's own admission about the infinite regress concedes the point. Finally, does the closing conclusion about machines follow from any of the claims in the passage that remain true after correction, or does it depend on the errors — and if it can be argued for independently, how much of the essay's reasoning would still be needed to get there?
