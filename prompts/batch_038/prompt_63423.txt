ok so last night i got into a genuinely long argument with a friend of mine who does commercial law, about whether a rule can stop itself from being changed, and it wrecked my evening, so I've decided to turn the whole thing into an assessment for the jurisprudence seminar I teach and make the students feel the same.

Here's the setup I want to use. A members' association has a written charter with three relevant articles. Art. 7: "Every member shall have one vote." Art. 23: "This charter may be amended by a two-thirds vote of the members present." Art. 24: "Art. 7 may not be amended." At the annual meeting the members pass, using Art. 23's two-thirds procedure, an amendment deleting Art. 24. Ten minutes later they pass, again under Art. 23, an amendment giving the founding families three votes each.

My friend says the second amendment is void, full stop: Art. 23's power to amend is conferred subject to Art. 24, so using it to delete Art. 24 was already outside the power, and everything downstream falls with it. I said: fine, but then tell me why the first step counts as an exercise of Art. 23 at all rather than a small revolution that happened to be wearing the right clothes, and tell me exactly where the bootstrapping becomes illegal — because Art. 23 does not distinguish between the two amendments. And if the two-step route always works then Art. 24 is a speed bump; if it never works then no charter can bind its own future, which looks like a reductio of Art. 24 rather than of my two-step. She said I was being cute. Possibly.

What I want from you is the assessment item. Write one take-home question built on this charter (students get 48 hours, 1500 words), a model answer of about 800 words, and a marking scheme that says specifically what seperates an A from a B. It must not be answerable by naming Hart's rule of recognition or Kelsen's Grundnorm and moving on — those labels should be scaffolding at most. I want the crux to be whether Art. 24 limits the *content* of what may be amended or the *manner and form* of amending it (by which I mean: the procedure through which a valid amendment gets made, as opposed to what the amendment says), and whether that distinction actually does the work my friend needs it to do or just shifts the problem up one level. There should also be a sub-question forcing students to say what would have to be true of Art. 23 for Art. 24 to bind anything, and to argue the best version of the side they reject.

Marking scheme out of 25: five or six moves a top answer must make, with marks attached, then the specific mistakes that should cost marks — e.g. treating this as a question about what a court would decide (there may be no court, and "someone will adjudicate it" is not an answer to "is it valid"), sliding between valid and enforceable, assuming the regress is vicious without arguing that it is, or concluding that entrenchment clauses are pointless just because they aren't self-executing. Finish with a short sketch of the bad-but-common answer so I can spot it in thirty seconds. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list — the students aren't required to cite anything and neither are you; everything they need should be in the question itself.
