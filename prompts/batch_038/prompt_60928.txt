I serve on the five-member board of a private family foundation. Two of our purposes are governed by restricted funds with fixed mandates — one directed at reducing the suffering of farmed animals, one at child survival in low-income settings — and a third, unrestricted pool of roughly $1.5 million a year is allocated by simple majority. I should disclose a bias at the outset: before joining the board I spent some fifteen years reading and posting in rationalist spaces about gains from trade, moral parliamentarianism, and the general thesis that two parties with different terminal values can almost always do better by exchanging support than by each defending its own program in isolation. I arrived on the board believing that trades of that kind were close to free value. The case now before us has unsettled that belief, and I would like a memo that handles it more carefully than I have managed to.

The case. Two proposals compete for this cycle's unrestricted pool, each seeking the full $1.5 million. The animal-welfare trustee's proposal is a legislative and corporate-pressure campaign; she reports that the coalition running it will not accept funding below $1.1 million, and that beneath that line the campaign simply does not happen — no partial credit, the outcome is zero. Funded in full, she estimates it at roughly thirty million years of cage confinement avoided. The child-health trustee's proposal is a distribution program that scales close to linearly: at $1.5 million he estimates two thousand under-five life-years saved, and about a thousand at $750,000. The board is split two–two with me in the middle. The obvious move — split the pool — therefore produces a thousand life-years and nothing whatever on the animal side, which both trustees agree is worse, by each of their own lights, than a deal that fully funds one proposal now and the other next cycle. As consideration for the deferral, the animal-welfare trustee has offered her vote, this cycle and in writing, for the standardized outcome-reporting resolution he has failed to pass for two years: her grantees detest it because confinement-avoidance is hard to measure, and adopting it will cost her program real administrative pain.

A third trustee and I have proposed a standing protocol to make such deals explicit rather than a matter of corridor logrolling. Each trustee whose mandate is touched states in writing what the deal is worth in her or his own units only — years of confinement avoided, life-years saved, or in one case a private weighting the trustee refuses to publish — and certifies that she gains. No exchange rate is computed and the units are never compared against one another; the secretary records only that every affected trustee certified a gain, and the deal proceeds solely on unanimity of those certifications.

The fifth trustee objects, and I do not think her objection is sentimental. Her points, as I understand them: a mandate held on behalf of beneficiaries who cannot consent is a duty rather than a preference available for swapping; the certifications are unverifiable, so the protocol supplies no discipline at all against a trustee simply asserting a gain in order to buy passage of what she wanted anyway; the two mandates are not equally liquid, since the child-health purpose can be advanced by many substitute programs and many other funders while the animal-welfare purpose has, in practice, only us, so a long run of individually certified improvements can hollow out a restricted purpose without any single step being objectionable; and next year's board is not bound by this year's bargain, which means the thing actually being traded is a promise we have no power to keep.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting, on the order of a thousand words. It needs to contain: a clear recommendation — adopt the protocol, reject it, or adopt it with named amendments — together with a motion the board could actually vote on, including what we do with this cycle's pool; a direct answer as to whether the fifty-fifty split is genuinely dominated for both sides, and whether the deferral-and-resolution bargain survives the possibility that next year's board repudiates it; an assessment of whether certifying a gain in one's own units really avoids interpersonal comparison or quietly reintroduces one somewhere I have not noticed; an argument for whether the board should treat this particular bargain and the standing protocol as separable questions, since my own view is that they are separable and that most of the fifth trustee's objections attach to the protocol rather than to the deal, though I want that position argued rather than assumed; and the fifth trustee's strongest objection stated at full strength, with an explicit account of what my recommendation concedes to it. Reason from the facts as given, with no references, citations, or suggested further reading, and please do not recite the general case for moral trade back to me — I wrote a fair amount of it myself, on a forum, years ago. I would rather the analysis stay intact in places where it makes the document uncomfortable to read aloud to the board.
