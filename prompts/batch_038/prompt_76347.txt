I am a senior engineer at a B2B software company of about 300 people, and I need help thinking through a disagreement I have failed to resolve for several months. I have no training in philosophy, so I cannot tell whether the problem I have run into has a familiar shape. I would rather understand what kind of problem it is than collect tactics for the next meeting.

For roughly four years the company has been run on a single nightly number: the count of "active accounts," produced by a warehouse job and displayed on a dashboard that every executive review opens with. Hiring plans, pricing changes, and board materials all refer to it. Around it we have a weekly ritual in which the analytics group predicts how the number will move and explains why. The two or three people who are consistently good at this have considerable informal standing, and the ritual is where junior employees get noticed by leadership.

Fourteen months ago we replaced our in-house event store with a third-party pipeline. Since then the number has climbed steadily, while support volume and cancellation notices have climbed as well. I spent about six weeks rebuilding the metric from raw server logs, which almost no one touches because it is slow and unwieldy. My version says active accounts have been declining since approximately the migration, and that the new pipeline widens what counts as activity in ways nobody approved or noticed.

Here is where I am stuck. The objections to my rebuild are never that my arithmetic is wrong. They are that my deduplication and session-boundary rules are my own choices; that the vendor's audit report does not match my figures; that raw logs are known to be incomplete; and — the one that unsettles me most — that the company has now made a year of decisions using the dashboard number and most of them turned out well. Meanwhile, since I began thinking at the level of raw events, I have gotten noticeably worse at the weekly prediction exercise. I used to be good at it. Now I hesitate, because the question being asked is not the question I have been living inside, and people have begun describing me as distracted. Last month a junior analyst asked me about the gap between the two numbers; within a week her manager had reassigned her, and I was told, kindly, that this was out of scope.

My questions:

1. Is there a principled way to characterize what my colleagues and I actually disagree about — a fact, or what would settle a fact?
2. Could any evidence I am in a position to produce be decisive for people whose judgment of the evidence and whose standing both depend on the existing number? If so, what would that evidence look like?
3. Is their distrust of me irrational, or a reasonable response to what is visible from where they sit — including the fact that I have gotten worse at the thing this group is genuinely good at?
4. If the dashboard number is what the organization acts on, and acting on it has real consequences, is it even correct to call it wrong? I worry that I am assuming there is a fact of the matter the number was supposed to match, when in practice the number has become the thing itself.
5. What should I do next, and is there a version of letting this go that is not simply surrender?
