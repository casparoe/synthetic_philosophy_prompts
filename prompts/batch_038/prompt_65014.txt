You are a philosopher of language and metaphysics who works on theories of truth. Think the diagnosis through carefully before writing it, and begin by sketching briefly what would have to be the case for each candidate diagnosis to come out correct.

The dispute between deflationists (Horwich's minimalism, Field's disquotationalism) and their substantive opponents (correspondence theorists, alethic pluralists of Lynch's sort) has run for decades and the standard moves are spent. The deflationist says the truth predicate exists to enable generalizations we could not otherwise form — endorsing what a witness said, or what a whole theory says, without enumerating instances — and that its theoretical role is exhausted by the equivalence schema. The opponent says this cannot account for truth's normativity, cannot explain the explorer's survival by appeal to his beliefs about the ice being true, and hands us a device rather than an account; the deflationist replies that these complaints either trade on a picture of explanation she has no reason to accept or simply restate the substantive thesis as an unargued requirement. Neither side budges.

My hypothesis is that they are not disagreeing about truth at all, but about a methodological principle: whether ineliminability in our best explanations entitles us to posit a property, an indispensability principle of broadly Quinean shape. Test that. Is this a substantive dispute, a verbal one about "property" or "explanation," two projects sharing one label, or a disguised normative question about what a theory of truth owes? Ground the answer in what each side is actually committed to, then say what would count as progress — a criterion of substantiality both could accept, a case on which the theories diverge, a change of question — or argue that none is possible and why.
