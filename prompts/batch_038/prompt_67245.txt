I spent thirty-four years running an industrial analytical chemistry lab — mostly GC-MS, a lot of arguing about trace contaminants and detection limits — and I retired two years ago, and since then I've been working my way through philosophy podcasts and library books, badly and out of order. Last week I listened to one of those "philosophy for normal people" shows and the host did an episode on Putnam's Twin Earth. He talks fast, so what's below is partly my transcription and partly my paraphrase of him, but I think it's faithful, and I've kept it as one block:

"Putnam asks you to imagine a planet, Twin Earth, that's a perfect copy of ours — same cities, same history, people who are duplicates of you and me down to the last molecule, with the same memories and the same behaviour. One difference: the stuff in their lakes and their rain isn't H2O, it's a different compound, which he calls XYZ. And here's the trick — nothing in how the two behave could ever tell you which one you've got. Same boiling point, same freezing point, same taste, both put out fires, both drink fine. So for every practical and scientific purpose they're the same substance, and Putnam sets the story in 1750 to make sure nobody on either planet has heard of hydrogen anyway. Which means the entire difference between Earth and Twin Earth is a difference in labels, not in facts. Putnam uses this to argue that meanings aren't in the head: Oscar and Twin Oscar are in identical brain states, yet Oscar's word picks out H2O and Twin Oscar's picks out XYZ, so what you mean depends on stuff outside your skull. From there he draws two conclusions. First, that experts fix the meaning of ordinary words — the chemists decide what water means and the rest of us tag along — so strictly speaking you don't know the meaning of most of your own vocabulary. Second, that water works like an indexical such as 'I' or 'here', which means its meaning shifts from speaker to speaker, which is why you can never be sure you and your neighbour mean the same thing by anything. He makes the same point with his famous example of the man who thinks he has arthritis in his thigh: the doctor tells him that's impossible, and the moral is that the community, not the speaker, sets what even an ordinary word means. Once you grant all this, the idea that meanings are Fregean senses — entities that determine reference — is dead, and it follows that psychology can't be the study of what's inside people, which is a good reason to think the brain sciences will never fully explain thought."

My alarm bells went off at the chemistry. H2O and any XYZ would differ in mass, in spectrum, in reactivity, in a hundred measurable ways, and Putnam was a serious person who knew what a mass spectrometer does. But I suspect I'm reacting to the wrong thing, and that the host has got some of the science backwards while getting the philosophy partly right, or maybe the other way round. I don't know where the line falls.

So could you go through this and tell me what to trust?
