I write novels for a living, but I also run a tiny podcast out of my kitchen — six or seven episodes a year, mostly me talking to academics whose books I've quietly stolen from. Next week's guest is a moral philosopher whose position is that hypocrisy doesn't just make blame clumsy or self-defeating or rude: it takes away your standing to blame at all, so the hypocrite's condemnation isn't badly delivered, it isn't hers to deliver. I've read the book twice and I still can't tell what she thinks the mechanism actually is, which is the one thing I want the hour to pin down.

Can you draft me eight to ten questions, each with a short follow-up attached that's keyed to the answer you'd expect her to actually give? Nothing soft, nothing about how she got into philosophy, and keep each question short enough to say out loud without sounding like I'm reading a seminar paper.

I want some of them built out of my own material, so they're questions nobody's put to her before:

- The mechanism. Her target's conduct really was bad, and what she says about it is true — so what does the blamer's own record do to the blame? Follow up on whatever she says about implicitly granting herself an exemption, using a blamer who grants herself nothing: she just failed, feels awful about it, and has already made restitution in private.
- A character from my manuscript. Dee publicly tears into her contractor for padding invoices; he did it, he deserves it; Dee has also been under-reporting her royalties for six years and no one will ever find out, and Dee thinks she's weak rather than special. Does Dee loose standing? And does it change if she's confessed to her accountant and started paying it back but hasn't said a word to the contractor?
- Scope. Does standing lapse only for the norm you've broken, or does it bleed sideways — a man who lies about money denouncing someone who lies about sex? Make her say how the edges of a norm get drawn, and follow up with whatever she'll use to dodge the arbitrariness.
- If Dee can't say it, her neighbor can, and the neighbor's sentence would be word-for-word identical. Is the defect in the target, the audience, or the speech itself? Follow-up: can the contractor waive it — can he say "I don't care, you're right" and hand standing back?
- Self-blame. Can Dee blame Dee? Does her view end up making self-blame the one kind of blame nobody can be a hypocrite about?
- The psychology. People seem to punish hypocrisy hardest when the hypocrite is visibly signalling virtue, even where the thing they blamed was real. Is that support for her account, or evidence that the folk concept is a mess? Get her to commit to one.
- The political consequence. If standing tracks your own compliance, is basically all public condemnation — politicians, columnists, the internet — standing-less? Ask her to either own that or show where the block is, and follow up on whichever she picks.

Then flag the two you think would be hardest for her to answer, because I want to build the turn of the episode — and, honestly, of a scene I'm stuck on — around one of them.
