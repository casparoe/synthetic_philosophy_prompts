Write the remarks I will give at the last run of my newspaper's printing press: a 1962 rotary web press the crew calls the Duchess, which will print one commemorative edition tomorrow morning and then be dismantled and shipped to a state museum. The room will hold about two hundred people — pressroom men and women, some of whom lost jobs in the consolidations of the 1990s and some of whom are being retrained this spring; a newsroom that will go on working for an owner that is now mostly digital; the executives who decided to end the print edition, one of whom introduces me; and a scattering of local officials who will say something warm about progress.

A friend who teaches the history of technology keeps pressing Langdon Winner's 1980 question — do artifacts have politics? — and I want the speech to answer it honestly rather than dodge it, but from inside a ceremony, with no reference to any author, any book, or any discipline.

Two constraints should carry the argument. The museum's plaque is already cast, and it reads: "The press that printed this city, 1962–2025." I cannot change it, and I must decide in front of the room whether to accept, correct, or quietly re-read the grammar of that sentence. And no sentence may make the machine, the industry, the technology, or the times the subject of a decision: what ended here was ended by people, and yet the speech must not turn into an indictment of the people sitting in the front row.

Six minutes. Give the crew their pride intact, in their own idiom rather than a theorist's, and end on the last run.
