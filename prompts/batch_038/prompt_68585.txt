I am writing this at half past one in the morning after four hours of argument with a friend — a postdoc in neuroethics, and the sharpest person I know on this particular question — which began when we walked out of a reading group on idealized-deliberation accounts of normativity and did not end at the diner where we had intended to end it. Both of us accept the general strategy of indirect normativity: rather than asserting the right answer directly, one specifies a procedure whose outputs are supposed to be the answer. Firth's ideal observer — omniscient with respect to non-moral matters, omnipercipient, disinterested, dispassionate, consistent, and in other respects normal. Brandt's cognitive psychotherapy test, on which a desire is rational only if it survives confrontation with all relevant information and with logical criticism. The deliberators of Rawls's original position; the participants in Habermas's ideal speech situation; the extrapolation proposal familiar from the AI-alignment literature, which aims at what we would want under conditions of greater knowledge, quicker thought, improved character, and closer acquaintance with one another. What we cannot agree on is why these procedures, devised independently by people with quite different theoretical allegiances, all seem to hum in the same key — impartialist, welfare-sensitive, suspicious of cruelty, broadly continuous with the liberal intuitions of the academics who proposed them. My friend says that is a real discovery about the structure of idealization itself: make an agent well-informed, vividly imaginative, impartial, and calm, and a great deal follows from that alone. I say the resemblance is an artifact of shared authorial background, and that each procedure smuggles in the conclusions its inventor would have reached anyway, with the idealizing clauses doing the work of making those conclusions look derived rather than assumed.

So here is the proposition I want a number on. Let P be: the mutual resemblance among the outputs of these procedures is explained primarily by the cultural, temperamental, and first-order normative commitments their authors share, rather than by the idealizing parameters as such — where primarily means that if you could hold the parameters fixed and vary the authors' background commitments, the outputs would move considerably more than if you held the authors' backgrounds fixed and varied the parameters within the range those authors themselves regard as legitimate. Give me your credence in P as a single figure, and then give me the decomposition. I want at least the following terms, each with a number and one line of justification: the probability that the procedures would in fact yield similar outputs if faithfully implemented, since P presupposes the very resemblance it purports to explain; the probability that the idealizing parameters have any content-generating force of their own — that raising the information requirement, or substituting vivid first-personal acquaintance for cool propositional assent, or replacing solitary reflection with dialogue among idealized agents who disagree, moves the outputs in a direction predictable from the description of the parameter alone; the probability that the outputs are determinate enough at the level of concrete cases for a comparison of this sort to be meaningful, as against agreeing only at a level of abstraction so high that nearly any account of morality would agree there; the probability that P's counterfactual is evaluable at all, given that an author's commitments and an author's choice of parameters are not independently variable; and any further terms you think belong. Then show me how you combine them into the headline number, and tell me which term that number is most sensitive to — which one, if you changed your mind about it, would move your figure furthest.

One further thing came up near the end of the argument, which I offer as a diagnostic and which she rejected. I claimed that anyone who writes down such a procedure ought to be able to predict, before being told anything about the inventor's moral views, how the deliverances shift when a parameter is dialed: what happens to an ideal-observer account if you retain the omniscience but strip out the omnipercipience, or retain the impartiality but let the agent deliberate from a state of acute grief or acute hunger rather than from calm. If making that prediction requires knowing what the author already thought about punishment, or redistribution, or animals, then the parameters are not generating anything. Tell me whether you regard that test as fair, and fold your verdict into the second term above. For calibration: I sit at roughly 0.75 on P, she sits at roughly 0.2, and neither of us budged an inch. Beyond your own number, I want you to locate the disagreement — identify the term or terms on which the two of us are most likely diverging, and say what a neutral third party should ask each of us in order to establish that. Please do not send me to the literature; I have read the obvious things, and what I want from you is reasoning, not references.
