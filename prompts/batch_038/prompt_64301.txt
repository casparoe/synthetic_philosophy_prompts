I am putting together a problem set for a formal epistemology seminar, and in trying to press the logical omniscience objection harder than it is usually pressed I seem to have overshot: what I wrote down does not show that possible-worlds semantics misdescribes some believers, it shows that there are no believers at all. My roommate, who works on epistemic logic for multi-agent systems, says nobody in his field loses sleep over this, and my seminar leader's only comment was that since the conclusion is false I must have made a mistake somewhere — which is not a diagnosis. Each step looked like something I would defend in any context, so I cannot find the exit.

Here is the argument as I stated it.

(1) On a possible-worlds account, an agent's doxastic state is a set of worlds, namely those the agent has not ruled out, and the agent believes a proposition just in case it is true at every world in that set.

(2) Propositions are individuated by the worlds at which they are true, so if p and q hold at exactly the same worlds, believing p and believing q are not two accomplishments but one: a single relation to a single item.

(3) A tautology holds at every world, so any agent whose doxastic state is nonempty believes every tautology, and all tautologies are the same proposition.

(4) For any p you believe and any tautology t, the conjunction of p with t holds at exactly the worlds p does; so believing p commits you to that conjunction, then to the conjunction of p with t and a further tautology, and so on without end.

(5) Belief is a state the agent has to be in rather than a bookkeeping fact about a model: to believe something is to have some grip on it, and grip comes one item at a time, since taking up an item costs the agent something.

(6) No agent takes up infinitely many items, and in particular no agent takes up the endless string of conjunctions generated in (4).

(7) Therefore no agent believes anything, and by the same route no agent knows anything.

(8) So the account in (1) is not a harmless idealization of belief, in the way a frictionless plane is an idealization of a surface; it empties the notion it was introduced to explain.

I want to keep (1) and (2), because they are what make the framework usable, and I also want to keep (5), because if believing places no demand whatever on the believer the word has stopped doing any work. I would rather not be told that belief is a relation to a sentence, since that strikes me as a surrender rather than a reply.

Tell me which step fails and how; explain why it looked unobjectionable when I wrote it down; and price the three most promising ways out — impossible worlds, awareness or two-component accounts, and fragmented or subject-matter-sensitive belief states — noting in whichever item covers your favorite whether (8) survives that move. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: the first two on the failing step and on its appearance of innocence, the remaining three on the alternatives.
