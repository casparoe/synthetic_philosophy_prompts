A rationalist blog post I read while procrastinating sums up Jeffrey conditionalization like this:

"Ordinary Bayesian updating assumes your evidence comes with certainty attached: you learn E, and every credence shifts to whatever you previously gave that claim conditional on E. But observation is rarely certain — you might be unsure whether the bird in the hedge is a goldfinch or a canary. Jeffrey's rule covers that case: you assign new probabilities to the observation claims themselves (say 70/30), then conditionalize on each of them separately and average the results using those new numbers as weights. So it isn't a rival to conditionalization at all, it's just conditionalization performed several times at once, and no Bayesian principle is given up. The only real catch is that it can't handle evidence that introduces a possibility you had no slot for in your algebra, and that the order of two Jeffrey updates can matter, which basically shows the weights are arbitrary."

I was inclined to buy this, mostly bc the formula really does look like a weighted sum of ordinary conditionalizations. Can you walk it sentence by sentence and tell me what a Bayesian epistemologist would accept, what they'd wince at, and what's flat wrong? Specifically: is there a hidden assumption doing the real work that the post never states, and when does it break? Does the "nothing is given up" line survive? And I have a hazy memory of a uniqueness result to the effect that if you demand certain conditional probabilities stay put, there's only one coherent way to update — if that's genuine, how does it cut against the arbitrariness charge in the last sentence?
