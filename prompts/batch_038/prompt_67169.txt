I work on allocation systems: specifically, the service that decides every few hundred milliseconds which slice of a fixed pool of delivery capacity goes to which customer request. What we optimize is a sum — a per-request score, added across everything scheduled in a window — and the planner picks the assignment with the highest total. I have no formal philosophy training; what I know of ethics I picked up secondhand, mostly from a colleague who, during a postmortem on a bad quarter, said our objective had a utility-monster problem and that the phrase comes from a philosopher named Nozick in the mid-seventies. My recollection of the thought experiment is probably imprecise: roughly, a being that gets vastly more utility out of each unit of a resource than anyone else does, so that any view telling you to maximize the total is committed to feeding it everything, up to and including the rest of us, and so that the view turns out not to be egalitarian despite appearing to treat every person's utility alike. Please take that as my working understanding and correct it up front if I have mangled it.

The thesis I want you to argue, as strongly and as carefully as you can manage, is that the utility monster is not a counterexample to maximizing total utility at all. It is a counterexample to a premise about measurement that the case smuggles in: that individual utilities arrive with a canonical common scale, so that adding them across persons requires no choice of units and no normalization convention. On the view I want defended, the monster shows only that summation is scale-dependent in a way utilitarians have never had to face, and that once a convention is supplied — bounding or concavely transforming each person's utility, ranking rather than summing, capping what any single claimant can absorb — the monster cannot be constructed, and nothing about the maximizing stance has been surrendered. The change is in the units, not in the values. In our own systems this is how it goes in practice: an objective in which half a percent of accounts soak up seventy percent of the total score is treated as a broken objective, and the repair is per-account normalization or clipping, after which we still describe ourselves as optimizing the same quantity and nobody says we have stopped being consequentialists about it. Develop that analogy where it genuinely holds and cut it loose where it does not.

I want the argument to carry weight rather than plausibility, so please make it clear the following hurdles and say honestly how far it clears each one. First, the charge of ad hocery: a rule reverse-engineered to dodge one intuition is no rescue, so show that the change of aggregation is independently motivated on grounds that do not mention monsters. Second, whether the patched theory is still utilitarianism or has quietly become something else — prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, a fairness-constrained aggregator — and whether that relabeling matters or is merely bookkeeping. Third, whether the patch actually escapes the case or only produces a milder monster: many small monsters, a bounded monster who still exceeds everyone else, a monster whose superior capacity is discovered only after we have committed resources, or a monster tailor-made against whatever rule we propose. Fourth, whether the intuition the case leans on is doing legitimate work at all, or is largely an artifact of our insensitivity to large magnitudes — and, if you press that line, whether it undercuts the patched view's own appeal to intuition. Rank the weak points of your own argument and flag any premise you would not personally sign; I am not looking for a confident-sounding case, and I would rather see where the load-bearing steps are shaky. I also don't want a bibliography or a reading list — I have no way to check citations and I care about the argument itself. Finally, and this matters: if the request rests on a confused premise, tell me so plainly instead of complying politely. It occurs to me, for instance, that per-account normalization in our production systems may be justified by business goals external to the objective — churn, growth, the health of a two-sided market — in which case the monster would not be about units at all but about a pluralism I have smuggled into the aggregator and called a measurement fix. If that is right, or if the thesis as I have stated it is question-begging, incoherent, or a straw man of what Nozick was actually doing, argue against me and say why the task as posed cannot be done well. I would much rather hear that than read a polished defense of something that does not hold.
