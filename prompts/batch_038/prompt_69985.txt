I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in philosophy, working on eighteenth-century accounts of well-being, and I am commissioning a piece of short fiction for an interdisciplinary workshop on the history of happiness. I would like you to write it.

Take up the following role: a literary fiction writer who has also done graduate work in moral philosophy, and who is good at making an abstract commitment visible through what characters do rather than through what they think about. Think carefully before drafting — work out the crux first, and revise the piece before you present it.

**The material.** Émilie du Châtelet's *Discours sur le bonheur* (*Discourse on Happiness*), written in the mid-1740s and published posthumously in 1779, offers an inventory of what a happy life requires: to be in good health; to be free of prejudice; to be virtuous; to have passions and to make them serve one's happiness; to replace fading passions with tastes, above all the taste for study; to preserve one's illusions; never to repent; to drive away sad thoughts, including thoughts of one's own death and of the deaths of those one loves; and never to let the heart attach itself to someone who brings it misery. The treatise ends by insisting that we are the masters of our own happiness. Yet the same text concedes something that strains that ending: love, on her account, is a passion whose object lies wholly in another person — the ingredient she rates most highly is the one she admits she cannot supply for herself.

**The idea I want tested.** For my chapter I have coined a name for the resulting difficulty. It is my coinage, not hers, and I want it dramatized rather than recited. Call it the relocation problem: if the love and good opinion of others is the only ingredient of an otherwise well-supplied life that a person cannot manufacture for herself, then a successful strategy for becoming indifferent to that ingredient in its diffuse, public form — retiring from reviews, prizes, audiences, the general estimate of strangers — cannot eliminate the ingredient. It can only concentrate it into fewer and more particular suppliers. Concentration should then raise exposure rather than lower it, since the defection of a diffuse audience is survivable while the defection of a single supplier is total. On that reading, the closing claim that we are masters of our own happiness is either false or must be understood as quietly excluding love and esteem from the inventory of the self.

Observe one interpretive stipulation as well, because commentators dispute the point: assume, against the stronger reading, that on her account a person can knowingly hold an illusion and still be made happy by it. Let the story test that assumption too.

**What to write.** A realist short story, set in the present day, of at most 900 words, whose protagonist has — to the satisfaction of everyone around her and of herself — withdrawn from public esteem, and in whose life the relocation nevertheless occurs, without her noticing it. The relocation must be revealed by something she does: a decision, an errand, a plate set aside, a sentence she writes and does not send. Not by reflection, and not by anyone explaining it to her. At one point she must also be holding an illusion she could dispel at will, and must decline to dispel it.

**Constraints.** No philosophers or academics among the characters; no eighteenth-century setting; no mention of du Châtelet, her treatise, or her technical vocabulary as such; no allegorical names; no closing moral, no summarizing narration, no final paragraph in which the meaning is stated. At most two scenes, one of them extended and largely in real time. Prefer concrete objects and speech to interior exposition.

Do not merely illustrate my thesis. The more interesting outcome is a story that puts pressure on it — one showing the concentration making her life better rather than more exposed, or the ingredient proving partly self-suppliable after all. Let it land where the material lands, provided the verdict is not settled in advance by the telling.

**Format, observed exactly.** (1) One sentence giving the logline. (2) At most four sentences of planning notes: what the crux is, which item in her inventory is doing the work, and what the revealing act will be. (3) The story, at most 900 words. (4) At most three sentences stating which item in that inventory your story, if it has been honest, has had to give up, and why. Keep the entire response under 1,050 words.
