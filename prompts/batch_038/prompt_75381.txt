Consider the career of Thomas Robert Malthus's *Essay on the Principle of Population* between 1798 and the present, and analyze it as a test case for a question I have been unable to resolve: whether a theory can be defended as "right but early," and what that defense is actually worth.

The facts I want you to work from, since you cannot look them up and I would rather you flag uncertainty than invent specifics: the anonymous first edition of 1798 argued that population, if unchecked, grows geometrically while the means of subsistence grow only arithmetically, so that any improvement in the condition of the labouring classes is competed away by the additional mouths it summons into being, and that population is held to its means of subsistence by what Malthus grouped as preventive checks (delayed marriage, celibacy) and positive checks (famine, disease, war). In the heavily expanded second edition of 1803 and the editions that followed to 1826, he added a great deal of historical and comparative material and gave a more prominent place to a third restraint — prudential delay of marriage until one could support a family, undertaken without the vices he associated with celibacy and contraception. Meanwhile the prediction failed: real wages and life expectancy in Britain rose for a century and a half after 1803, agricultural output grew far faster than arithmetic, and in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries the societies that got richest saw their fertility fall rather than rise.

I want the analysis to run in both directions, using the following apparatus, which you should treat as a proposal to be stress-tested rather than as settled vocabulary. A theory earns the description "right but early" only if all three of these hold: (i) it identified a real mechanism rather than a spurious regularity; (ii) the mechanism was misjudged in magnitude, not in kind, because some countervailing process was invisible or unquantifiable to its author; and (iii) the theory can absorb that countervailing process by a change of parameters or of scope while leaving the mechanism itself intact. I also want you to keep separate two things the phrase tends to fuse — temporal lateness, where the theory becomes true later, and domain restriction, where the theory was true all along but over a narrower range than its author claimed. Call the second "right but bounded" and tell me whether the difference matters.

On that basis, address the following, in order and with the mapping made explicit at each step:

1. Whether the 1803 revision is best described as an ad hoc rescue, as a progressive problemshift, or as neither — and whether your answer changes depending on whether you assess it by what it added to the theory's content or by what happened to it over the following two centuries. Confront the standing objection that admitting prudential restraint as a check makes the theory compatible with any outcome whatsoever, since population is then said to be limited by misery, by vice, or by restraint, whichever turns up. Say whether that objection lands, given that Malthus also committed himself to a specific dynamic about what happens to wages when subsistence improves.

2. Whether the demographic transition satisfies condition (iii). Fertility fell as incomes rose, which appears to be the reverse of the sign Malthus's preventive check predicts, not merely a smaller effect of the same sign. If the sign is wrong, is there any version of condition (iii) that survives?

3. Whether modern accounts of long-run growth, which treat most of recorded history as a regime in which technological gains were converted into more people rather than richer ones, constitute a vindication under the "bounded" reading — and what we should say about a theory that holds for thousands of years and fails for the two centuries that follow. Does the failure of a theory precisely when the world stopped obeying it make the theory right, wrong, or merely dated?

4. Whether anything available in 1803 could have discriminated "early" from "wrong." If nothing could, then say plainly whether the defense is an epistemic virtue or a refusal to update, and whether it suffers from an availability asymmetry — that it can only ever be mounted after the outcome is known, and hence by people who no longer need it.

You may briefly test the apparatus on one later forecast of the same family, such as the resource-exhaustion predictions of the 1970s, but only to the extent that it sharpens the verdict; do not let it become a survey. I am not asking for sources, further reading, or page references, and you should not supply any. Where a fact I have given you is contestable or where your account depends on one you are unsure of, mark it as such in the text. Close with an explicit verdict on whether Malthus's theory is right but early, right but bounded, rescued into emptiness, or simply wrong, and with a short statement of what this episode shows about the general intelligibility of the "right but early" defense — including any place where the framework runs out and you have to admit the question is being settled by stipulation rather than argument.
