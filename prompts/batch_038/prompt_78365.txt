I am the Recording Secretary of the Ashford Lyceum, a student-run legislative society at my high school of about forty members. The Society began three years ago as an after-school game in which the rules themselves were the plaything; we kept the game and lost the rest. We are now governed by a one-page charter whose Article VII originally permitted amendment by two-thirds of the members present, one-third of the membership constituting a quorum. Admissibility questions are decided by a five-member Charter Committee, which publishes a short written ruling in each case; I sit on it, and the full membership can overturn a ruling only by three-fourths.

Our published rulings to date, in order:

R-1. An amendment abolishing the single Chair and vesting its duties in a three-member panel, carried by two-thirds of those present with quorum satisfied. Held admissible. We treated this as unremarkable.

R-2. An amendment repealing Article VII and substituting "this charter may be amended by a simple majority of the members present," carried by the two-thirds then required. Held admissible. We wrote that the amendment power includes power over the manner of its own exercise.

R-3. An amendment inserting "no amendment shall lower the threshold for amending this charter below three-fourths of the full membership," carried by three-fourths of the full membership. Held admissible, with an express further holding that the floor could itself be repealed by any later vote meeting the floor it sets. We have never resolved whether R-3 also silently restored a three-fourths requirement, or merely forbade future reductions below one; two members read it differently. The point is moot for the case below.

R-4. An amendment providing that a sanction adopted against a named member would apply to conduct occurring before adoption, carried correctly. Held inadmissible in that respect. We held that the amendment power is prospective: it changes what the rules will be, not what they were.

R-5. A challenge to an amendment adopted at a meeting held after the quorum rule had been changed by an earlier amendment, the challenger arguing the meeting should be judged by the old quorum rule. Challenge rejected. We held that the rules in force at the moment of the vote govern that vote, and that a later amendment cannot cure a vote that was defective when taken.

R-6. An amendment providing that the charter may not be amended and that no future amendment may repeal or amend that article, carried by three-fourths. Held inadmissible. We held that the amendment power cannot be used to extinguish itself, distinguishing R-3 on the ground that a threshold floor leaves the power alive.

The case before us is Amendment G, noticed last week, with the vote scheduled for next Thursday. It was carried on its reading by four-fifths of the full membership at a meeting at which the entire Society was present, so its adoption is not in doubt under any reading of the threshold. Its text:

(i) Article VII is amended to require, for any amendment, four-fifths of the full membership.
(ii) Clause (i) may be repealed or amended only by four-fifths of the full membership together with the written consent of the Society's Founder.
(iii) The Founder is the student who convened the first meeting. He graduated three years ago, holds no office, is not a member, and has not attended a meeting or answered correspondence in over a year. The consent requirement is not waived if he cannot be located.
(iv) Clauses (i) through (iii) apply to every amendment pending on the date of adoption, including the amendment noticed last month that would create a paid faculty adviser position.

The Committee is divided two to two and I hold the tie. Two members would strike the amendment in its entirety; two would admit it in full. I have a hunch about which way I lean, but I would rather you not have it, so I have left it out. Please work from our own record rather than from outside authority.

What I need: identify the candidate general rules that are jointly consistent with R-1 through R-6, showing that more than one fits; state what each yields for each clause of G; recommend the ruling that best coheres with our own precedents, including how we should treat severability; name the closest analog among the six rulings and where the analogy breaks; state candidly what principle we would be adopting by admitting and by striking; tell us whether our precedents are in fact jointly consistent or whether one of them must be overruled, and which would be cheapest to overrule; and name the further case that would most sharply discriminate among the candidate rules.
