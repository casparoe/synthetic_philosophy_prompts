I've spent more years than I care to admit reading rationalist blogs and forums, and one analogy keeps doing heavy lifting in the recurring fight over expected-value fanaticism — roughly, the claim that a rational agent must accept arbitrarily large certain costs for a sufficiently tiny probability of a sufficiently enormous payoff. Whenever someone objects that this makes a mockery of ordinary moral and prudential reasoning, a defender will say that the fanatic is simply an actuary: an insurer charges a certain, modest premium to guard against a rare, catastrophic loss, and nobody calls the insurance industry fanatical. On that telling, the objection to fanaticism is really an objection to expected-value reasoning as such, and the burden shifts onto the critic, who now owes us an account of why the same reasoning is prudent at small stakes and absurd at large ones. A variant I see in the same threads makes the point from the casino side instead: the house wins because it plays the average rather than any one hand, so a rational agent should do likewise.

I want an audit of the analogy, not a general verdict on fanaticism. Start by laying out the correspondence explicitly — what plays the role of the insurer, the policyholder, the premium, the pooled risk, the actuarial table, the regulator, and the background fact that the house gets to keep playing tomorrow — and then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the intended use from the ones that are merely colorful. I'm particularly interested in the following, though you should follow the argument wherever it leads rather than treating this as a checklist:

- Whether the analogy survives the gap between a repeatable game run across many parallel trials and a one-shot wager on a single trajectory, and what follows if the agent cannot actually realize the average it is maximizing.
- Whether it survives the difference between a premium priced by a market with continuous feedback and a probability assigned from the armchair to events that have no base rates at all.
- Whether insurance is even about maximizing expected monetary value, or about something else — smoothing consumption, limited liability, avoiding an absorbing state from which there is no recovery — and, if the latter, whether the defender has borrowed the credibility of a practice whose actual logic is hostile to their conclusion.
- What the analogy quietly imports about the identity of the party who pays the premium and the party who collects, given that in the fanatical cases these are different people, living at different times, with no contract between them.

Then tell me what the analogy can carry. Does it genuinely shift the burden onto the critic of fanaticism, or does it establish only that some large-stakes, small-probability reasoning is respectable, leaving the interesting cases untouched? Is the casino version stronger or weaker than the actuarial one, and does one of them fail for reasons that don't touch the other? Finally, if neither supports the intended conclusion, is there a better source domain — one that preserves whatever is genuinely right about expected-value reasoning at the extremes while making the relevant structural features visible instead of hiding them? Say what that analogy would be and what it would license, or say why no analogy in this neighborhood can do the work and the argument has to be made directly.
