A quantitative geneticist (Party A) and a philosopher of science who works on social-scientific methodology (Party B) have spent a year trading replies in a methods journal about whether heritability estimates should be reported outside technical contexts. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration. Draft the joint statement they produce.

Party A holds that narrow-sense heritability is a well-defined quantity with a genuine causal role — it is the parameter that predicts response to selection in the breeder's equation — and that its limitations are well understood by specialists and routinely overstated by critics who blur broad-sense and narrow-sense estimates. Party B holds that the statistic is indexed to one population in one range of environments, that its name invites inferences it cannot support, and that publishing it in non-technical settings predictably generates those inferences no matter what caveats are attached.

Treat the following as stipulated facts about a fictional trait T, operationalized as years of completed schooling, measured in a single population. They are supplied for the exercise and are not claims about the actual literature:

(i) A twin design yields a heritability estimate for T of 0.75; an adoption design applied to the same population in the same period yields 0.48. Both figures circulate in that literature simply as the heritability of T.
(ii) A proposed policy would, if implemented, cut the environmental variance component of T by 80 percent from its current value, leave the additive genetic variance unchanged, and raise the population mean of T by half of its current standard deviation. For the arithmetic, assume an additive variance decomposition with no genotype-environment correlation or interaction.
(iii) After the policy, two subpopulations P and Q each show a within-group heritability for T of roughly 0.8, and their means differ by 0.6 standard deviations.

The joint statement must contain, in this order:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. It must include: the recomputed post-policy heritability of T under each of the two estimates in (i), with the resulting range stated numerically; an explicit statement of what happens to the population mean and to the heritability at the same time under (ii); and a statement of what (iii) does and does not license about the cause of the P–Q difference.

2. A disagreements table with four columns: the dispute in neutral terms; Party A's formulation of their own position, in wording A would accept; Party B's formulation of their own position, in wording B would accept; and whether the row is substantive or verbal. The twin-versus-adoption discrepancy in (i) must appear as one row.

3. The cruxes: three to five sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one party. For each, name the argument, dataset, or case that both parties already accept as bearing on it, and record each party's prediction in advance, phrased so that it could later be checked rather than reinterpreted.

4. A closing annex of no more than 100 words that (a) identifies which of the year's exchanges were merely terminological and says which term was doing the damage, and (b) offers one public-facing sentence of at most 25 words that both parties would sign about what the post-policy figures in (ii) show and fail to show.

Write in American English. The entire document must not exceed 1,000 words. Do not include a bibliography and do not cite real studies, authors, or estimates; work only from the stipulations above and from general knowledge of what heritability is and is not.
