My sister and I had to decide whether to move our father into a care home, and I ended up deferring to her: she has thought about these things far more than I have, and I did not want to reason my way into a comfortable answer. Months later, though, I cannot shake the feeling that this was somehow a cop-out, that a decent person is supposed to have worked out for themselves why the thing they did was right. I have read that philosophers find this feeling puzzling, and that they give two rather different explanations of it: one says that deferring leaves you without understanding of the moral reasons behind your act, even when you act rightly; the other says that the trouble is not in your head but in your heart, that a person who defers has failed to care about the right things, whatever they believe. Please lay out the most important implications of each of these two explanations for my case. If the worry is about understanding, does deferring still leave me with something second-best that I should try to repair, and can I repair it after the fact? If the worry is about caring, is my defence that my sister had thought about it harder exactly the wrong kind of defence? And tell me which explanation you think commits a person like me to the more demanding picture of ordinary decency, and whether either one leaves room for the thought that trusting another person's judgment might sometimes be a virtue rather than a failure.
