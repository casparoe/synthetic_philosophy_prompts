I am assembling a collection in which every story is built around one piece of epistemology, and I have come to the one I cannot crack. Write me, in British English, a story of roughly 1,500 words about a woman who cannot keep a belief she has voiced: the instant she commits a conviction to speech, the conviction drains out of her and only the sentence is left standing, true but unsupported. Her every sincere utterance therefore takes the shape of a plain report of how things are, followed by a disclaimer about the state of her own mind — and I want the story to press on a distinction that fiction almost never makes, between that hollow form, in which she says a thing is so and simply has no view about it, and the far more troubling form, in which she says a thing is so while somewhere in her believing the opposite.

Construct that second kind out of a split I can dramatise rather than describe: a shallow register governing what her mouth will endorse, and a deep one governing what her hands do, so that a promise made aloud in perfect sincerity is already false in the way she behaves afterwards. Give me at least one scene of each kind, and let the second cost her something the first never could.

Do not name the paradox, do not let any character hold forth about assertion or belief, and do not dissolve the difficulty: close with her obliged to tell someone a true and consequential thing, to a listener who will act on it only if she appears to stand behind it.
