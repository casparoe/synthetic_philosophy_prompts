Our town voted down a $2.3 million performing arts center back in March — failed by 91 votes — and as the compromise the selectboard redirected a chunk of the money into a free summer series in the park. I'm on the parks and rec commission (I actually run the library's summer reading program, the commission seat is sort of a punishment) and they've asked me to give the opening remarks at the first one, second Saturday in June. Here's the awkward part: a big slice of the no-vote came out of a book group that reads through the town library, and last fall they did Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert about the Geneva theater, and whether or not anybody admits it, that book is a real part of why the arts center died. I was in the group. I want the speech to actually make that case out loud, in plain words, because I think it's a better case than the one that got printed in the local paper, which was entirely about mill rates. So the version I'd want to make is roughly this: a theater's whole business is to please, so it takes an audience exactly as it already is and flatters it, which is why it can't reform anybody, no matter how noble the play; a comedy has to make the decent, blunt, honest person a little ridiculous or nobody laughs, and the charming worldly one who knows how to handle people ends up being the hero the audience roots for; tragedy's pity costs you a ticket and a handkerchief and then you go home and do nothing about anything; and the actors' trade itself — being somebody else for a living, in exchange for applause from strangers — is not what Rousseau thinks you want citizens doing. Then the positive half, which is the part I actually care about: his replacement for the theater is basically a post stuck in the middle of the square with flowers on it, everybody gathered around it, no stage, no tickets, no one up there and no one down there, people getting the show by looking at each other instead of at a representation. That's the festival we're having. Ground-level ring of hay bales, a potluck, a fiddler who plays at the VFW, a nineteen-year-old square dance caller who learned on YouTube, and me on a flatbed trailer borrowed from the hardware store with a PA system thats basically a megaphone.

Now the bind. Half the crowd will be the yes-vote people who lost: the community theater company, the high school drama teacher, a woman who has run a regional Shakespeare-in-the-park thing for fourteen years, plus the Grange hall regulars whose stage has a leaky roof. A couple of them are coming to be polite, a couple to sulk, and at least one to make sure we dont get away with anything. The other half are the no-vote people, most of whom never read the letter and just wanted to say no to a tax. I need the speech to be genuinely for the participatory thing — not watered down into "there's a little something here for everybody" — without turning into a victory lap, without telling people who have given their lives to putting on plays that what they do is corrupting or worthless, and without letting the tax hawks hear their own argument back. And there's the obvious problem I can't pretend around: I'll be standing on a raised platform, in front, delivering lines I wrote in advance, to a seated audience, for about eight minutes, which is theater by any honest definition. I want the speech to own that instead of dodging it, but not to turn it into a cute in-joke that only the book group catches. Also, the compromise I already agreed to and now have to sell: the drama teacher's students are doing a five-minute scene in the middle of it, after which everybody's supposed to get up and join the dance. Rousseau would say I've let the spectators back in through the side door, and she thinks her kids got demoted to an icebreaker. I want the speech to make that odd little arrangement feel like the actual point rather than a fudge.

So: write the remarks, first person, around 700 to 900 words, in language that survives being read out loud to people sitting on blankets with a lawn mower going somewhere in the distance. Name Rousseau at most once. Warmth for the theater people has to be real and not condescending, and the ending should get people off the hay bales. Don't put words in Rousseau's mouth — I don't want fake quotes — and leave out the stretch of the letter about women and modesty, which I am not repeating in a park in front of my neighbors. Then, after the speech, give me two or three sentences on what you think it costs me to take this line: where the Rousseau argument is just wrong about the people who'll be standing at the back.
