The passage I want to work on introduces Ellsberg's 1961 result. I have typed it out in full:

> Long before behavioral economics became fashionable, Daniel Ellsberg published a short paper that put the standard theory of rational choice in genuine difficulty. The theory, in the form given to it by Leonard Savage, says that a person choosing under uncertainty can always be described as though he held definite personal probabilities for the events that matter to him and took the option with the greatest expected value, provided only that his choices satisfy a modest consistency condition usually known as the sure-thing principle. Ellsberg's counterexample requires nothing more than two urns and a hundred balls apiece. In the first urn exactly fifty balls are red and fifty are black. In the second, the hundred balls are red or black in proportions nobody has been told: that urn may be all red, all black, or anything whatever in between. Offered a prize if a red ball is drawn, most people choose to draw from the first urn, where the odds are at least known. Offered the same prize if a black ball is drawn, most people choose the first urn again. As Ellsberg observed, those two preferences cannot both be reproduced by any single assignment of personal probabilities, for to take the known urn on red is to behave as though the second urn held fewer than fifty red balls, while to take it on black is to behave as though it held more than fifty. What the subjects are doing, in other words, is not assigning the second urn a probability at all; there is no number they could honestly write down. Nor is this a piece of carelessness that better schooling would remove, because there is a fact of the matter about that second urn — it contains some particular number of red balls whether or not anyone has been told what it is — and the person who declines to draw from it is simply admitting that he is in no position to say what that number is. A theory that insists he act as though he knew it is demanding something impossible, and that is why the result is properly described as a refutation of the theory rather than as a curiosity about it. Ambiguity aversion, as the tendency came to be called, is only risk aversion carried to its limit: the same dislike of losing that leads people to insure their houses leads them to decline bets whose odds cannot be calculated. Once economists understood this they largely abandoned expected utility theory, which is why textbooks today explain decisions under uncertainty by reference to how confident or how nervous a person feels rather than by reference to probabilities.
