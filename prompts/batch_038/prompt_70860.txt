I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science; my dissertation concerns the justification of error rates in institutional decision-making, and I have been reading the replication-crisis literature alongside the legal architecture of drug approval. I want to test a hypothesis about the latter, and I would like a careful reconstruction rather than a survey. I am not asking for references or a bibliography — I want you to reason from the practice as I describe it below, and to flag any part of my description that strikes you as legally or historically off.

The practice is the evidentiary standard for marketing approval of a new drug in the United States. The governing statute defines the standard of proof for effectiveness as "adequate and well-controlled investigations, including clinical investigations," plural, conducted by qualified experts, "on the basis of which it could fairly and responsibly be concluded by such experts that the drug will have the effect it purports or is represented to have." The agency's settled reading of that plural is two independent studies. The implementing regulation then specifies what makes a single study adequate: it must use "a design that permits a valid comparison with a control to provide a quantitative assessment of drug effect," with assignment to groups ordinarily by randomization, blinding, and a protocol stating the objectives and the methods of analysis in advance.

Four features of this regime puzzle me, and they seem to pull in different directions.

First, the number. Nothing in an error-rate argument appears to yield two rather than one or three; the numeral looks borrowed from engineering redundancy or from a legal burden of proof, not derived from the long-run frequency properties that are supposed to be the point of the design requirements. And the two studies need not agree in population, dose, comparator, or even in the precise measure of the endpoint, so what is being replicated is not obviously the same hypothesis in the sense that the replication literature means.

Second, the statute itself was amended to permit the Secretary to treat data from "one adequate and well-controlled clinical investigation and confirmatory evidence (obtained prior to or after such investigation)" as sufficient. The parenthetical is what interests me: confirmatory evidence obtained *after* the investigation counts. So a promise to replicate can apparently stand in for having replicated — which is how the surrogate-endpoint pathway works in practice, with the confirmatory trial deferred to post-approval and sometimes delayed for years.

Third, the regulation licenses weaker designs precisely when the effect is large. Historical controls are "usually reserved for special circumstances," and the named examples include diseases with high and predictable mortality and studies "in which the effect of the drug is self-evident (general anesthetics, drug metabolism)." Design rigor is thus a decreasing function of effect magnitude. But randomization and concurrent control are justified in the regulation's own preamble as the way to distinguish drug effect from spontaneous change, placebo response, and biased observation — a rationale about error sources, not about how big the true effect is.

Fourth, the asymmetry between benefit and harm. To establish effectiveness the sponsor must clear the two-investigation structure; to withdraw an approved drug on safety grounds, the same section requires only that "clinical or other experience, tests, or other scientific data show that such drug is unsafe." One signal suffices in one direction and two structured studies are required in the other.

So: what theory of evidence does this practice presuppose? In particular — (1) is the two-study default an error-control device, a redundancy device, a burden-of-proof device calibrated to the gravity of a wrong approval, or an anti-gaming device aimed at sponsor bias, and how would you tell these apart from the practice itself? (2) Does the self-evident-effect license commit the regime to a Bayesian epistemology — large likelihood ratios overwhelming weak designs or skeptical priors — while the rest of the machinery is written in a frequency idiom, or can it be given a purely frequentist reading? (3) What view of evidence makes post hoc confirmatory commitments equivalent to completed replications: is evidence here a property of the data, or of an ongoing institutional process? (4) Is the benefit/harm asymmetry a single coherent stance about where the default sits and who bears the risk, or two incompatible epistemologies sharing a statute? (5) The stated standard — what experts "could fairly and responsibly conclude" — is a standard about the character of judgment, not about probabilities, yet the operational content is thresholds, power, and prespecified analyses. Which of the two is doing the work? Please distinguish throughout what the practice *strictly* presupposes from what it merely sits well with, since I suspect a Bayesian, a Neyman-Pearson decision theorist, and a social epistemologist could each claim much of it; say explicitly where the practice is underdetermined. Finally, tell me what would have to change in the regime if the standard were replaced by an explicitly Bayesian one (a posterior probability of benefit above a threshold, with utilities for harm) and what would have to change if it were replaced by a consistent Neyman-Pearson procedure applied at the level of the whole literature rather than the individual study.
