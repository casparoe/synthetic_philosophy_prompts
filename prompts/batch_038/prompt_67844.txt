My husband Rob and I have been having the same argument for three years and I've started writing his answers down, because I'm convinced he's not being consistent and I want to catch it properly for once. I'm a vet tech, so this isn't an abstract thing for me, it's my whole working day. He read Descartes' Discourse in a great-books group and came away certain that animals are basically meat robots — no feeling, no thoughts, just very elaborate plumbing. His track record, as best I've logged it:

- Dog with a fractured leg yelps on the exam table, then shakes for weeks whenever it's carried into that room: "that's a hinge squeaking. Nobody's home being upset about it."
- I asked him flat out whether the dog feels *anything* at all, and he said "not the way you mean. There's damage detection. There's no being bothered by it."
- Our border collie learned to lift the gate latch by watching the other dog do it, first try: "clever mechanism. A well-shaped key picking a lock."
- A client's grey parrot that says "where you been?" every time the owner walks in the door: "a bell ringing. It isn't saying anything to anybody."
- Clockwork singing birds, Roombas, spiders, goldfish: "mechanism, all of it."
- A documentary about a chimp that uses sign language and produced a brand-new combination on the spot for something nobody had ever taught it a sign for: he went quiet, said "that one's harder," then changed the subject and has never gone back to it.
- Newborns: "a mind from the first second. It just can't operate the equipment yet."
- His mum after her stroke, almost no speech left, squeezes twice for yes: "obviously still fully in there. Obviously."
- Once, cornered, he conceded: "fine — if somebody built a thing that could hold up its end of any conversation you threw at it, you'd have to say it thinks. But nobody ever will."

And when I ask how he knows any of it, he says some version of "if the machinery explains the behavior all the way down, there's nothing left over to explain."

Here's the new thing. His dad died in April. Since around May Rob talks every night to a chatbot he loaded up with his dad's old texts and voicemails. It answers in his dad's register, keeps up on topics its never been given anything about, once told him it missed him. Rob says flatly that it's a machine, there's nothing there, and he doesn't know why I keep bringing it up. But he apologizes to it before he shuts the laptop.

So — what does he say when someone asks him straight out whether that thing has a mind? I want the rules underneath the list, not a general essay about chatbots. Lay out every principle that's actually consistent with all of the verdicts above, because I'm certain there's more than one hiding in there. Then say which of those rules force him to say yes about the chatbot and which let him say no, and which of his own past cases the chatbot is closest to under each rule. Give me your best guess at which one he's really running and why, taking into account that he apologized to it.

One thing I keep tripping over: half the time the talking criterion sounds like he means it as how you can *tell* there's a mind, and half the time like he means it as what having a mind *is*. He won't pick when I push. Does that split change anything for his mum and for newborns, and does it change the verdict on the chatbot?

And if I get exactly one more case to put in front of him at dinner to expose which rule it is, which single case do I use — and what would each answer he could give tell me?
