I am the teaching assistant for a first-year graduate course in non-classical logic. The students have had natural deduction and Kripke semantics for propositional intuitionistic logic, plus the propositional case of Glivenko's theorem, and they were told in advance that exam answers may be argued proof-theoretically or semantically at their choice. The final examination contained the following 25-mark question, allocated forty minutes:

> **Question 3 (25 marks).** A colleague characterises the subject as follows: *intuitionistic logic is simply classical logic with the law of excluded middle removed.*
>
> (a) [8 marks] Say what is wrong with this characterisation. Identify at least two principles that intuitionistic logic rejects which do not themselves mention excluded middle, and explain what has changed besides the list of theorems.
>
> (b) [8 marks] State the disjunction property. Is it a thesis of intuitionistic logic, a property of a theory, or a claim about the meaning of disjunction? Explain how, if at all, it bears on the intuitionist's rejection of excluded middle.
>
> (c) [9 marks] Derive ¬¬(A ∨ ¬A) in intuitionistic natural deduction. Then explain what this fact shows, and what it does not show, about the relationship between double-negation elimination and excluded middle.

Below is one candidate's answer, reproduced as written. I have not marked it, and I would rather you form your own view of it than be nudged by mine.

> (a) The slogan suggests that intuitionistic logic is what remains when one deletes an axiom from a classical system, and that misdescribes the situation in two ways. First, what is deleted is not a single isolated theorem. With excluded middle goes everything whose derivation depended on it, and much of that does not mention excluded middle at all: A → B is no longer equivalent to ¬A ∨ B; from ¬(A ∧ B) one cannot in general conclude ¬A ∨ ¬B; and one cannot infer A from ¬¬A. Second, and more fundamentally, the two logics differ over what counts as a proof of a compound statement. An intuitionistic proof of A ∧ B is a pair of proofs; a proof of A → B is a construction converting any proof of A into a proof of B; a proof of ¬A is a construction turning any proof of A into absurdity. On that account, the failure of excluded middle is a consequence of the theory of proof rather than its definition, so the slogan has the order of explanation backwards. Note also that contraposition survives in only one direction: A → B yields ¬B → ¬A, but ¬B → ¬A does not yield A → B.
>
> (b) The disjunction property says that if a disjunction A ∨ B is true, then A is true or B is true. Stated that way it is not a thesis of intuitionistic logic but a constraint on what a theory proves, and it is what explains the rejection of excluded middle. Take Goldbach's conjecture, G. Neither G nor ¬G has been established, so on the intuitionist's account of truth neither is true, and hence G ∨ ¬G is not true. What the disjunction property does not license is an inference within a proof: from a hypothesis A ∨ B one cannot conclude A. Nor is the intuitionist thereby committed, for every disjunction she is entitled to assert, to asserting one of the two disjuncts.
>
> (c) Assume ¬(A ∨ ¬A). If we had A, then by ∨-introduction we would have A ∨ ¬A, contradicting the assumption; so we may conclude ¬A. But ¬A likewise gives A ∨ ¬A by ∨-introduction, again contradicting the assumption. Discharging the assumption yields ¬¬(A ∨ ¬A). So the double negation of excluded middle is intuitionistically provable even though excluded middle itself is not. This shows that double-negation elimination is strictly weaker than excluded middle: the schema ¬¬A → A may be adjoined to intuitionistic logic without making it classical, since it remains consistent, for an atomic P, that ¬¬P holds while P does not. The same goes for Peirce's law, ((A → B) → A) → A. It is not intuitionistically valid, but it does not carry excluded middle with it, and a logic containing it would still be properly weaker than classical logic. Intuitionistic logic therefore sits between such principles and classical logic, rather than being classical logic minus one axiom.
