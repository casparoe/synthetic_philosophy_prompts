ok, so here's a view I've slowly talked myself into and I want it properly stress-tested before I build an argument on top of it.

The view: multipolar traps only ever actually get escaped when somebody acquires the power to unilaterally enforce the cooperative outcome. Leviathan-shaped enforcement — a state, a monopolist that can punish defectors, a court with real sanctions behind it, a hegemon that can lock you out of the market. Everything that *looks* like a decentralized escape (norms, reputation, tit-for-tat in repeated play, industry self-regulation, certification schemes, mutual monitoring) is really one of three things: (a) it was never in a trap of the relevant kind to begin with, (b) it's downstream of enforcement power that already exists and is doing the work quietly, or (c) it's temporary and gets eaten by defectors eventually.

What would count against this? I want a ranked map rather than a pile of objections — which defeaters would be fatal, which are merely awkward, and which ones I'd be within my rights to explain away. Some specific places I already suspect I'm exposed:

- The folk theorem stuff. Repeated interaction is supposed to sustain cooperation with no enforcer at all. Does my view survive by saying the discounting and observability conditions just fail to hold in the real cases, or is that a dodge? How do you tell the difference between "the conditions don't obtain" and "the thesis is being protected by an escape hatch"?
- Long-lived commons — irrigation systems, fisheries, alpine pastures that held for generations with no state in sight and no single enforcer. Is that a genuine counterexample *class*, and if so how much of my thesis does it take down? Note I'm tempted to say those systems do have enforcement, just diffuse and social — tell me whether that move is available to me or whether it collapses the thesis into triviality.
- The hedges. I've got "of the relevant kind," "downstream of," and "eventually" doing a lot of work. Give it to me straight: has this become unfalsifiable? And if it has, is that actually a problem for a thesis of this shape, or is that just how general historical/structural claims have to be stated? I genuinely don't know where I sit on that.
- What does my view *predict* that the rival view (call it: decentralized cooperation is often enough, no enforcer needed) does not — something you could actually go and look for? If there's no such thing, that's a big mark against me and I want to hear it rather than have you soften it.

And for each defeater, flag whether you think a defender of this thesis probably already knows about it and has a canned reply, versus whether it tends to get silence. What I'm actually trying to work out is whether the view is worth keeping at all, or whether I should downgrade to the much weaker "enforcement helps a lot, more than people assume" — and if the weaker version is where I should land, tell me what it still gets me that's non-obvious.
