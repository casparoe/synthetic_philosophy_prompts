I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been asked to do something outside my usual work: deliver the closing remarks at the decommissioning of a national forecasting center, where for six decades a duty forecaster sat at one particular desk and signed every public warning by hand. The room will be mixed — retired meteorologists who came up through hand-plotted charts, a deputy minister, several local emergency managers, and their families.

The retiring chief forecaster is a determinist in the ordinary physical sense and utterly unsentimental about what that buys you. Her position, held for forty years, is that the atmosphere obeys equations that fix every future state, and that no measurement of the present will ever be sharp enough to select which one is coming; the craft was therefore never about seeing the future but about managing a spread of them. I'd like the speech built around a single decision she has told me about at length: a winter storm thirty years ago when the consensus of her model runs stayed mild and a small minority went violent, and she had to choose, before the deadline, whether to close schools and roads on the strength of that minority.

The hard part is what the speech may not say. It cannot celebrate the conquest of prediction, which is false on her own account. But neither can it retreat into the comfort that the future is open or undetermined, which she would consider a category error and half the room would hear as mysticism. The tribute has to be paid to something else: skill exercised under a ceiling that no amount of better data removes, and the decision to act on a tail rather than a mean. Roughly eight minutes, warm without being wet, legible to people who have never heard the word ensemble while making the forecasters feel precisely seen. Please write it in American English.
