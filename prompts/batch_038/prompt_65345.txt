I am a staff software engineer at a company that sells analytical products. I have no formal training in philosophy — no coursework, nothing beyond the occasional article — so please write for someone who knows systems design inside out and knows none of the technical vocabulary of metaphysics. Please do not include a reading list, footnotes or citations; I want a document I can circulate to my team, not a bibliography. Please write throughout in British English.

The situation. We run a platform that stores analytical artefacts: vendor datasets, curated datasets, trained models, dashboards and generated reports. The storage layer mutates in place. Rows are overwritten, columns are added, components are replaced one at a time during refactors, and nothing is copied unless someone explicitly asks for a copy. Two obligations depend on getting identity right:

- Contracts and provenance. Some vendor data is licensed on terms that distinguish the licensed dataset from derivative works built on it. When a licensor asks whether the thing now sitting in our tables is the artefact we signed for, we need a defensible answer.
- Audit. We are required to show which model produced a given decision. At present we answer by pointing at a row identifier. In some cases that identifier has referred, successively, to eleven rewrites.

The pattern that broke us is this. One physical set of tables is currently, at the same time and under the same row identifiers, both (a) the vendor dataset as delivered and (b) a curated report dataset built on top of it by overwriting and extending those very rows in place. There is no second copy. The two have different histories and, importantly, different survival conditions: the vendor dataset could survive deletion of the report, and the report could not survive a wholesale replacement of the vendor data underneath it. When we asked our architects whether that is one artefact or two, we got three different answers in the same meeting, and it emerged that every downstream schema choice — fork on write, version in place, point foreign keys at rows or at logical names — silently assumes one of those answers.

A colleague who has a philosophy degree tells me this is the statue-and-the-lump-of-clay problem wearing a systems-engineering disguise, and that the disagreement in that meeting was a genuine metaphysical disagreement rather than a muddle that better naming would fix. I should like to know what, if anything, that buys us operationally.

What I want is a decision procedure my team can run during design review.

1. A numbered sequence of questions, in the order they should be asked, for deciding whether the artefact in front of us now is the same artefact as the one referred to at some earlier date. For each question, state what counts as a sufficient answer, and which answer sends us down which branch.

2. For each step, one plain-English sentence naming which position about material constitution that step encodes — for instance, that identity is only ever identity relative to a kind term, or that an artefact is a four-dimensional whole of which each snapshot is a temporal part, or that where two candidates coincide we should keep only the one that matters — followed by one sentence on what would change in our systems if we adopted the rival position instead. I want to see the fork in the road, not only the road you chose.

3. Explicit markers for where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a human, on the grounds that the question is one of stipulation or convention rather than something to be discovered, together with the reason it stops there. Likewise, tell me which questions have determinate answers once we have fixed what kind of thing we are talking about, and which are genuinely vague boundary cases that no amount of further information will settle.

4. Worked runs on four real cases of ours:
   (i) the coinciding vendor dataset and curated report described above;
   (ii) a model that was continually fine-tuned over eighteen months until not one of the original parameters survived, while its name, endpoint and recorded lineage remained continuous throughout;
   (iii) a table that was dropped by accident and rebuilt from backup twenty minutes later, byte-for-byte identical to what it had been — did the artefact cease to exist during the gap, and does it matter that nothing downstream could tell the difference;
   (iv) a fork, where one dataset is copied into two environments that then diverge, so that both descendants have an equal claim to the original name.
   Please make at least one of these runs come out against me. I expect the procedure to tell me either that something I have been calling one artefact is two, or that something I have been treating as destroyed is intact, and I would rather hear it from you than from the licensor.

5. A short closing section on what the procedure cannot do, where it will misfire if applied mechanically, and which of its steps are judgements dressed up as procedure.

Constraints of use, so that you can calibrate the level: this will be run by platform engineers with no philosophy training, roughly two or three times a month, in about twenty minutes per case, and the outcome has to be recorded in a design review that our data governance lead signs off. It must therefore be a procedure somebody can follow correctly without agreeing with it. Please keep the whole thing to about 1,500 words.
