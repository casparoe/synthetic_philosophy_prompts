For the closing session of my graduate seminar on the medieval debate over causation, I want a poem rather than another summary. Write a ghazal in English, of at least six couplets, on al-Ghazālī's attack on the philosophers' account of causality in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa: the fire and cotton, where what he denies is not that burning follows contact but that contact necessitates burning, and where the constant conjunction is explained by God's habitual practice rather than by any power residing in the fire.

The form should carry the doctrine rather than decorate it. Choose a radif — a repeated word or short phrase standing after the rhyme — together with a qafia, the rhyme that precedes it, and let the opening couplet carry the pattern in both of its lines, as the tradition has it, so that the habit is instituted once and thereafter merely followed. Each later couplet must be semantically self-sufficient: no couplet may depend syntactically, narratively, or argumentatively on the one before it. The only thing binding them is the returning refrain, and it should read as custom, not consequence — the sequence present, the necessity absent. Make the couplets long enough that the refrain arrives rather than twitches. Somewhere in the middle, let the objection in: that a world of mere sequence is a world in which nothing can be known, and that the philosophers' charge is precisely this, the one Ibn Rushd pressed in reply — but do not let the poem concede it. Close with a maqta that signs the poem under a takhallus and turns on the thought that the habit could have been otherwise, that the cotton might have come away unburned, which is what leaves room for the miracle.

Accuracy matters more than atmosphere: a scholar of kalām should recognize the argument and not a vaguely mystical East. Keep the label occasionalism out of the poem itself.

After the poem, add a note of about 150 words saying where you have taken liberties: whether making the refrain an image of divine custom commits you to the standard occasionalist reading of the Tahāfut or to the contested one that leaves genuine room for secondary causes; whether the poem flirts with casting al-Ghazālī as a Humean regularity theorist, and how you would tell the difference; and what argument or textual consideration would persuade you that your framing of him is wrong.
