I'm an infectious disease modeller — compartmental models, SIR/SEIR, force of infection, network models, the lot. I've been put on a panel about herding in financial markets, so I've been reading the information-cascade literature: agents lined up in sequence, each seeing what everyone before them did, each with a bit of private information, and after a couple of steps everyone stops using their own signal and just copies. My problem is I can only think in epidemic terms and I've quietly started translating — someone who hasn't acted yet is susceptible, someone who's adopted is infected, watching your predecessors is contact, and a cascade looks for all the world like an outbreak with Re comfortably above one. One of my students even fitted a transmission model to some herding data and got a very respectable R0 out of it, which is what has me worried.

Do the translation properly for me, in my own vocabulary: the compartments, the flows, what the force of infection is actually a function of, what a reproduction number would mean here, what the herd immunity threshold would be. Then the bit I care about — the residue report. Which parts of the cascade story come across intact, which only come across if I squint, and which have no counterpart at all. I have two suspicions I want tested. First, that recovery and immunity have no analogue, because your agents act once and never leave the infected class. Second, that herd immunity comes out with the wrong sign: in my models every new infection protects the susceptibles left, whereas in a cascade every extra copier seems to leave the people behind them worse off, which is an externality pointing the other way.

Then give me the verdict, bluntly. Is the breakdown a real limitation of epidemiological language — it can say 'caused by contact' but has no way of saying 'inferred from evidence' — or does the residue suggest the cascade people are making less of a point than they think, and that what they've found is just correlation dressed up as contagion?
