I run the policy review side of a mid-sized city's Office of Equity — ten to twelve proposals a month land on my desk (zoning changes, fee waivers, pilots, moratoriums) and for each one we owe the city manager a recommendation. Our current screening tool is useless: it's basically three questions about who got consulted, whether the affected neighborhoods were notified, and whether the applicant thought about disparities. Everything passes. Including things that pretty clearly hurt poor people. I want to replace it with something grounded in Rawls, specifically the difference principle, because that's the theory our equity work keeps gesturing at without ever actually using.

Act as a political philosopher who has spent real time converting theory into institutional procedure — not someone summarizing a textbook. Think it through first: sketch what has to survive the translation and what can be dropped, then commit to a design.

Deliverable: a screening procedure my two analysts (neither trained in economics or philosophy) can run in about half a day per proposal. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a no, where it terminates in "the tool can't settle this — here's the value question for council," and what evidence or data each step actually needs. It has to be explainable to a councilmember who's never read Rawls, so no jargon without a plain-language gloss, and whatever it produces goes to my director for sign-off before it leaves the office.

Things I want you to make a call on rather than hand me a menu: whether least advantaged means worst-off individuals or a representative social group, and how an analyst picks that group from data we'd plausibly have; how the index of primary goods turns into something measurable; what to do with the priority of liberty at city scale — keep it, drop it, or fold it in; and whether our old tool's step where staff are told to imagine they don't know which neighborhood they'd live in is a legitimate use of the veil or a confusion, and if it stays, what work it's doing.

Then run it on three live ones: (1) upzoning within a half mile of our two rail stations, which should produce a lot of market-rate units plus a modest set-aside of income-restricted ones; (2) fare-free buses paid for by a half-cent sales tax bump; (3) a one-year moratorium on new multifamily permits in two neighborhoods, requested by a tenants' union to slow displacement. At least one of these should come out in a way I won't like — say so bluntly instead of softening it.

Last section: what the procedure cannot do, where it will systematically misfire, and what you had to drop to make it runnable. I know the difference principle was written for something much bigger than a city permit decision — tell me what breaks when you scale it down, and whether what's left is still the difference principle or something else wearing the name. Be commited throughout: take definite positions and give your reasons, don't lay out the options and leave me to pick.
