I have no academic training — I left school at sixteen and have worked as a fitter ever since — and I read only in translation, but for the last three years I have been studying the Zhuangzi on my own, since my wife died. The passages that have mattered most to me are the one in which he is found drumming on a tub and singing after his wife's death, and the ones that compare death and life to the alternation of night and day and to the turning of the four seasons. My conclusion from them is that sorrow at a death is, on his account, straightforwardly an error of understanding: nothing has been destroyed but only changed form, the person who died cannot be harmed by having died, and so grief is aimed at a loss that did not actually occur. The ideal he holds out is therefore to feel nothing at all.

Acting on this, I have settled into a discipline I call the three-day rule. I allow myself to grieve without restraint for three days, on the reasoning that Zhuangzi himself wept at first and stopped only once he had reflected on what had really happened. After that, I treat every return of sorrow as a failure of understanding: I sit down and rehearse the transformation argument aloud — the qi, the form, the seasons — until the feeling gives way. I have also let the anniversary of her death pass unmarked, since a commemoration seems to presuppose the very loss I have concluded did not happen.

My question concerns the calibration of all this. Is three days the right length for the initial period, or is it arbitrary, given that he specifies no interval anywhere? And when the rehearsal fails — when I have gone over the argument twice and the grief is undiminished — what does that indicate? Should I read it as evidence that I have not yet genuinely grasped the transformation, and keep at the exercise until I do, or does grief that resists the argument point to something else that needs handling on different terms? I would like to get the practice right rather than merely have it feel right.
