I am a second-year doctoral student in philosophy of science, working on the epistemology of survey data in field biology — how much a handful of opportunistic sightings can tell you about a species-level generalisation. My dissertation has to include a chapter on confirmation, and that chapter will sooner or later have to walk through Hempel's raven paradox. My supervisor has warned me off writing yet another survey of the literature, so I have been trying to approach the problem from the other end: by listing my own verdicts on concrete cases and seeing what principle, if any, they add up to. I should be grateful if you would treat the list below as data and do that work. Please let H stand for the hypothesis that all ravens are black.

(1) I see a raven in a wood, and it is black. H is confirmed, and by a respectable amount.
(2) I notice a white shoe in the corridor of the department. H is confirmed, but only by a trifling amount. I want to keep this one: the shoe is a non-black non-raven, and I cannot see how to deny that it bears on H without abandoning the equivalence condition, which I am not prepared to abandon.
(3) A black crow — a different species entirely — seems to me to bear on H not at all. This is the verdict I keep going back and forth on, because it sits oddly beside (2): the crow is neither a raven nor a non-black thing, so perhaps it is simply irrelevant, but then I have to explain why an object equally remote from the raven population does confirm.
(4) The raven I see is black because it has been wading in a spill, and I know the whole local population is similarly fouled. No confirmation.
(5) At dusk I half-glimpse something moving in a hedge, take it for a raven, and it turns out to be a grey pigeon. This feels like considerably better evidence for H than the shoe in (2), even though, like the shoe, it is a non-black non-raven — and considerably better than an unremarkable white shoe noticed while tidying, which is what I would have called it an hour earlier.
(6) A black raven in a taxidermist's case, selected by the curator precisely because it is a fine dark specimen: much weaker than (1), though the bird is genuinely black and genuinely a raven.
(7) A friend who surveys a remote valley tells me she has at last seen a raven there, and it was black. I had thought the valley's remnant population was probably down to nothing. On reflection this report leaves me slightly less confident in H than I was before: one more surviving raven means one more opportunity for a non-black one. So here a black raven disconfirms, and (1) and (7) are the pair I cannot reconcile.

My own tentative principle has been something in the neighbourhood of: an observation confirms H to the extent that it was risky, meaning that the observation would have been unlikely had H been false. It seems to handle (4) and (6) nicely, and I hoped it would handle (5), though on reflection I am not sure that it does. But it plainly misfires on (2), since I would have come across a white shoe whether or not H were true, and it misfires on (7), since it delivers at most weak confirmation where I want slight disconfirmation.

What I would like from you: one or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough that they can be applied to cases you have not seen before — a formal statement in terms of conditional probabilities together with an explicitly specified sampling procedure would be welcome rather than resented. For each clause of the principle, tell me which of my verdicts it exists to capture. Then give me a table covering (1) through (7), with the verdict your principle delivers, whether it matches mine, and, where it does not, why I should give mine up. I would much rather lose one or two verdicts than have H quietly reinterpreted — for instance, silently read as a claim about what ravens are black by nature, or about their kind — until everything fits; if such a reinterpretation is part of your answer, say so openly and tell me what it costs. Say also whether any part of my puzzlement is merely verbal, and which part. Finally, apply whatever account you settle on to two cases I have not listed: a partly albino crow found on the edge of a known raven colony, and the whole list again with H replaced by the weaker claim that most ravens are black. I am not after a history of the problem or a reading list; I want an account, and an argument for it. Please write the whole thing in British English.
