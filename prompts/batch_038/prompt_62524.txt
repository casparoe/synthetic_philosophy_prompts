I'm handing out my own position paper as a punching bag for the first week of a grad seminar on the calculation debate, and I want a hostile read before the students get it. Here's what I actually hold:

(1) Mises's original argument is a calculation argument, not an incentive one — the incentive material is a later bolt-on, and the calculation version is the philosophically interesting one.

(2) Lange's trial-and-error board doesn't answer it, because a board can only iterate over prices for goods and uses that are already on the menu; it can't generate the new combinations that entrepreneurship keeps throwing up.

(3) That said, I think the old computational objection is dead. With enough telemtry and compute you can approximate the equilibrium solution for a given menu of goods, so the static calculation problem is solved in principle.

(4) What survives is tacitness: the operative knowledge is inarticulable and only shows up in what people actually do, so it can't be fed to a solver no matter how fast the solver is.

(5) Big corporations are also, internally, planned economies — capital, labor and intermediate inputs get allocated by administrative decision and internal shadow prices rather than by exchange, and they do it competently.

Which of these pull against each other, and how badly? A couple of the pairings only look bad, so tell me which ones dissolves and why, and rank the real tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally awful. For each real one, say what the cheapest revision costs. Then commit: which single claim should I strike from the handout? I want your pick and your reasons, not a menu I have to choose from.
