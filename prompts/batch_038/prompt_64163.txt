The core arguement is that the way the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution always gets introduced — Nash's solution minus IIA plus monotonicity — hides what's actually interesting about it, and that the interesting thing is also its dirty secret.

**Setup, as I have it.** Two players, convex comprehensive feasible set S, disagreement point d, ideal point b where b_i is the most i can get in S. Nash picks the point maximizing (x1−d1)(x2−d2); KS walks from d toward b along the straight line and stops where it hits the frontier. Same thing said differently: KS is the point where both players capture the same fraction t of the gap between their no-deal payoff and their best case, with t as large as feasibility allows. Equal proportional gains, equal proportional concessions, pick your end of the segment.

**Example 1.** Let S be everything under y = 4 − x²/4 in the positive quadrant, d = (0,0). Then b = (4,4), the ray is the diagonal, and (4t,4t) lands on the frontier when 4t = 4 − 4t², i.e. t² + t − 1 = 0, t = (√5−1)/2 ≈ 0.618. So KS ≈ (2.472, 2.472). Nash maximizes x(4 − x²/4), so 4 − 3x²/4 = 0, x = 4/√3 ≈ 2.309, y = 8/3 ≈ 2.667. Products: 6.111 at KS vs 6.158 at Nash, so KS gives up less than one percent of the Nash product while shifting about 0.16 of payoff from player 2 to player 1. The two solutions are not disagreeing about the size of the pie here, only about who holds the knife.

What strikes me about this example: the frontier is wildly asymmetric (marginal rate of substitution runs from 0 to 2 along it) and KS still handed out identical payoffs. It did that purely because b1 = b2. Curvature in KS never affects the split of proportional gains — it's pinned at 50/50 by the symmetry axiom — curvature only sets how far along the ray you get, i.e. how big t is. Related claim I want checked: Nash and KS coincide exactly whenever the Pareto frontier is a straight line segment, whatever d is, and also of course whenever the problem is symmetric. So the interesting disagreements live in asymmetric-and-curved problems only.

**Example 2, the one I like best.** Same S, same d, but now throw in one extra feasible agreement, (5, 0.5) — an absurd deal that nobody would ever sign, way out on player 1's axis. The new frontier runs along the old arc only until the tangent line from (5, 0.5) touches it, which solving p²/4 − 2.5p + 3.5 = 0 puts at p ≈ 1.683, i.e. P ≈ (1.683, 3.292); after that it's a straight run to (5, 0.5). The ideal point becomes (5, 4), the ray gets steeper, and KS moves to about (2.868, 2.294). Player 1 gains roughly 0.40. Player 2, whose own options never got any worse at any level, loses roughly 0.18 — because player 1's *dream* got bigger.

That's the point I want to build the piece around. Monotonicity, if I've got it right, is a one-player-at-a-time axiom: it protects i when the set expands in a way that improves i's attainable payoffs while leaving the other players' attainable-frontier functions unchanged, and it's silent about j — and Pareto then pretty much guarantees j is the one who pays. If that's the right reading, then the slogan "a player with better options shouldn't do worse" is doing much less work than the textbook presentation implies, and the thing KS is actually sensitive to is the two endpoints of the feasible span, which are the cheapest objects in the whole problem to inflate. Add one far-out point on your own axis and you rotate the ray in your favor.

Which sets up the tension I think is genuinely interesting rather than just cute. KS's answer is *sayable*: you can put it in a negotiating room as "each side concedes the same fraction of the distance between its no-deal position and its best case, and we take the largest fraction that's feasible" — no calculus, both delegations can check it against their own numbers, which is presumably why proportional-concession language keeps resurfacing in burden-sharing talks where the parties have wildly different scales. But the number it makes salient, your best case, is exactly the number you have the strongest incentive to lie about. Nash's answer is unsayable but harder to move with a bluff, since it's pinned by the local trade-off rate where you actually land.

Two worries I have about my own draft: (a) if Nash and KS really do coincide on all linear frontiers, maybe my local-versus-global contrast is about a special case, not the heart of the matter; (b) I have a vague memory that the whole thing misbehaves past two players — something about the characterization not surviving n ≥ 3 — and if fairness intuitions are supposed to be about fairness, they shouldn't care how many people are at the table.
