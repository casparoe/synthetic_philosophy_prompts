For some years I have been drafting settlement proposals in two-party disputes over a shared productive asset — capacity allocations, joint-venture splits, cost-sharing between adjoining operators — and I have converged, essentially by accident, on a mechanical rule. For each side I compute two numbers: what that side would be left with if no agreement were reached, and the best it could conceivably extract from any arrangement under consideration. I then select the arrangement that gives each side the same fraction of its own span between those two numbers. With zero walk-away values and a surplus of 100 that either side could in principle take entirely, the rule returns 50/50, which is what plain intuition wants, so I have kept using it.

One preliminary question, which I would like answered but which is not the point: I strongly suspect this rule is not mine, that it has a name, and that it is the standard rival to the rule that instead maximises the product of the two sides' gains over their walk-away values. If I have reinvented something, say what, and say what its defining property is normally taken to be.

The substance of my question is a pattern I keep running into and have no word for.

First case. Suppose side A is capacity-constrained — no arrangement under discussion can give A more than 50 — while B could in principle take all 100. My rule then awards A 33⅓ and B 66⅔, each side receiving two-thirds of its own best conceivable figure. Now suppose A's constraint is lifted: a new outlet opens, so that A could in the limit take the whole 100. No previously available arrangement disappears. The rule now returns 50/50 — an arrangement that was on the table all along. B's payoff falls by more than sixteen points, not because anything about B's own position changed and not because anyone selected one of the new extreme arrangements, but purely because the number representing A's best conceivable outcome grew. I have checked the arithmetic, and it is not a knife-edge: B's award declines smoothly as A's new ceiling rises, and for every value of that ceiling the arrangement the rule lands on is one that was available before the constraint was lifted. Only the division moves. The product rule, by contrast, returns 50/50 in both states of the world.

Second case, which troubles me more. In practice my rule reads the two ceilings off the set of arrangements under consideration, and that set is largely whatever the parties have tabled. A side that tables an arrangement worth 160 to itself and 20 to the other — one it would never accept and the other would never sign — has raised its own ceiling and hence its target fraction, and the settlement drifts decisively in its direction. Nothing here depends on anyone being deceived. The tabling is transparently strategic; the rule consults no one's beliefs, only the menu. (I should add that the rule treats any blend of two tabled arrangements — partial adoption, time-sharing, a side payment — as an arrangement in its own right; in the few cases where I forbade blends and required it to pick an actually tabled proposal, the effect vanished, though I do not know whether that is general or an artefact of my examples.)

What I want from you:

1. Does either case have an established name? I have been told that the rule I use is normally defended by a property governing what should happen when one side's best achievable outcome improves, and normally attacked by a property concerning alternatives that ought not to matter. Which of the two does my first case fall under? Is my complaint simply a complaint about the rule's signature virtue, so that the two cannot be separated, or is it a distinct hazard that would survive even in a defender who accepts the virtue?

2. Why is the product rule apparently immune, if it is immune? Its inputs are the same walk-away values and the same set of arrangements; it simply never consults the ceilings. Is there a term for the family of rules that do, and does this exposure come with membership in that family?

3. I half-remember a result on the non-cooperative side of bargaining to the effect that an outside option changes the outcome only when it is better than what the party would get by remaining at the table. My rule appears to let an option that is worse than the settlement — one that will never be exercised at all — move the settlement anyway. Is that a recognised tension between the two approaches, or have I misremembered the result?

4. Is there an established distinction between gaining by altering what is genuinely attainable and gaining by altering what has been tabled, or what the arbiter takes to be attainable? My two cases feel like different species, and I cannot tell whether they are treated together or separately.

5. Where are the edges? Specifically: does the effect cancel when both sides inflate their ceilings together; does it change when the walk-away values are non-zero and asymmetric; does it require arrangements that only one side can reach?

If the phenomenon has a name, give it and tell me how my description departs from the standard formulation. If it does not, say so plainly and coin one — but I want the carving, not the label: a definition, the nearest established concepts it must be distinguished from, and the boundary cases above. The reason I am asking is that I need to decide whether to keep proposing settlements under this rule at all, and a bare term of art will not settle that.
