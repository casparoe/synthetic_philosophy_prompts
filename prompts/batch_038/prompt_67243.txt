Late last night I lost all track of time arguing with a friend who is a mechanism-design economist about what the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem actually licenses people to say about electoral reform, and I came away unsure which of us had been talking nonsense. Since I am about to present the version of the view I defended to a room considerably less friendly than he was, I would rather find the cracks now than there.

In three weeks I give a talk at a public policy school's workshop on electoral reform. The room is mostly political scientists who study the adoption of ranked-choice and runoff systems in American cities and who are temperamentally reformers; two mechanism-design economists; and one computational social choice person who will be irritated by any loose talk of complexity. Nobody else there is a social choice theorist, and two of the political scientists have written widely circulated op-eds invoking the impossibility result as grounds for modesty about institutional design.

My thesis is that the theorem has been misfiled. Properly read it is not a claim that manipulation is pervasive but a claim that a certain guarantee is unavailable: given a deterministic rule that is onto and defined on unrestricted strict orderings over at least three alternatives, it says nothing at all about how often a successful manipulation exists. So strategy-proofness should be demoted from a design criterion to a benchmark, replaced by what I call a manipulation budget — the share of profiles at which a randomly drawn voter, with a small perturbation of her true ranking, can improve her outcome — handled the way an engineer handles a tolerance in a building code, with an explicit acceptable threshold rather than a binary property. My supporting moves are that the theorem's escape clauses (single-peaked and otherwise restricted domains, randomization, and the practical difficulty of computing a beneficial misrepresentation) are where all the real design work happens anyway, and that the correspondence result linking strategy-proof voting procedures to Arrow's conditions shows the dictatorial punchline is about guarantees rather than about the density of strategic incentives in practice.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: the seven or eight objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each does to the demotion thesis, and phrased the way each subfield would phrase it — a reform-minded political scientist does not object as a mechanism designer objects, and neither objects as the computational social choice person will. For each, a two-minute reply and a candid note on where that reply is thinnest. Then tell me which objection I should concede ground on in the first ten minutes so that I am not trapped by it in the fortieth, which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive, and the question I am hoping nobody asks, whether it concerns the arbitrariness of my threshold or something worse about the demotion itself.
