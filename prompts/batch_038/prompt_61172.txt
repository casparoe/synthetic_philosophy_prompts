A row blew up at my parents' house over my brother and I couldn't referee it. My sister says he's deceiving himself about their mother's death two years ago — he still sets a place for her at Christmas and insists he's totally fine. My brother-in-law says self-deception is a myth: you can't believe p and not-p at once, and nobody deliberately sets out to fool themselves, so all she's got is a man who's mistaken. (2) She said fine, call it that — but then he can't say what makes our brother different from someone who simply misread the evidence: he rearranges everything around the belief, treats the anniversary as a date like any other, and goes for anyone who raises it. (3) He said that's confirmation bias and motivated reasoning, which everybody does, so it isn't self-deception, just ordinary bad thinking — and anyway the traditional picture she wants is incoherent for a reason nobody ever mentions: if you had to intend to get yourself to believe something you knew was false, you'd also have to hide that intention from yourself, so the paradox comes straight back a level up. (4) She said if its only bias then it's something that happens to him rather than something he does, and we've no standing to confront him about it, which is absurd. (5) He said that's a false dilemma, since we confront people about bad thinking all the time without needing them to have done it on purpose. Nobody budged. Can you take the five moves in order and say which of them actually land, who's more right overall, and what the whole argument turns on that neither of them brought up?
