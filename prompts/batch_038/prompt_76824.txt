I write about science and ideas for a general-interest mag and I've got a piece brewing on opera that I can't get straight in my head. Not a review — more like, what does the institution think it's doing.

Here's what's bugging me. Walk into basically any major house today and the opera is being sung in Italian or German or Czech or whatever, with the words projected above the stage or on a little screen on the back of the seat in front of you. That surtitling setup only really took off in the 1980s and became standard in the 90s, but now it's just assumed. And the titles are heavily compressed: lines get cut or flattened so they fit on one line, the syntax gets ironed out, and an operator punches a button at a mark in the score to make each one appear. Meanwhile the notes are treated as sacrosanct. Nobody would dare add a bar, re-orchestrate a passage, or let a tenor drop in a showier aria from a different opera, and if a conductor takes a tempo the composer didn't indicate there will be letters. Same with the language: original text, always. But then a director moves the whole thing into a 1970s airport lounge with the cast in business suits, and that's fine, that's just "a production." One big London company — the English National Opera — built it's entire identity on the opposite rule, everything sung in English so you don't need titles at all, and it's treated as the eccentric exception.

So what theory of what an opera is does the mainstream bundle actually presuppose? Which rival accounts does it quietly rule out — opera as drama that has to be understood, opera as a vehicle for voices, opera as a text? Are the parts even consistent with each other, or is one of them free-floating? And how much of this is philosophically underdetermined, where several very different accounts of the work would license the exact same stage practice?

I've been reading Bernard Williams on opera, and my rough takeaway is that he treats opera as drama rather than a concert with costumes, but I'm shaky on him. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I couldn't use it — just reason from the practice. And finish by telling me what would concretely have to change on stage and in the auditorium if a house actually committed to the drama-first view instead of the sound-surface-first one.
