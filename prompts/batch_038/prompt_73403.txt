I taught myself the basics of Bayesian epistemology over the past year, and the part that stuck with me is the family of arguments that says a set of beliefs or preferences is defective because someone could take your money: the classic argument that incoherent degrees of belief expose you to a combination of bets you'd each accept but that guarantees a loss, the pump that goes around intransitive preferences, the version aimed at agents who don't revise by conditionalizing, and the one aimed at groups whose members' degrees of belief can't be pooled into something coherent, so the group as a whole is exposed even if no member is. I keep seeing these treated as interchangeable, as if they all establish the same conclusion with the same force. I suspect they don't.

Please sort them into tiers by how much of the gap each one actually closes between "you would lose money under these circumstances" and "you are irrational." Give each a number from 0 to 1 on that dimension and a sentence or two on why it lands where it does. For each, name the auxiliary assumptions doing the work — whether the agent has to accept the bets at all, care about money in some particular way, not learn anything between the bets, remember its earlier commitments, or be assumed to have preferences about bets rather than about the outcomes the bets describe. If any two turn out to be the same argument in different clothing, say so and keep only one. Finally, tell me whether these betting arguments look stronger or weaker than the arguments that don't involve money at all and instead say that incoherent degrees of belief are guaranteed to be less accurate than a coherent alternative, and if you think one member of the family is considerably weaker than its reputation suggests, say which one and why. Please answer in American English.
