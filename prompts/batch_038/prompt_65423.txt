I'm TA'ing a first-year seminar next semester that's supposed to be half political philosophy, half literature, on the theme of "how words get used politically," and I've paired Orwell's Politics and the English Language (the 1946 essay) with Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. On the surface the pairing is almost too easy: Orwell's whole argument is that political prose goes cloudy because people have to say things they can't face saying plainly, that euphemism and pretentious diction exist mainly to make the indefensible sound like something else, and that you can diagnose corrupt thinking from the behavior of the sentences. Stevens the butler looks like exhibit A — he narrates his employer's efforts to rehabilitate Nazi Germany in the vocabulary of gentlemanly honor and "unofficial" conferences, and never once just says what he was helping with. My last cohort read Orwell and nodded and thought it was trivially true, and I want to hand them something that isn't.

Here's the tension I keep circling and want your help getting clear on. Orwell's diagnostic kit is basically stylistic — latinate fog, dying metaphors, words used as though they carried meanings nobody could pin down. But Stevens's prose fails almost none of those tests. It's formal, precise, orderly, weirdly clean. And yet it manages to say nothing about the one thing that matters, and to make his own moral abdication sound like professional pride. So it seems like the novel is showing that a style-based test is the wrong shape of test: the evasion isn't in the sentence, it's in the choice of what sentences get written at all. Related to that, Stevens's whole self-conception hangs on dignity, a word he treats as a technical term with a definition and then quietly redefines whenever it's threatened — which feels like the thing Orwell is most worried about politically, except that nothing in Orwell's essay would flag it as bad writing. And then the ending, where Stevens resolves to practice bantering for his new American employer: he decides the way forward is to learn a new register of speech, which is oddly close to Orwell's own prescription, except that what he learns is a way of being cheerful and non-committal, not a way of being plain.

So: give me a real reading of the novel through Orwell's account of language and political thought, not a summary of either. I want it honest in both directions — where Orwell genuinely illuminates the book (point me at maybe four or five scenes I'd have students reread, by description rather than by quote: the conference at Darlington Hall, his father's death while Stevens keeps serving, the exchange with Miss Kenton about the novel she's reading, the pier at Weymouth), and where the novel resists him and a fair critic has to say so. I especially want you to press on two questions. One: does Orwell's second claim — that plain, concrete English is the remedy, that honest style and honest politics come as a package — survive contact with a narrator whose style is beautiful and whose politics are a vacuum? Or does the book suggest clarity can be a better hiding place than vagueness ever was? Two: is there something in the novel that Orwell's framework can't accommodate at all, like the idea that euphemism is sometimes mercy or social glue rather than a lie-machine — Stevens's understatement with Miss Kenton reads to me as two people protecting each other, not only as two people lying, and Orwell has no category for that. You can end by telling me what the novel adds to the debate about political language that the essay can't argue for on its own. No bibliography or reference list please, I'll do the sourcing myself, and don't quote either text verbatim — I've got both books on my desk and I don't need a paraphrase wearing quotation marks.
