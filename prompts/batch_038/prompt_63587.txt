I would like a referee's assessment of an argument I witnessed but was not a party to. Last term I was permitted to sit in on the informal discussion hour attached to a graduate ethics seminar, and two of the participants — I'll call them Hana and Marek — worked their way into a sustained dispute about the so-called paradox of deontology. Neither of them asked me anything, and I have no stake in the outcome; what I want is a careful, round-by-round judgment about whose moves actually land.

The case they were both working from, fixed by agreement before the argument began: an engineer at a processing facility can either divert a lethal release into a sealed chamber where one worker is trapped, deliberately killing him, or do nothing, in which case five people in another part of the facility die. They also adopted the following stipulations, which both treated as binding throughout:

1. The engineer bears no responsibility for the situation, and if she does nothing, the five deaths are in no way attributable to her.
2. Diverting counts as intentional killing under any plausible account of intention, not as a merely foreseen side effect of something else she does.
3. There is no third option, no uncertainty about consequences, and no slippery-slope, generalization, or institutional considerations available to either side.
4. The norm she would violate is a prohibition, not a mere permission to refrain; nothing in the argument may trade on converting one into the other.
5. Neither may appeal to the separateness of persons, or to inviolability, or to the special value of agency as a brute stopping point without saying what explanatory work the notion is doing.

Here is the exchange as I recorded it.

**Round 1.** Hana opened by stating the problem. Everyone present, including the deontologist, agrees that five survivors is better than one. So the deontologist is committed to the claim that the engineer ought to bring about the worse state of affairs. Hana stressed that this is not yet the difficulty; the difficulty is that the only account anyone in the room had of what makes an act required is that it produces the best outcome, and the deontologist has no competing account. She has an intuition about killing, not a theory of requirement.

**Round 2.** Marek replied that Hana's framing already contains her conclusion. Reasons, he said, come in more than one shape: some are reasons to see that a state of affairs obtains, others are reasons not to perform a certain kind of act oneself. Once the second shape is admitted, comparing states of affairs is simply no longer the question, and Hana's charge that the engineer brings about a worse state of affairs loses its force — worse for whom, and assessed from where? Hana had built an agent-neutral metric into the setup and then expressed surprise that deontology scored badly on it.

**Round 3.** Hana said this redescribes the intuition in technical vocabulary rather than explaining it: what stood in need of explanation was why there is a reason of the second shape here, and why it carries the weight her case assigns it. She then pressed a dilemma. Grant agent-relative reasons; the deontologist still owes an account of how they combine with agent-neutral ones. If the agent-relative reason wins regardless of how many people die, she has posited a lexical ordering, and the problem merely relocates — why does this reason have lexical weight? If it can be outweighed beyond some number, she is a threshold consequentialist, and the problem is dissolved only by ceasing to hold the view.

**Round 4.** Marek said the dilemma is unfair because both horns assume that what needs defending is a weighting function. On his account the constraint is not a heavy counterweight on the agent's side of a scale; it is a different kind of normative consideration, one that excludes certain options from deliberation rather than competing with others. Considerations of that kind have no weight available to be traded against outcomes, and asking how much they weigh is a category mistake.

**Round 5.** Hana answered that this is either trivial or question-begging. Trivial, because the consequentialist can say exactly the same about maximizing: it too is not weighable against anything else, it simply sets the terms. Question-begging, because the live dispute is whether any option may be excluded from deliberation independently of its consequences, and the availability of exclusionary reasons as a class does nothing to show that this particular exclusion is genuine rather than a dressed-up squeamishness.

**Round 6.** Marek conceded that his last move cannot by itself establish which exclusions are genuine, but argued that Hana had been pressing a demand weaker and more general than the paradox she announced in Round 1. If what she wants is a derivation of the constraint from premises a consequentialist already shares, then she is not raising a special problem for deontology at all; she is raising the problem of how any substantive normative view can be justified to someone who does not already accept it — a problem that bears equally on the claim that one's reasons are agent-neutral, since that claim is no more derivable from neutral ground than the constraint is. On that demand, he said, there is no paradox, because there is nothing deontology-specific left to explain.

**Round 7.** Hana said that even if Round 6 is correct, it evades the narrower version of her challenge, which does not require convincing an outsider. It requires only that the deontologist make her own position intelligible to herself: she says the five deaths are worse and that she must nevertheless let them happen. Hana asked whether Marek could name any state of affairs in which the deontologist's own values are better realized by the forbidden act — and if he could, in what sense the engineer prefers the outcome she actually produces.

Marek's closing reply was that the deontologist need not prefer any outcome, because she is not in the business of preferring outcomes; Hana's question assumes that every agent is a locus of ranked states of the world, when in fact she is someone whose conduct is constrained by what she may and may not do to other people.

What I am asking for:

(a) Take the seven rounds in order and mark each one — specifically, the move made by whoever is speaking in that round — as a misstep, a partial success, a clear success, or decisive. Justify each mark in a sentence or two, and say explicitly where a round contains both a successful and an unsuccessful component.

(b) Identify the round, if any, at which the argument stops being about the paradox of deontology and becomes about something else. Does either participant notice the change of subject, and does the change help or hurt the person who effects it?

(c) Does Marek's Round 4 appeal to exclusionary considerations survive Hana's Round 5 objection, or does her trivial-or-question-begging dilemma genuinely close it down? I want a verdict on that specific pairing, not a general comment on exclusionary reasons.

(d) Within the stipulations above, what should each of them have said that neither did? I am interested in the best available move that went unplayed on each side, and in whether either such move would have ended the argument.

(e) Overall: who is more right, and by how much? And at the end of Round 7, is there a substantive disagreement still standing between them, or have they drifted into a merely verbal difference about what counts as preferring an outcome?
