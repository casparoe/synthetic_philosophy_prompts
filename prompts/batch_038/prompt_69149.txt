I am the head of welfare science at a company that rear black soldier fly larvae and mealworms at industrial scale for aquaculture feed, and since last year we have also run a small cultured-cell line producing animal-free protein in bioreactors. I sit on an internal welfare committee that meets every six weeks and receives between eight and twelve proposals a quarter: changes to rearing density, new stunning or killing methods, a request to stop a feed-deprivation step before processing, sieving and grading routines that we currently treat as purely mechanical, enrichment trials, and end-of-life handling for our breeding colonies of adult flies. The committee has an operating veterinarian whose training is entirely in mammals and birds, a process engineer, a sustainability lead, and me; it can approve up to €250,000 of capital spend on its own authority, and anything above that goes to the board, which wants a single page of justification. Decisions have to be reached inside a two-hour meeting, and the whole thing has to be auditable, because a certification body has asked us for a documented scheme that assigns our various biological systems to welfare tiers, and because a competitor has started marketing "high-welfare" mealworms in a way that will make any inconsistency of ours visible.

What we use now is unusable. It is a two-question screen inherited from a consultant who works in farmed fish: does the animal have nociceptors, and does it show conditioned avoidance of a location where it was harmed. It is binary where our cases are not, it silently assumes the morphology of a vertebrate, and it gives us nothing at all to say about life stages — the insects we process are larvae, whose behaviour is dominated by reflex and whose evidence base is much thinner than that for crustaceans, while the adults in our breeding colonies are a different behavioural animal again. A philosopher we brought in for a half-day briefing told us, sensibly, that moral status is better thought of as coming in degrees than as a switch, that the evidence for those degrees is a matter of capacities, and that under uncertainty we should weight the possibility of a negative outcome by how bad it would be if we were wrong. That was the right shape of an answer and gave me nothing to hand to the vet on Tuesday. Please write the missing instrument.

You are, for this task, a philosopher of mind and ethics who has actually sat inside industry animal-welfare committees and knows what survives contact with a spreadsheet and what does not. Begin by thinking carefully and laying out, in a few paragraphs of brainstorming before any procedure appears, the two or three conceptual decisions that have to be made before this can be operationalised — for instance, whether the procedure scores evidence of sentience separately from the welfare significance of a harm once sentience is granted; whether status attaches to a species, to a life stage, or to an individual; and what direction the precaution should run when the evidence is thin and the cost of a false positive is real money. Tell me where you land on each and what the alternative would cost us. Only then give me the procedure itself.

The procedure must meet these requirements:

- It must be runnable in roughly ninety minutes per proposal by someone with a biology degree and no philosophy, and it must produce a completed one-page scoring sheet as its output. Include that sheet.
- It must be an ordered sequence of questions, with the questions asked in the order that matters, and for each question a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, what counts as an insufficient one, and what to record when the answer is simply unknown.
- It must convert evidence into a graded status rather than a yes/no, and it must say explicitly which of the standard evidential markers — nociception, evaluative or associative learning, motivational trade-offs against a valued resource, analgesic or self-directed wound-care responses, integrated state-dependent behaviour — carry weight for a larval insect whose behavioural repertoire is limited, and which are effectively unavailable to us at that life stage.
- It must handle the fact that we are dealing in enormous numbers. Our throughput is measured in billions of individuals per year, so a harm that is trivial per individual can dominate a harm that is severe but rare. I suspect this is going to make the procedure rank some cheap, boring change above an expensive one that I care about a great deal; if so, say so plainly rather than designing around it.
- It must specify where it stops with a no — that is, which kinds of proposal are ruled out at which step, and what the record should say when it does so.
- It must specify where it hands the decision to a human being because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. In particular, how much throughput a company should sacrifice for an organism whose status is genuinely uncertain is not something a procedure can answer; mark that boundary clearly and say what the committee should be shown at that point.
- It must be honest about which of its steps are judgment calls wearing a numeric costume, and it must not pretend to a precision our evidence does not support.
- No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, and no appeals to named researchers. I cannot look anything up and I do not want references; I want the reasoning written out.

Then run the procedure end to end on three cases, showing the scoring sheet completed for each. First: replacing our current thermal kill step for black soldier fly larvae with an electrical stunning stage before it, at an estimated €400,000 of capital cost and a measurable throughput reduction — a change I personally favour and expect to be vindicated, so tell me honestly if the procedure does not vindicate it. Second: a request to stop a twelve-hour feed-deprivation period before grading and processing, which is nearly free to abandon but which the process engineers argue improves product consistency. Third: our rearing halls are now managed by a learning-based climate controller that adapts its setpoints to larval behaviour; a junior colleague has proposed, half as a thought experiment and half in earnest, that we log something like its "preference satisfaction" as a welfare-relevant quantity. The procedure should be able to say something defensible and unhysterical about that case, and it should not do so by a special exemption bolted on at the end — if it can handle a controller and a larva with the same machinery, show me the machinery doing both.

Close with a section headed "What this procedure cannot do": the questions it will get wrong, the kinds of proposal it will systematically misjudge, and the specific ways a motivated manager could game it to get a preferred answer while remaining formally compliant. I would rather know that now than discover it in an audit.
