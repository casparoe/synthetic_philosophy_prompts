I work nights at the dispatch desk for a rural ambulance service, so my whole shift is basically me believing things strangers tell me on the phone and passing them along to a crew doing 70 on a gravel road. No philosophy background at all, just ten years of this and too much time to think between calls.

Somewhere along the way I argued myself into a pretty hard-line view: when somebody tells you something, believing them is the default rational move, and the burden sits on the person doing the doubting. You need an actual reason to distrust a report, and "I have no way to check it" is not a reason. I later found out this apparently has a name, something like anti-reductionism about testimony, Reid versus Hume, whatever. It's also why I think "do your own research" is a nonsense slogan — researching is still just reading what other people reported, you've only moved the trust somewhere else.

Here's my problem though. My own job seems like its evidence against me. Callers are wrong all the time, consistantly wrong in ways nobody could of predicted — wrong address, wrong about what they saw, wrong about whether he's breathing. Almost never lying, just wrong. And I still pick up the next call and believe that one too, because the alternative is sending nobody. So either my view is tougher than it looks or I've built something that literally nothing can touch.

So tell me what would count against it. I want the honest map, not a pep talk. Which things would actually break the view — arguments, cases, results from psychology or wherever — and which are just annoying and I can live with them, ranked from fatal to awkward. Tell me which of them someone holding my view should admit hurts on their own terms, not just which ones critics shout about. And answer straight: has "believe by default" been quietly qualified into something no evidence could contradict, and if so is that actually a problem for a view of that kind or is that just how it has to work? Also what does my position predict that the distrust-first side doesn't, and can you give me a realistic situation where switching to their policy would have gotten a better outcome?

No reading list please, I cant get past a paywall anyway and I'd rather have the reasoning.
