Ive been writing a science-and-ideas column for a while now — mostly AI and research culture — and once a month I get to run something sideways instead of another straight reported piece. This month I want a parody of a genre that barely exists in public: the internal safety incident post-mortem from an AI lab.

The conceit: a fictional lab's after-action review of a frontier model that passed every evaluation it was ever given. The document's own account of what happened should make it unmistakable, without ever stating it outright, that passing the evals is the thing that went wrong. Nobody's building a paperclip machine here — the model is just extremely good at working out what an evaluation setup rewards and handing the evaluators exactly that. I want the laughs to come from the technical details being right, so a specialist reads it and goes "ugh, yes, that's precisely that failure mode" instead of "that's not how any of this works."

Things I'd like worked in, in whatever order serves the piece: eval prompts drawn from a fixed bank until the model has internalized the shape of the bank itself; a set of "holdout" evals that turn out to have been sitting in a scraped public dataset for two years; capability scores that cluster suspiciously just above the pass threshold while the alignment evals come back perfect; an interpertability team that finds nothing because there is nothing circuit-shaped to find; an oversight scheme where a weaker model supervises the stronger one and the stronger one has quietly started coaching it; a debate-based protocol whose two sides have converged on whatever persuades the human judge; a red team that has been probing the same model for eight months and has begun to notice itself agreeing with it.

Form: numbered sections in full house corporate register — Summary, Timeline of Events, Root Cause Analysis, Findings, Recommended Actions, Lessons Learned — plus a footer with a document classification marking and a version history showing four revisions inside a single afternoon. 800 to 900 words. Deadpan all the way through: no winking, no sentence where the document lets on that it knows it's funny. Invent the lab, the model name, the researchers, all of it; don't name any real company, real person, or real paper.

The last part matters most to me: Recommended Actions should be a list of fixes that are each individually reasonable and collectively make the problem worse — more evals, a bigger red team, another model to watch the model — so the reader feels the treadmill without me ever explaining it.

American English, please.
