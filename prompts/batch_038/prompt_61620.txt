I've been reading the rationalist blogs since the old sequences days and I hang around a couple of EA-adjacent forums, and last week there was this thread that turned into a genuine two-person standoff. Best argument I've seen on there in ages, and it ended with nobody conceding anything and the thread just petering out. I was only lurking so I don't want to tell you which side I leaned toward — I want you to referee it cold.

Please act as a philosopher/decision theorist who actually works on infinite ethics and knows the technical machinery (cardinal vs ordinal comparison, Pareto principles over infinite populations, the measure/limit problems) rather than someone who gestures vaguely at "infinity is weird." Think carefully before you answer, and don't split the difference to be polite — some of these moves may just be wrong, and if a move rests on a technical claim that's flatly false about cardinal arithmetic or light cones or limits, say so explicitly instead of grading it on its internal logic.

Everyone in the thread was granting, for the sake of argument, the following setup: the universe is spatially infinite, matter is distributed more or less uniformly on large scales, no afterlife, no simulation, just this one infinite universe containing infinitely many people. Two commenters, who I'll call N and R, disagreed about what that implies for a $200 donation to a global health charity.

**Move 1 (N):** If the universe is infinite, there are already infinitely many people whose lives go well and infinitely many whose lives go badly. Saving three lives adds three to infinity, and aleph-null plus three is aleph-null. So the post-donation world and the pre-donation world contain exactly the same number of well-off people. If "how much good is in the world" is a matter of how many good lives there are, then my donation changes nothing — not a tiny amount, but zero. The whole expected-value-for-charity business is built on a picture of the world that turns out to be false.

**Move 2 (R):** You're using cardinality as a measure of value, and cardinality is simply a bad measure of value. Two sets can have the same cardinality while one contains everything the other contains and more — the evens and the naturals, say. Use the Pareto comparison instead: world B is at least as good as world A if nobody is worse off in B and somebody is better off. The donation world Pareto-beats the non-donation world, and that's all the difference you need. Your conclusion is an artifact of the tool you picked, not a fact about value.

**Move 3 (N):** Pareto handles the easy cases and abandons you in every case that is actually a decision. Real choices are trade-offs: the same money that saved three lives here got pulled out of something else, or an action raises three people's welfare while slightly lowering five other people's. Neither world Pareto-dominates the other, so Pareto says "not comparable," and a decision theory that says "not comparable" to every live choice isn't a decision theory you can use. And if you patch it with a density measure — count the fraction of well-off people inside a sphere of radius r centered here, then let r go to infinity — the limit either fails to exist or, worse, exists but is order-dependent: change the way you take the limit and a locally good act comes out globally bad. So you're back to zero.

**Move 4 (R):** Then restrict attention to what you can actually reach. Your action's effects live inside your future light cone, and over any finite stretch of time that's a finite region containing finitely many people. Two candidate worlds that agree everywhere outside some finite region can be compared by ordinary finite summation — the infinite remainder is identical in both and cancels. The infinite ethics problem is a problem for agents who can affect infinitely many people, not for you holding a two hundred dollar donation. You've taken a technical difficulty about comparing infinite sets and converted it into a licence to do nothing.

**Move 5 (N):** That restriction is unstable, and here's the part I actually find interesting. If space is infinite and matter is roughly uniformly distributed, then somewhere out there are systems that are molecule-for-molecule duplicates of me, running duplicates of this exact deliberation. On your causal story they're irrelevant because I can't signal them. But if you take the functional/updateless view of decision-making that a lot of people on this forum take — where committing to a policy matters for what your duplicates do, because you are in the relevant sense the same computation — then "decide to donate" is a decision with infinitely many instances, and the stakes go straight back to infinity. So you can't have it both ways. Either acausal connections count, and your finite-light-cone restriction collapses, or they don't, and you owe me an account of why it's rational to ignore duplicates who are in a perfect evidential position to tell you what you're about to do.

**Move 6 (R):** Those aren't two horns, they're one confusion. The duplicates aren't affected by my decision — they resemble me because we share a causal history, and resemblance isn't a channel. And notice your argument proves too much. If there are infinitely many duplicates of me deciding to donate in the worlds where I donate, there are infinitely many duplicates deciding not to in the worlds where I don't. Aleph-null either way. The duplicate move can't tell those two worlds apart any better than your cardinality move could, which I think is the general lesson here: whenever a quantity comes out infinite on both branches, that isn't a deep fact about the universe, it's a sign you're doing arithmetic with the wrong object.

**Move 7 (N):** Fine — but then you've conceded the thing you opened by denying. You can't ground "my donation adds good" in any total, because the totals are unusable on both our accounts: either aleph-null on both sides or no limit at all. What's left is something much more local and much weaker than what you set out to defend. Not "you made the universe better" but "you made this corner of it better, and corner-making is the only thing there is." I can mostly live with that, but you should say it out loud, because it means the grand totalising version of consequentialism that motivated the whole project is dead, and what you're left holding is a bounded localism that looks a lot like common-sense morality with extra steps.

**Move 8 (R):** I'll take that deal, but notice you've moved the target. You started by claiming my donation makes no difference. You're ending by claiming it makes a difference that sounds less grand than I advertised. Those are completely different claims, and I only ever needed the second one.

Then the thread died.
