I spent two hours last night arguing with a friend — a historian of economic thought, formerly of my reading group, now I think just stubborn — about whether Ramsey refuted Keynes on probability, and we did not advance one inch. I would like a diagnosis of that impasse rather than a verdict on it, and I am asking you to play the part of a professional philosopher who works at the intersection of formal epistemology and the history of economic thought: someone who can read both the *Treatise on Probability* and a representation theorem without flinching.

The dispute, as I will try to state it neutrally. Keynes's 1921 *Treatise* treats probability as a logical relation of partial implication between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, held to be objective, not always numerically measurable, and not always even comparable; and alongside the probability of an argument he posits a second feature, its weight, which turns on the amount of relevant evidence rather than its balance. His own summary is that "New evidence will sometimes decrease the probability of an argument, but it will always increase its 'weight.'" Ramsey attacks this, in his 1922 Cambridge Magazine review and then much more thoroughly in the paper on truth and probability written in 1926 and published posthumously in 1931, by insisting that degree of belief is a dispositional matter — something that shows up in how an agent would choose — and by showing how, given a supply of ethically neutral propositions of probability one-half, both the probabilities and the utilities underwriting an agent's choices can be recovered from the pattern of those choices alone.

The two camps, as my friend and I each caricature the other. His camp says: Keynes's objective probability relations are either idle (they do no explanatory work that the agent's own degrees of belief cannot do) or incoherent (a relation nobody can be said to perceive, which permits incomparability, cannot constrain reasoning, and so is not a logical relation at all); and the Ramsey construction is not merely adequate but uniquely adequate, because it makes probability answerable to something public and testable, namely choice. My camp says: Ramsey's theorem is a representation result, and a representation result shows that a coherent agent can be described numerically, not that real inference is numerical, and certainly not that evidential weight is an illusion; and it says that Keynes's insistence that "Is our expectation of rain, when we start out for a walk, always more likely than not, or less likely than not, or as likely as not?" admits of cases where none of these hold anticipates ambiguity, sets of priors, and imprecise probability, all of which the Ramsey camp is then obliged to dismiss as second-order vagueness rather than engage with.

Three further things kept derailing us, and I want them treated as part of the object of diagnosis, not as distractions.

First, my own hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as something I am confident of: that we are not really disagreeing about whether objective logical probability relations exist, because neither of us thinks that question is decidable on the evidence we have, but are answering two different questions under one heading — one question about the formal coherence and representability of non-numerical probability, the other about whether any decision-theoretic framework can express the difference between a probability resting on a thin body of evidence and the same probability resting on a thick one. If that is right, the argument is unwinnable by construction.

Second, a distinction I improvised at about the one-hour mark, which my friend immediately said was relabelling. Call it the two-keys reading: probability and weight are irreducibly distinct dimensions of an epistemic state, so that no single ordering of anything — not of worlds, not of priors, not of options — can carry both. Call it the one-key reading: weight is a second-order feature of a probability model, so that a suitably rich model (a family of probability functions rather than one, say, or a model with a rule for updating the family) carries both features in a single structure, and Keynes's error was only to suppose they needed separate theoretical treatment. I want you to tell me whether that distinction is load-bearing or a verbal manoeuvre, and which of the two camps is actually committed to which reading — including whether the Keynes camp can hold the one-key reading without giving up the claim that makes it Keynesian.

Third, the exegesis question that kept resurfacing. My friend holds that the later Keynes — the chapter of the *General Theory* on the state of long-term expectation, with its account of how investors in practice do not calculate but rely on convention, sentiment, and instinctive optimism, and the 1937 restatement insisting that for some questions, such as the prospect of a European war, there is no scientific basis on which any calculable probability can be formed — had abandoned the *Treatise* in all but name, having been moved by Ramsey. I hold that this later material is an application of the *Treatise*, not a departure from it: that what looks like irrational behaviour under uncertainty is exactly what you would expect from an agent whose probability relations are non-numerical and whose weight is low. Notice that each of us needs our own version of this exegesis in order to win the first argument, which makes me suspect the third disagreement is doing hidden work in the first two.

What I want from you, therefore, is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not an adjudication of it. Work through the candidate accounts explicitly: is it a verbal dispute over the word 'probability'; a substantive metaphysical dispute about what sort of thing probability is; a methodological dispute about standards of adequacy — specifically about whether decision-theoretic representability is a criterion an account of belief must meet, which is a claim about the philosophy of science rather than about probability; two questions being answered under a single name; a hidden formal question, such that a theorem either way would close it; or a genuinely ill-posed question that has been kept alive by the fact that both sides have respectable things to say about different aspects of it. Begin with a short brainstorming section in which you list the candidate diagnoses and note, for each, what would have to be true of the dispute for that diagnosis to be correct — I would rather see you generate and prune than see you commit early. Then argue for one, or for a weighted combination, and actively argue against at least two, including against mine if mine is wrong. Support the diagnosis with what each side actually says and does: the way the Keynes camp responds to ambiguity-aversion results, the way the Ramsey camp treats non-comparability, and the fact that both sides happily concede large parts of the other's technical apparatus.

Finally, tell me what would count as progress given the diagnosis you settle on. Be concrete: is it a distinction that has not yet been drawn, a formal result or impossibility theorem of a particular shape, a change of question, an abandonment of one of the two camps' auxiliary commitments, or an experimental or behavioural finding about how people actually revise under thin versus thick evidence? If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, say so and give me the reason, and tell me whether the debate is worth having anyway and for what purpose.

Two constraints on how you write this. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I cannot check them and a guess is worse than a paraphrase, so where you are reconstructing a position, reconstruct it in your own words and flag it as a reconstruction rather than a report of a text. And please separate, throughout, what is settled among people who actually work on this from what remains genuinely contested: if there is no consensus about, say, whether imprecise probability vindicates or merely relocates Keynes's worry about weight, then say plainly that there is no consensus and describe the shape of the disagreement, rather than presenting one side as the settled view. Where you are unsure, say so, and where you think my own hypothesis or my two-keys/one-key distinction is confused rather than merely false, say that too — I am not looking for support. Please write the whole thing in British English, with clear headings, and aim for something I could hand to my friend without either of us feeling that the other was being quietly written out of the argument.
