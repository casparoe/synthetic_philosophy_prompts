My dissertation advisor retires at the end of next month and the department stuck me with the toast at the dinner. Basically his whole career went into defending reductionism about testimony -- the view that testimony itself can never be part of your reason for believing what you're told, the credit has to come from somewhere else, from perception and memory and induction. His signature classroom example was the unsigned postcard: a card arrives saying your sister has been taken to hospital, no return address, no postmark, nothing -- and then he'd ask what exactly you're relying on when you get in the car and drive there. Everyone in the philosophy half of the room has heard that postcard at least four times.

The other half of the room is his wife, three kids, a brother who frames houses for a living, and something like nine grandchildren, most of them under twelve.

Here's the constraint I want to build the thing around, because I think its the only honest way to toast him. On his own view, everything he ever told us about himself -- where he grew up, why he went into philosophy, how he felt about his own work -- doesn't actually count as evidence about him. So the speech should be built out of what the third-person side can corroborate: what hundreds of students witnessed in the seminar room, the marginalia in the library's copies of his book, forty years of answering student email within a day, never once cancelling office hours.

Write me a five-minute toast that obeys that rule strictly for most of its length, lands with both halves of the room, and then right at the end has to deal with the one thing nobody in that room can corroborate: that he told each of his students, separately, in different years, that they were the best thing that ever happened to his career. I dont want the ending to break the rule as a cheap sentimental trick -- it has to earn it, and I'd rather it be a little funny going in. And keep the jargon out of anything the family has to sit through: if the word reductionism shows up at all, the eleven-year-olds need to survive it.
