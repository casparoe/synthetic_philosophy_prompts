So I've been going back and forth on the Malcolm/Dennett fight about whether dreams are experiences, and to me it looks like the paradigm case of a debate that neither side can lose. Malcolm's line, as I understand it, is that there's no fact about what happened in your head at 3am over and above what you sincerely say when you wake up — the dream concept is welded to the waking report, so asking whether the dream "occurred during sleep" is a confused question rather than an open one. Dennett's reply is basically that Malcolm has smuggled in an empirical claim his ordinary-language method isn't licensed to deliver, and that once you start imagining the report being triggered at the moment of awakening instead of recalled from something earlier, the whole thing turns into a question about how reports get produced — which is a job for science.

All these decades later people are still publishing papers in which each side says the other is changing the subject, and nobody moves.

My hypothesis, which I want you to actually stress-test rather than nod along with: this isn't one disagreement. It's at least two tangled together — a verbal one about what "experience" has to be for something to count as one, and a hidden empirical one about confabulation that neither Malcolm nor Dennett could run an experiment on. And the reason it never resolves is that each side treats the other's question as the wrong question, so every new piece of evidence gets absorbed instead of biting.

Here's the wrinkle I don't think either camp handles, and it comes from my own crappy dataset. I kept a voice-memo dream journal for about a year, recording within ten seconds of waking, roughly eighty entries. Almost none of them have any sense of elapsed time in them. No durations, no felt sequence, just a static scene or a compressed plot with no felt lenght. When I try to recall those now I genuinely can't tell whether I'm remembering something or rebuilding it. But a handful of entries feel completely different: they have duration, I have a rough sense of how long they took, and that "this was live" feeling survives for weeks.

So: is that split data for one side, data for neither, or data that both sides will happily eat? The Dennett-adjacent people say the duration-y ones were the real experiences and the timeless ones were confabulation — which conveniently means the reports that would count against the theory get reclassified as non-experiences rather than as evidence. The Malcolm-adjacent people say the felt difference is exactly the kind of first-person datum his account was built to render irrelevant. Both moves look suspicious to me and I can't tell if they're the same suspicious move twice.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict and definitely not a survey of the literature on dreaming. Specifically:

1. Is there a substantive disagreement under here at all, or is the whole thing a fight about "experience" (and maybe about "occurs")? If it's verbal, spell out what each side would have to mean by the word for the dispute to still be worth having, and whether there's any residual non-verbal core left over after you've done that.

2. Is there a hidden empirical question doing the load-bearing work? If yes, state it precisely enough that a sleep lab could design around it — what measurement would distinguish "had an experience and remembers it" from "generates the report at the moment of waking"? And is that measurement actually possible, or does the Orwellian/Stalinesque problem — memory getting rewritten afterward versus the experience itself being written late — mean the two are permanently indistinguishable from the inside, and maybe from the outside too?

3. Given whatever you land on, what would count as progress here? A distinction, an experimental paradigm, a change of question, or an argument that it should be abandoned as ill-posed — and if ill-posed, name the assumption that breaks it.

No bibliography, I'm not looking anything up, and please don't hedge by laying out five readings and refusing to pick. Take a position about the structure of the disagreement and defend it. And you're welcome to tell me my journal data is worthless, but tell me why it's worthless rather than just that it's anecdotal.
