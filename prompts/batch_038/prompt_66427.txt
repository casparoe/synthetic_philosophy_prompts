I'm putting together the fourth problem set for a graduate reading group on epistemic game theory, and I've hit a pedagogical wall I'd like your help engineering around. Over the first three weeks we worked through announcement puzzles and the standard results about what agents can learn from one another's beliefs, and my students have come away with the impression that "common knowledge" is just a florid way of saying "everyone knows, and everyone knows that everyone knows, and so on" — which is, of course, true, but which has led them to conclude that the "and so on" is decorative. It isn't, and I want a case that proves it by construction rather than by assertion. So: design me a thought experiment in which the two players are perfectly rational, in which each of them knows the rules of the game and the payoffs, in which each knows that the other is rational, in which knowledge of rationality is mutual to some specific finite depth *d* that you choose and state, in which it is **not** common knowledge, and in which — as a direct consequence of that truncation and nothing else — rational play permits a course of action that is strictly better for both players than the outcome that common knowledge of rationality would force on them. The underlying structure should be centipede-like: a sequence of decisions at which each player can either grab a larger immediate share or pass and let the joint pie grow, with the pie eventually collapsing if nobody grabs. Give me at least six decision nodes and explicit payoffs at every one of them, so the arithmetic is checkable. The fiction should be an ordinary setting — a staged negotiation between two firms, a sequential fee dispute between a hospital and an insurer, an escalating series of committee votes, that sort of thing — not an abstract game tree and not an actual centipede; but you must supply the tree alongside the story, and the story must motivate in-world why the hierarchy of beliefs about rationality stops exactly where you say it does, without anyone holding a false belief and without anyone being unsure whether the other is rational at all.

Please deliver four things. First, the scenario itself in a single paragraph of prose, followed by a compact listing of the nodes and payoffs. Second, the depth analysis: state *d* explicitly, show that mutual knowledge of rationality genuinely holds at every depth up to *d* and genuinely fails at *d*+1, and identify the last node at which taking is still forced by the hierarchy you've granted, so I can see how much of the cooperative surplus your construction actually recovers relative to the common-knowledge outcome. Third, a consistency check on the hierarchy: verify that nothing you have granted at depth ≤ *d*, taken together with the players' knowledge of the rules and of each other's situation, entails the depth *d*+1 belief you are withholding — if some such entailment exists, the case quietly collapses back into the common-knowledge case, and I would much rather hear that from you than discover it myself. Fourth, an honest post-mortem. Tell me which single feature of your construction is doing the work, tell me what happens to the permissive outcome if I grant one more level of knowledge (does it survive at *d*+1? at what depth does it die outright?), and then give me the strongest available reply from someone who thinks backward induction never needed anything stronger than a sufficiently deep finite hierarchy — someone who will insist that a truncated hierarchy is just an incomplete-information game in disguise, with types indexed to reasoning depth, and that once you write your case down in extensive form with a chance move over those types you have reintroduced exactly the payoff-or-rationality uncertainty you claimed to have excluded. That reply is, for what it's worth, in the same family as the reputation account of cooperation in finitely repeated prisoners' dilemmas due to Kreps, Milgrom, Roberts, and Wilson. Say whether you think it succeeds against your case and why.

To fix what counts as cheating: the point of the exercise is to show that the move from mutual knowledge to common knowledge can change what rational play permits, so a construction that merely stipulates that the players cooperate, that produces cooperation by making either player uncertain about the other's payoffs or about the length of the game, that produces it by giving either player any probability mass on the other being irrational or a committed reciprocator, or that produces it by letting anyone believe something false, is not what I'm after. If you think my specification is jointly unsatisfiable as written, don't quietly weaken it — tell me which constraint is responsible, say what would have to give, and then build the nearest case you can.
