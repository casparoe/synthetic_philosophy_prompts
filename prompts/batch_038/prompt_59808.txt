For about twenty years I have read forecasting blogs, prediction-market postmortems, and the comment threads that reliably appear every decennial anniversary of some famous failed prophecy, and I have come to think the arguments never terminate because nobody has specified what a successful long-horizon forecast would look like in the first place. So before I read one more defense of Malthus, or one more retrospective scoring of the 1972 Club of Rome simulation, I want the specification rather than the contenders.

Here is the job the specification has to be good for. Imagine a standing public ledger that accepts long-horizon claims about civilizational variables — food supply, resource scarcity, demographic transition, technological substitution, sea level — and rules on them. The ledger must say, in real time and in language a non-specialist can check, whether a claim has been confirmed, disconfirmed, or merely not yet due; and it must also support a second, harder verdict about whether acting on the claim before the ruling was warranted. Judge every requirement you propose against that purpose and not against the purpose of settling Malthus's reputation.

The phenomena that most need handling are the ones where the ordinary vocabulary breaks down. A claim made in 1798 that population grows geometrically while subsistence grows arithmetically had its stated mechanism badly damaged within a century by yield increases, and yet its qualitative pressure claim keeps getting vindicated locally and, arguably, globally later. A famous ten-year wager struck in 1980 on the inflation-adjusted prices of five metals was won by the optimist when all five fell — and yet the loser's theory is routinely defended as directionally correct, the winner's proxy (price as a measure of scarcity) is attacked as invalid, and replaying the identical wager across other decades reverses the result often enough that the outcome looks like an artifact of the window chosen. A computer simulation published in 1972 puts its overshoot and collapse in the middle of the present century, so it is currently unscoreable and will remain so for a while. And a whole class of forecasts concerns preventable catastrophes, where the only thing that can falsify them is also the thing that makes people act, so the avoided disaster leaves no trace to be scored against.

Write the desiderata: the requirements any account of when a long-horizon forecast has been empirically tested must satisfy to run that ledger, with the case or consideration behind each. Mark which are non-negotiable and which are the local preferences of camps — strict falsificationism, mandatory quantification and calibration, mechanism realism, the demand that any auxiliary hypothesis be independently novel. Then tell me whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable given the structure of long-horizon prediction; that is the part I most want, because if the ledger is impossible in principle I would rather know it now. Also identify the single requirement that excludes the most candidate accounts.

Several specific problems I would like the list to take a position on rather than glide past. Whether displacing a deadline is an auxiliary hypothesis of a different and worse kind than refining a mechanism — my intuition says extending the date is more evasive than changing the model, and I cannot say why, so either give me the reason or overturn the intuition. Whether an account must require a forecast to beat a null forecaster, since over fifty years a persistence or random-walk baseline gets a great deal right for free. Whether the operationalization of the target variable must be validated independently of the outcome, given that the metals wager was contested on exactly this point after the fact. Whether the account needs a clause on constructing a reference class when there is only one Earth history and no replication, or whether it may legitimately lean on within-history subsamples such as other countries, other resources, other epochs. And whether a claim that must come true eventually for arithmetical or thermodynamic reasons — finite stocks, unbounded growth — has any content at all, or is true but empty, and how the ledger should treat it.

Finally, test your list on two disputes I have made up so that nothing hinges on knowing the real literature. In the first, an agronomist in 1908 models a river delta becoming agriculturally unviable by 1960 through irrigation-driven salinization; it does not happen; by 2030 it does, by precisely the mechanism she specified, at roughly a third of her rate. In the second, a 1975 forecast of a global protein shortfall by 2000 fails because of aquaculture and substitution, and the forecaster's successors now say the model was right about demand and wrong only about supply elasticity, having published a version with an explicit elasticity term that fits the data from 1975 to the present. Say what the ledger rules in each case and which of your requirements does the ruling.

After the list, briefly and only briefly, say how the obvious approaches do against it: naive falsificationism, research-programme methodology with its progressive-versus-degenerating distinction, Bayesian scoring by proper rules over time, and a strict pre-registration view under which anything added after the fact is inadmissible. I want to know whether "right but early" can ever be a legitimate verdict rather than a euphemism, and if it can, what has to be true in advance for it to be available. Close by applying your own list to itself: state which of your requirements your account most clearly fails.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists, even though the material is list-shaped. And do not cite anything or append sources; I am not asking for a bibliography and cannot check one, so give me the reasoning and leave the names out.
