I help run a small film series at a community centre: once a month, one film, chosen by a committee of four volunteers. None of us has any training in philosophy. I have read around the subject on my own for years, but the argument below is ours rather than something I lifted from a book, and I am setting it out as carefully as I can because the two people involved have asked me to send it to someone who will go through it coolly.

Owen and Nadia are the committee's two most active programmers. Last month's meeting produced a disagreement that neither has dropped, and neither accepts the other's summary of it, so I have written it down in order and numbered the moves.

(1) Owen proposed a film nobody on the committee had seen, on the strength of one enthusiastic review and two friends' recommendations, and wanted it booked. Nadia objected that you cannot know a film is good because somebody says it is good; she said she would not put her name to a choice she could not defend by pointing at something in the film itself.

(2) Owen answered that her rule proves too much. The committee books a visiting musician on somebody's word, takes a mechanic's report about the van, accepts a doctor's account of what an X-ray shows. In each case we are relying on another person's eyes and judgment rather than our own. If that is not enough for the film, it is not enough for any of them either.

(3) Nadia said there is a difference in kind. What the mechanic reports is a fact about the van that would be there for anyone to check who knew what to look for; whether the film is good is not a fact of that sort, because it depends on the response the film draws out of a viewer. The mechanic's report hands you a fact, whereas the film report at best hands you a forecast about your own reaction.

(4) Owen said a forecast can be knowledge when it is well founded, and his is: for about six years he has kept a record of every film recommended to him by each of a handful of people whose taste he knows, together with whether he ended up enjoying it, so he can say with some confidence that one of those two friends has been right about three films in four for him. On that basis, he said, calling the film good is not a bluff but a supported expectation, and if the expectation comes true he was right.

(5) Nadia said then he should say what he means: I expect you will enjoy it, rather than it is good. Saying it is good invites the question what in it makes it good, and he has nothing to answer with. He would be spending an authority he has not earned.

(6) Owen said everybody spends authority they have not earned, and the practice works. He knows the restaurant across town is good without having eaten there. If Nadia is right, nobody knows anything about any film they have not seen, which would make the whole business of criticism pointless, since critics write for readers who have not seen the film and readers act on what they read.

(7) Nadia said criticism is not pointless, but it does something narrower than he thinks: a review that describes the pacing, the performances, what the ending does and what the film keeps returning to gives a reader something to judge with, and from that the reader can work out whether he will value the film. What a review cannot do is hand over the valuing. Owen's proposal was of the second kind, a verdict with no description attached.

(8) Owen said that if description is what matters, his record of hit rates is better than description, because it is based on results rather than on someone's ability to put into words why they liked something — and plenty of the people whose judgment he trusts are hopeless at explaining themselves but reliable all the same.

Two things happened afterwards, and both have been fed back into the argument.

The film was screened by another group in town, and Owen and Nadia both went. Owen liked it a good deal; Nadia thought it handsome and empty. Owen said this showed his testimony-based judgment had been correct and that seeing the film for herself had done nothing for Nadia's position. Nadia said he had been lucky: he would have been exactly as confident about a film that turned out to be hollow, and being right when you could just as easily have been wrong is not the same as knowing. Owen then pointed out that Nadia had herself programmed a film two seasons ago that she had seen and disliked, on the grounds that it was important and that the audience ought to see it — so she was willing to substitute a judgment of a quite different kind for an aesthetic one, which on her own account sits further from the thing, not closer.

What I want is an adjudication of the exchange as it actually happened, not a general essay on the topic. For each numbered move, say whether it strengthens the position of the person making it, leaves things where they were, or backfires, and say why in a sentence or two. Then tell me who has the better of the dispute overall, and on which points. Two matters I especially want settled: whether Owen's six years of records change the kind of thing he has, that is, whether a well-documented hit rate can turn a bare verdict into something that deserves to be called knowledge; and whether Nadia is entitled to the distinction she draws in move (3) between a fact anyone could check and a judgment that depends on the person judging. Finally, tell me whether there is something one or both of them are reaching for and failing to say, and if there is, say it better than they did.
