Chapter three of my dissertation argues that the rigidity condition in Jeffrey conditionalization is doing far more work than its proponents admit, and I'm presenting it next Thursday to our formal epistemology reading group. I'd like to prepare for the question period.

The thesis, compressed:

(1) Rigidity — the assumption that the experience which shifts P(E_i) leaves P(H|E_i) untouched for every cell of the partition — is not a technical convenience but a substantive claim about perceptual psychology, and it is false in the very examples that motivated probability kinematics. When I look at a swatch in bad light and my confidence that it is green rather than teal moves, the experience that moves those cells also moves what I would say about the dye chemistry conditional on each cell, because the qualitative character of the experience carries information that no partition cell encodes.

(2) The unit of epistemic input is therefore not a posterior over a partition but a constraint on the posterior: the experience licenses something of the form "the expected value of f under my new credences is c," where f is a function of the world rather than of my concepts.

(3) Given such a constraint, the update should be the information projection of the prior onto the constraint set — minimum relative entropy — which recovers Jeffrey's rule as the special case where the constraint pins down a full partition and nothing else, and extends it along the lines Diaconis and Zabell sketched.

(4) Upshots: the input analysis problem was never a problem about picking the right partition; commutativity becomes a question about constraint sets rather than cells; and rigidity violations stop being puzzles and start being expected.

The room is about ten people. Three work on imprecise probability and sets of credence functions and regard precise Bayesianism as a mistake made at the first step; two are philosophers of science working on confirmation and the old-evidence problem; one is a statistician who does variational inference and probabilistic programming and attends for the amusement; one works on the epistemology of perception and is openly hostile to the whole formal enterprise, holding that it misdescribes what seeing is; the remainder are graduate students who broadly sympathize with subjective Bayesianism.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense:

- The eight or so objections I'll actually get, ordered by how much damage each does to the chapter as stated, each attributed to which of these people raises it and phrased in their vocabulary — the imprecise-probability trio will not put it the way the statistician does, and neither will put it the way the perception person does.
- For each objection, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, plus a blunt note on where that reply is soft: which premise I would be assuming that they do not grant.
- What I should concede in the opening ten minutes so that the rest of the room stops trying to score it off me, and the single reply that has to land for the chapter to be taken seriously.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks — the one I presently have no answer to.

Skip the objections that any audience raises at any talk of this kind (what exactly is a credence, isn't this all just Bayes, does the formalism matter). I want the ones specific to this thesis and this room.

Please be direct about which of these objections you take to be merely uncomfortable and which you take to refute the view as stated; I would much rather learn that from you than at the front of the room. Then close by telling me what would change your own assessment: which consideration, if I convinced you of it, would make you reorder the objections or conclude that the constraint-based proposal is unsalvageable rather than under-defended — and where in your reconstruction of my argument you are most likely to have gone wrong.
