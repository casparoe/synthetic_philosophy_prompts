ok so my school has this tiny philosophy club and last week we read some excerpts from Popper's Poverty of Historicism, which honestly half of us only skimmed. afterwards my debate partner Jonah and this other kid Ravi got into a really long argument and I was supposedly the referee but I lost the thread about halfway through and never said who won. can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step, and then who's actually more right overall? here's how it went:

1) Ravi said Popper's point is that there are no laws of historical development, so nobody can predict where history is going, and anyone who claims they can (like the big "end of history" or "communism is inevitable" types) is doing prophecy, not science.

2) Jonah said that's obviously false, because we predict social stuff all the time: economists project growth decades out, demographers project population to 2100, public health people projected infection curves, and they're not clearly worse than long-range weather models. So there ARE social regularities you can extrapolate, and Ravi's position would have to make all of that nonsense.

3) Ravi said those are trends, not laws — a trend only holds if the conditions holding it up stay in place, and it's really a singular statement about a place and time, not a universal one. Plus the projections are openly conditional: if fertility stays around X, then population does Y. That's different from saying history has a destination.

4) Jonah said fine, but then Popper's own argument is self-refuting: Popper claims nobody will ever be able to predict the future growth of knowledge, and that's itself a claim about all future science. If it's true, we DO know something about every future discovery in advance — namely that nobody can know it in advance. So he's pulled off the exact thing he says can't be done.

5) Ravi said that confuses predicting the content of a discovery with predicting that there will be discoveries. Being told you can't guess the next theory doesn't give you the next theory. Jonah came back with: by the same logic a historicist can say knowing the direction isn't the same as knowing the details — you can know a river's slope without knowing every ripple, and that's all historicism ever claimed.

6) Then Ravi brought up the Oedipus effect, that a published prediction about society changes the society it's about, so even a decent prophecy can defeat or fulfil itself, which is another reason social prediction isn't like dropping a ball. Jonah said that backfires, because if prophecies self-destruct when published then you can never catch a historicist being wrong — he'll just say the prophecy failed *because* it got out. Which makes Popper's own anti-prophecy unfalsifiable too, and Popper wrote a whole book complaining about theories that can't be refuted.

7) last move: Jonah said Popper's argument quietly assumes ideas and knowledge are the engine of history, which is basically Marx flipped over, and if you think calories, disease, energy or climate drive history then the key premise is just false and prediction is back on the table. Ravi said that misses the point because even material drivers now run through human decisions informed by knowledge — like a famine being mostly about policy and information, not about rain.
