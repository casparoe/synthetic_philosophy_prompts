Third year, philosophy of psychiatry, and I've just realised I've spent two years arguing about looping effects without ever pinning down what a theory of them is actually supposed to do. So write me the spec: the desiderata any adequate account of the interaction between classifications of people and the people classified has to meet. Hacking's setup, as I take it: indifferent kinds like quarks or granite don't care how we sort them, but kinds of people do, because once you're inside the category you can describe yourself with it, resist it, organise around it — and then the category has to move to keep up with what it helped produce.

Purpose first, so adequacy isn't floating free: I want a theory good enough that a revision committee could use it — something that tells you, of a candidate diagnostic category, whether introducing, renaming, narrowing or retiring it will change who falls under it and what those people are like, and whether that change counts as a cost or a gain. That's deliberately normative, which isn't how Hacking usually gets read; he's descriptive and faintly ironic about the whole business. So part of the job is telling me what extra burden a theory picks up when it's used as a criterion of validity rather than just as a phenomenon to notice.

Now the list, with the case or consideration behind every item, and each one marked as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Cases it has to get right, at minimum: multiple personality, where a category appears, flowers and all but vanishes inside a generation; the 1973 decision on homosexuality in the DSM, where the people in the category organised and changed it from the outside, and where it matters whether there was a fact of the matter before the vote; the current wave of adult self-identification with ADHD and autism, where the loop runs through short-form video rather than through a clinic, so the classifier is a recommendation system and not a doctor; a proposed subtype whose name got into parent forums and the press long before anyone had data on it, and which then reshaped how the teenagers who heard it described themselves; and at least one category that doesn't loop, because the theory has to be able to say why, or else everything is interactive and the thesis is empty. That last one might be the requirement that does the most excluding — tell me if it is.

Distinctions it must not collapse: made-up versus not real; a loop that changes the extension of a category versus a loop that changes its content; a kind that genuinely loops versus one that's merely socially patterned or stigmatised; and self-understanding altering behaviour versus behaviour altering classification. Things it must stay consistent with: the relative stability of some core presentations across cultures and decades; the fact that clinicians can be right about a patient who lived before the label existed; and the fact that the same label loops differently in the clinic, the school, the courtroom and the welfare office.

Then tell me where the list breaks. My suspicion is that strong constitution (the kind is partly made of the classified people's own self-understandings), predictive usefulness for the committee, and retrospective truth about pre-label cases can't all be had together — but if the real unsatisfiable cluster is somewhere else, say so, since that's the bit I most want.

Finish with three or four lines per contender — realist/naturalist kinds talk, strong social constructionism, and quietism about the whole thing — scored against the list only. No reading list, I've got one. And please write the whole thing in British English; my supervisor notices.
