I am a second-year MPhil student in political philosophy, currently taking a module on value pluralism and the theory of social choice. In three weeks I present a work-in-progress paper at my department's graduate seminar — ninety minutes, roughly thirty for the paper and the remainder for discussion. The paper concerns compromise between competing value systems, and in particular the claim that evaluative pluralism generates gains from trade. I would like a preparation sheet for the question period.

Here is the argument I shall be defending, in the form it currently has.

1. Where two agents hold different but internally coherent value systems, and each can affect what the other cares about, there are trades in which each advances the other's values by more than they sacrifice of their own. This is the familiar Pareto-style case for moral trade, and I accept it at the level of the example.
2. But any such trade presupposes a common scale. Each party must be able to state how much of what they care about they are forgoing, in units the other party can weigh against their own concerns. In practice this means a cardinal measure over some shared currency: expected wellbeing, satisfied preference, or monetary value.
3. My central claim is that adopting the scale is not value-neutral. For a good many views, part of what the view asserts is precisely that some of its concerns are not commensurable with the currency in question. To represent those concerns on the shared scale is therefore to represent them falsely. The trade makes each party better off by the scale's lights while making each worse off by their own lights in a respect the scale is structurally incapable of registering. I call this unregistered loss the residue.
4. It follows that the Pareto premise is metric-relative. Trade can be Pareto-improving with respect to the shared metric and yet not Pareto-improving with respect to the parties' own views, all things considered. My contention is that the literature on gains from trade has been presenting a metric-relative improvement as though it were a neutral description of the situation, which is why the gains look costless.
5. As a constructive proposal I offer a dual-ledger rule. Parties trade on the shared cardinal ledger, but each must additionally declare the residue — the respect in which the ledger misdescribes their view — and a trade is admissible only where the declared residue falls below a threshold fixed in advance. The threshold is to be calibrated from each party's own considered judgements about cases in which they would refuse the trade however large the shared gain.

The audience will be about twenty people: two or three faculty and the rest graduate students. I know its composition reasonably well. There is a Kantian value theorist who holds that certain commitments are not fungible and who will be unhappy with my treatment of persons as parties to a trade; a convinced utilitarian with cardinal welfarist commitments who will want to know why the residue cannot simply be entered as a further cost on the shared ledger, at which point my second ledger collapses into the first; a social choice economist who works on aggregation and knows the impossibility results well, and who I suspect will say that my translation between ledgers either does not exist or is covertly dictatorial; a political liberal in the Rawlsian tradition who will press the claim that the shared currency is not a neutral device but a substantive conception of the good doing unacknowledged work; and two or three students doing applied work on the allocation of charitable funds, who will want to know how any of this could actually be operated and what stops the declaration of residue from being strategic.

What I need is a prep sheet rather than a defence. Please give me:

- The eight or nine objections I am most likely to receive from this audience specifically — not the objections a general readership would raise, and not the ones I would get at an interdisciplinary workshop. Order them by how much damage each would do to the paper if it landed unanswered, and for each state who raises it, how they would phrase it in their own idiom and with the technical vocabulary they would naturally reach for, how likely it is to come up at all, a reply I could deliver in about two minutes while standing up, and a candid note on where that reply is soft — the precise step at which a competent objector would press again.
- A separate short section identifying which objections I should neutralise in the opening ten minutes by conceding something, and exactly what to concede, since I would rather give ground early than lose the room later.
- The single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session, together with what I lose if it does not.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks: the one that goes directly at the weakest joint in the argument above, and the least evasive thing I could say if it is put to me.
- For two of the objections, the narrower version of my thesis I could retreat to under pressure, and what each retreat costs me in interest.

Please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations, and do not name particular papers or authors in support of the replies; I want the objections and the answers themselves, and I have the references on my own shelves. Write throughout in British English.

Finish with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by the short label of the objection you judge most dangerous to this paper, and nothing after that line.
