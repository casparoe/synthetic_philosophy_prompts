Wagner read Schopenhauer in the autumn of 1854, and within a few years had produced Tristan und Isolde (composed 1857–59, premiered 1865), which he and most of his interpreters have treated as the musical embodiment of Schopenhauer's metaphysics: unquiet willing that finds release only in death. I want that episode analyzed against Schopenhauer's own doctrine of music, not against the biography of the influence, which I know. Set up the mapping briefly — what Schopenhauer says music is (an immediate objectification of the will itself, where the other arts copy the Ideas), the hierarchy running from the bass as the lowest grades of objectification up through harmony to melody as the highest, and why he thinks music acts on us more directly than any other art — and then put the harder question: would that doctrine endorse Tristan? Run it through a test I'll stipulate, the substitution test: strip out the drama and hear the Prelude and Liebestod as pure instrumental music, as they are commonly performed in concert, and ask whether the Schopenhauerian content Wagner took himself to be expressing survives the removal of the words and the plot, or whether it was those that were carrying the metaphysics all along. Keep two awkward facts in view: Schopenhauer thought music debased itself when it served a text or set about depicting particular objects and passions, and Wagner's own earlier theory of the music drama had made music the means and the drama the end, the reverse of Schopenhauer's ordering. Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the doctrine rather than about Wagner — does his appropriation confirm it, exploit an ambiguity in it, or reveal that it supplies no criteria for ranking particular works and so could neither bless nor refuse Tristan? Where the doctrine is silent, say so plainly instead of making it speak. Prose only, please: no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, just continuous paragraphs of the kind you would write in a letter.
