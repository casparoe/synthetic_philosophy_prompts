I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm working on a piece about digital money — CBDCs, stablecoins, the lot. Nearly every economist I talk to reaches for the same line: Berkeley the bishop already settled this in The Querist, money is nothing but tickets and tokens, it makes no difference what they're made of, their only job is to get people working, so a digital token does the job as well as gold does. It's the move that licenses the conclusion that we should stop fussing about the substrate.

Can you audit that analogy for me rather than just repeating it? Tell me what maps onto what — chip, table, game, the house that issues the chips — where the mapping starts to break, and what the token picture quietly imports that money doesn't have (a fixed pile of chips, a game you can step out of, a house that cant go under). I also want to know whether Berkeley himself would sign off on the conclusion these people draw from him, seeing as he was writing about a place with hardly any money in circulation rather than too much, and whether scarcity was load-bearing for his tokens. And if the chip/ticket analogy is carrying more weight than it can hold, is there a better one I could put in the piece instead — one that keeps the useful part and drops the rest.
