Last night I spent four hours arguing with a close friend about the paradox of deontology, and neither of us has prevailed, so I have resolved to continue the correspondence in a lighter register: I want a joke. Let me set out the shape of the disagreement, because the joke will fail unless it is built from that material rather than from stock philosophy-department humour.

The proposition under dispute is the familiar one. An agent-relative constraint of the form "do not lie" forbids me from lying even in a case where my single lie would, as a matter of fact, abolish lying altogether. To sharpen the point, my friend invented an apparatus we spent most of the evening calling the Compliance Engine: speak one falsehood into its mouthpiece and it renders every human being thereafter physically incapable of falsehood. The option set is therefore "one lie, authored by me, followed by a world without lies" versus "no lie from me, and a world of unending lies authored by others." His charge is that the deontologist who declines the Engine is not tracking anything in the world at all; he is merely keeping his own ledger clean at everyone else's expense, which makes the view a species of consequentialism whose utility function counts one agent's acts, plus a veto.

My reply, which he regards as a dodge, is that the Engine's billions of subsequent lies would be caused by me but not performed by me, and so are not mine to weigh against my own act: the constraint does not rank states of the world but restricts what I may do with my hands. He calls this agent-relative bookkeeping, and maintains that naming the problem is not solving it.

What I would like from you is a single joke of no more than 120 words whose punchline turns on the asymmetry between these two positions — specifically on what the deontologist is committed to saying about a world in which lying has been abolished by someone else's lie, or about who owns the lies that result. It should land for someone who has actually run this argument, not for someone who merely recognises the vocabulary: no puns on the terminology of the categorical imperative, and no two-philosophers-walk-into-a-bar frame with arbitrary content poured into it. It must also be fair. Neither position may be reduced to a straw man, and my friend should be able to forward it unchanged; ideally it should sting me at least as much as it stings him.

After the joke, add one sentence of no more than thirty words identifying the precise step in the argument that the punchline exploits. Nothing else — no title, no preface, no alternate versions — and keep the whole response within 150 words.
