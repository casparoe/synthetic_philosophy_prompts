The task was a 600-word problem-set answer on this question: "Schlick argues that work cannot give life a meaning but play can. Explain his argument, and assess whether his notion of play can carry the weight he puts on it." Below is the answer I'm currently on. I'd rather not tell you what I make of it — I've read twenty of these in a row and I'm pretty sure I've gone numb to the confident-sounding ones, so I want you to come at it fresh.

> Schlick begins from a pessimistic mood: the sense that when you ask what all the effort is for, nothing answers back. His diagnosis is that people look for meaning in the wrong place, namely work. Work is activity valued for its results rather than for itself, and most of those results go towards keeping us alive so we can work again, so the search for meaning in work runs in a circle. Nor can it be found in work's products, since these are largely consumed by further work, nor in some purpose beyond our own lives — God, the progress of the species, future generations — because that only pushes the question back a step, each purpose becoming the means to another. Schlick then reverses the ordinary hierarchy. We treat play as what children do, an apprenticeship for the serious business of adulthood, and work as the mature and valuable thing. He says the opposite: play is activity whose value lies in the doing of it, which carries its purpose in itself, and only something of that kind could give life a meaning, since whatever is valued as a means is valued only for the sake of something else.
>
> Because Schlick was the founding figure of the Vienna Circle it is tempting to think he would dismiss the question of life's meaning as a pseudo-problem, a piece of metaphysics to be dissolved by logical analysis. In fact that is roughly what he does here: the essay shows that the question, properly understood, is not asking for a fact about the world, so what he offers is a clarification of what we are already doing when we value things rather than a substantive claim about them.
>
> The other key idea is youthfulness, which Schlick does not mean biologically but as a disposition — a capacity to take things up for their own sake that adults mostly lose under the pressure of necessity. His model is the artist, whose activity looks like labour from outside and is play from inside, and he insists that work itself can become play when it is done for the satisfaction of doing it.
>
> Assessment: the view is attractive but I don't think it holds. First, Schlick is a hedonist elsewhere and it shows here — play is supposed to be the meaningful thing because it delivers pleasure instead of being a means to it, so 'play' is just a label for whatever produces the most pleasant experience. But then the work/play distinction does no work of its own: a tedious job would count as play so long as the pay is pleasant enough, and the argument collapses into a claim about which experiences feel best. Second, if play is what carries its value in itself, then only people with the leisure or the talent to turn their activity into art can have meaningful lives, which is a conclusion Schlick clearly doesn't want and never argues for. It is worth remembering that he was shot dead by a disturbed former student, and the essay reads like someone holding onto a bright view of life against the pessimism of his moment — but optimism is not an argument.
