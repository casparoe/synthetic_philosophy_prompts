I co-run a small reading group here — about half philosophy grad students, half people from the econ department who do mechanism design — and for two whole terms we've been stuck on Aumann's agreement theorem, and I've started to worry we're just burning our Wednesday evenings. Everyone knows the set-up: the 1976 result says two Bayesian agents with a common prior who have common knowledge of each other's posterior beliefs can't actually disagree, so "let's just agree to disagree" is not an option for ideally rational agents. The theorem is like six lines long and nobody in the group disputes the math. What we can't settle is what it's *for*.

One camp — call them the normativists, mostly the econ people plus two of our epistemologists — says the theorem has genuine bite. Their line: disagreement between people who are roughly rational and roughly sharing their evidence is evidence of malfunction somewhere, and the theorem tells you which malfunction to look for (bias, motivated reasoning, evidence you never actually pooled, or a failure of common knowledge that further conversation would fix). They lean on the follow-up result by Geanakoplos and Polemarchakis, where agents who just announce their posteriors back and forth converge in finitely many steps under common priors, because that seems to answer the "but nobody has infinite iterated knowledge" objection: you don't need it in advance, talking manufactures it. They also point out that if you weaken common knowledge to near-common knowledge, the posteriors get correspondingly close, which they read as showing the idealisation is a matter of degree rather than a cliff. The other camp — the deflationists, mostly philosophy — says all of this is a category error. Their line: the theorem is a statement about a class of models, not about people. Real agents don't have common priors, and Morris's 1995 paper on the common prior assumption is basically an argument that economics never justified the assumption in the first place and just adopted it for convenience; a prior is supposed to encode what you'd believe before evidence, but in practice it smuggles in a whole conceptual scheme, and there's no neutral way to compare two people's priors. They also say the state space is a modelling choice, so two people who "disagree" may not be assigning probabilities to the same proposition at all, which no theorem can reach. And they observe that real conversation frequently produces polarisation rather than convergence, so the Geanakoplos–Polemarchakis result is not evidence for anything.

Nobody has moved an inch, and our shared doc of arguments looks exactly the same as it did in October. My working hypothesis — which I want you to attack, not endorse — is that this isn't a substantive disagreement about epistemology at all but a fight about the word "rational": one camp reads the hypotheses of the theorem as a *standard* that agents fall short of and should be criticised for falling short of, the other reads them as a *description* of which models the theorem ranges over, and on the second reading there's simply nothing for the first camp to be wrong about. A secondary hunch is that the real fault line is about priors — whether a prior is itself assessable as evidence-sensitive or is the thing against which evidence gets assessed — and that the "common knowledge" business is mostly smoke. Tell me whether that's right, or whether I'm mis-describing it: is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work here (something testable, or something a new theorem could settle), are the two camps answering different questions under one heading, or is the impasse a sign that "does the theorem have normative force" is just a badly posed question? I want a diagnosis with reasons, drawn from what each side actually says, not a literature survey — and please don't give me any citations or bibliography, I'm not looking up anything and it won't help.
