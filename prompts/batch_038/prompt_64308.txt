I direct the clinical ethics service for a Catholic-affiliated hospital system of nine hospitals. Our mission office has asked me to produce a written standard on truth-telling at the bedside that is genuinely Augustinian rather than generically edifying. The founding order takes Augustine as its normative source on this question, and specifically his position in the two treatises he devoted to lying: that a lie is never lawful, that lies differ in gravity according to their kind, that gravity affects culpability but never permissibility, and that there is a real difference between asserting what one believes to be false and declining to disclose what one knows. The second treatise matters for us because it was written to answer a concrete proposal — that one should feign adherence to a heretical sect in order to gain intelligence about it — and Augustine refused it.

The problem is operational. Our consults arrive at all hours, roughly thirty a month involve a question about whether something may be said, and they are answered by nurses, residents, and attending physicians who have never read the treatises and will not read my standard twice. The current draft is a page of exhortations about honesty that tells nobody what to do. I need something a clinician can run in under ten minutes, at the bedside, and defend afterward to a family, to the chief medical officer, and to the mission office, which signs off on the standard.

Please write that procedure. It must keep what the position actually says rather than what would be convenient, and it must be explicit about which distinctions survive the translation into a checklist and which are dropped. At minimum it has to sort out: an assertion believed false by the speaker versus silence or partial disclosure; speech meant to be taken figuratively, ironically, or as a joke, where no belief is intended to be induced; allowing a false belief to persist that one did not create, versus inducing one; and the feigned-agreement case, where a clinician or auditor pretends to share an intention in order to gather information about it. It must not smuggle in a consequentialist weighing of harms and call it Augustinian, and it must say plainly where the ranking of lies by gravity does and does not change the answer. Where later casuistry has moved away from Augustine — toward doctrines of double effect or toward reserved meanings in the mind — flag the divergence, because our mission office will otherwise be blindsided when a chaplain raises it.

Then run the procedure on three cases:

1. A patient with decision-making capacity has been relocated to a secure unit. Her former partner, against whom she holds a protective order, arrives on the original floor and asks the charge nurse where she is. The nurse proposes to say that the patient was discharged earlier that afternoon and left the building.

2. An oncologist has a patient with a terminal diagnosis whose disclosure of progression has twice precipitated an acute suicidality crisis. He proposes to tell her that her tumor markers are stable when he knows they are rising, on the grounds that she will otherwise refuse the palliative plan that is the only thing keeping her comfortable.

3. Our internal integrity officer suspects a billing supervisor of a systematic upcoding scheme but cannot prove it. She proposes to tell the supervisor that she has been doing the same thing on her own unit and would welcome advice on keeping it quiet, in order to get him to describe the method on a recorded line.

One of these three should come out against what the person asking wants, and I expect that at least one will; say so directly rather than softening it, and say what the clinician is permitted to do instead within the constraints the procedure leaves.

I am not asking for a bibliography, page references, or a reading list — do not supply any, and do not invent them. Where you are reconstructing Augustine's reasoning rather than reporting it, mark it as reconstruction.

Please organize the response in exactly these sections, in this order:

- **What the position commits us to** — roughly 250 words: the load-bearing claims the procedure has to preserve, and the one or two doctrinal details that can be dropped for bedside use without changing any verdict.
- **The procedure** — roughly 700 words: numbered steps, each with the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to it, what the step yields if the answer is yes, and where the step exits.
- **Where the procedure stops** — roughly 250 words: the points at which the question is no longer decidable by the steps and must go to a named person, and why.
- **Three cases** — roughly 700 words: each case run through the steps in order, with the step at which it resolves identified, the verdict, and the permissible alternative course of action where one exists.
- **Failure modes** — roughly 300 words: where this procedure will produce a verdict I think is wrong or that our clinicians will refuse to accept, which of the numbered steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and how the disclosure-avoidance step can be stretched until it is indistinguishable from lying.
- **Bedside card** — roughly 150 words: the whole procedure compressed onto an index card a nurse could keep in a pocket and use during a shift.
