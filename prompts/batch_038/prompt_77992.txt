I cover neuroscience and the philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the past year reporting on pain — on analgesia, on the clinical syndromes in which pain comes apart from suffering, and on the animal-welfare arguments built out of them. That reporting has moved me to a position I intend to assert in print, and before I do, I want you to map its exposure. Write as a philosopher of mind who is also genuinely conversant with the clinical neurology of pain. Think the case through carefully first — sketch the rival accounts, brainstorm the defeaters, stress-test the clinical data — but keep all of that working-out out of the final answer; I want the finished analysis only.

The position: pain asymbolia shows that pain's badness is a separable phenomenal component rather than a feature of the sensory quality itself. In asymbolia — classically after damage in and around the left insula and the adjacent parietal operculum — patients localize a noxious stimulus, grade its intensity, may even volunteer that it is pain, yet display no distress, no avoidance, and say it does not bother them. My reading is that the affective channel is where the badness lives and the sensory channel merely locates and measures. I take that to carry two further claims I also want to assert: that an intervention which abolishes the affect while leaving sensation untouched is genuine and complete relief of suffering, and that an asymbolic patient, so far as the pain goes, is not harmed.

Tell me what would have to be true for this to be false. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say what the world would have to look like: which patterns of patient report, which lesion profiles, which behavioral or pharmacological dissociations, which consequences the view is committed to that a defender cannot swallow. Work through at least these specifically: the reports of patients who say the pain is unpleasant but does not bother them; the possibility that the same lesions damage introspection and speech as well as affect, so that the reports are not a window on experience at all; the rival reading on which what is lost is salience or attentional capture rather than badness; congenital insensitivity to pain, where the dissociation runs the other way; and the worry that the phenomenal-component view collapses into a motivational or evaluative account, leaving the intrinsic badness doing no explanatory work.

Then tell me what my view predicts that its rivals — the imperative account of unpleasantness, the evaluative account, and the view that unpleasantness is one indivisible dimension — predict differently, and whether those predictions are actually testable given that our only instruments are self-report, behavior, and imaging, all three of which admit of the same redescription. I am especially worried that the position has been qualified into safety: a patient who says the pain is awful but who does not care can be redescribed as intact affect plus a motivational deficit, and a patient who says nothing bothers them can be described as having lost the affect. Is that a live escape hatch or a principled one? Is there any observation that would genuinely break the model? Say also which objections a committed defender of the two-channel picture would themselves concede as landing and which they would swat away, and whether that line is drawn on principle or on preference.

For what it is worth, I would find it most persuasive to be shown a case where the sensory and affective channels are dissociated in a way my account cannot accommodate without ad-hoc machinery, and I would find pure armchair appeals to the inconceivability of unfelt-but-unpleasant pain unpersuasive; tell me if I have that backwards.

I want a committed answer, not a survey: take a definite view on whether this position survives its best defeaters, and on whether its insulation is a vice for a claim of this kind, and give me your reasons. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — your verdict — and only then the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my question, no closing summary of what you have covered.
