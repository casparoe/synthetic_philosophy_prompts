I am a junior, and I help run a game theory club at my school that meets once a week; most of us work through material somewhat past what the curriculum offers. Last Thursday two of the older members, Julian and Marta, got into an argument in the middle of a problem session, and the rest of us could not decide who had won. They have asked me to write the exchange up and send it to someone who can referee it properly. I have cleaned up the wording so that both sides come out as clearly as I can make them, but the arguments are theirs, and I have tried not to tilt the reconstruction toward either one.

We were working with the following two-player game. The row player has three strategies, A, B, and C; the column player has two, X and Y. The row player's payoffs are: A yields 3 if the column player chooses X and 0 if she chooses Y; B yields 0 against X and 3 against Y; C yields 1 against either. We were not yet concerning ourselves with the column player's payoffs.

(1) Julian said C should be deleted as strictly dominated, because the fifty-fifty mixture of A and B yields 1.5 against X and 1.5 against Y, beating C's 1 in both cases. Marta objected that C is not beaten by anything a player could actually decide to do — A loses to C if Y is played, B loses to C if X is played — and that a lottery over A and B is not a strategy in the sense that matters, since nobody selects a lottery in the way one selects a row. Julian replied that a mixed strategy is a strategy by definition, that the mixed extension is part of the game, and that domination is defined over it.

(2) Marta said that the only reason anyone deletes a dominated strategy is that no rational player would play it, and that a player has reason to randomize solely insofar as she wants to keep her opponent from predicting her; if the opponent will know her choice anyway, or if she herself knows her choice before play begins, the mixture is not genuinely on offer to her. So deleting C, on her account, is not a claim about the row player's rationality at all but a claim about the column player's uncertainty, and Julian was passing off an assumption about one player as a fact about the other. Julian answered that the comparison is state-by-state: the mixture beats C against X and it beats C against Y, so there is no way the game could turn out in which someone who chose C would not have done better by mixing, and nothing about anyone's beliefs enters the argument.

(3) Marta said that "would have done better" was where the smuggling happened. Better according to what evaluation? Weighting the two states equally already assumes the player values the mixture in proportion to the probabilities, and that assumption is what is in dispute. A player who cares about her worst case, or who values receiving 1 with certainty more than a fifty-fifty chance between 3 and 0, is not thereby irrational, and for such a player C is not dominated. Julian replied that once you allow preferences that are non-linear in probabilities you lose strict domination by pure strategies as well, since a player who cares only about her worst case might prefer a strategy that is beaten in every single cell — so Marta's objection proves far too much and destroys the very tool he was using.

(4) Marta then shifted ground slightly. She said that if the expected-utility comparison is what licenses the deletion, it licenses deleting weakly dominated strategies too, since the same calculation yields "at least as good in every state and better in some," and Julian therefore owed an account of why strictness enjoys any special status. Julian said strictness does enjoy special status, because a weakly dominated strategy can still be a best response and can still figure in a Nash equilibrium, so weak deletion can destroy equilibria; and because, unlike the strict case, the set that survives weak deletion depends on the order in which the deletions are performed. He produced a small two-by-two game in which deleting a row first leaves a single outcome while deleting a column first leaves two. Marta said that order-dependence is a defect in the procedure rather than evidence for the intrinsic value of strictness: if each individual deletion were genuinely licensed by a fact about a player's own rationality, performing them in a different order could not possibly change the result, so the order-dependence shows that Julian's account of what justifies each step is wrong.

(5) Julian said the dispute was academic in any case, because iterated deletion is what solves the games people actually care about, and he pointed to the guessing game in which everyone picks a number from 0 to 100 and the winner is whoever comes closest to two-thirds of the average, where iterated deletion of dominated choices lands on one unique answer that everyone agrees is the right one. Marta said that example supported her rather than him: the reason to stop considering the high numbers is not that any high number is beaten by some other number in every state of the world, but that you believe other people believe other people will not choose them, and so what Julian had been calling domination for the previous hour was really a chain of assumptions about other players' reasoning.

Neither would concede anything. Could you go through this move by move and say who is right at each step — not merely who wins overall, but whether each claim actually made is correct, incorrect, or correct only under assumptions nobody stated? I would especially like to know whether Julian's reply in step (3) holds up, since that was the moment the room split. And please say what, if anything, both of them are missing, including whether the two things they were each calling domination in step (5) really are the same relation.

One further request. I expect that some of this is settled and some of it is a matter of live disagreement among people who work on it seriously. Please mark that distinction explicitly as you go: tell me which of your verdicts reflect results that are not in dispute, and which reflect a contested position, and where Julian or Marta has stumbled onto something that experts themselves argue about, say so rather than presenting your preferred reading as though it were the consensus view.
