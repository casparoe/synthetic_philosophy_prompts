I'm writing a paper for a political philosophy seminar on the claims that states and their members can make on outsiders, and I've hit a policy pattern I can't make sense of. Most wealthy democracies are, by and large, orthodox free traders: they argue from comparative advantage, treat consumer welfare as the measure of a good bargain, and regard tariffs, subsidies, and local-content requirements elsewhere in the economy as presumptively objectionable. Yet when the traded good is cultural, that orthodoxy quietly collapses. France requires private radio stations to devote a minimum share of the songs they broadcast — forty percent, with a further sub-quota reserved for new acts — to recordings in French, and it levies a tax on cinema tickets whose proceeds fund French film production. South Korea has long required theaters to set aside a minimum number of screening days each year for domestic films. Canada maintains Canadian-content rules for broadcasting and has recently moved to extend them to streaming platforms. France pressed a "cultural exception" during the trade negotiations that produced the World Trade Organization, Canada wrote a carve-out for cultural industries into NAFTA, and audiovisual services remain among the least liberalized sectors in world trade law.

What I want is not a verdict on whether these policies are wise but an account of what they presuppose. Start with the theory of value they seem to require. The harm the policies are meant to prevent is not, on the face of it, a harm to identifiable individuals: viewers and listeners get more of what they say they want, domestic audiences pay somewhat more, and foreign producers lose sales to which they had no prior entitlement. So the loss must be borne by something other than persons. What is that something — a language, a culture, a national artistic tradition — and does the practice require it to exist as a bearer of value, or can the loss be redescribed entirely in terms of individuals? Note also the asymmetry between the two halves of the policy: these states both fund domestic production and restrict domestic consumption. If the concern were the welfare of domestic artists, subsidy with open borders would seem to serve it better, so the consumption restriction is doing separate work. What is that work, and does it depend on the idea that preferences are endogenous — shaped by what is available — such that what people actually choose to watch is not authoritative about their welfare? That would sit oddly beside the same states' free-trade reasoning elsewhere, and I'd like the tension stated precisely rather than waved away.

Then take up the boundary itself, which I suspect is the philosophically loaded part. An instrumental defense — that variety in the world's cultural output is valuable the way biodiversity is — would be cosmopolitan rather than nationalist: if diversity is the good, it shouldn't matter which government pays for which films, and quotas keyed to nationality would be the wrong instrument. So the practice seems to assume that the relevant unit of protection is the nation and that compatriot producers have a claim on us that equally talented foreign ones lack. Is that assumption separable from a claim about national character, and how does it square with the fact that these same states officially repudiate essentialism about peoples — insisting that belonging is a matter of individual commitment, that no one should be treated as a representative of their group, and that the nation is civic rather than ethnic? Please sort out which rival views the practice rules out (cosmopolitanism about culture, welfarist individualism, the pure diversity defense), which of its features are licensed by several competing theories at once (infant-industry economics, preservation of a vulnerable language, the need for a domestic arena of news and drama in which a democracy can argue with itself, the professional infrastructure artists need), and where the practice is therefore underdetermined. Finish with the counterfactual: describe the regime a consistently cosmopolitan theory would produce — production subsidies, translation and distribution funding, vouchers, no restriction on what people consume — and say whether it could deliver the goods the quotas actually target, or whether those goods cannot be had without telling an audience what it may watch. I'm after the argument itself; I don't need references or a bibliography.
