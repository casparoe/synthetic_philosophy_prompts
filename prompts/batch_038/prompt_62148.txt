Just got out of a three hour arguement with a friend and I think I lost it, or at least I couldn't say why I hadn't. The phrase at issue was "above and beyond the call of duty." Context: a mutual friend of ours does maybe 20 hrs a week at a hospice and gives away basically all her spare money. I said that's admirable but obviously not required — nobody's obliged to be a saint, it's above and beyond. My friend, who is the demanding-consequentialist type, said that phrase was doing all the work and none of the explaining. His line: if what she does is better than the alternative and costs nothing to anyone else, then it's required, and "above and beyond" is just a polite word for "we'd rather not be blamed for doing less." I mumbled something about the technical term being supererogation, which is true but useless, since the whole dispute is about what the phrase commits you to.

So: can you take "above and beyond the call of duty" apart and give me the menu of precise claims it could be making? I want the readings laid out as a list, from the ones that are barely more than tautologies up to the substantive and controversial ones, and for each whether it's actually plausible and whether it blocks his inference. Stuff I suspect is hiding in there but can't articulate: whether "the call" implies somebody with standing to call, so that the phrase is really about who can demand what rather than about how good the act is; whether "beyond" is about cost to the agent, about comparative goodness, or about both, and whether those come apart; whether an act can be beyond the call of duty and still be what you ought to do, which seems to turn on "duty" and "ought" not being the same word; whether the phrase is about act types or about this particular person's circumstances, since the same act might be required of one person and supererogatory for another; and whether it entails praiseworthiness or just the absence of blame for not doing it.

So one register treats the phrase as marking something genuinely optional and creditable, and the other treats it as marking an unpaid expectation that is in the process of hardening into a duty. Are those two uses even making the same kind of claim?
