Please write a short story of roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words that dramatizes a precise mechanism from game theory, and that makes the mechanism legible to a reader who has never met it. Give the story a title.

The setting is a negotiation conducted under what is sometimes called program equilibrium. Treat the following mechanism as exact and non-negotiable; the story must not soften or fudge it.

- Two parties are playing a one-shot prisoner's dilemma over the division of something both need. Neither acts in person. Each submits a computer program to act on their behalf, once, with no second round.
- Before choosing, each program is handed the complete source code of the other, and may also inspect its own.
- Each program contains a proof-searcher with a fixed proof system and a bounded proof budget. The standard design cooperates if and only if it can prove that the opponent's program will cooperate against it, and otherwise defects. When two such programs meet, each can derive the other's cooperation from its own, and both cooperate: mutual defection is not the inevitable outcome of a one-shot dilemma once the players can read one another.
- A cruder design cooperates only if the opponent's source is character-for-character identical to its own. This also yields cooperation, and it is ruinously brittle: a renamed variable, a different compiler version, a single added space, and two entirely willing partners defect on each other.
- The same transparency that makes cooperation provable makes threats provable. A program whose source plainly reads *defect unless the opponent cooperates* cannot be bluffed, doubted, or out-stared. The opponent can read the commitment, prove it is real, and — if its own design is to cooperate whenever cooperation is provable — has no alternative but to comply. Being readable is being disarmable.
- A proof is always relative to a proof system. Two wholly honest programs built by different institutions on incompatible formal systems may find nothing provable about each other in either direction, and defect with no bad faith anywhere in the building.

The protagonist is a handshake architect: the person at one of the two parties who writes the program that will be submitted. A week before the exchange, they establish that the other side's program is a provable and immutable threat — committed months earlier by a committee that cannot reconvene in time to retract it, so the threat is not a posture and cannot become one. They also establish that their own institution operates under a standing auditability charter, adopted years ago for entirely unrelated reasons of public accountability, which requires any negotiating program to be fully readable by an outside reviewer. That charter silently rules out every opaque design that would be immune to the threat.

The live options, which the story should stage rather than list, are: yield and comply, knowing that their own source is public and that compliance is now a permanent feature of their institution's design; submit a bulwark that provably defects against anything containing a threat, which will also refuse honest partners whose fairness it cannot prove inside its own system; or deliberately corrupt their own program so that nothing whatsoever about its behavior can be proved — the only defense against a verifiable threat being to become unverifiable, at the cost of every cooperative equilibrium they could ever have reached with anyone.

Craft requirements: a named protagonist, at least one scene in real time, and a deadline that presses on the prose. Convey the mechanism through what people do and say — including one moment where a junior colleague proposes the obvious fix and is shown, concretely, why it fails. No epigraphs, no glossary, no named corporations or governments, no dialogue that sounds like a survey article. Do not resolve the story with a speech about trust, and do not let the ending be cheap: whichever way the architect goes, something real should be lost, and the reader should be able to say what.
