Three months ago I took over as director of a small battlefield memorial site — 40 acres, a stone wall with about 1,100 names on it, a visitor center that hasn't been repainted since I was in middle school. It's run by the county historical society board, and I grew up eight miles from the gate. My great-great-great-grandfather is on that wall and my cousins still put up a tent on the grounds every August for the family reunion, so this is not a neutral situation for me and I know at least two board members know that. I've spent the whole spring drafting a redesign of the exhibit, and it goes before the board next Tuesday for a vote: I get twenty minutes and then the floor is open, and there's a line item in the county budget request next month that depends on them saying yes.

Here's the spine of what I'm proposing, since the objections only make sense if you know it. The new exhibit puts four cases in one room, side by side, with no wall text ranking them: the Lost Cause, Masada, Kosovo, Dunkirk. Not to say they're morally equivalent — I say so explicitly in the intro panel — but to show that a lost cause is not a consolation story, it's an instrument, and the instrument has three moving parts. First, the deferred verdict: the outcome gets re-described as contingent (numbers, weather, a betrayal, a general's bad health, boats that didn't come) so the cause is never actually refuted by losing, only unluckily denied its test. Second, the transfer: the political claim that failed is converted into a moral claim that can't fail, so the loser ends up holding something the winner doesn't. Third — and this is the part I think does all the real work, and the part my exhibit spends most of its square footage on — the standing obligation: the loss is booked as a debt owed by people who weren't there to people who weren't there either, which is how a story about 1865 or 73 CE or 1389 or 1940 becomes a live claim on what a county does with its schools or its land or its flag this year. And then the bit that's going to cost me: I argue that this machinery is not a pathology of losers. Dunkirk was a retreat re-narrated into the founding temper of a country that went on to win; Masada was rebuilt as a civic ritual by a state that exists; the Kosovo poems were sung for centuries and written down much later by people who needed something. So the standard critical move — treat the Lost Cause as a uniquely distorted regional fever and debunk it — is too easy, and the exhibit refuses to make it. Instead of panels that announce "this is a myth," the design shows visitors the three gears turning in all four rooms and lets them catch themselves enjoying it.

The board, precisely, because that's the thing I can't model on my own. Chair is a retired ag-bank manager who has kept the site solvent for eleven years and thinks about membership renewal numbers more than interpretation. Two academic historians consult for us: one does Civil War campaigns and is openly impatient with theory — his idea of a good exhibit is that the order of battle and the casualty figures are right and that nothing on a label could be contradicted by a regimental history — and the other works on collective memory and will absolutely want to know whether I've read anyone, and will be disappointed either way. There's the woman who runs the schools program and gets about nine hundred fifth-graders through the gate every spring; her question is always some version of "what will they remember on the bus ride home." The treasurer is worried about two donor families whose ancestors are literally carved into the wall and who fund the maintenance endowment. Two seats are held by a lineage organization, one person courteous and one who has already sent me a letter. And one member is a county commissioner up for re-election in November. What I need from you is the prep sheet, not a defense — I've got the defense, I've been writing it for months. Give me the eight or so objections this room actually produces, ranked by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it, phrased the way each of those people would phrase it (the military historian does not talk like the memory person, and neither of them talks like the commissioner). For each: a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, and an honest note on where that reply is soft, because I'd rather know going in. Tell me what to concede in the first sixty seconds so I'm not spending the whole evening defending ground I don't need. Tell me the single thing that has to land for the redesign to survive the vote. And tell me the question I'm praying nobody asks — I have a guess about what it is, but I'd like to know if you think it's a different one.
