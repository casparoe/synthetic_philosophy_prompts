Nurses newly rotating onto our dementia and geriatric psychiatry unit receive ninety minutes of ethics orientation, which I run. They arrive with one undergraduate ethics course each, with the assumption that personhood is a binary threshold property of individual human beings, and with a hazy notion that ubuntu is about being warm toward people. I want to make the relational conception of personhood developed in Southern African philosophy — personhood as a status constituted and sustained through relationships, and therefore as something admitted in degrees rather than simply had or not had — usable for them on the ward rather than merely introduced to them. Plan the session for me instead of expounding the view: identify which pieces must be in place before the relational claim can be grasped at all (I suspect that personhood is a status rather than a substance, and that moral standing is not the same thing as personhood, both have to come first), name the single ward scenario that should carry the whole explanation and say what it must exhibit and what it must deliberately avoid exhibiting, map the misconceptions I should expect together with the precise point in the sequence at which to head off each one — I anticipate that the framework collapses into collectivism, that a resident who can no longer reciprocate thereby ceases to be a person and can be released from care, and that all of this is motivational-poster sentiment rather than a theory of status — and give me one case to put to them at the end that would reveal whether they can deploy the framework rather than recite it. Finish by stating what I should leave out at this level and announce as left out.
