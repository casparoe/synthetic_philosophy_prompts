I've been writing environmental impact statements for about fifteen years now, mostly highways and transmission lines, so my entire working life is scoping, describing the affected environment, calling things significant or not significant, and then walking the mitigation hierarchy: avoid, minimize, restore, offset. No philosophy training, no machine learning math. I understand roughly what a penalty term in an objective function is and thats about it.

I've been reading about a debate in AI safety that I can't stop thinking about, because its exactly like an argument we have constantly in my field and never notice we're having. The question is whether you can tell an agent to keep its impact low without first telling it what you value. One camp says yes, you can build an impact measure that doesn't encode anybody's goals. You count how much an action shrinks the set of states the world could still get to afterwards, they call that reachability. Or you measure how much it degrades the ability to pursue a big grab bag of unrelated goals. Or you measure how far the resulting state sits from some baseline of what would have happened had the agent done nothing. Their pitch is that this is close to value-neutral: it preserves options and avoids irreversible change, so you can run a capable system safely before you've worked out what it should want. The other camp says that's a dodge. They say every such measure needs a weighting over which futures count and which goals make the grab bag, and a weighting is just a value function wearing a lab coat. They say the baseline is arbitrary, since you can pick inaction, or the present world, or the world before the agent was built, and each gives a different verdict. They say preserving optionality preserves bad states too, so a measure that scores a locked-in injustice and a decent society the same isn't neutral, it's conservative with a straight face. And they say the intended-versus-side-effect cut doesn't track anything that matters, because plenty of side effects are wonderful and plenty of intended effects are terrible.

So here's what I want. Take both camps and restate the whole exchange in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: baseline and reference conditions, significance thresholds, a finding of no significant impact, direct versus indirect versus cumulative impacts, the affected environment chapter, the mitigation hierarchy, no net loss, offsets and the question of whether they're really fungible, adaptive management, scoping, the comment period, the written response to comments, the record of decision, judicial review. I want to see how much of their argument lands in the language of an EIS.

Then give me the residue report, claim by claim, not as a general vibe. Which of their moves translate cleanly, and onto what exactly. Which ones you had to bend to get through the door, and what you bent. And which ones have no expression at all in environmental review. Where you bent something, say so out loud rather than letting the fit look better than it is. And don't smuggle my framework's commitments in and then credit yourself with a successful translation.

There's one thing in particular I want you to run down. In my world, significant always means significant to something, a valued resource, a statutory purpose, a species, a town's use of a river, and we handle the arbitrariness not by measuring better but by process: you scope it in public, people comment, you answer them in writing, and somebody signs a decision they can be sued over. If that process is what makes an impact judgment legitimate rather than merely asserted, then the AI people look like they're trying to buy value-neutrality by throwing away the only thing that was ever doing the work. Is that right? Or does the residue point at something my framework genuinely cannot say, something about an actor that moves faster and reaches further than any project proponent, where there is no public to comment and no court to sue in? Tell me which way it goes, and where the line falls between the two.
