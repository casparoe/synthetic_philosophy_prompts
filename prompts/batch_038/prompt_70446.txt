I retired four years ago after thirty-nine years in industrial fermentation and mammalian cell-culture engineering, and I have since been reading my way through the philosophy of law with the patience of a man who once spent a decade characterising a single protein. The exchange between Hart and Devlin that followed the Wolfenden Report has absorbed me more than anything else I have encountered, chiefly because it seems to me to turn on an empirical question that both sides settled by intuition rather than by evidence.

I am attempting to test my own understanding by constructing a case of my own devising, and I should like your help in dramatising it.

The case. Suppose a national legislature is considering a bill I shall call the Human Tissue Products Act. It makes it a criminal offence to manufacture, import, advertise, or sell for human consumption any food produced by the culture of cells taken from a living or deceased human being. The bill is drafted so that no harm-based rationale is available to its supporters: the cell donor has given written, revocable, unpaid consent; nobody is killed, injured, deceived, or exposed to pathogenic risk; the product is certified by the food-safety authority as chemically and microbiologically indistinguishable from ordinary cultivated meat; and consumption is not itself an offence — only production, importation, advertising, and sale are. The explanatory memorandum attached to the bill expressly disclaims any appeal to health, welfare, dignity, or the interests of donors, and rests the case on the assertion that a society which permits the commercial manufacture of human flesh for the table ceases to be the society its members take it to be. Polling in this imaginary jurisdiction shows revulsion in the high eighties per cent across every age cohort, with no discernible trend over the fifteen years since the process became technically feasible.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 1,300 to 1,600 words between two witnesses giving evidence to the committee on this bill.

Let the first be a Hart-style liberal: a former regulatory economist who accepts that the harm principle requires qualification, who concedes that widespread revulsion is a social fact of some consequence, and who is not permitted to treat the absence of an identifiable victim as a trump card that ends the argument. Let the second be a Devlin-style legal moralist: a secular sociologist of law who rejects natural law and divine command entirely, and who holds a thesis I want you to press rather than parody — namely that prohibitions and permissions are not symmetrical in their effects upon a shared morality, because a prohibition encodes a common judgement that members can inhabit and reason from, whereas a permission is merely silent; so that a society can accumulate new liberties without loss of cohesion, but cannot withdraw an old prohibition without paying some price in the fabric that holds it together.

I have the following requirements.

1. Both witnesses must be as able as the best version of their position in the literature. Neither may win by fiat, and neither may be allowed a cheap closing line.
2. They must argue about this bill — the irrelevance of consent as a defence, the exemption of the eater alongside the prosecution of the producer, the disclaimed harm rationale, the fifteen-year flatness of the polling — and not merely about the Wolfenden Report in general, though either may invoke that history.
3. Three cruxes must be engaged directly: whether the stability of a revulsion over fifteen years is evidence that it tracks something, or only evidence that it has not yet been seriously tested; whether the disintegration claim in the memorandum can be restated so that some observation could count against it, and which observation that would be; and whether the liberal's distinction between the morality a society happens to hold and the morality it ought to hold can be applied here without assuming the conclusion.
4. Please have one of them raise, and the other answer, a difficulty of my own. I keep two lists. The first records revulsions that were once close to universal in Europe and have since dissolved: the dissection of the cadaver, which in eighteenth-century Britain was felt to be indignity enough to be added to the sentence for murder; cremation; the transfusion of blood; the inoculation of the healthy with material drawn from the sick. The second records revulsions that have proved extraordinarily durable in the West: the eating of horse, and the eating of dog. Both lists are available to both witnesses, and I want to see them quarrel over which list this bill belongs on, and over what would make that determination more than a matter of taste.
5. There must be genuine back-and-forth: each speech should take up something specific said in the one before it, and at least one witness should be forced to revise a formulation mid-argument.
6. Do not put citations in their mouths. Neither witness may refer to books, articles, judicial decisions, or surveys other than the material I have supplied above; where they refer to Hart or to Devlin, let them paraphrase the argument in their own words.
7. Each witness must concede at least one point that costs them something real.
8. The dialogue must not end in agreement, compromise, or synthesis. It should end with the sharpest available statement of what precisely remains in dispute between them.

Afterwards, please add a short note of your own — six to ten sentences, in your own voice rather than either character's — assessing which of the two made claims that a person of my training could in principle put to the test, and sketching one study design, however impractical or unappetising, that would discriminate between the moralist's asymmetry thesis and the liberal's denial of it.
