So I'm TA-ing an intermediate philosophy of mind seminar and I told the prof I'd hand out a one-pager with my own actual views on original vs. derived intentionality, so the students have something live to attack instead of a textbook summary. There's a Searle partisan in the group and a very loud Dennettian who will absolutely go for the throat, and I'd rather find the cracks before Thursday. Can you audit the list for me? Here's what's on the sheet:

(a) The Chinese Room does what Searle says it does — the guy in the room doesn't understand Chinese, and no amount of passing input/output tests settles the question. I also dont think the systems reply is any good: telling me the man is just the CPU and the whole system understands doesn't answer the challenge, and it would be exactly as unsatisfying if you ran the program straight onto silicon with no human anywhere in the picture.

(b) I buy Dennett's two-bitser. You build a little device out of a two-bit register and wire it to register one thing or another — whether a coin came up heads, say — and there's nothing in the device itself that privileges one of those readings over the others. So intentionality that depends on how a state is hooked up isnt second-rate for that reason, and I don't think the original/derived distinction can carry the metaphysical weight Searle puts on it.

(c) Where does content come from then? History, roughly. Something like proper function: a state is about what it was selected to track, by natural selection for the basics and by learning and design for a lot of the rest. Not fussy about the label.

(d) I'm not a fictionalist or an eliminativist about belief. There are facts about what my states are about, and they aren't just facts about which ascription scheme happens to be convenient for me. I don't want content to be interpreter-relative the way tall-for-a-sapling is relative.

(e) Verdicts I'd defend in print: Deep Blue genuinely believed its king was in danger and genuinely wanted to control the center of the board. No scare quotes, not a figure of speech.

(f) But the ABS module in my car doesn't believe the wheels are locking, and the thermostat doesn't believe the room is cold. Those states are about nothing except by courtesy of the engineers who wired them, and saying otherwise is precisely the slide into homuncularism I want to police.

(g) Method rule I hold: the homunculus always has to be discharged. You can credit a subsystem with content only if the account eventually bottoms out in parts that have none, and any theory that explains a capacity by installing a small version of that capacity inside is cheating.

(h) Also, consciousness isn't required for having beliefs, and Searle's positive story about the brain's causal powers strikes me as a placeholder rather than an explanation.

What I want back is an audit, not a lecture on the state of the debate. Go through the pairs that actually pull against each other and rank them by severity — I don't want eight equally weighted flags, I want to know which one or two would sink the handout if somebody pressed on them, and for each of those what specifically would have to give and what it would cost me to give it up. Just as important, tell me which apparent tensions aren't real: I'm pretty sure a couple of these look worse on the page than they are, and I'd rather you show me why than hedge and flag everything so you can't be wrong. I'm deliberately not saying which of (a)–(h) I'd be willing to sacrifice, because I honestly don't know — that's the question. Finish with the single cheapest revision that would clean up the most damage at once. And please don't send me a reading list or citations, I can't chase sources this week, I just need the reasoning laid out so I can check it myself.
