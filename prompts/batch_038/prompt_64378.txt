In about three weeks I'm giving a talk at a mathematical economics seminar — the room is two growth theorists who use overtaking criteria in Ramsey models every day, an axiomatic social choice person, a couple of grad students, and two philosophers who tagged along. Nobody there works on population ethics.

My thesis: when utility streams are infinite, the overtaking criterion is too weak because it leaves oscillating differences unranked, so we should rank outcomes by a shift-invariant generalized limit — basically a Banach limit of the difference sequence — and say x beats y only when every shift-invariant mean of the difference comes out positive. That buys completeness on cases like a difference that alternates +1, -1, forever, and I'm upfront about what it costs: any improvement confined to finitely many periods gets weight zero, and so does one that decays like 1/t, so Pareto goes out the window and I'm not trying to sneak it back in.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they do, phrased the way a growth theorist would phrase them as seperate from the way the social choice person would. Each one needs a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections are really the same objection in different clothes, what I should concede in my first two slides so I don't have to concede it later, and the question I'm praying doesnt come up. Assume they know the standard impossibility results about stationarity, continuity, anonymity and Pareto, but have never read the philosophy of the infinite, and will want to know what actual policy question my criterion changes the answer to.
