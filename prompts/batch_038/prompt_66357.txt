I am preparing a chapter for a collected volume on disagreement and have been running a graduate seminar alongside it, and the chapter has stalled at exactly the point where my own intuitions stop behaving. The subject is compromise between people who hold different value systems — not the intrapersonal problem of an agent uncertain which values are correct, though one of my cases below blurs that line deliberately, and not the political-theoretic question of whether compromise is democratically virtuous in general. What I want is a principle for when a particular agent may strike a particular bargain with a particular counterpart. My working formulation, which I now think is too crude to survive my own cases, is this: a compromise is permissible for an agent just when, reckoned entirely by that agent's own lights — their own values together with their own degrees of belief about which values are right — they come out ahead on balance; and impermissible when they come out behind. The thought is that moral trade is like any other trade, that the gains are real, and that refusing a mutually beneficial swap out of fastidiousness is a way of making the world worse for no one's benefit.

Here are the verdicts I keep reporting in seminar, in roughly the order in which the seminar reaches them.

1. A colleague whose giving is governed entirely by concern for animal welfare and I, whose giving is governed almost entirely by concern for global poverty, agree that each of us will redirect a fifth of our annual donations to the other's highest-priority charity. Fine — indeed, the two of us would be foolish not to, and I think we would each be mildly criticizable for refusing.

2. In a faculty senate, a colleague and I trade votes: I support a curriculum revision I think mildly harmful, she supports a hiring line I think valuable and she thinks wasteful. Fine, though faintly distasteful in a way I cannot yet locate.

3. The same arrangement, except that the measure I am asked to support would remove an accommodation that disabled students currently rely on. Not fine for me, and the difference from case 2 is not merely one of degree — I feel the shift as a change of kind, which embarrasses me, since my formulation recognizes only magnitudes.

4. On a hospital ethics committee, a colleague who attributes full moral status to fetuses and I, who do not, agree that I will vote against permitting a certain procedure and she will vote for expanded prenatal funding. Not fine. But note the asymmetry I want explained: I think the mirror-image trade is perfectly permissible for her, and I would not blame her for accepting it. Each of us is doing something different in the bargain, though each of us is one vote.

5. A major funder informs me that unless my forthcoming report contains a paragraph endorsing a practice I regard as unjustifiable, it will withdraw support from a programme that keeps a great many people alive. I include the paragraph. Fine, or at least not blameworthy — though I also think that someone who refused would be admirable, and I do not think the refuser would be making a mistake.

6. I tell a colleague that unless she supports my candidate for a departmental prize, I will use my editorial position to slow-walk her submissions. Not fine, and contemptible in a way that has nothing to do with the size of the stakes. Structurally this is the mirror of case 5, and my formulation gives the same verdict on both, since in both the agent comes out ahead.

7. A collaborator whose fundamental values I find myself genuinely uncertain about — call it forty per cent confidence that she is right and I am wrong — proposes that we each accommodate the other's priorities in a joint grant. Fine, and I am inclined to say more than fine: trading here looks like a rational response to my own uncertainty rather than a concession to someone else's error.

8. A colleague whose values I am confident are vile — he believes resources should be allocated partly by ethnic origin — proposes a bargain under which the resulting policy is a mixture of his view and mine, and therefore considerably less bad than what he would enact alone. Not fine. I feel contaminated by it. Yet the mixture is by hypothesis better than the alternative on every view in the neighbourhood except his, and my formulation therefore permits it. This is the case I most want a principle to explain rather than explain away.

9. A pattern of small trades of the sort in case 2, sustained in one department over ten years. Each trade, taken alone, I still judge fine. But I think the person at the end of the decade no longer holds the convictions with which she began, and I judge that outcome worse than the sum of the individual transactions. I cannot tell whether this is a genuine moral consideration or aesthetic squeamishness dressed up as one.

10. Two colleagues who share an office agree never to press their conflicting views in one another's presence. Neither gains anything either values; both avoid friction. Fine — but I suspect this is not compromise in the sense my other cases involve, and that my formulation is equivocating between bargain and truce.

11. Role variation. As an individual I would refuse a trade of the sort in case 3; as department chair, committing resources that are not mine alone, I think I ought to accept it and would be criticised for refusing; as trustee of a fund given for a stated purpose, I think I may not trade with it at all. Three verdicts, three roles, and my formulation says nothing about roles whatsoever.

So: is there a repair? I would like you to treat my formulation as a first draft to be amended rather than discarded — the gains-from-trade insight seems to me too important to abandon — and to add whatever clauses are needed, saying for each clause which of the cases above motivated it and what work it is doing. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the amended principle cannot deliver, and where I should simply give one up. I am especially interested in whether cases 7 and 8 can be separated by anything other than an appeal to how much I happen to dislike my counterpart, and in whether the mirror pair 5 and 6 admits of a principled difference or whether I should concede that they differ only in who is already in the wrong. I am not looking for attributions, a survey of positions, or suggestions about what to read; please argue from the cases themselves and from whatever distinctions you can construct.

Please organise the response under the following headings, in this order. **Bottom line** (about 150 words): the amended principle in one or two sentences, plus a one-sentence statement of which of my verdicts it costs me. **The principle, clause by clause** (about 700 words): each clause stated precisely, with the numbered case that motivates it and an account of what the clause excludes that the unamended version permitted. **What it gets right and what it overturns** (about 400 words): a compact reckoning over all eleven cases, marking for each whether the principle confirms my verdict, reverses it, or delivers something more complicated than either. **The pair I cannot reconcile** (about 450 words): cases 7 and 8, and then cases 5 and 6, with a straight answer on whether the differences are principled. **Predictions on cases I have not raised** (about 350 words): three further compromise cases of your own devising, chosen to probe places where the amended principle is likely to be unstable, with the verdicts the principle commits me to. **The simpler alternative** (about 300 words): a less baroque principle that abandons two or three of my verdicts outright, and your assessment of whether the simplicity is worth the loss.
