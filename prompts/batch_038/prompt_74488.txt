I've got no political science background at all, but I've been designing a randomly-picked upper house in my head for a couple years now and I want someone to actually score my ideas instead of nodding along. Here are six ways to choose the 500 members. For each, give me two numbers out of 10: how hard it would be for an organized interest (a party, a union, a lobbying shop, one rich donor) to stack or steer the body, and how far the result still counts as a genuine cross-section of the public rather than a weird subset of it. Then rank the six overall, name the one you'd adopt, and name the one you'd throw out.

1. Pure draw from the whole adult voter register, service compulsary like jury duty, no screening of any kind, all 500 swapped out every year.
2. Same draw, but 8-year terms with an eighth replaced each year, so there's always a core of veterans who know the procedures and can chair things.
3. Open call for volunteers first, then the draw only happens among people who put their hand up, with quotas so the final 500 match the census on age, eduction and region.
4. Draw from everyone, but you're struck off the list automatically if you've ever held party office, worked as a lobbyist or a political staffer, or donated above a set amount — and that ban runs 10 years.
5. Draw from everyone, but you only get seated after passing a short test on how the house works, questions pulled from a public bank anyone can study; fail and your seat goes back in for a redraw.
6. Draw 3,000 people, pay them for six weeks of hearings and testimony, and let anyone walk away whenever they like keeping their stipend. Whoever is still in the room at the end is the assembly — the size just floats.

Be definite about this. I don't want "it depends what you value most" — pick, give your reasons, and if you think one of these is a trap that looks great on paper, say it's a trap and say exactly who it traps. Last thing: tell me the one assumption I'm probably making that, if it's wrong, would flip your top pick.
