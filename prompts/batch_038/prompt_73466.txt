Write a literary short story of between 2,000 and 2,600 words.

The story should turn on a distinction drawn in John von Neumann's posthumous Silliman Lectures, published in 1958 as The Computer and the Brain. You will not need the book; the material you require is set out here. Von Neumann argued that the nervous system's message system is not digital in the sense an engineer would use the word. A nerve impulse is an all-or-none event, yet on his account what it conveys is not fixed by the identity of the impulse but by statistical features of the whole train of impulses — how many arrive, at what rate, in what pattern over what interval. The precision of any single component is therefore very low, and the notation of the system as a whole is arithmetical rather than logical: it works with quantities, not with tokens governed by categorical rules of combination. He argued further that the system's reliability cannot come from the accuracy of its parts, since those parts are individually far less dependable than anything a switching engineer would accept, and must come instead from depth of parallel operation and from redundancy. Finally, he borrowed from physiology the notion that organs are degenerate — that one structure commonly serves several functions, and one function is commonly served by several structures.

Do not expound any of this. Build the story out of a case.

The setting is a present-day British estuary port. The clinician is a consultant neuropsychologist preparing a report for the Harbour Authority's Medical Panel. The patient is a licensed pilot in his early sixties with thirty-odd years on the bar who suffered a left-hemisphere infarct eleven weeks earlier. The Panel requires an answer in a form it can process: restore the licence, revoke it, or suspend it pending further review. Three boxes, one tick. Since the infarct, the pilot has become unreliable at exactly the tasks whose answers are categorical — saying whether two tones are the same or different, keeping a running count of discrete events, applying a regulation expressed as a conditional — while remaining uncannily sound at those whose answers are quantities: closing speeds, swing of a vessel, whether a berth has room to spare, whether a trim is about right. His errors, when they come, are small and graded. They are never gross.

Observe the following constraints.

1. Tell the story in two interleaved registers that do not share a vocabulary. Passages drawn from the report may use only categorical, all-or-none language — intact, impaired, present, absent, correct, incorrect, yes, no — together with the numbered headings of the test battery. Passages drawn from the pilot's own account may use only graded and comparative language — nearly, more or less, roughly, enough, too much, less than before, about right — and may make no categorical assertion of any kind.

2. The test battery you invent must include at least one task whose correct answer is a quantity and at least one whose correct answer is a category, and the story must show the same man performing both.

3. Include one scene in which a test is administered and the outcome is genuinely ambiguous: the very same performance can be scored as a clean pass or a clear failure depending on which register it is described in, and both scorings must be visible to the reader on the page.

4. Show the pilot's surviving competence as carried by structures doing work they were never designed for, without ever naming this. Six weeks after the first assessment he returns for retesting and does markedly better on the tasks he failed — at a price that falls somewhere the report has no column for, a capacity nobody thought to measure having been quietly spent. The reader must come to understand what has been traded. No character may.

5. No character may state, explain, hint at or name the theory. Von Neumann, the lectures and the book are not to be mentioned anywhere in the story. The distinction must be recoverable only from what happens and from the collision of the two vocabularies; a reader who has never encountered it should still feel the collision.

6. End with the two registers contradicting one another about the same decision, and do not settle which is right. Both verdicts should be survivable, and both should cost something.

Write in British spelling and idiom, with the compression of a case report and the ache of a story about a man whose work was his judgement. Be precise about clinical and maritime detail — exact enough to be believed, never so much that it becomes instruction. No chapter titles, no epigraphs, no footnotes, no notes on your sources and no explanation of your choices.
