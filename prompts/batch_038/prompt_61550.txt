So I'm rebuilding the first third of my phil-of-mind survey for next term and I keep circling back to a long feature a science writer did in one of those Sunday magazines a couple of years ago. I wont name it, it doesn't matter, but the author had clearly done some homework and one of my grad students swears by it, so before I stick it on the reading list I want to know whether it's a decent on-ramp or whether I'd be feeding them something they'll have to unlearn later. Here's the core of it, typed up from the piece:

"In the last decade or so a single idea has quietly taken over both neuroscience and the philosophy of mind: the free energy principle. The brain on this view is not a camera that passively records the world; it is a prediction engine. It constantly generates expectations about the sensory input it is about to receive and compares them with what actually arrives. What it cares about is not the input but the mismatch -- prediction error -- and perception is what happens when the brain revises its model to shrink that mismatch. The same machinery can shrink error the other way round, by acting on the world until the world matches the prediction, which is why seeing a glass of water and reaching for one turn out to be the same kind of process. The quantity being minimized throughout is called free energy, an upper bound on surprise, where surprise just means how improbable an organism's own sensory states are: the technical version of how unexpected you are to yourself. Minimizing it is how living things keep out of the disordered states that would kill them. And because every mental state on this account is a hypothesis being tested against incoming evidence, one equation now covers perception, action, attention, emotion, delusion and autism. The most striking corollary comes from the Markov blanket: any system of this kind is marked off from its environment by a set of states that render what's inside statistically independent of what's outside. Each of us therefore sits behind a boundary of our own making. We never contact the world, only our best guesses about it, and the old problem of how the mind hooks onto reality gets dissolved rather than solved. Notice too that this is not a theory you could refute with an experiment, since free energy minimization applies to anything at all that has a Markov blanket -- which makes it closer to a mathematical truth about being alive than to an empirical hypothesis. That is not a weakness; it is the source of its power."

My instinct, having read a bit of Clark and skimmed some Friston, is that this is pretty good. A colleague in cog sci sniffed and called it mush with equations attached, but wouldn't say where. So: give me an accuracy audit rather than a book report. Walk through it claim by claim and tell me what a working person in this area would nod at, what is a harmless simplification I can let stand, and what is a misrepresentation that would actually cost a student marks if they repeated it in a paper. Two spots I especially want pressure on. First, whether the piece is running predictive coding, active inference, and the free energy principle together as if they were one theory, and if so whether that conflation changes any of the substantive claims it goes on to make. Second, whether the Markov blanket move at the end -- from a statistical independence claim to we never contact the world -- is licensed by anything in the framework, or whether that's the bit my colleague was smelling; I'd like to know what a defender of the framework would say here and whether internalist and world-involving readers of it disagree with each other about this passage. And then whether the closing claim that unfalsifiability is a strength survives once the earlier stuff is corrected, or whether it's leaning on the errors.
