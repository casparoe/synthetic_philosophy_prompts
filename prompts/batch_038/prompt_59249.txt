I write a monthly ideas column for a general-science magazine — the kind of thing people read on a plane, not a journal. Next month I've got about 3,500 words and my editor has promised to kill me if it turns into a literature review, so no bibliography, no reading list, no "for further discussion see..." — I just need to know where to spend the space. The topic is Samuel Scheffler's afterlife conjecture: the claim that a lot of what we care about depends on humanity continuing to exist long after we die, so that if we became convinced everyone would be wiped out shortly after our own deaths, a big swath of our values would come apart — including, weirdly, values about things we'd have sworn were purely about the here and now. I find this genuinely gripping, but I know the surrounding debate has grown alot of branches and I can't tell which ones are load-bearing and which are just philosophers enjoying themselves.

So: rank the open questions in and around the conjecture by how much they deserve to be in a piece like this, and defend the ordering out loud. Tell me the criteria you're using — how much other stuff depends on the answer, how far the field actually is from settling it, whether a smart non-specialist can follow the stakes in three paragraphs, whether getting it wrong would change how a reader lives — and say how you're trading those off against each other, since they obviously pull in different directions. Then give me the two or three you'd actually build the column around, and the two or three objections you think I should cut because they look deep but dissolve once you push on them. And tell me what would change your ranking: what kind of new argument or finding would move something from "cut" to "lede."

One twist I want folded into the prioritization, because it's the hook I'm already committed to. Most of the discussion seems to assume the "afterlife" in question is more of the same — future people who are recognizably like us, who'll read our books and fight about our arguments. But suppose the future is populated by beings that carry on doing things without anyone like us doing them: uploads or machines that are extremely good at making music and proving theorems but are utterly indifferent to the stuff we currently care about, or care about it for reasons we'd find unintelligible. Does the conjecture predict we'd still care about that future, or that we'd find it barely matters? And is that even a question the conjecture is equipped to answer, given that it's supposed to be a claim about what our values actually depend on rather than a claim about what they ought to depend on? I honestly can't tell whether that's a real philosophical problem or just sci-fi garnish, and part of what I want from you is a verdict on that — where it lands in the ranking and why, not a diplomatic answer that says it's interesting either way.
