I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy, and this term I've been sitting on a small working group charged with replacing our university's student conduct code. The group has five members; the two who matter here are Nadia, a faculty member from our department, and Owen, who directs the conduct office and has handled several hundred cases over the years. I was there as the graduate representative, took notes, and want an outside adjudication, because their argument kept sliding between a practical question we actually have to answer and a much more abstract one that I recognize from my coursework, though neither of them ever used the technical names for it. I've tried to reproduce their exchange faithfully and in order, and I don't think I'm favoring either of them, but you should assume I may have.

The choice in front of the group is between two drafts. Draft A is an enumerated code: named prohibited behaviors, tiered sanctions, and lists of aggravating and mitigating factors. Draft B is a short statement of three or four commitments — something like treating others with respect, acting with integrity, and not damaging the conditions under which other people can study — with an explicit provision that application is case-by-case by a trained panel.

Step 1, Owen for Draft B: enumerated codes get gamed by whoever is most articulate and best advised, because once the wording is public people shape their behavior to sit just inside it, and he has watched students do exactly this. The codes are also under-inclusive, so the genuinely harmful novel behavior falls through the gaps. And worst of all, a checklist changes how the panel reasons: the moment the list exists, people treat matching the list as the whole question and stop looking at the case.

Step 2, Nadia for Draft A: what stops the outcome under Draft B from tracking who the panel happens to like? Consistency is a requirement of fairness, and consistency requires criteria that can be stated in advance, so that a respondent knows what they are being held to and can contest it. Under Draft B, a student who is sanctioned cannot know whether the same conduct will be sanctioned next year, and cannot argue that they are being treated differently from someone else, because there is no published standard to compare the two cases against. Discretion that is invisible is discretion that cannot be reviewed.

Step 3, Owen: Draft A doesn't eliminate discretion, it relocates it and hides it. The choices that matter — which behaviors got written down, which rule the office decides to charge under, and how the inevitably vague words in the rules get applied, words like "disruptive" or "unreasonable" — are all discretionary, and none of them are appealable by the student. And when the panel does make its contextual judgment first, it will then go find the clause that fits, which is post-hoc rationalization with a citation attached.

Step 4, Nadia: relocation isn't neutral. Upstream discretion is exercised by officials once, in a public document, and is contestable by everyone affected by it; downstream discretion is exercised by whoever happens to be seated on the panel, repeatedly, in private, and is contestable only by the person being sanctioned at that moment. Even granting that both drafts involve discretion, Draft A makes it general and visible, and visible generality is the only form discretion can take while still being held to account.

Step 5, Owen: then you've conceded the substance. You aren't defending rules as capturing anything true about the wrongs they prohibit; you're defending them as instruments of transparency. Fine — but then the question is empirical, and you have no evidence that publishing criteria produces fairer outcomes, given that the criteria themselves remain indeterminate at every point where a real case gets difficult.

Step 6, Nadia: indeterminate is the wrong word; they're incomplete, and those are different things. A standard can run out at the edges without having no content at the center. "Deliberately disrupting someone's teaching" is not vague in the core cases — you know perfectly well what it covers, and you could tell a student in advance. And your position taken seriously implies that the panel member who has heard a hundred cases has nothing more to hand a new member than a slogan. But of course she does hand something over, and whatever she hands over must be generalizable in some degree, or training would be impossible and every panel would start from zero.

Step 7, Owen: what she hands over is a trained eye, not a rule. Ask her to state what she is applying and she will produce either a formulation containing a term whose application is just as case-sensitive as the case itself — "unreasonable," "disrespectful," "in a way that shows disregard for" — or a disjunction of every exception anyone has ever managed to raise, which is a list of cases wearing a principle's clothing. Either way the principle is idle: it never tells you anything you couldn't get by looking at the case directly, and it never rules anything out that looking wouldn't have ruled out.

Step 8, Nadia: idle is the wrong description. Even a principle that cannot be fully spelled out does something a slogan cannot: it fixes which features are candidates for relevance in the first place, so that a panel member who says he counted it against the respondent that the respondent is a sophomore can be stopped and made to justify that. Under your Draft B nothing is out of bounds and no such challenge has a purchase. The principle's job is to create a demand for a reason of a certain shape, not to supply the answer to the case. And notice that you are arguing with a generalization of your own: you have asserted that codified discretion behaves a certain way across universities, documents, and decades, so either that is a claim you think survives counterexamples or it is a guess you are prepared to abandon. Which is it?

Step 9, Owen: it's a heuristic about administrative practice, not a moral principle, and I am glad to revise it. But that isn't a concession, it's the actual difference between us. I never said there are no generalizations. I said that none of the moral ones earn the title of principle, because each either fails to survive its counterexamples or survives only by absorbing so many qualifications that the qualifications rather than the generalization are doing all the work.

That is where the meeting ended, unresolved, and the draft is due in three weeks.

Could you go through the exchange in order and tell me, at each of the nine steps, who is right — not who won the round rhetorically, but whether the move actually establishes something? In particular I would like your view on whether step 3 is a genuine objection to step 2 or only a restatement of Owen's preference in the language of an objection; whether step 4 shows that visible upstream discretion is better or only picks a different location for the same problem; whether step 5 is entitled to call step 4 a concession, or whether Nadia can hold both instrumental and principled reasons for Draft A at once; whether step 7's dilemma is fair, or whether there is a third option available to Nadia that the exchange never considers; whether the tu quoque in step 8 lands given the answer Owen gives in step 9; and whether step 9's distinction between a heuristic and a moral principle can bear the weight he puts on it, or whether it is drawn exactly where he needs it to be drawn in order to protect his position. Then tell me what the two of them are missing between them: is there a stance available that neither occupies, and would occupying it change what we should actually publish? Finally, give me your verdict on who is more right overall, and on the narrower question the working group has to answer — whether the abstract disagreement makes any difference to which draft we should adopt, or whether each of them is free to prefer either document on other grounds entirely.

You don't need to name any philosophers or cite anything; I want the arguments assessed as arguments, not a survey of who has said what. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables. Take the steps in order, but in sentences. I would much rather have a shorter piece that commits to a verdict at each step than a longer one that hedges at every turn.
