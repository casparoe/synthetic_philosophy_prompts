I'm a third-year PhD student and I've drawn the slot at our department's reasons-and-rationality reading group next month, and I want to go in defending something people will actually have to argue with rather than nod along to. So: build me the strongest case you can for the view that there are no wrong-kind reasons at all — that whenever it looks like you have a reason to adopt an attitude that has nothing to do with thier object, the right description is that you have a reason to want to have the attitude, or a reason to bring it about that you have it, and never a reason for the attitude itself. The demon who'll punish you unless you admire him: overwhelming reason to want to admire him, overwhelming reason to arrange your life so that you come to admire him, not one scrap of a reason to admire him. Belief is the easy case for me, since on this view the thesis about belief basically collapses into evidentialism, so I don't want the argument to lean on belief. I want the hard attitudes done properly: fear, admiration, guilt, resentment, gratitude, intention — the ones where "you can't just decide" is supposed to bite, and where people say the reduction makes a hash of the fact that these reasons still show up in the agent's deliberation and still seem to weigh against each other with object-directed reasons.

Two obstacles I'd like you to run straight at instead of around. First, the asymmetry that keeps nagging me: the demon case seems to behave differently for different attitudes. He's threatening to torture you, which arguably makes him genuinely fearsome, and dangerousness looks like just what makes fear fitting — so the reason to fear the demon looks object-directed, while nothing whatever about him makes him admirable. If that's right, the right-kind/wrong-kind distinction isn't doing any work in the fear case at all. I think that's good news for my view, not bad, and I want you to argue exactly that: that the asymmetry is best explained by the objects of the attitudes differing in how tightly they're tied to the agent's interests, not by reasons differing in kind, and that the view predicts where the asymmetry will and won't show up. Second, the objection that the reduction is idle plus its sneakier cousin. Idle: you can't admire somebody as a means any more than you can believe at will, so "you have a reason to want the attitude" leaves the agent in front of the demon with nothing to do right now — tell me whether that's fatal, and if it isn't, say concretely what the view tells her to do. Sneakier cousin: if I have a reason to want to admire him, and admiring him is a means to wanting to admire him, doesn't means-end transmission hand me back a reason to admire him, so the wrong kind of reason sneaks in through the back door? I want a real answer to that, not a shrug about transmission principles being restricted.

Finally I want a diagnosis of why there's a problem here in the first place, because my hunch is that the whole debate is generated by running together two different questions — what makes an attitude fitting, and what an agent ought, all things considered, to do about her own attitudes — and that once you pull them apart the demon stops looking like a counterexample to anything. Argue for that too. I know a weaker cousin of my thesis has been defended before, so don't give me the cousin: give me the version that denies wrong-kind reasons outright, and say what extra explanatory work the strong version is doing that the weak one isn't. And be straight with me about the cost — I'd much rather hear which intution the view has to give up, and why giving it up is survivable, than a story that pretends everything is saved. No need to point me at sources or a bibliography, I'll chase those down myself once I've seen whether the argument holds together.
