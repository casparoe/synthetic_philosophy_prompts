So I run a small grants program at a private foundation, about $14M a year, mostly climate-intervention research, and next cycle we have to decide whether we'll fund anything that actually builds deployable capability (stratospheric aerosol injection field tests, marine cloud brightening hardware, that sort of thing) or whether we stay entirely in the modeling/monitoring/governance lane. Our board's standing slogan is "no deployment before governance," and our reviewers basicly always vote to slow down and study more, so in practice we fund almost nothing that could be pointed at the sky.

I've been re-reading von Neumann's mid-1950s stuff on whether we can survive technology — the Fortune essay and the Yale lectures around the same time — and my takeaway of his argument is roughly this: the big technics are neutral instruments that can be aimed at any purpose; once something is knowable it can't be kept unknown or slowed down, because any restriction just gets evaded by whoever wants it badly enough; the actual danger isn't the machinery but that the planet has become too small and too politically under-organized for the reach of what we can now do, with climate tinkering as his example of a capability one country could exercise unilaterally while everyone else absorbed the consequences; and therefore safety has to come from more and better technics plus better political structure, not from restraint.

Tell me the most important implications of that position for how I should run this program — not for the climate question in the abstract, specifically for a funder with my constraints: I can move money in 6 to 12 month cycles, I can't set policy, and if we fund something that blows up publicly the whole program gets shut down. I especially want to know what his argument implies about the "slow down and study" default, about whether funding governance work makes any sense if the capability can't be stopped regardless, and about the fact that he thought unilateral climate modification was destabilizing *because* it was feasible rather than in spite of it.

Format: exactly five numbered implications, no more and no fewer, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion — just the list. If you think one of the five is weak or doesn't really follow from von Neumann, say so inside that same item rather than in a separate section. And dont dissolve everything into "it depends" — give me the implications he'd draw even where you disagree with him. American English, please.
