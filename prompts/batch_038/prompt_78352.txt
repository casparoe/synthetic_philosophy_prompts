I sit on the three-member appeals tribunal of a national grant agency, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion ahead of next week's hearing. My two colleagues are already entrenched on opposite sides, and I would like a draft I can defend rather than a summary of interpretive methods.

The scheme. Since 1981 the agency has administered a rebate on capital expenditure by small operators. The operative provision reads: "A rebate of five per cent of the qualifying expenditure of an applicant, up to a maximum of fifty thousand dollars per applicant in any year." A separate eligibility clause confines the scheme to enterprises with fewer than 250 employees; the enabling instrument describes its purpose as defraying the costs of small operators.

The case. For forty-four years every applicant has been a single firm. Some 38,000 decisions have issued under the provision, and the largest rebate ever paid was $41,200, so the maximum has never actually bitten. This year a growers' cooperative — eleven employees of its own, thirty-one member farms, each independently within the 250-employee limit — filed one joint claim aggregating $1,620,000 of qualifying expenditure across its members. The assessment officer applied the maximum once, to the claim as lodged, and allowed $50,000. The cooperative appeals, contending that the maximum attaches to each person whose expenditure is claimed, that thirty-one maxima therefore apply, and that the rebate is the full $81,000.

Note the shape of the dispute, because it is what makes this hard rather than merely contested. The single-cap reading and the per-member reading give the identical figure for every claim in the scheme's history, since no prior claim aggregated the expenditure of more than one person and none approached the maximum. This appeal is the first that discriminates between them. The drafting file consists of a single annotated draft: the maximum clause was inserted in a different hand, with no marginal note and no explanatory memorandum, and there is no evidence that joint claims were considered. A footnote in the agency's internal guidance manual, present since 2009 and drafted by an outside contractor, states that the cap applies to the claim as lodged; the manual has no legal force and was never consulted on. Eleven further consortium claims are stayed pending our decision.

The panel's disagreement. One colleague holds that the provision meant one thing in 1981 and means it still; our task is to identify which reading that is, and if we identify it correctly the eleven stayed claims must go the same way whatever the administrative cost. The other holds that nothing in the text, the drafting record, or forty-four years of uniform decisions settles the question — both readings are equally consistent with everything the agency has ever done — so that we are not finding the meaning of the provision but adopting a rule for the future; on that view we should say so openly, decide on grounds of policy and fairness, and confine our ruling's effect prospectively so that the stayed claims are undisturbed. The opinion must handle a complication for the second view rather than wave it away: if the provision really did not determine an answer, this cooperative is being subjected to a rule that did not exist when it filed; and if it did determine an answer, my colleague has misdescribed what we are doing.

Please also take up a third position that will be raised at the table: that the question what the provision meant before we answered it is empty, because the meaning of the clause consists in nothing over and above the way those who apply it are disposed to apply it — a disposition documented, on this account, by the 2009 footnote and by the officer's practice. Say why that is attractive, and then say what it cannot explain. At a minimum: a disposition is a prediction about what will be said, whereas our question is what ought to be said, and the two can come apart; and the footnote records the settled habits of people who never faced the question, which is a thin foundation for a determination that will cost this applicant $31,000.

Deliverables:

1. A written opinion of 800–1,100 words addressed to the panel, recommending a determination of the appeal and specifying what follows for the eleven stayed claims and for the guidance manual. It must reach a definite bottom line — allow, dismiss, or allow in part — and state the orders I would propose.
2. Reasons a dissenting colleague could actually engage with. Take the strongest consideration on the losing side at its full strength before rejecting it. It is not open to you to resolve the case by announcing that the correct reading is obvious, nor to invoke a presumption (strict construction, beneficial construction, deference to the agency's long practice) without explaining what makes that presumption the right one here rather than its opposite; the panel has already heard all of them.
3. An annex of no more than 250 words, in plain language for the other two members, stating what would have to be the case for the quarrel between "we found the answer" and "we made the answer" to be a difference in description rather than a genuine disagreement — and whether you think it is.

Do not cite cases, statutes, or academic literature, and do not appeal to the law of any real jurisdiction. Argue only from the materials described here. Write so that the opinion could be adopted as the tribunal's reasons if a majority agrees with it.
