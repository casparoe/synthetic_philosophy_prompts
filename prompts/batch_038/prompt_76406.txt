My students already believe that intellectual property causes gridlock, and that belief is threatening to ruin the class I want to teach. They are 18 upper-level undergraduates in a seminar on the ownership of ideas — roughly half pre-law, half philosophy majors — and we have a single 75-minute session on the question of when rights in an idea make it less usable rather than more. They have already met the incentive-based justification, Locke on mixing one's labor, and an argument that a resource fragmented among too many rights-holders can end up useless to everyone. I do not want to lecture on any of it, and I certainly do not want them nodding along.

Design an in-class game that makes the second failure land as a discovery rather than a confirmation. The mechanism has to leave each veto-holder acting rationally and even sympathetically, so that the deadlock turns out to be nobody's fault; if there is a villain available in the game, my students will locate the problem in the villain and the exercise will be wasted. I also want them to feel the asymmetry between the resource itself — which loses nothing by being used — and the reward attached to it, which is strictly positional.

Build it as three rounds over the same tasks and the same paper components, changing only the rules:

- Round one: components sit in a shared pool, anyone may take any, and the reward goes to whoever finishes a task first.
- Round two: each student owns the components dealt to them and must license them from one another to complete anything.
- Round three: components are free to take, but every submission must display the complete chain of attribution for each component it uses, including components derived from other components. I expect this round to look absurd on first description; I want it designed so that it does not, since of the three it is closest to how credit actually behaves in research.

Give me the component deck and what is written on each card; the tasks and how many components each requires; a payoff scheme using no money that converts into something these students will care about for 75 minutes; my own role, including how many disputes I should expect to adjudicate simultaneously; a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact wording of every rule I need to read aloud so that students cannot litigate it later; and the debrief, which maps particular moments from each round onto the concepts and does not supply the name of the second failure until the students have described it themselves.

Then tell me what will predictably go wrong on the first run — including the student who refuses to license on principle, the pair who collude to lock everyone else out, and the round that finishes in four minutes because one student simply hoards — and how to handle each without breaking the game. Include a version compressible into 20 minutes in case the first half of the session overruns. Finally, state frankly which feature of the second failure a single class period cannot reproduce; I suspect it is duration, since real thickets persist for years while people invest in working around them, and a game that resolves in minutes will suggest the problem is solvable by patience. Say also which live disputes about intellectual property each round corresponds to, and where that correspondence breaks down.
