He says it's the best thing he's read on the topic and that the whole office has been arguing about it, and our reading group is doing "civilizations under stress" on Thursday, so I'd like to be able to say something smarter than "huh, that was interesting." I'm not going to read three books to answer him, so I'm hoping you can. Here's my close paraphrase of the middle stretch of the article, where it lays out its case:

"Easter Island is the clearest example we have of a society destroying itself. The Polynesian settlers arrived on an island covered in dense palm forest, and over the centuries they cut it down — chiefly to haul the enormous statues they were obsessed with carving, and to clear ground for gardens. By the time the first Europeans showed up the forest was gone, the soil had washed away, the population had crashed from maybe fifteen or twenty thousand to a few thousand, the clans were at war, and the survivors had turned to cannibalism, which the islanders' own later traditions confirm and which archeologists can see in the bones. The lesson, in Diamond's telling, is that they were smart enough to see it coming and did it anyway: he asks, in effect, what was going through the head of the last person to fell the last palm. The Maya went the same way — they stripped their landscape, the rains failed, and the whole civilization disappeared into the jungle, where it stayed as ruins until explorers found it again. The Bronze Age is the same story at a larger scale: by around 1200 BC the eastern Mediterranean was so tightly networked that when a few centers were hit by invasion and famine everything from the Aegean to Egypt came down together, and within a generation there were no palaces, no international trade, and no writing anywhere in the region. What these cases establish is that complexity is a trap: societies keep adding layers of organization until the marginal cost of the next layer exceeds its benefit, and then they don't decline gradually, they fail outright. The academics who insist that collapse is a loaded word and that these societies were perfectly resilient and merely reorganized are making a political point rather than an evidential one — people starved and cities were emptied, and dressing that up as transformation is comfort food for anyone who can't stand the idea that civilizations can actually fail. The only real question left for us is whether we're cleverer than the people of Easter Island."
