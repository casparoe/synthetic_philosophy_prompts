I would like help systematizing a set of verdicts I have arrived at concerning the relation between phenomenal consciousness and access consciousness, and what global workspace theory should properly be taken to claim. I am not after a survey of the debate and I do not want references or a reading list; I want a principle — one, or at most a small number of rivals — stated precisely enough to decide cases that I have not yet considered.

Two stipulations about my usage. By *access* I mean that the content in question is available to the subject for report, for reasoning, and for the voluntary guidance of action. By *phenomenality* I mean only that there is something it is like, in the moment, for the subject to have that content. I treat report as evidence bearing on phenomenality but never as a definition of it, since reporting is a downstream act with enabling conditions of its own.

My own first attempt at a principle is this: phenomenality is the content that *would* be available for report were the right cue delivered in time; access is the content that is actually so available. I think this is close but that it fails, and I cannot say exactly where.

My verdicts:

1. A twelve-letter grid is flashed for fifty milliseconds and a tone then names one row; the subject names all three cued letters reliably. At the moment the tone sounds, all twelve letters are phenomenally present to her, though only three are ever accessed. Phenomenality here outruns access.

2. The same display, but the cue is delayed long enough that the sensory trace has decayed, so that report of the cued row falls to chance. Here I want to say the twelve letters were *not* phenomenally present, or were so only for a vanishing instant. Yet — and this is the case I cannot settle — I stipulate that the introspective character of the two conditions is identical: in both, subjects are equally confident and equally disposed to insist that they saw the whole grid. I thus deliver opposite verdicts on phenomenality in two situations that are, from the inside, indistinguishable, and I have no account of why that is not arbitrary.

3. A patient with a scotoma discriminates location above chance while sincerely maintaining that he sees nothing and is only guessing. I deny visual phenomenality in the field. But I am equally unwilling to call this access without phenomenality, since the information is not available to him for report or for reasoning. My two-concept scheme appears to have no room for the box this case falls into.

4. A subject counting basketball passes fails to notice a person in a gorilla suit crossing the scene. My verdict is that there was nothing it was like for him to see the gorilla — not a case of something seen but unnoticed.

5. In a flicker paradigm a large object changes color between flickers; the subject registers that something has changed somewhere but cannot say what. Here I grant phenomenality of the changed region, in a way I withheld it in case 4. My rationale is that he has a determinable phenomenology — something there, changed — while the counting subject has no determinable phenomenology of the gorilla at all. I suspect this is a dodge.

6. A masked prime shifts a subsequent judgment without anything being reported. No phenomenality, I think; but I also resist calling this access, even though the content demonstrably shaped a decision.

7. The tip-of-the-tongue state: genuine phenomenology, but contentless — what it is like is the searching, not the word. If phenomenality can obtain with no determinate content at all, I worry that the determinable/determinate move in case 5 collapses.

8. The cerebellum, which contains more neurons than the rest of the brain combined: neither phenomenology nor access, and I regard this verdict as idle, since nothing about the case ever tempted anyone to posit experience there.

9. A subject in a condition where the fronto-parietal ignition signature that global workspace theory treats as the mark of consciousness is absent, but who reports a vivid scene fluently and immediately; likewise a subject with extensive prefrontal damage whose imagery and reporting remain rich. I want to say ignition is not necessary for phenomenality. I also want to say that a theory making phenomenality detectable only through report cannot be right. Those two commitments strain against each other and I do not know which to soften.

10. A machine built to the global workspace architecture that announces it sees red gets no phenomenality from me on that basis alone. Whatever principle I end up with must not be a purely functional or availability-based criterion, or this verdict goes with it.

What I want from you:

- A principle, or a short list of candidate principles, stated with enough precision to be applied mechanically. For each clause, name the case above that motivates it.
- A ruling on three cases I have not listed: (a) a subject who discriminates the orientation of an unattended grating at seventy-five percent while reporting that nothing is there; (b) a densely amnesic subject whose momentary discrimination and momentary report are intact but who retains nothing for a minute — is each moment of his experience as rich as mine; (c) the delayed-cue condition of case 2, except that the trace is destroyed by a masking stimulus rather than by elapsed time — does the masker abolish phenomenality or only access?
- A repair of my dispositional principle, or an explanation of precisely which case kills it.
- An honest statement of which of my verdicts the best principle cannot accommodate. If the cheapest principle that covers everything costs me cases 2 and 5, say so plainly, and tell me what should replace them. I would rather be told that one of my verdicts is wrong than be handed a baroque apparatus designed to save all of them — but if the baroque apparatus is genuinely the right answer, defend it.
