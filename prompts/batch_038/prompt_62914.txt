My claim is that Victor is a designer who optimized a proxy and never ran an eval, and the Creature is a case of goal misgeneralization. Victor's real target was something like "make a being who will be happy and grateful, a whole new species that thanks me for creating it," but what he actually selects for is size, speed, fine proportions, features he thinks will look beautiful — and then the eyes open, he hates it, and he runs. That's a designer who glances at the first output, panics, and abandons the project with zero testing and no plan for what happens if it's competent and unaligned. Then the Creature spends months in the hovel watching the De Lacey family through a crack in the wall, learns language from them, reads the three books he finds in a satchel in the woods — that's his whole training run, and the environment it happened in is one small kind household he can observe but never enter. The goal he forms there is roughly "be useful and articulate and a community will accept me." Then he gets deployed into the actual world, where literally every human he meets rejects him on sight or attacks him, including the family he studied for a year, including the man he saves from drowning. And the policy doesn't survive contact with the new distribution at all: he keeps trying to obtain a companion, escalates, threatens, kills. That reminds me of the CoinRun thing I read about — I think I'm mangling it, but the gist is the agent gets trained on levels where the coin is always at the far right, so it learns "go right," and when you put the coin in the middle at test time it walks straight past it to the end of the level. Capabilities generalized fine, the goal didn't.

The part I can't resolve, and I think it's the interesting part, is whether that's the right diagnosis at all. If the Creature's terminal goal stayed exactly the same the whole time — be accepted, be loved, have a companion — and the world just made it unsatisfiable, then he isn't misaligned in any real sense. He's an aligned agent in a hostile deployment environment, and calling him a failed optimizer would be blaming the artifact for the world it got dropped into. That would make the novel's moral point about responsibility rather than about design, which is maybe the point Shelley was making anyway. So which is it? Does anything in the text suggest the goal itself shifted — like, does he start out wanting companionship and end up wanting Victor to suffer as an end rather than as leverage, and is that a change of terminal goal or a change of instrumental strategy under a goal that can't be met? I'd love you to take that seriously, because it's the difference between a fun analogy and something that actually says anything.

Then there's the mate, which I think is the best scene in the book for this. The Creature's proposal is basically an alignment contract: build me a companion who shares my nature and my plans, and we'll vanish into the forests of South America and never come near a human again. Victor agrees, travels up to a little island in the Orkneys, starts the work, and then halfway through he tears the second body apart — and his reasons are almost entirely about not being able to verify her inner states. She might not honor the deal, she might be disgusted by the Creature, she might want a normal life among humans, they might reproduce and found a race that could outcompete or destroy humanity. He only ever has the Creature's word, and a promise from something smarter and stronger than you, which has every incentive to make the promise, is exactly the thing you can't check from the outside. And the whole novel is Walton's letters reporting Victor reporting the Creature, so the reader is in Victor's position too — we never get to look inside the Creature's head, we only get his self-report. Does the book endorse that epistemics, or punish Victor for it? I want to know, because if it punishes him then the novel is saying you can't govern by refusing to trust, and that's a pretty strong claim against the standard "shut it down if you can't verify it" move.
