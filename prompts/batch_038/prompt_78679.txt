I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student TA-ing the graduate metaphysics seminar this term, and next week's session is time travel. My advisor is a committed Lewisian and I've gotten tired of how alot of these sessions go: somebody recites the standard menu of responses to the grandfather paradox, everyone nods, and nobody ever puts the candidates on a common axis so you can actually compare them. So I made one up, and I want you to use it honestly and without hedging. Call it deliberative friction. Fix a resolution R of the paradox and imagine Tim knows R for certain — not as a live hypothesis but as something he's fully internalised — at the exact moment he's standing over Grandfather with the gun. Question: how much damage does knowing R do to Tim's ordinary practical reasoning? A 0 means knowing R changes nothing: Tim deliberates exactly as he would have, and his ordinary beliefs about what he's able to do, about what evidence bears on his chances, and about what he should try all stay trustworthy and transparent to him from the inside. A 10 means knowing R wrecks deliberation — either it paralyses him, so that he can't form any intention without begging the question about whether he'll succeed, or it makes his ordinary reasoning systematically unreliable in a way he has no internal means of detecting. Intermediate scores should mean intermediate things; please don't cluster everything at 5 or 6 just to play it safe.

Here's the list. Put these seven in a tier list from lowest friction to highest, give each an integer from 0 to 10, and say in a sentence or two exactly which feature of the view is doing the work in your score. (i) Lewis's own answer: Tim can kill Grandfather but could not have, because the act isn't compossible with the actual past, and the paradox trades on sliding between those two claims. (ii) The ability-based dissent, Vihvelin's line roughly: an ability requires that you succeed in some nearby world where you try, and there's no such world for the Tim who actually travelled back, so the ability claim is simply false and Lewis's two-senses machinery is surplus to requirements. (iii) Sider's autoinfanticide case — Tim goes back to kill his infant self — but read as a resolution rather than an objection, i.e. as showing that the real content of the paradox was never about ancestors at all. (iv) The consistency/coincidence family: nothing is impossible, physics just keeps throwing up obstacles — the gun jams, a bird flies accross the line of fire, Tim's nerve goes — and the coincidences required are wildly improbable rather than nomologically banned. (v) Deutsch–Lockwood many-worlds branching: Tim lands in another branch, so the Grandfather he kills was never his. (vi) Chronology protection: closed timelike curves can't be built or can't be traversed, so the setup never arises in the first place. (vii) The quiet fatalist line: no backward causation, so the attempt never happens, and there's nothing further to explain.

Two follow-ups that I care about more than the tier list itself. First: does your ordering survive if you re-run it on a target whose continued existence Tim's own doesn't depend on, and where the historical record is silent about whether anybody ever made an attempt — say an unremarkable stranger who merely went on to live a long life? I suspect (iii) and (iv) swap places, but I can't tell whether that's an insight or just me wanting it to be. Second, and more importantly: is low friction even a virtue in a resolution? My worry is that the rubric rewards whatever says least about the physics, since a view that stays silent about why Tim fails is also a view that leaves his ordinary reasoning untouched — which would mean my nice clean ranking is really just measuring reticence. If you think the rubric tracks something worth tracking, defend it against that worry. If you think it's a bad axis, say so plainly, propose the replacement you'd use, and score all seven on yours as well. And finish by telling me which single candidate I should build the fifty minutes around.
