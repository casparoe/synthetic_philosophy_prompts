I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and a big chunk of my beat is foreign-policy-adjacent — historians, think tanks, the argument-of-the-week cycle. Over maybe a decade of reading and assigning peices I've kept hitting the same failure mode and I've never been able to find a name for it. Help me work out whether it's a real thing with a concept attached, or whether it needs one.

Here's what happens. Someone writes that a current concession is Munich 1938 — appeasement, the aggressor learns that force pays, and so on. A historian replies with the disanalogies: there's no revanchist industrial power sitting in the same position, the domestic politics don't line up, the "credibility" being defended isn't the same kind of thing. And then the weird part: nobody refutes the historian, and the analogy doesn't get weaker. Instead the whole argument relocates. Now the question on the table is "is this appeasement?" and "who's playing Chamberlain?", and to object you have to answer those questions in those words. The historian wins the exchange and loses the argument, because the exchange is now happening inside a vocabulary the analogy supplied. Facts that don't fit the script don't get rebutted — they just don't register as the same sort of fact. A trade dispute becomes a test of resolve; economic interdependence is somehow off-topic.

Same shape with the Thucydides trap stuff. Once a story is written as rising power versus ruling power, everything gets sorted into that grammar, and the only counter anyone has is a dull negative sentence — "this isn't 431 BC" — against a vivid one. The debate then continues at full volume for thousands of words. Nobody is shutting it down. It's that the analogy has decided what the questions are.

So the thing I want a handle on isn't "an analogy misleads," and it isn't framing in the loose sense — every description frames something, that's not the failure. It's specifically that the analogy stops being a premise you can weigh and becomes the vocabulary in which the situation gets described, so that arguing against it first requires stepping outside the language it hands you, which almost nobody on a deadline does. The diagnostic I keep using informally: you can tell it's happened when the questions being argued are the analogy's questions rather than the situation's.

Things I've considered and don't think fit. Thought-terminating cliché — kind of the opposite, the talk multiplies. Theory-ladenness of observation — that's about perception, and here both people can see the same facts fine. Kuhnian paradigm — too tied to scientific communities. Loaded question — right flavor, but it's one interrogative, not a whole vocabulary. Begging the question — nobody states the analogy as a premise, so there's nothing to catch.

What I want back, in American English please:

1. Does this have an established name? If it does, or if something comes close, give me the two or three nearest concepts and be precise about exactly where each stops short of what I described. I care way more about the carving than the label.
2. If nothing fits, coin one and define it properly: the necessary conditions (what has to be true before you're allowed to say it happened), the test that separates a captured debate from an ordinary well-framed one, and two or three boundary cases with your verdict on each — does it still count when the analogy is actually apt? when the person using it is arguing in good faith? when a counter-vocabulary exists but is just less fun to write?
3. No reading list, no citations, no author-date name-dropping — I can't chase sources for this and a guessed-at reference is worse than none. Just name the concepts and say what each claims.

For the part I'll actually reuse in a column, end with a table using exactly these columns: candidate concept | what it claims (one line) | where it falls short of my description | fit (1-5) | keep or discard. Then, immediately after the table, one JSON object and nothing else, with these fields: "term" (the name you endorse or coin), "definition" (one sentence), "mechanism" (why the disanalogy argument fails to land even when it's correct), "necessary_conditions" (array of strings), "diagnostic_test" (string), "not_this" (array of objects with "concept" and "distinction"), "boundary_cases" (array of objects with "case", "verdict", "reason").
