You're a mechanism designer who teaches a lot on the side — specifically someone who has actually run prediction-market games with undergrads and lived to tell the tale, including all the ways they fell over. Please think carefully and show me some of your working before you commit to a design.

Here's the situation. I'm running a 90-minute session for 24 people: mostly final-year PPE students, plus about six folk from the local council who signed up through our public engagement programme. Ages 21 to 58. Nobody has done anything like this before. Roughly half have heard the word 'futarchy' in passing and not one of them could tell you what it means. Room has six tables of four, a whiteboard, and a printer. There's no budget, so no real money — tokens, sweets or points are all fine, but whatever scoring system you use has to genuinely change how people behave, not just be decorative.

What I want them to walk out seeing, in their bones rather than from my mouth: that a conditional prediction market — the engine of futarchy, where you vote on what you want and bet on what will happen, and the choice gets made by whichever option the market likes better — has a structural problem that no amount of clever trader behaviour can fix. Concretely, I want them to run into these four things in this order, and I don't want to name any of them until the end:

1. Only the policy that actually gets adopted ever resolves. So people should finish the game holding paper in the losing conditional that is worth nothing, and should gradually realise that nothing they or anyone else could ever do will show whether that price was right.
2. At least one role should have a payoff that depends on which policy wins rather than on being correct about it — someone for whom an honest price is bad news. I want the class to notice, late, that this person's trades look exactly like an informed trader's trades from the outside.
3. The market is thin. With 24 people and two policies, one or two token whales end up effectively setting the price, and the wisdom of crowds turns out to be the mood of whoever is loudest and best capitalised.
4. The price feeds back into the choice, so the prediction partly causes itself.

And the punchline I'm really after: I want a round in which the market resolves accurately on the adopted policy, everyone congratulates the mechanism on having worked beautifully, and it then dawns on them that the decision was still the wrong one — because all the information about the rejected option was garbage that will never be tested. Futarchy can look like it succeeded even when it failed, and from inside the game there is no way to tell the difference.
