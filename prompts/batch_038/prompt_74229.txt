I chair a nine-member Mosquito Abatement and Vector Control Advisory Board for a county whose jurisdiction includes a 3,100-acre brackish marsh complex bordered on two sides by incorporated towns of roughly 18,000 people between them. Last season we recorded four locally acquired cases of a mosquito-borne arbovirus, our sentinel flock seroconverted at three of five stations, and the state health department has now issued a directive requiring suppression of adult biting pressure below a specified threshold before the season peaks. Three options are on the table. Option A is season-long area-wide ultra-low-volume adulticiding with a pyrethroid, fourteen truck-mounted and six aerial applications; our staff model an approximately 78 percent reduction in biting pressure and estimate direct non-target mortality of adult insects in the hundreds of millions per application across the treated area, including pollinators in the residential fringe. Option B is targeted larviciding with a microbial agent plus habitat management, applied by helicopter on a ten-day rotation for about eleven passes; staff model a 55 percent reduction and direct non-target mortality two to three orders of magnitude lower, with an uncertain effect on non-target midge larvae that constitute the food base of a marsh bird population already in regional decline. Option C is source reduction, public education, and personal protection only: essentially no insecticide mortality, a modeled 20 percent reduction, and the state directive unmet.

The complication is a clause this board adopted three years ago, drafted when we were thinking about herons, terns, and a resident rail, but written broadly: the district shall select the effective option imposing the least expected harm on non-target animals. By its terms that covers insects, and we have never decided what to do about that. The board is split in a way I do not think is merely temperamental. Our district entomologist has filed a two-page memo arguing that insects are unlikely to be sentient at all, pointing to the absence of anything like the centralized architecture found in cephalopods and crustaceans and to the implausibility of valenced experience in animals with lives this short and fecundity this high, and warning that the alternative is sentimental paralysis in the face of a genuine public-health obligation. A member appointed by an animal-protection organization argues the opposite: that even a one-in-twenty credence in insect sentience, multiplied by the numbers in Option A, swamps every other consideration, so Option C is the only defensible choice regardless of the directive. A third member, a wetland ecologist, argues that both of them are looking at the wrong ledger, because Option B's suppression of midge biomass exports harm to birds and juvenile fish whose sentience nobody on this board doubts, and because a single bird's death may aggregate more bad experience than thousands of insects'. There is also a fourth argument that keeps surfacing and that I cannot resolve: several members contend that the insects killed would simply be replaced within weeks by others leading lives that are, on any plausible view, worse than the ones cut short, so that killing is not the harm it appears to be, and one of them suggests the conclusion is that Option A is close to welfare-neutral. My own instinct, formed in twenty years of public-health administration, is to treat the state directive as decisive and the rest as commentary, which I recognize is not a neutral starting point.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the June meeting, roughly 1,200 words, recommending one of the three options, with conditions, phasing, or monitoring triggers if you think a bare recommendation is unwise. I need it to do five things. First, state the decision rule explicitly and defend it: how much weight uncertain moral status should carry, whether harms should be aggregated across individuals at all or whether the aggregation is doing illicit work here, whether to discount by probability of sentience or to adopt some precautionary posture, and how the bird-and-fish externality enters the same calculation rather than a separate one. Second, say plainly whether the least-expected-harm clause is determinate as written; if you conclude it is not, propose replacement language specific enough that a future board could apply it without relitigating this memo. Third, give the strongest case for the option you reject in terms its own advocate would recognize as fair, not a strawman, and say what that advocate should concede in return. Fourth, identify which empirical findings during the coming season would flip the recommendation, what data we should collect to detect them, and at what point in the season a mid-course review should be scheduled. Fifth, close with a paragraph of about 150 words suitable for the public record, addressed to residents who are either frightened of aerial spraying or angry about mosquitoes and who will not read the rest. Do not build the case on citations, references, or reading lists, and do not gesture at literature you cannot summarize on its own terms; I want the reasoning to stand on the facts I have given you plus argument. Please write the memo in American English.
