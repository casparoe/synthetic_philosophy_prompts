I'm three years into a political philosophy PhD and this term I got talked into a cross-listed seminar on the ethics of space settlement, where roughly half the room are aerospace engineering grad students who are extremely tired of philosophers waving their hands. So for my response paper I decided to do the opposite of hand-waving: I tried to write out, as a numbered argument, the strongest version of the position I keep hearing — that we have a duty to become a multiplanetary species — intending to knock it over cleanly at the end. Two problems. I can't knock it over, and it turns out to be arguing for things I definitely don't believe. Here's what I've got:

(1) If you bring it about that there exists a person whose life is worth living, and who wouldn't have existed otherwise, then you've made the world better. (2) Where a worthwhile life is lived doesn't change how worthwhile it is: the same life under a dome on Mars or in a rotating habitat counts for exactly as much as that life lived in Kansas. (3) The number of worthwhile lives the rest of the solar system could support, over the time the universe has left, exceeds by many orders of magnitude the number Earth could ever support. (4) Betterness adds up — more worthwhile lives, other things equal, makes for a better world, and there's no ceiling on how much better. (5) So colonizing space makes the world vastly better than not colonizing it, and the gap widens without limit the further out you extend the horizon. (6) Nothing achievable on Earth — halting the extinction event we're currently in the middle of, stabilizing the climate, even averting human extinction this century — closes any appreciable portion of that gap. (7) When you could bring about a vastly better outcome at comparable cost, you ought to. (8) Therefore we ought to be putting our resources into settlement, Earth-side projects are at best instrumental steps toward it, and if the choice ever arose between preserving the biosphere and getting a self-sustating colony off the ground, the colony wins.

The trouble is how much of myself this eats. (1) plus (4) seem to commit me to having as many children as I can possibly support, which I am not doing and which nobody in my family would countenance. (4) plus (3) seem to say that a world of trillions of people living in arcologies at just barely worth-it levels is better than ours, and that we should pave the oceans and the last old-growth forest if doing so buys more colonists — so an argument I built to defend the Earth turns into an argument for consuming it. (6) says everything I actually work on, which is environmental justice, is a rounding error, and my advisor's comment on the draft was that this is a red flag and not a discovery. And (7) doesn't look like it can stop at (8): if the colony wins over the biosphere, why doesn't it win over the entire current generation's wellbeing, provided the colony still gets launched? Half the seminar said (6) was obviously fine and half said it was where everything collapses, and neither side could say why, which is why I'm asking you rather than them.

So: which step do I reject, or, if the chain is actually valid and each premise is defensible, which one is cheapest to give up? I should tell you what I'm attached to. I don't want to touch (2) and (3) because they look like empirical claims and I have no standing to argue them with the engineers. I'd like to keep the first half of (8) — settlement is a genuinely good idea, I think — without the second half about the biosphere losing. Please don't just tell me the answer, tell me why the step you pick looked completely innocent to me when I wrote it down, and then price the exits I can see: if I drop (1), what happens to the ordinary thought that having a kid is a good thing rather than a neutral one; if I drop (4), am I forced to say a universe teeming with worthwhile lives is no better than an empty one; if I drop (7), have I conceded that ethics has nothing to say about how civilizational-scale resources get allocated. Also feel free to tell me (5) is where I've quietly changed the subject, because I have a nagging sense the comparison in (5) isn't between the two futures I think it is, but I can't get the thought into words.
