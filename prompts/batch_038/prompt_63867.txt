I build multi-agent systems — agents that negotiate, hand off tasks, and make commitments to each other — and I've been reading Austin on performatives and Searle on speech acts to work out whether "promising" is something I could actually implement. My native vocabulary is the systems one: messages on a wire, message types, preconditions and postconditions, commits and rollbacks, idempotency, at-least-once vs exactly-once delivery, state machines, policy/authorization checks, and the assumption that a message is either well-formed and admissible or it gets rejected. I'm fluent in that and only vaguely fluent in the philosophy.

Translate the speech-act picture into it as honestly as you can: what plays the role of message type (illocutionary force), precondition check (felicity conditions), state transition (the thing done in saying it), unguaranteed downstream effect (perlocution), and what direction of fit turns into if it turns into anything at all. Don't quietly assume my framework is the right one — if a claim only translates by pretending the world is a database, say so rather than doing it silently.

Then the part I actually care about, the residue:

1. uptake — Austin seems to think the act can fail if the hearer doesn't take it up in the right way. Is there any counterpart to that in my vocabulary, or is it a hole?
2. insincerity — a promise can be correctly made and still hollow. In my system that would be a commit that succeeded and wrote garbage. Is that the right rendering, or does it lose something?
3. indirect acts, the can-you-pass-the-salt case, where the type field says question but what lands is a request with an obligation attached. My protocols have no room for that. Is that a limitation of protocols, or a sign the phenomenon is being over-described?

For each, say whether the untranslatable bit is a defect in my vocabulary or in the theory.
