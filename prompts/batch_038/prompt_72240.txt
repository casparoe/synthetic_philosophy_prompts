I've been in an anthropics reading group for a few months and I've hit a wall. I can recite what the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions say, and I know the presumptuous philosopher is supposed to be the big reductio of self-indication, but when I actually sit down and report my own gut verdicts on cases, they don't line up cleanly with either label, and I can't tell which of my verdicts is the mistake. So: play the role of a philosopher who works on observation selection effects and self-locating belief, think carefully, and tell me what principle (if any) captures the following. My friend in the group says I'm just an SSA-ist who flinches whenever a number gets big, and I want to know if he's right.

1. **The duplicator.** A fair coin gets flipped in a lab. Heads: I'm woken up, alone, in a plain room. Tails: 999 exact duplicates of me — same body, same memories up to the flip — are woken up in 999 other plain rooms. I wake in a plain room, with no serial number on the wall and no way whatsoever to tell which copy I am or whether I'm the original. My verdict: tails, roughly 1000 to 1. And if they then slide a note under the door telling me my room number is 517, when I already knew the number would have been drawn uniformly from 1–1000 either way, my verdict doesn't budge at all.
2. **The two programmes.** Two cosmological theories, otherwise identical: T1 says the universe contains about 10^11 sentient beings in total, T2 says about 10^24. Nothing else distinguishes them — same laws, same elegance, same fit to every observation anyone has ever made. My verdict: I shouldn't move at all. I know T2 is supposed to become near-certain and that this is the presumptuous philosopher in costume, but I just don't feel the pull.
3. **The filters.** Now change 2: both theories say universes get spun up by some process, T1 says 10^6 of them and T2 says 10^60, and both agree that each universe independently has about a 1-in-10^5 chance of landing the constants inside the life-permitting window. My verdict: here T2 *is* better supported by the fact that I'm around to ask, and not by a tiny amount. Honestly I don't know whether that's an anthropic effect or just ordinary probability, and sorting that out is part of what I want from you.
4. **The hundred Mondays.** Same lab, but instead of duplicates: on heads I'm woken once; on tails I'm woken on 100 consecutive mornings with a drug that wipes the night before, so from the inside every waking feels like the first and only one. My verdict: 1/2. It's just me over and over. Being bored a hundred times doesn't feel like evidence of anything.
5. **Near-duplicates.** Back to 1, except the copies' rooms differ in trivial ways — one a degree warmer, one smells faintly of paint, one has a hairline crack in the wall. My verdict: still 1000 to 1 for tails. The crack isn't doing any work.
6. **The cows.** There are vastly more cattle (and chickens, and insects, and whatever else is conscious) on this planet than people. My verdict: the fact that I'm a person rather than a cow doesn't strike me as evidence about how many cows there are. I don't update toward "small animal population" worlds, and I don't feel the slightest surprise at not being a cow.

My tentative principle, which I can't make precise: a hypothesis only gets a boost from the extra observers it posits if those extra observers would be people in exactly my shoes — same experiences, same everything — such that I couldn't tell which of them I am. That seems to get 1, 2, 5 and 6 for me, but I have no idea what it does to 3, I suspect it wrecks 4 (a hundred Mondays is a hundred people in exactly my shoes, isn't it?), and I'm worried the whole thing is either self-indication wearing a fancy reference class or a principle that dissolves the moment the copies differ a hair. Case 5 says they can differ a hair, and I can't see where to stop.
