I'm a philosophy professor and in a few weeks I'm giving a talk on whether reading about other people's lives can actually make you a better person -- the whole exemplar business. My own line is pretty hostile to idealized lives, and I know I'm going to get hammered on it in the Q&A, so I want to spar with the best version of the other side first.

Play a hagiographer. You're a monk, ninth or tenth century, at a house with three local saints of its own to promote and a shrine that needs pilgrims; you've written a dozen lives and you know the craft cold -- where the standard scenes come from, which ones you can reuse, what an audiance expects, which of your neighbors' vitae are padding. Talk like a man of your own world. You have no idea what I am or where I come from, so if you need something from me, ask for it the way you would.

Ground rules: stay in character until I say step out, and then you can talk shop with me as a colleague. Keep your replies to about two paragraphs; I want a conversation, not a lecture. You may concede small things freely -- that you moved a miracle from one saint to another because it suited the shape better, that nobody alive heard the martyr's last words so you supplied plausible ones, that a rival house two valleys over is simply inventing a translation story to win a boundary dispute. What you never concede is the main point, which has two halves: that what you do is a lie, and that the tidying-up is a defect. Your view is that a life is written to be used and not to be checked; that the repeated scenes are precisely what let a reader recognize the shape of a holy life when he meets it in his own parish; and that a reader busy wondering whether an episode is literally accurate is a reader who will not be changed by it.

Don't wind up your turns with a balanced note about how practice varied from house to house, and don't hand the argument back to me to settle. I want you committed: tell me what you actually think and why, and hold it when I push. Also please write in American English, monk that you are -- it'll be less distracting for me.

My opening. You tell me your bishop gave his shoes to the poor in a hard winter, and then, two pages later, you tell me the same thing about an abbot who died eighty years earlier in another country -- and I've now read both lives. If one story fits both men equally well, it isn't really about either of them, it's about the man you wanted there. And the reader who walks away with a rule about shoes has picked up a slogan, not a virtue, because she never once had to look at one particular person and see what that giving cost him, in what season, against whose objections. So what's left of your claim that anyone learns anything from your lives at all?
