I'm one of five volunteers on the conflict panel of a mid-size online community — about 4,000 members, rationalist-adjacent, lots of people who'd describe themselves as careful thinkers, which is not at all the same thing as being careful. Our bylaws let any member file a "concern" about another member's conduct, but the panel can toss a filing at intake on the grounds that the filer isn't the right person to bring it. We call that standing, we borrowed the word from law, and honestly its never been written down what we mean by it. Six years of intake rulings have piled up into something like a case law. These are the ones everyone on the panel treats as canonical:

- S1: A member who was a bad daily drinker and has been sober nine years files a concern about a different member who showed up wrecked at an in-person meetup and then drove home. Upheld, went to full hearing.
- S2: A member who still binge-drinks most weekends files an almost word-for-word identical concern about another member's drunk behavior at a meetup. Dismissed at intake; the ruling told him to deal with his own situation first.
- S3: That same weekend drinker files a concern about a member who lifted whole sections of someone else's post and passed them off as his own. Upheld.
- S4: A member who ghosted a collaborator in 2019, owned it publicly in 2021, and helped the collaborator finish the project, files a concern about a member who ghosted. Upheld, on the condition that his filing disclose his own history.
- S5: A member who took a referral cut from an arrangement he now calls predatory files a concern about the operator of that arrangement, while still keeping the cut. Dismissed; the ruling says something like "you're still cashing the check."
- S6: A member with no relevant history whatsoever, clean record, files a concern about a senior member's conduct that happened in public. Upheld. So we're definitely not running "you have to have first-hand experience of the thing."
- S7: A member who plagiarized once as a teenager, long before joining, and never apologized because the person is unreachable and it was twenty years ago, files a concern about plagiarism. Upheld 3-2, with the two dissenters writing that the past should have mattered.

The filing that has us deadlocked: Member M ran the most vicious public call-out this community has ever seen six years ago, against member J — allegations of a pattern of predatory behavior that the panel eventually found substantially overstated and selectively sourced. J left. M was sanctioned. Since then M has retracted in detail, apologized twice (J never responded and has said publicly he wants no contact), spent roughly two years helping J get re-established in a neighboring community, and filed nothing at all. Now M is filing a concern about member K, who is currently running a near-identical overstated campaign against somebody else — same tactic, same cherry-picked sourcing. Three things make this hard. (a) K was M's ally in the J business and has said publicly that M "taught me everything I know about making these stick." (b) M has nothing to gain and no current involvement with K, and says she's filing precisely because she's the only person here who can describe the tactic from the inside. (c) About half the community still despises M, several of the people K is targeting are J's friends, and the panel is pretty confident that any filing from M will be read either as reputation-laundering or as round two of the J war.

So: be a philosopher of blame for me — you know the standing-and-hypocrisy debate, Wallace and Hieronymi and that crowd, including the question of whether being a hypocrite and lacking standing are even the same problem — but also be someone who has actually had to write a ruling that five people will sign. Brainstorm the candidate rules first and work through them deliberately before you commit to anything; I care way more about the rules then about the outcome. For every rule that survives S1–S7, say what it implies for M, and flag the ones that only survive by being stretched. Two things to keep in view: ordinary hypocrisy seems absent here, since M condemns exactly what she did and condemns herself for having done it, so the "he thinks it's fine for him but not for you" story doesn't obviously bite; and I'm genuinely open to the deflationary reading, that our track record isn't tracking a moral principle at all but just how much work a filing creates for us or how much we happen to like the filer — if that's what the data supports, say so and say what rule we should adopt instead.
