> "The puzzle of thought experiments is easy to state. When Galileo imagined a cannonball tied to a musket ball and dropped together, he showed that Aristotle's account of falling bodies contradicts itself — no tower, no timing, no new data. So where did the new knowledge come from? There are basically two answers on the table. The deflationary one, John Norton's, says it came from nowhere at all: a thought experiment is just an argument wearing a costume. Since any decent argument is either deductively valid or inductively strong, its conclusion is already contained in its premises, so whatever you seem to learn was already in what you believed. The narrative and the pictures are decoration — you can always strip them out and write down the plain argument, and nothing is lost by doing so. That's why thought experiments are never a source of evidence in their own right, and why the only interesting question left about them is psychological: why the costume is so persuasive. The other answer, James Robert Brown's, says that in the best cases the thinker genuinely sees a law of nature — not with the eyes, but with the mind's eye. Brown calls this intuition, and intuition on his account is a sixth sense for Platonic laws which, unlike a hunch, cannot be wrong: if you clearly intuit the outcome, you have the law. Brown knows how this sounds, but he thinks it's the only way to explain why the good cases work. Once you notice that a private sixth sense nobody can check is no explanation at all, and that Norton's account is far more parsimonious, the debate is essentially over. What remains is a curiosity about how clever prose works on the brain. (The inclined plane experiments came later, and were mainly for people who couldn't follow the argument.)"
