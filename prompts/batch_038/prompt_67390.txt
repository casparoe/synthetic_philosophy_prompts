I am a second-year doctoral student in history, and this term I am sitting in on a philosophy-of-history seminar whose current unit is the inevitability debate: whether large-scale outcomes — industrialization in northwestern Europe, say, or an Allied victory in the Second World War, or the collapse of the Soviet system — were going to occur regardless of what particular people chose to do. The seminar has split. One side holds that such claims are unfalsifiable mush dressed up as explanation; the other holds that dismissing them is a philosopher's reflex that ignores how the notion is actually deployed in historical argument. I have been asked to write a short methods memo for the group that settles, in advance of any particular case, what an account of historical inevitability would have to deliver in order to be worth arguing about. That memo is what I want from you: the specification, not a verdict on any historical question. Please do not include references; I can attach the standard names myself, and I would rather you describe positions than cite them.

Here is the raw material I have gathered, which you should organize, evaluate, prune, and supplement rather than merely restate:

1. The account must not be trivially satisfied by determinism. If the world is deterministic, everything that happened was inevitable in some sense, and a notion that makes that true of every event explains nothing — yet the account also cannot simply assume indeterminism, since many of the historians making these claims are perfectly happy with a deterministic universe.
2. It must say what is held fixed and what is varied. An inevitability claim looks like a claim that the outcome survives a range of changes to the antecedent conditions, so the account needs a principled way of selecting that range. My worry, which I want you to test, is that this selection does all the explanatory work and the notion of inevitability is left with nothing of its own.
3. It must handle granularity. "An industrial revolution in Britain" and "steam-driven cotton spinning in Lancashire in the 1780s" are different descriptions of overlapping stretches of events, and it seems possible for one to be inevitable while the other is not. The account must say whether inevitability attaches to events, to event-descriptions, or to something else, and what follows if it attaches to descriptions.
4. It must keep inevitability distinct from high probability, and from convergence — the idea that many causally independent routes led to the same outcome. I would like to know whether these three can genuinely come apart, and if not, whether the distinction the seminar is fighting over is illusory.
5. It must explain what the claim is for. In practice historians use it to argue that some agent's choices made no difference, or that a particular alternative was never really on offer. An account that cannot make sense of that use is not an account of the notion in play.
6. It must face the evidence problem: we observe one history, so what could ever support such a claim? Candidates that have been floated in seminar include near-simultaneous independent inventions and discoveries, comparative cases across regions that reached similar outcomes by different routes, structural constraints that shrink the space of feasible alternatives, and the cliometric strategy of estimating what an economy would have looked like without some technology. The account should say which of these, if any, are legitimate support, or else say why the question cannot be asked.
7. It must handle agency and redundancy: if the outcome was inevitable, in what sense were the people who brought it about causally responsible for it? Here the account has to say something about backup causes and preemption, or it will be incoherent about the very sentences historians write.
8. It must be usable by someone who is not a metaphysician. If the account requires a fully developed general theory of counterfactuals first, it inherits every one of that theory's disputes and is useless as a piece of historical methodology.

Treat the memo as the deliverable and do the following with this list: for each requirement, state it as precisely as you can, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of one camp — and if it is a camp preference, say whose and what they gain by insisting on it. Then tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidate accounts. Most of all, I want to know whether some subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, since if so the list is itself the result the seminar needs. My own suspicion is that requirements 1 and 2 collide with 6: making the notion non-trivial requires restricting the variations considered, but any restriction principled enough not to be arbitrary may be one that no evidence drawn from a single actual history could ever confirm. Say whether you think that diagnosis is right, or whether the genuine collision lies elsewhere — including if you think two of my requirements are really the same requirement wearing different clothes. A compact note on how the main available approaches fare against the list is welcome at the end, but keep it to a paragraph or two; I will build the scorecard myself.

Finally, two requests about how you write it. Be as committed as the material allows: for each item I want your own verdict on whether it is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of my side of the seminar, not a survey of what one might say, and where you are speculating rather than confident, mark it in a clause rather than hedging the whole entry. And if you think the framing itself is defective — if "inevitability" is not one notion with a spec waiting to be written, or if a memo of the kind I have described cannot be produced without smuggling in a substantive position on counterfactuals and so would quietly beg the question against half my seminar — say that plainly at the top and explain what I should write instead, rather than handing me a polished list that pretends the problem away.
