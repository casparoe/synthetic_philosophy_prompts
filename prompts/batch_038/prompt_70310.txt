**Condensed draft**

*Working title: The Fixed Point and the Feedback Loop: Why Gödel's Construction, Not Gödel's Theorem, Is the Interesting Part of the Analogy*

§1. Framing. When Gödel's incompleteness theorems are recruited into the philosophy of mind, they are almost always recruited as a theorem of limitation: formal systems have boundaries, minds appear not to, therefore minds are not formal systems. I want to argue that this framing has impoverished the analogy, and that the analogy's real content lies not in the incompleteness result but in the constructive lemma that delivers it. I distinguish *limit analogies* from *construction analogies*. A limit analogy takes a theorem of the form "no system of kind K can do X" and asks whether minds do X. A construction analogy takes a theorem of the form "any system of kind K rich enough to encode its own syntax thereby acquires elements that behave as though they were about themselves" and asks whether systems rich enough to model themselves acquire states that behave as though they were about themselves. The first invites a negative, exemption-seeking argument; the second invites a positive, mechanism-proposing argument. Nearly all the ink has been spilled on the first.

§2. The Lucas-shaped argument, briefly and (I hope) charitably. If the mind were a formal system M whose axioms and rules we could survey, we could form M's Gödel sentence and, seeing that M is consistent, see that the sentence is true; since M cannot prove it, we are not M. I set out the three standard pressure points — we would need access to a specification of M in order to construct the sentence at all; the move from consistency to the sentence's truth is available only conditionally, and the condition is precisely what is in doubt; and the argument assumes our own consistency while denying the machine's — not to refute the argument, but to note that all three pressure points are about *the conditions under which a fixed point can be known*, rather than about the fixed point itself. That observation is the hinge of my essay.

§3. Penrose's refinement and what it changes. Penrose shifts the weight onto the second theorem: if the truths ascertainable by an idealised mathematician were captured by some sound algorithm, then that mathematician, coming to know the algorithm's soundness, would thereby know its consistency, which the algorithm cannot do. I argue that this shift, though usually read as strengthening the anti-mechanist case, actually strengthens mine: the whole argument now turns on whether a system can *come to know* a fact about its own specification. Penrose needs the answer to be yes for the mathematician and no for the algorithm; my reading is that the question is the same in both cases and is a question about feedback, not about proof.

§4. What the diagonal lemma actually gives you. For a theory able to represent the relevant recursive functions and any formula φ with one free variable, there is a sentence G such that the theory proves G equivalent to φ applied to G's own numeral. Three features of this are routinely blurred in the philosophical literature. (i) The fixed point is produced by a uniform, effective construction — it is not discovered, and its existence is not mysterious. (ii) The self-reference is secured by a *theorem of the theory*: the sentence is not intrinsically about itself, it is about a number, and the theory proves that number to be its own code. I call this *derivative self-reference*. (iii) Nothing happens to the fixed point when anyone comes to believe it, prove it, or encode it in a new system; the construction is static, and the sentence's content does not depend on its being entertained.

§5. The feedback gap. Minds, if they self-model at all, do not exhibit derivative self-reference. A self-model is one of the causal components of the thing it models, so revising the model changes the target. I call this *constitutive self-reference* and I claim it is what Hofstadter's talk of strange loops and tangled hierarchies is groping at: a hierarchy that closes on itself in such a way that the higher level is not merely a description of the lower level but a part of it. My central interpretive claim is that Hofstadter's analogy therefore draws on the diagonal lemma's construction and on the record-player allegory — the idea that for any player of a given design there is a disc it cannot play without wrecking itself, and that adding resources to the player changes which discs are fatal — rather than on the incompleteness theorem as such; and that his resistance to the Lucas-style argument is not a side note but follows from the same reading. If that is right, the Gödelian source material is being asked to do something it cannot do: the lemma yields a static fixed point, whereas what self-awareness supposedly requires is a moving one.

§6. An alternative source analogy, and a serious objection to it. If constitutive self-reference is what matters, the better model is a self-hosting system: a compiler written in the language it compiles, or a program whose output is its own source. Here the encoder must handle its own description as part of its input, and any revision to the encoder changes what the compiled artefact is; the system is always, so to speak, one revision behind itself. I call this the *self-model lag*, and I use it to motivate a claim about first-person authority: because there is no fixed target for the self-model to be true of, the model's authority is not a matter of accuracy but of authorship — closer to a policy one is committed to than to a description one checks.

I then raise the objection myself. A self-hosting compiler involves no reference at all: it processes a text that happens to be its own source, but processing is not aboutness, and there is no fixed-point lemma in the background guaranteeing anything. The analogy may therefore be a pun on "self", and my reply — that closure under a transformation, not aboutness, is the operative feature, and that aboutness is closure under a transformation that also alters its own input — is the weakest part of the essay.

§7. Payoff. If self-reference in minds is constitutive rather than derivative, the incompleteness theorems have no purchase in either direction: they cannot show that minds exceed formal systems, and they cannot show that minds are formal systems. The debate between Lucas and his critics is then not settled but dissolved, since both sides assume that self-reference is the sort of thing a theorem could be about.
