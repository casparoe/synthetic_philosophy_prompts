So last night I got into a nearly three-hour argument with my friend Dewi about whether Rome fell, neither of us won, and the annoying thing is I've now got to give a version of my own view as an actual talk in eleven days. I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, not a Roman historian — the period is a side interest, my real angle is what kind of question "did X fall?" even is — which is why I'm nervous about the Q&A and want a prep sheet rather than another argument. The thesis I'm defending: the catastrophist/continuity fight, roughly the Ward-Perkins side against the late-antiquity-transformation line that goes back to Peter Brown's book in the early seventies, is mostly a scale confusion, because "Rome" is doing duty for at least four distinguishable things that came apart at wildly different speeds between about 400 and 700. (a) A fiscal-military machine: tax in kind, the annona, armies paid and supplied by the state, the imperial arms factories, the whole grain-and-transport circuit. (b) A package of mass material consumption: wheel-thrown pottery travelling long distances, roof tile, amphorae, cattle that are a decent size, an ordinary household being able to get a serviceable pot. (c) A political identity people claimed and got recognised for: calling yourself Roman, being ruled by someone who claimed the title, having an emperor sitting in your mental map of the world as a live option. (d) An institutional and legal grammar that successor regimes kept borrowing: bishops in Roman sees, Latin, Roman ranks and titles, talk of a res publica. My claim is that (a) and (b) collapsed hard and fast in the West, and that the collapse of (a) caused the collapse of (b), because once tax stopped paying for bulk long-distance transport the whole consumption package stopped being affordable; that (c) went strange rather than ending — the East kept it alive for real and western kings kept renting it off them; and that (d) basically never stopped. So "did Rome fall" has a determinate answer once you say which of those you mean, and the reason both camps sound so smug is that each is scoring on the scale where it's strongest while pretending there's only one scale.

The room is the problem. It's the winter lecture of a county archaeological society, about sixty people. Most are retired amateurs whose Roman Britain is enthusiastic, specific and forty years old, who think 410 is a date and the legions leaving is the event, and who would quite enjoy any talk that agreed things got worse afterwards. Two are actual professionals: early medieval archaeologists who work on post-Roman settlement and ceramics, hard materialists, who will want to know what evidence could tell my four scales apart and who can smell unfalsifiable identity talk from across the room. And there's one medievalist from the nearby university who leans transformationist, who'll push on whether (c) and (d) are genuinely two scales or one fudge, and who will accuse me of keeping the word "fall" in the title purely for the poster while doing all the real explanatory work with something like reconfiguration. I have forty minutes, twenty for questions, maybe two slides, and I do not want to spend the question period defining terms.

So: give me the prep sheet. Eight or so objections I'll actually get from this specific room, sorted by who raises them — the amateurs, the two archaeologists, the medievalist — since those won't overlap much, and then ordered by how much damage each does if I fumble it. Phrase them the way those people would phrase them, not in my vocabulary: I want the amateur one to sound like a man in a fleece who's read a lot about the Saxons, and the archaeologist one to sound like someone who is bored of theory and cares about stratigraphy and pot fabrics. For each, a reply I can get out in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is soft, and whether I should concede it up front in the talk or wait to be pushed. Then tell me the one or two things I have to hold no matter how hard they press, which single joint in my own argument is weakest — I suspect it's my causal claim that the tax collapse drives the material-culture collapse rather than just tracking it, but tell me if the soft spot is actually somewhere else — and the question I'm praying nobody asks. Someone will definately bring up lead in the water pipes, and probably also the weather, the volcanic cold snap in the 530s, and possibly Christianity sapping the martial spirit; tell me whether to swat those during the talk or handle them live, and whether there's a way to use them without sounding like I'm talking down to the room.
