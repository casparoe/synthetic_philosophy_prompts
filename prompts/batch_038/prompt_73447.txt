You are a philosopher of mind who works on the dispute between realist, instrumentalist, and eliminativist accounts of the propositional attitudes, and who also has real experience designing cases for graduate seminars. Please think carefully about the specification below before writing anything, and please write throughout in American English.

I teach a seminar on the metaphysics of mind in a department that skews toward cognitive science, and most of my students arrive already half-convinced that the brain, not the person, is doing the deciding. Each year I assign an exercise in which they build a case that discriminates between three positions on belief and desire: (1) realist folk psychology, on which beliefs and desires are genuine internal causal states of roughly the sort our ordinary practice posits; (2) Dennett-style instrumentalism, on which attributing such states is a predictive strategy whose success does not by itself settle that anything answers to the attributions; and (3) Churchland-style eliminativism, on which nothing does answer to them, and the folk taxonomy is slated for replacement rather than reduction. The trouble is that in almost every case students invent, (1) and (2) make identical predictions and diverge only about ontology, so no behavioral evidence can tell them apart; and (3) then loses by default, since a theory that never parts company with its rivals looks unfalsifiable rather than vindicated. Last year two groups reached for a perceptual illusion and a spider phobia, and the discussion was in familiar grooves within ten minutes.

I want a replacement case, built to order. The specifications are these:

- The scenario itself is one paragraph of no more than about 200 words, set in a single ordinary environment — a workplace, a household, a rehearsal room, a waiting room — not a laboratory and not a battlefield. The agent is one neurotypical adult. No implants, lesions, drugs, conditioning apparatus, or speculative technology of any kind.
- The scenario is written as a bare behavioral record: what the agent does and says, and under what circumstances. No theoretical vocabulary is permitted inside it — no talk of beliefs, desires, dispositions, associations, representations, or the brain. A reader should be able to form a prediction from the record before meeting any gloss.
- The three positions must be forced apart *predictively*, not merely ontologically. There has to be a specified observation, available to a third party who has only the public evidence contained in the scenario, on which (3) is right and at least one of (1) and (2) is wrong. Tell me plainly which position you have winning; do not hedge.
- Banned shortcuts: no phobias or conditioned fears, no perceptual illusions, no implicit-bias or association-test material, no ordinary weakness of will, no self-deception, no confabulating patients. Find another route to the same structure — that is the point of the exercise.
- The eliminativist's winning model must be concrete enough to be checked: name the variables it uses, the eliciting conditions for each, and what governs behavior when they conflict. Say what in that model, if anything, is not a belief or a desire wearing different clothes.
- No stipulated verdicts. The divergences must be generated from what each position independently commits to, not built into the description of the agent. If the case turns out to be one where (1) and (2) genuinely cannot be separated predictively, say so rather than manufacturing a separation — and then tell me what *would* separate them, and whether the case is still worth assigning.

After the scenario, deliver the demonstration: (a) for each of the three positions, the prediction it is committed to and the reasoning that gets it there, including where realism and instrumentalism fail for the same reason and where they fail for different ones; (b) the discriminating observation and why it falls out as it does; (c) the single structural feature of the case that does the work, identified explicitly; (d) what the case looks like with that feature removed — whether it degenerates into a routine prediction error that any of the three could have anticipated; (e) the strongest counterattack available to whichever position fares worst, stated in its best form rather than as a straw man, including the charge that the winning model is a notational variant of belief-and-desire ascription with the vocabulary scrubbed out, and your reply to it; and (f) one closing paragraph on how narrow the win is: whether a case of this shape licenses eliminating the attitudes across the board or only within a restricted domain, and what would have to be true for it to generalize.

Begin by sketching two or three candidate settings in a sentence or two each, saying why you set aside the ones you set aside, and only then write the case.
