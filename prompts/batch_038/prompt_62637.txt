I am writing a novel whose central consciousness is a middle-aged police reservist who spends the summer of 1942 shooting people he has never met, and who in 1962 tells an interrogator that he was not that kind of man. I have been working through the Browning–Goldhagen quarrel — the situational account of ordinary men and the account that makes the killers willing and convinced — and it strikes me that the two camps have never been made to sign a single page together. Draft that page: the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between a committed situationalist and a committed dispositionalist about perpetrator conduct would produce after a year of good-faith argument.

One scene is why I need it. My reservist receives an order in front of his unit that comes with an explicit and costless opt-out: the commander says aloud that anyone who does not feel up to the task may step aside, and nothing whatever follows for those who do. I want to know whether each camp can render the same man both taking that offer and declining it without making him a different person, and what each camp holds the choice proves.

Please structure the statement under these headings, in this order:

**What Both of Us Accept** — a numbered list of eight to twelve claims both sides can sign about the battalion, the killing, the peer group, and the postwar self-portrait, each phrased so neither side's vocabulary does covert work. About a page.

**Where We Still Part Company** — a table. Each row states one disagreement twice: once in wording a situationalist would sign without amendment, once in wording the dispositionalist would sign without amendment. Five or six rows.

**Cruxes, With Our Predictions Registered in Advance** — three sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of us. For each, say what kind of finding both camps agree would bear on it (describe the evidence by type — comparative records across units, testimony patterns, the distribution of refusals — rather than by citation), then record each side's prediction in a single sentence that could be checked later. Roughly six hundred words.

**What Turned Out to Be a Dispute About Words** — if part of the quarrel is really a confusion between explaining why a man was standing in that clearing and describing what it was like for him to stand there, say so, say which part, and say what remains genuinely substantive once the confusion is removed. About two hundred words.

**Coda to the Novelist** — two short paragraphs, one per camp, each stating what that camp licenses me to put in the character's head at the moment of the act and what it forbids, including whether either camp is committed to his having been able to do otherwise that day.
