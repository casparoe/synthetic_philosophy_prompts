Half my lab has been having the same argument for about a year and I want an outside read on it, because I genuinely can't tell anymore who's cheating. Context: we build decision-making agents, and one camp treats Good's theorem on the value of information as settled law. Their statement of it goes roughly: if your utilities are bounded, your beliefs update by conditionalization, the decision problem you face after learning is the same one you faced before -- same available acts, same utility function -- and the evidence is genuinely free to obtain, then the expected utility of choosing after seeing the evidence is at least as high as the expected utility of choosing now, because at worst you ignore the evidence and do what you were going to do anyway. From that they conclude you should never decline free information, and that the whole "information hazard" way of talking is confused, since a hazard requires that knowing something make you worse off and the theorem says it can't.

The other camp says the theorem is either trivial or inapplicable, and usually both. Trivial, because "you can always ignore it" is the entire content of the claim and the conclusion is smuggled into the setup; inapplicable, because every interesting case violates a clause. Learning things changes what you want -- that's literally what education is -- so fixed utility fails exactly where it matters. Bounded utility does quiet work wherever payoffs are open-ended. And the assumption that the post-learning policy gets picked by an unbounded reasoner is false of everything we actually ship, so an agent that knows it will overreact or get talked into something is *right* not to look, and that's a genuine negative value of information rather than a failure to meet the hypotheses. The case that keeps coming up is prompt injection: should an agent read a document it suspects contains instructions designed to steer it? The theorem camp says reading is free information and the hazard is a property of the reader's bad architecture, not the reading. The other camp says that's exactly the point -- the act of learning isn't separable from the rest of the problem, so "same decision problem before and after" is false by construction and the theorem never got a grip.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two sides are answering different questions -- one asking what coherence demands of an idealized agent, the other asking what a bounded agent with accurate self-knowledge should do -- and Good's theorem is being used as a bridge between them that it structurally can't bear. But I might be wrong, and there could be a real formal issue buried underneath instead, something about whether the ceteris paribus clauses can be discharged one at a time, or whether the partition has to be finite, or whether conditionalization is doing all the work and the dispute is really about updating. So diagnose it for me. Is this a verbal dispute about what "the value of information" even means, two different questions sharing a name, a disguised empirical question about how real agents respond to evidence, or an unresolved formal problem? Work from what each side actually says, not from which one you find more sympathetic. And then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis -- a distinction that dissolves it, a counterexample constructed so it survives both camps' standard replies, a theorem, a change of subject -- or argue that no progress should be expected and say why. I don't want a survey of value-of-information methods, and I definitely don't want "both sides have a point."
