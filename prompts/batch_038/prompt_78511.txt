I am in the final weeks of a political theory seminar on freedom, and for my closing problem set I constructed an argument against the republican account that I can no longer tell whether to accept or to abandon. My seminar leader read it, said it was either a fallacy I could not see or a reductio that proved considerably more than my premises warrant, and declined to say which. The target is the claim, pressed by contemporary republicans such as Philip Pettit and Quentin Skinner, that freedom as non-domination is a third concept of liberty alongside Berlin's negative and positive ones, irreducible to either: not the absence of interference, but the absence of anyone's arbitrary power to interfere with you, whether or not that power is ever exercised. My argument accepts the republican's own starting point and tries to show it does not stay where he puts it.

Here it is. (1) On the republican account, you are unfree with respect to some choice to the extent that another has the capacity to interfere with that choice on a basis not controlled by your own interests, so the slave of a kindly and indulgent master is unfree even while nothing is ever done to him. (2) The facts that make this true are therefore modal: they concern what another could do, not what he does. (3) But almost anyone has, as a matter of raw capacity, the power to interfere with almost anyone else; what stands between me and your arbitrary interference is always something contingent — your habits, your fear of punishment, the present disposition of the authorities, the fact that no one has yet thought to exploit the opening. (4) So on (1)–(3) each of us is dominated by a great many others, and non-domination can be true of anyone only where those contingencies are replaced by institutions guaranteed not to interfere arbitrarily. (5) Such a guarantee cannot itself consist in another contingent disposition, on pain of repeating (3) one level up; it must consist in those subject to the power having control over it. (6) Having control over the powers that shape the range of your options is just having a share in authoring the rules you live under, together with the capacity to govern yourself by them. (7) Hence non-domination obtains only where freedom is understood as self-rule, and republican liberty turns out to be positive liberty under a new label. (8) If so, Berlin's caution applies in full force: once freedom is identified with a properly constituted will rather than with the absence of obstruction, the person constrained by the general will can always be described as coerced only by himself and therefore free, which is precisely the formula regimes reach for when they insist their subjects are free because the regime expresses their real interests. (9) So either non-domination is not a distinct concept of liberty at all — it is a thesis about justice and security that sits alongside negative liberty rather than replacing it — or it is the concept Berlin was warning us against.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey of the debate. Which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent in context? I would particularly like to know whether the slide happens between (2) and (3), where a modal analysis of domination seems to get turned into the claim that every bare capacity counts, or between (5) and (6), where control over a power is equated with authorship of the rules; whether the regress threatened in (5) is genuine or is already blocked by the arbitrariness clause in (1); and whether (8) actually transfers Berlin's objection to republicanism or merely restates it in terms the republican need not accept. Then please price the alternatives for me. If I give up the step you identify, what does the republican owe that the advocate of pure non-interference does not, and how does he answer the Benthamite insistence that any coercive law necessarily removes some liberty? If instead I accept the argument, what do I lose beyond the third concept — and specifically, does the case of the well-treated slave survive as an objection to a purely interference-based account, or does my argument show that the republican's intuitions about that case were doing all the work and are equally well explained by values other than liberty? Finally, test your diagnosis on a case the republicans like to press: an employee whom the employer may dismiss at will, without cause, for any reason or none. Does your preferred repair yield the verdict that such an employee is unfree, and if it does, does it yield that as a claim about liberty or as a claim about justice? For what it is worth, if I must sacrifice something I would rather abandon the distinctness of the third concept than abandon either the well-treated slave intuition or Berlin's warning — but I do not want to make that trade if the argument does not actually force it.
