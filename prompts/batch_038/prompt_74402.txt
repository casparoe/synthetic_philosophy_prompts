I am moderating a forty-five-minute public Q&A with a historian of the Atlantic slave trade whose central claim is that archival silences are produced rather than merely suffered. On her account, the near-total absence of first-person testimony from enslaved people in plantation, port, and parish records is not an accident of decay but an output of the same administrative apparatus that generated the surviving documents; a gap in a collection should therefore be read as evidence about the recorders rather than as an absence of evidence about the recorded.

Please draft six or seven questions in American English that press the substance of that position instead of inviting her to restate it. Include at least one question on each of the following, and for each of those, a one- or two-sentence note on the answer she is most likely to give together with a follow-up built for that answer:

- how she distinguishes a silence that was manufactured from one caused by fire, damp, vermin, budget, or the plain fact that a community never used writing, and whether clustering of gaps around moments of legal jeopardy is diagnostic or just what survivorship looks like from the inside;
- what observation about a particular collection would count against her reading of it, given that the missing records cannot be consulted;
- whether reconstructing the interiority of the recorded from police reports, account books, and ship manifests reproduces the surveillance relation she objects to, and if she answers that she reads such documents only for what they reveal about the recorder, what is then left of the claim about the recorded;
- whether her framework privileges populations inside literate bureaucracies, licensing strong claims about what was suppressed while saying nothing about what was never suppressible in the first place;
- what a descriptive finding aid should concretely do with a gap she regards as produced, and whether naming that gap smuggles interpretation into a layer that archivists treat as neutral.

Nothing biographical, and nothing about how she came to the subject.
