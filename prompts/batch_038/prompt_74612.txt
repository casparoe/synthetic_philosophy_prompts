So, twenty years of rationalist blogs has left me with one incurable habit: I want the spec before I read any of the candidate answers, otherwise I just get anchored by whichever one I picked up first. This time the spec is the whole job.

Here's the situation. I'm putting together a forecasting exercise on the survival of long-lived political entities — empires, dynasties, city-state leagues, sovereign trading companies, and empire-adjacent things like the papacy or the various composite monarchies, plus modern candidates people will want to argue about (the UN, the EU, the current form of the US constitutional order). Forecasters get a list of entities that are alive today and predict whether each still exists in something like its current form in 2075, and then I score them properly: Brier, calibration curve, resolution, the works.

The problem is that every base rate I could possibly offer depends entirely on how I slice the class, and the classes are tiny, and half their members are still alive so the duration data is censored, and the units of analysis are contested before you even get to statistics. That famous figure going around for about fifty years now — empires last 250 years on average, ten-ish cases spanning three millennia — is exactly the kind of number I want to make impossible to produce by accident. I cant tell from it whether the author fixed the class first or the answer first.

So: write the desiderata. What does a theory of reference-class construction — or more precisely a procedure, I care about what someone actually does at the desk — have to deliver before I'd trust the base rate that comes out of the other end? Give me the full requirements list, each one with the case or consideration that motivates it, not just the label. And mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are just the taste of one camp of forecasters dressing up as method.

Stuff I suspect belongs on the list, include it if you agree and push back hard if you don't:

- it has to fix the outcome definition and the individuation of units before the class gets chosen, or class-selection is just motivated reasoning with a spreadsheet. What counts as Rome ending — 476, 1453, never, or does the papacy count as a continuation? Different answers give you wildly different lifespans from the same history.
- it has to adjudicate that very old fight in the philosophy of probability where one side says use the widest class you have reliable statistics for, and the other side says use the narrowest class that's genuinely statistically uniform with respect to the outcome. Those two rules point in opposite directions and I'm surely not the first person to want both at once. Say which wins here and why, and whether the answer changes when n is eleven.
- it has to handle right-censoring and survivorship without hand-waving, because most of my entities are alive, and alive is not the same as long-lived.
- it has to have an account of when this time is different is a legitimate move and who carries the burden of proof when someone makes it. Nukes, printing, administrative capacity, the fact that modern states can lose a war and not stop existing. If the answer is never, that's a position too, and I want to see it defended.
- it has to be pre-registrable: after the fact I should be able to tell whether the class was picked before or after anyone saw which number it produced. Relatedly, it should say what to do when the honest class choice gives a number the forecaster already knows is going to be wrong.
- it has to say how to combine the multiple classes any single case falls into. Every empire is also a state, also a pre-industrial or industrial polity, also a conquest dynasty, also a member of some regional cluster. Average the base rates? Weight by specificity? Pick one and eat the error?
- it has to have some story about what a base rate is even for in this domain, given that the whole point of a forecast is to be better than the base rate. If the procedure's only output is a prior, say so and tell me how much updating it licenses.
- it should be runnable by one person with a laptop and an afternoon. I'll admit up front this might be my laziness rather than a real requirement, so tell me if I should drop it.

Then the two things I actually want most:

1. If some subset of these can't all be satisfied at the same time, say so explicitly — I have a strong suspicion that specificity, small n, and non-stationarity form a triangle where you get to keep two — and tell me which corner to give up for this particular project. If the list itself is the result, that's fine, that's what I'm after. Don't soften it.

2. Which single requirement does the most work knocking out the ways people actually do this in practice? I want one, with the bodies counted.

Last part, keep it short — a paragraph each, no more: how do these four do against your list? (a) naive average of the lifespans of the dead ones; (b) survival analysis / hazard curves with censoring handled properly; (c) a hierarchical Bayesian model that borrows strength across overlapping classes; (d) skip the theory entirely and just let a large crowd pick its own reference classes and report the median.

No reading list, no bibliography, no names-dropping of papers — I'm deliberately working offline and a guessed-at citation is worse than none. Just the requirements, the cases behind them, and where they break.
