I'm teaching one 80-minute session in a law school elective on probability and proof — mostly 2Ls and 3Ls who've done two weeks of likelihood ratios, plus three JD/econ PhDs who'll swallow the room if I let them. Slides are banned in this course, so it's me and a whiteboard. Topic is the common prior assumption and Aumann's result that Bayesians sharing a common prior can't agree to disagree once their posteriors become common knowledge. I want them leaving able to say something intelligent about what two dueling expert witnesses still disagreeing actually proves, and right now they're going to walk in assuming it proves one of them is lying or bought.

Don't explain the theorem to me, plan how I should teach it. What order the pieces go in and what depends on what — my guess is that "your posterior is just a function of your prior plus your own private information" has to be nailed down before anything else, but tell me if that's the wrong first move. Build the session on one example with two experts in a case that looks like a real one (no hats, no envelopes, no muddy children) and it has to do double duty: first showing that identical priors plus different private evidence legitimately produce different numbers, then getting turned around so those same two people, once they've said their numbers out loud across a deposition table, are squeezed into agreement. Say what that example has to have and what it must not have.

Then map the misconceptions and pin down the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off. My guesses: common prior = they'd have agreed before the trial started, common knowledge = both of them know it, and "different priors" as an all-purpose get-out card. Two of the econ students will have read somewhere that the assumption isn't even testable, since it only constrains beliefs about evidence nobody actually has — where does that objection get raised, and do I concede it? Also tell me whether there's room for the version where they announce and re-announce and the numbers iterate together, or whether I cut that and just do the static result. And the no-speculative-trade cousin of all this: ten minutes, or one sentence and move on?

Last, give me something I can run in the final five minutes that shows whether the idea landed rather than whether they liked me, and tell me what I should refuse to cover at this level and say out loud that I'm skipping it. No reading list, they won't read it and I won't check.
