I am co-organising an interdisciplinary workshop on personal identity and the metaphysics of quantum theory, and I have undertaken to circulate a short statement of my own position in advance. A colleague in the physics department, having read a draft, tells me unhelpfully that the position is incoherent but declines to say where, so I should like a rigorous audit of it before the volume goes to the editors. My package is this. (i) Unitary quantum mechanics is complete: there is no collapse, every measurement-like interaction produces branching, and every branch of non-zero amplitude is equally real and equally inhabited by persons. (ii) Identity is not what matters in survival; what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness, which can hold one-to-many, and in a branching world there is no further fact, over and above the obtaining of those relations, about which of the resulting people is me. (iii) It follows, I think, that I have prudential reason to care about all of my successors, and that the strength of that reason does not vary with how many of them there are, nor with the amplitude or squared amplitude of the branch on which any of them lies: a branch of weight one part in a thousand million stands, so far as my concern goes, on exactly the same footing as a branch of weight 0.999. (iv) Nevertheless I accept the decision-theoretic programme that claims to derive, from premises about rational preference alone and without assuming any probabilities, the conclusion that a rational Everettian agent must rank acts by the squared-amplitude-weighted average of the outcomes realised across the branches; I regard an agent who ignores branch weight as making a substantive mistake rather than a merely stylistic one. (v) Among the premises of that programme I accept the one to the effect that an agent should be indifferent between two acts that deliver precisely the same reward on every branch but differ in that one of them causes branching and the other does not. (vi) I hold a deprivation account of the badness of death: death harms the person who dies by depriving them of the good life they would otherwise have had, and the harm is comparative and agent-relative rather than a positive feature of anyone's experience. (vii) I find the quantum-suicide argument cogent — the premises seem to me to entail that if I repeatedly subject myself to a device whose lethal mechanism is controlled by the outcome of a spin measurement, I am, from my own point of view, certain to go on living — and yet I hold with equal firmness that it would be gravely imprudent to act on it, and that anyone who did would be making a serious practical mistake. (viii) I also accept a parity claim: a patient who consents to an operation carrying a one-in-a-hundred chance of survival, the alternative being death within the year, is not reckless, since survival is a live possibility and nothing better is on offer; and I think the Everettian gambler occupies a structurally similar position.

Two cases, offered to test the package rather than to decorate it. In the first, the device assigns weight 0.999 to instantaneous and painless death and 0.001 to unimpaired survival, and I run it a thousand times; the weight of the branch on which I survive every trial is therefore vanishing beyond expression, though not zero. In the second, the device assigns weight 0.98 not to death but to survival with catastrophic and irreversible neurological damage of a kind I would myself judge to render life not worth living, and weight 0.02 to unimpaired survival.

What I want from you is an audit, not a survey. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to; order the genuine tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list; and for each, state the cheapest revision and what that revision would cost me. Where a tension is merely apparent, identify the misreading that generates it — I suspect at least one of my commitments looks as though it belongs on the other side of a dispute than the side it in fact serves. Then tell me which single revision would repair the greatest number of problems at once, and whether the refusal embedded in (vii) can be earned from the rest of the package or must simply be surrendered as an unintegrated intuition. Finally, deliver verdicts on the two cases, say which of my commitments are doing the work in each verdict, and say whether the two verdicts are mutually consistent on any available reading of the package. Assume familiarity with the Everettian and Parfitian literature: I want the argument and not the apparatus of references, and I would much prefer a definite diagnosis to a diplomatic inventory of options.
