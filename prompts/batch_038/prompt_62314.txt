I have spent the better part of a decade on econ and forecasting forums watching people invoke Hume's specie-flow mechanism as though it settled something about monetary stimulus, and I have come to think both sides are arguing past each other because nobody has written down the spec. Before I read another line of the monetary essays — or of Cantillon, Thornton, or the modern non-neutrality literature — I want the requirements list: what any theory of the short-run real effects of a change in the quantity of money must deliver, in a world where money is a produced commodity that also crosses borders and where the point of the theory is to adjudicate arguments about stimulus and about whether a nation's price level is a competitive liability.

Fix the testbed as three injections into one kingdom, equal in total magnitude: (i) a one-time doubling of the coin stock from a newly worked mine; (ii) the same sum arriving gradually across thirty years as a merchandise surplus; (iii) the same sum seized at the border into the sovereign's treasury and never spent. A theory must be able to say something different about these three, or explain at length why it need not.

Deliverable: exactly five numbered desiderata, each one or two sentences, and nothing else — no preamble, no closing assessment, no scoring of Hume against the list. Each item must state the requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste, and mark it as non-negotiable or as the preference of a particular camp. Where two of the five seem jointly unsatisfiable, say so inside the later of the two, since that is the part I most want; and inside whichever item you judge to eliminate the most otherwise attractive theories, say that as well. At least one item should bind only under commodity money, with a clause on what it becomes under paper credit.
