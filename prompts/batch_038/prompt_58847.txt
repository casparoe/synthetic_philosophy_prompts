I'm a second-year political theory PhD student applying for my department's summer research stipend — three months funded, the only deliverable is one article-length paper, and the committee is about six people including one who clearly thinks anything written before Rawls is a hobby. I need a two-page proposal, call it 900 words, built around an idea I can't get into shape yet. Every reading I know of Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert comes in one of two flavors: either he's running a moral panic about the theater (actors traffic in their own persons, comedy rewards the wrong people, pity gets spent cheaply in the dark instead of acted on), or he's doing the civic-republican bit everyone quotes, where the answer to the spectacle is the public festival and the spectators become the show. My hunch is that both of those are downstream of an argument nobody treats as the main one, and its about esteem as a mechanism. On my reading Rousseau's actual complaint isn't that plays depict vice or that audiences are passive, it's that a theater is an institution for reallocating social standing: admiration migrates from the honest, prickly, unamiable Alceste to the agreeable Philinte, and once standing is allocated by how pleasant you are to watch, a small city loses any reliable public signal about who deserves it. That would also explain why he thinks a theater is worse for Geneva than for Paris rather than merely more avoidable — the same institution amplifies whatever mores it finds, so the harm depends on what the esteem economy looked like before. So the question I want to defend is roughly: does Rousseau have a coherent account of esteem as a scarce public good with an allocation procedure that politics can itself run, and does that account do explanatory work in the Letter that the passivity and corruption readings can't?

Write it as a proposal, not an essay — I don't want my reading argued for, I want the plan for arguing it. So: tighten the question until it has a determinate answer; say what the two standard readings get right and why neither settles this; then give the method as an actual week-by-week schedule for the three months (what I reread and in what order, what I'm hunting for on each pass, when I stop reading and start writing, and how I'd keep notes so the paper doesn't turn into a pile of excerpts). Be explicit about what would count as the project failing. In particular I want you to name the kinds of arguments in the Letter that would refute the esteem reading if I found them carrying independent weight — my worry is that the material about the actor's own morals, and the one about tragedy purging pity so the audience feels discharged, might not reduce to esteem at all, and if they don't, say whether the project is dead or just narrower, and which parts survive.

Two housekeeping things and one judgment call. Housekeeping: no bibliography and no literature review by citation, I'll fill that in myself; and don't quote Rousseau or anybody else, just describe the arguments in paraphrase and flag any place where you think a specific passage is decisive so I know to go pull it up. Judgment call: tell me whether the obvious closing section — generalizing from literal stages to anything where being watched allocates standing — earns two pages or whether it would make the committee read the whole thing as an op-ed. Give me a recommendation with a reason, not a menu of options. And end with an honest line on scope: if three months isn't enough for what you've laid out, say what to cut, because I'd rather hand in a smaller proposal I can actually finish than a big one they don't fund.
