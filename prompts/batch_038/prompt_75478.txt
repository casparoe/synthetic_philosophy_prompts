I supervise firmware for industrial control hardware — something like twenty years of real-time scheduling, worst-case execution-time analysis, interrupt latency budgets, watchdog timers, and the unglamorous work of proving a task set is feasible before it ships. My mathematics reaches about as far as convergent series and a little elementary analysis; my philosophy reaches about as far as having read the description of Thomson's lamp and one reply to it by Benacerraf, which a colleague sent me and which I followed only in outline. I would like the supertask debate translated, as faithfully as it can be made, into the vocabulary I actually work in.

The source material, as I understand it: a lamp is off at the start of a minute, is switched on after half a minute, switched off after a further quarter, on again after an eighth, and so on, so that infinitely many switchings are completed by the end of the minute. Thomson argues that at that final instant the lamp can consistently be described as neither on nor off — every interval in which it is on is followed by one in which it is off, and vice versa — and concludes that such a task could not have been performed at all. Benacerraf grants that the description fails to settle what state the lamp is in at the end of the minute, but denies that this yields a contradiction, since the state at that instant is simply not determined by the states before it. Now do the translation: the switching sequence as a job with a convergent series of execution times and no final release; the question about the end of the minute as a question about the value of a state variable at a point the run was supposed to have reached but never does; Thomson's conclusion as an infeasibility result about the schedule. Use my terms deliberately — release times, deadlines, jitter, minimum dwell time, contact debounce and pulse-width limits, bounded versus unbounded preemption, a task that never executes versus a task whose state is undefined at an instant, a specification that is unsatisfiable versus one that is merely underdetermined — and tell me for each which ones do real work and which are decoration. One thing I want handled explicitly: the asymptotic processes I deal with daily converge, a capacitor approaching its setpoint, a controller settling; the lamp's state sequence does not converge at all, it oscillates. Is that difference load-bearing in the philosophy, or incidental?

Then give me the residue report, in three buckets: which claims and replies translate cleanly; which translate only after being bent, and exactly how they are bent; which have no expression whatsoever in scheduling language. I expect the third bucket to be the interesting one, and I want you to police yourself while filling it — if the reason something "cannot be expressed" is that my vocabulary assumes every state has a minimum dwell time, or that a specification is feasible unless proven otherwise, or that an instant is a sampling point rather than a limit, then say that plainly and do not bill it as a discovery about the philosophers.

I also want my own view attacked rather than flattered. My instinct is that the whole paradox is the residue of a bad specification: no schedule with an infinite job sequence in a finite horizon is feasible, the device cannot be built, the state variable at the deadline is simply never assigned, and what remains is not a contradiction but an unimplementable requirement written down by someone without a feasibility check. Either show me that this deflation is correct, or show me that it changes the subject — since Thomson's point is supposed to hold for an ideal switch with no hardware limits at all, and since I have read arguments that Newtonian gravitation permits bodies arriving from spatial infinity in finite time, which suggests the escape route through physical impossibility may not even be available. Work the translation through three cases: the lamp as described; a variant in which the toggling is a relay flipping a single bit in memory, so that no contact physics, wear-out, or pulse-width limit is in play at all; and the supertask computer people discuss, which runs an unbounded brute-force search — over all natural numbers, say, to settle a conjecture or to decide whether an arbitrary program halts — and is supposed to emit an answer when the search finishes, where the question is what, in my terms, the output register contains at the deadline.

Finally, come down somewhere. I am not asking for a survey of positions or a list of options for me to weigh; I want a definite verdict on where the fault lies in the residue — whether the engineering vocabulary is genuinely missing something that Thomson's argument depends on, whether his argument was less clear than it looked and Benacerraf's reply is the whole story, whether my deflation succeeds, and whether the three cases I listed come out the same way or differently — with your reasons stated and with the steps in your reasoning that are judgment calls rather than consequences flagged as such.
