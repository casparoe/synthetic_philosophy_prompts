For a graduate seminar on perception, each participant has been asked to circulate in advance a numbered list of the commitments he or she actually holds about the relation between cognition and perception, so that the rest of the group can attack it. Mine is below. I have not been able to work out which of these support one another, which merely sit side by side, and which cannot all be true, and I would like that settled before I send it round.

1. Perceptual experience is cognitively penetrable in the substantive sense: what a subject believes and what she wants can alter how things look to her, and not merely how she is disposed to judge or report on what she sees.

2. My criterion for when an alteration counts as penetration is demanding, counterfactual, and content-specific. Two subjects count as differing in a penetrated way only if, matched on proximal stimulation and on perceptual learning history, a difference in some cognitive state produces a difference in how things look that tracks the content of that state. Differences in arousal, alertness, motivation in the generic sense, or the allocation of processing resources do not qualify.

3. Applying that criterion, I think most of the standard exhibits are misclassified. What the radiologist, the ornithologist, and the wine taster are said to see is a matter of perceptual learning and of the skilled deployment of attention, and is therefore excluded by the learning-history clause in (2). The same goes for the effects of fear and of bodily arousal on apparent size, slope, and distance, which I take to be cases of affect changing judgment and action-guidance rather than changing the experience.

4. Penetration is nonetheless real and not a merely verbal possibility. There are cases in which a verbal suggestion or a standing expectation alters the apparent colour or shape of an ambiguous figure, and in which the subject's experience itself, not her disposition to describe it, is what changes. These survive my criterion.

5. Phenomenal character is exhausted by how things look. I do not posit an additional layer of non-conceptual feel over and above the looks, and I do not think there is any phenomenological residue left once every look has been specified.

6. What entitles a perceptual experience to justify a belief is its phenomenal character. How things look is the justifier; the causal and developmental history of the experience matters only insofar as it issues in how things look.

7. The justificatory force of perception depends on its independence from belief. That an experience is not itself an output of the subject's theorizing is part of what entitles it to arbitrate between theories, and it is why a perceptual disagreement is not simply a doxastic disagreement restated.

8. Early vision is encapsulated. At the earliest stages of visual processing, computation is not open to the subject's beliefs and desires, and I want to retain that claim rather than treat it as an empirical prejudice.

Please treat what follows as an audit rather than a survey. Identify every pair or larger set that is in genuine tension, and rank those tensions by severity instead of reporting them as a flat list. For each, state which commitment would have to be abandoned or weakened, what the cheapest available revision is, and what that revision costs elsewhere in the list. Separately, identify the pairs that look as though they conflict but do not, and explain what makes them look that way. Conclude by naming the single revision that would repair the most damage at once. I have no antecedent view about which item is most expendable, and I would rather not be told that the list is under-described or that the questions are contested in the literature; I want to know what my own package commits me to.
