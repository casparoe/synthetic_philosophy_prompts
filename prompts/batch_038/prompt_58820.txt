I've been on rationalist blogs and forums long enough that the dutch book story is basically reflex for me: your credences are supposed to obey the probability axioms, and if they don't, someone who's cleverer than you can build a set of bets that drains you no matter how the world turns out. That's the version I'd have given a confident talk about ten years ago. Now I run a small nonprofit forecasting platform — about 250 active forecasters, real money paid out on a Brier-based formula that's already published and contractually locked until the end of the year — and every single cycle somebody's numbers are logically incoherent, with their own or with the rest of the set. 0.05 on a candidate taking the nomination and 0.30 on that same candidate being president in November. 0.6 on "company announces a partnership with A" while the three named partners sit at 0.45 each. So the idealization I grew up on is now my ops problem.

What I want is a procedure my team can run, not another argument about whether coherence is the right norm. Two people, no graduate training in logic, spreadsheets, about two hours per cycle. Anything that would change a payout has to go to our board's program officer, which means the procedure needs a default outcome that doesn't require sign-off.

I've read enough to know the naive view is contested and I want the procedure to actually absorb that rather than wave at it: Hintikka's impossible possible worlds and the surface/depth information distinction, Stalnaker's point that a single belief state can be represented against more than one way of partitioning possibility space so two "incoherent" numbers may be answering different questions than you think, and the old-evidence problem, where coming to see that A entails B is itself learning something and not a free move. Also whatever the empirical literature on unpacking and subadditivity says about which direction violations actually run. But I dont know how to turn any of that into steps.

So, deliverable: an ordered procedure that starts from a flagged cluster of related forecasts and terminates in one of a small number of actions — ask the forecaster to explain, offer a revision window, split the pair into separate scored items, exclude the cluster from payout, or score it as-is and say nothing. For each step: the question asked, what counts as a sufficient answer, what terminates the procedure early, and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values call or a wording call rather than a fact. Build in the checker's own limits — we can only catch the entailments we can compute — as a stated boundary rather than pretending we can be exhaustive. And handle the incentive problem: if incoherence starts costing people money, the cheapest response is to sandbag whichever leg is easy to game, and we can't fix the formula this year anyway.

Two hunches of mine I want you to either sharpen or kill:

1. I've been half-jokingly calling it the one-minute entailment rule — only flag a pair if a smart non-expert could see the entailment in under a minute, and only if both questions resolve by the same body on the same date. Tell me if that's the right cut or if it throws away exactly the cases worth catching.
2. I suspect violations aren't all one kind. Credence higher on the logically stronger claim than the weaker one feels like a categorically different failure from the reverse, and I dont know whether that's a real distinction with a real difference in remedy or just me pattern-matching onto noise.

Then run it on three cases. (a) The nomination/presidency pair above, with the wrinkle that the two resolve six months apart and the forecaster says they had the nominee dropping out or a contested convention in mind. (b) The partnership unpacking, where the three named partners are the only ones our resolution source would accept. (c) Our highest-paid forecaster of the last two years — top decile on Brier every cycle, reliably better than the crowd, and flagged grossly incoherent across six related questions about a single ongoing conflict. Tell me what the procedure says about all three, including if the answer on (c) is "pay them and don't say a word," which is not what I want to hear but which I suspect is right.

Mark the steps that are judgment calls wearing a procedure costume. And end with what the procedure cannot do — which kinds of incoherence it will structurally never catch, where it will false-positive, and what it costs us in forecaster goodwill to run it at all.
