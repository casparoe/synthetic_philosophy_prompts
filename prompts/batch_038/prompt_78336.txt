Act as a formal decision theorist with a background in welfare economics and philosophy of social choice, and write for an audience that is quantitatively fluent but has never taken a philosophy course: define every technical term in one plain sentence the first time you use it, and don't assume I know any of the literature. Think carefully and work through the cases one at a time rather than reaching for a summary answer. Before you commit to a principle, give me a short paragraph of brainstorming that maps which families of positions my verdicts point toward and which they rule out outright. I do not want references, a literature survey, or names of papers and authors — I want your own reasoning, stated precisely enough that I could turn it into code.

Here is my situation. I build an allocation system: every hour it distributes a scarce resource across a few thousand users by summing per-user utility scores that come out of a learned reward model, multiplied by weights I can tune. A colleague told me this isn't an arbitrary design choice, because of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem — as I understand it, roughly: if each individual's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the expected-utility axioms, and the social ranking over those same prospects also satisfies them, and society never remains indifferent where the individuals are unanimously indifferent, then the social ranking has to be representable as a weighted sum of the individual utilities, with equal weights if it also treats people symmetrically. If that's right, my implementation is forced on me by the mathematics, and my unease about it is just confusion. So I wrote down my actual intuitions, case by case, and I want to know what they add up to.

My verdicts:

1. Two users need the resource and only one can have it. I think a fair coin flip is genuinely better than handing it to either one for certain — better, not merely equally good — even though whichever way the coin lands, the person who loses would have preferred the other allocation. I hold this strongly, and I can't articulate why beyond the word fairness.

2. At the same time, I evaluate any risky prospect situation by situation: I look at what each user gets in each situation, aggregate there, and then take expectations across situations. I want to keep that structure, partly because it makes the system auditable — every ranking it produces can be justified by pointing at concrete per-user scores in concrete situations, rather than by appealing to anything about how the situations were bundled together.

3. It should not matter who gets what, only how much: permuting the utility levels among users should never change my ranking of two prospects.

4. A user whose expectations have been ground down — someone who has learned to be content with very little because asking for more has never worked — should not have that contentment counted at face value against a user with ordinary expectations. Equal scores are not equal welfare in that case.

5. My system spins up ephemeral worker instances. Stipulate, purely for the sake of the argument, that each has some positive welfare while it runs. I do not think that creating a million contented instances makes the outcome a million times better than creating a hundred, or better at all, if everything else is held fixed. Averaging over instances seems fine to me; summing over them does not.

6. A detail from the code that I suspect is philosophically load-bearing. Each user's score comes from a separate model head trained on that user's own feedback, so the raw scales are not comparable across users. I therefore z-score each user's scores over the trailing week before summing. I have always thought of this as a neutral engineering fix. I would like to know whether it is one.

7. The one I keep going back and forth on: whether a user's weight should depend on how much they have already received this week. Part of me says obviously yes; part of me says that is double counting a fact that has no bearing on how good this hour's allocation is.

What I want back:

Is there a single principle — an aggregator together with explicit commitments about prospects, populations, and the comparability of utility scales — that delivers all seven verdicts, or the largest consistent subset of them? If yes, state it precisely enough to implement. If no, give me two or three candidate principles, and for each one say which clause exists to handle which numbered verdict, what that clause costs, and exactly which of my verdicts the candidate forces me to abandon and why giving it up is a loss rather than a correction.

Then, for each candidate, give me its verdict on two cases I have not listed: (a) three users, where the resource can either go to one of them for certain or be split evenly between the other two; and (b) a prospect that every user prefers to another one in advance, but where the outcome that actually obtains is worse for everyone than the outcome the dispreferred prospect would have produced.

Finally, tell me directly which hypothesis of the theorem I am rejecting, if any — unanimous indifference, the expected-utility structure of the social ranking over prospects, the fixed set of individuals, anonymity, or the assumption that utility scales are interpersonally comparable — and whether verdicts 4 and 7, both of which depend on a user's history rather than on the situation in front of me, are compatible with verdict 2 at all.

End with an honest calibration section: state how confident you are in each of your main claims, and single out the one point you are least sure of, including what evidence or argument would most likely change your mind about it.
