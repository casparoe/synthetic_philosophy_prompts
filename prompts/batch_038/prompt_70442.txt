I do admin for a county invertebrate impact panel — six of us, mostly parks staff plus two appointed members, and nothing gets sprayed, dusted, baited or released on county land without us signing off. We've been going five years now and I want to write up our own case law before the new member joins in the spring, but I cant figure out what rule we're actually running. Here's the record, roughly in order:

1. Broad-spectrum pyrethroid perimeter spray around a middle school, target was ticks and a paper wasp nest under the bleachers: rejected. The minutes mostly talk about everything else in the flower beds dying too.
2. A bacterial spray on street trees for an outbreak of defoliating caterpillars: approved unanimously. Target-specific, ingested, they stop feeding within hours and go over a couple of days.
3. Pheromone mating disruption out at the county orchard: approved, almost no discussion, nothing gets killed at all.
4. Sterile-male releases against an invasive fruit fly at the edge of the nature reserve: approved 5–1, the one dissenter arguing it's still a program of industrial-scale production and disposal of insects. The sterile males die out within weeks and the eggs never hatch.
5. A larvicide in the stormwater retention ponds that keeps mosquito larvae from ever emerging as adults: approved, but only after someone moved to add a sentence to the minutes noting that the animals affected "never reach the stage at which the question arises." Nobody objected to that sentence and nobody defended it either.
6. Glue traps in the maintenance sheds, target was mice and not insects at all: rejected, hard and fast, explicitly on the grounds of how long it takes.
7. A kill-jar collecting permit for a citizen-science bioblitz, mostly beetles: approved 4–2, the no's saying we shouldn't be in the business of authorizing killing for curiosity.
8. Direct nest injection with a fast knockdown product against a ground-nesting wasp colony ten feet from a playground: approved. Small number of individuals, essentially instant.

Now the live one. An invasive ant has got into the riparian corridor and is gutting the food base of a native ground-nesting bird. The proposal is a fungal bait that workers carry back into the nest — very narrow host range, effectively zero bycatch, but it works slowly: the colony goes down over two to three weeks, large numbers of individuals, and nobody can tell you what dying of that fungus is like from the inside. The stated justification is entirely about the birds. There is no human health angle whatsoever, nobody's getting bitten.

Queued right behind it, so we'll decide them the same month: treating a native paper wasp nest at a picnic area with the same fast knockdown from case 8, because one kid got stung last summer. Small numbers, quick death, but the target is native and the reason is a nuisance.

What does the panel do on each? I'm less interested in the two verdicts than in the principle we've been applying without knowing it. Lay out the candidate rules that fit all eight precedents at once — I count at least three, something about how long dying takes, something about whether the harm lands only on the intended target, and something about that developmental-stage line somebody slipped into 5 — and show me exactly where they come apart on the ant proposal. I want to know which one I'd bet on and why, which precedent is the closest analog for each of the two new cases, and what we'd be committing ourselves to whichever way we vote, since a decision here becomes binding on everything downstream. Then name the cheapest additional proposal we could put in front of us next year that would discriminate between the candidate rules, because I think our record is genuinely ambigous and I don't want to write it up pretending otherwise.
