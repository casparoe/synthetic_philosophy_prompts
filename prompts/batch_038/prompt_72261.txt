Last term I sat in on a reading group about the meaning of life that spent two sessions on Schlick's short essay on the subject — the one that turns up in the collected English translations of his papers and is generally dated to around 1930, close to *Problems of Ethics*. Only one of us owned the anthology and none of us read German, so I will give you the argument as we reconstructed it and ask you to work from that rather than from memory of the text: please do not supply quotations from the essay or any bibliographic apparatus, and if you think my reconstruction is off, say so explicitly rather than quietly substituting your own. As we had it, Schlick begins by separating two things the question "what is the meaning of life?" runs together — meaning in the sense of a purpose that something serves, and meaning in the sense of value or significance. A life taken as a whole, he argues, cannot have a purpose in the first sense, since any purpose assigned to it from outside would either reduce the whole of life to an instrument or push the question back a step; so the question must be recast as one about the character a life has when it is lived well, lived more in the manner of play or art than of work, where activity is engaged in for its own sake rather than for what it yields. And the conclusion the group kept reaching for, the formula that by the end of the second week everyone was throwing at everyone else, was that life's meaning is youth.

I want to stress that this formula is our shorthand and not a sentence I am quoting from Schlick, which is part of why it proved so slippery: the same handful of words did at least three incompatible jobs in the room, and the people who used it agreed about almost nothing else. One member deployed it to argue that Schlick had quietly moved the meaning-of-life question out of ethics and into aesthetics — that on his view the issue is not what to pursue but what shape a life has, and that this is a real and underappreciated alternative to the goal-based accounts we had read earlier in term, on which a life is meaningful insofar as it is devoted to worthwhile purposes that it actually furthers. A second used the identical formula for the opposite end: that the essay's conclusion is not a claim at all but the expression of an attitude, and that a philosopher whose whole programme treats evaluative and metaphysical talk as cognitively empty cannot coherently close an argument on a vitalist flourish about youth — the same person noted, with some satisfaction, that Schlick's murderer told the court that this anti-metaphysical philosophy had unsettled his moral convictions and left him without bearings. A third used it dismissively, as a platitude: of course life feels meaningful when one is young, so the essay is a truism in holiday dress. I could see for myself that the formula might be read chronologically, as locating meaning in a phase of a life, or structurally, as using youth to name a property of activities rather than of ages, but I could not organize the possibilities, and I suspect that difference decides which of the three uses is even available.

So please lay out, as a menu rather than as a single favoured interpretation, the distinct precise claims this formula could be making. For each reading, state exactly what it asserts about the three terms that carry the weight — which sense of meaning is in play, what life ranges over (a single human life, the course of a life, life as a phenomenon, or a culture's life), and whether youth picks out an age, a stage, a temperament, or a structural feature of action — and whether the copula is doing the work of identity, of constitution, of temporal location, or of prescription. Then sort the readings: which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which, if my second member is right, are not truth-apt at all. For each of the three uses I described, say which readings would actually license it and which would not, including whether the aesthetic reading genuinely yields an alternative to purpose-based accounts of meaning in life or simply collapses back into one on which play is the worthwhile purpose. Finally, tell me which reading the argument I have summarized actually requires at its conclusion, and which reading people who invoke this essay most often have in mind while unknowingly sliding between the others. Close by reporting how confident you are in these verdicts and singling out the point you are least sure of — in particular, flag any verdict that would shift if my reconstruction of Schlick's argument misstates something, and say what sort of misstatement would shift it.
