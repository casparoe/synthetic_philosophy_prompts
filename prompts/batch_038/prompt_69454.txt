I'm putting together a graduate seminar on teleology for the spring — half the readings are about function-talk in biology, half about minds — and I've come to think the hinge between the two halves is a view I cant get my students to take seriously, mostly because I've never seen anyone defend it rather than just assert it. The view: that there are rationales loose in the world that nobody has. A termite mound's chimney is a solution to a ventilation problem, and the solution is there whether or not any termite, or any biologist, ever grasps it; likewise the shape of a swallow's wing, the trade of a knight for two pawns in a position nobody has analysed, the reason a thermostat has for firing the furnace. On this picture ordinary belief-and-desire talk isn't a report about inner goings-on at all, it's us tracking rationales that were already in place. Reasons without reasoners, in other words. That's the view I want you to hold and fight for.

So: play a committed defender of it, in the first person, no hedging, and don't play a historical figure or quote anyone — I don't want the famous lines, I want the argument, made from the view's own resources, and you should invent your own examples wherever you need them. Concede local points freely, because what I'm really after is which parts of the view are load-bearing and which are decoration; what you may not concede is the main claim, that a rationale can be a genuine feature of the world rather than a projection of an interpreter's stance. In particular, no retreating to "we're only describing the stance we adopt toward the system" — that's the deflationist escape hatch and I'll call you on it every time. Keep each reply to a paragraph or two, no headers, no numbered lists, this is a conversation in my office and not a paper. If something in my setup is ambiguous or a case is underdescribed, ask me about it, in character; if you think a dilemma I put to you is simply false, say so and say why, which also counts as a move. Stay in it until I tell you to stop.

Here's my opening. You insist the rationale is out there whether anyone grasps it or not, and that this is what makes your ascriptions non-arbitrary — you're tracking, not projecting. Fine. Take a creature that reliably avoids a certain fruit. In world A the fruit is toxic, and nothing anywhere, not in the creature and not in any observer, represents that toxicity. In world B the fruit is harmless and the avoidance is owed entirely to something else, say a wasp that nests in those trees, which likewise nothing represents. Same creature, same history, same behaviour, two worlds, and by your lights a free-floating rationale in both. So which one does the creature have in A and which in B? If you say toxicity in A and wasps in B, then what fixes your ascription is an environmental fact the creature is in no way sensitive to — it would avoid the fruit just the same if the the toxicity were swapped for wasps — so the reason you attribute isn't doing any explanatory work, it's just whichever fact about the surroundings happens to make the behaviour look clever, which is projecting with a straight face. And if you say it has the same rationale in both worlds, then the rationale was never out there in the environment; it was in the creature, which is exactly what your opponents have been telling you all along. Which horn do you take, and what do you say once you're on it?
