I'm auditing a philosophy-of-logic reading group that met in the library on Thursdays and spent a month on Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise — the one where the Tortoise accepts A and B, then agrees to the hypothetical C, then to D, then to E, and each time still declines to draw Z until the next conditional has been entered in the notebook, on the principle that, as he puts it, "Whatever Logic is good enough to tell me is worth writing down." Most of us came away with the familiar moral: a rule of inference is not a further premise, and the Tortoise's error is to demand that inference be represented rather than performed. A first-year in the group thinks we all stopped one step short. She says the Tortoise's demand can be turned around and used as an argument rather than a cautionary tale, and she has it scheduled for the department's graduate workshop in March. She calls the argument the Tortoise's Revenge, and it runs as follows.

(1) To reason from P to Q in accordance with a rule R is to accept that R licenses the move from P to Q. (2) That acceptance is a state with propositional content, namely the content that R is valid, or, for non-deductive rules, that R is reliable. (3) Any state with propositional content is one its subject could have failed to be in, and being in it rather than not is something that calls for a reason. (4) But a reason for accepting that R is valid would itself be a state with propositional content, so by (1) through (3) being in it would call for a further reason, and so on without end; the chain of reasons has no first member. (5) A chain of reasons with no first member confers no warrant anywhere along it — an endless debt is still a debt. (6) Therefore no acceptance of a rule as licensing is ever warranted, and hence no transition from P to Q in accordance with a rule is ever a warranted inference, deductive, inductive, or abductive alike. She then heads off the obvious escape. (7) Suppose the chain is allowed to terminate not in a foundation but in a loop, so that R is used to establish that R is valid, as people have repeatedly suggested doing with induction. (8) Such a loop licenses its conclusion only if the applications of R inside it are already licensed, which is precisely what was in question, so the loop begs the question. (9) And if question-begging circularity did confer warrant, then any rule whatever could license itself, including a rule permitting one to move from any P to any unrelated Q, since that rule too can be used to produce the sentence asserting its own validity; so the loop either begs the question or makes every rule as good as every other, and (6) stands on either horn. She is entirely happy that (6) applies to her own argument, and says that is the point rather than an embarrassment: she does not claim to be warranted in the reasoning, only that it is sound, and a sound argument can be one nobody is entitled to trust.

I can't find the exit, and I don't want to walk into the workshop and simply assert that regresses are often benign. For what it's worth, I'm most attached to (1) — I want an inference to be something an agent does rather than a transition that merely happens to her — and I'm confident (6) is false, since I performed several inferences in following it. I have no particular affection for (5), but I can't see how to reject it without insisting that an endless chain of reasons is somehow all right, which sounds like granting the Tortoise his point in new vocabulary. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look innocuous when we first read it through? I'd like the diagnosis to be specific — say exactly what is being conflated, or what assumption is being imported, at the step you pick — and then price the alternatives: what would it cost to give up (1) instead, or (3), and is (9) independently defective even if the rest of the argument survives? I'd also like an assessment of her closing move. If (6) is true she has no warrant for the reasoning that produced it, yet she maintains it is still true; is that a stable position, or does (6) collapse the way global debunking arguments are sometimes said to collapse, so that she ends up with self-refutation rather than a reductio of the project of grounding inference? Please be explicit about which parts of your diagnosis are settled and which remain genuinely contested among people who work on inference and rule-following — I don't want a disputed view, about (1) especially, handed to me as though it were the consensus. Write your answer in American English.
