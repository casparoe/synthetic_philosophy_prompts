For thirty-four years I was an applied entomologist in a national agricultural research service, working chiefly on the population dynamics of cereal pests and on the field evaluation of insecticides. I retired four years ago and am now in the second year of a part-time MA in philosophy. I must submit a dissertation proposal of roughly 1,400 words to my department's approval panel; the dissertation itself will run to 12,000 words, written between October and the following August. I should be grateful if you would turn my half-formed idea into that proposal. Please write throughout in British English.

The idea comes out of my old discipline rather than out of philosophy. Crop protection has for decades relied on a decision rule built around the economic injury level: the lowest density of a pest population at which the damage it will go on to cause equals the cost of controlling it. In practice one intervenes at a lower action threshold, so that the treatment takes effect before the population reaches the injury level. The rule is a piece of applied economics. It counts yield forgone and money spent, and treats the pest population purely as a negative quantity to be suppressed.

The moral literature on insect control, so far as I can make out, asks a different question: how many insects are killed or caused to suffer per hectare, and whether that number is large enough to matter. My suspicion is that the two frameworks are not merely different but incommensurable in an instructive way, owing to a fact the philosophical discussion tends to neglect. Insect populations are strongly density-dependent: remove most of the individuals from a field and the survivors commonly breed earlier, more, and more successfully, so that the number of insects born over a season may be little reduced — or may be increased — by an insecticide that killed a great many of them. If that is so, then counting deaths at a given moment may fail to identify the morally relevant quantity altogether. The choice of control regime may instead determine the shape of a population's trajectory: how many lives there are, how long they run, how they end, and which individuals they are, since different regimes produce entirely different insects — a non-identity structure, as I understand the phrase, on an agricultural scale.

What I want the dissertation to ask, precisely, is this: could an injury-level-style threshold be reformulated as a genuinely moral decision rule for pest control — one whose input is expected welfare across a population's dynamics rather than yield and price — and if so, what must it presuppose about the unit of moral accounting (individuals, whole lives, cohorts, or entire regimes), about aggregation across lives of differing length and quality, and about deciding under uncertainty over insect sentience?

Please give me: a crisp statement of the question, narrowed as far as you judge wise; an account of why the obvious existing approaches leave it open, argued from the positions themselves rather than from the literature, since I shall supply all references myself and want no bibliography; a chapter-by-chapter plan showing what I would actually do and in what order; a statement of what a successful result would look like, for which I had in mind a worked reformulation of the threshold for a stylised single-pest, single-crop situation of the kind I handled professionally, with the demographic parameters left as placeholders that I can fill from my own data; an equally explicit statement of what would count as failure; and a frank assessment of whether 12,000 words and ten months suffice, with a recommendation as to what should be cut if they do not.

Two further requests. Where I have assumed something philosophically contentious — the coherence of aggregating welfare across insects, the moral relevance of the non-identity structure, the thought that a threshold rule can be action-guiding at all — please say so rather than accept it. And if you judge the question as I have framed it to be unanswerable, or trivially answerable, tell me so plainly and offer the nearest tractable version in its place.
