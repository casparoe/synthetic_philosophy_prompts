I'm a third year CS student, mostly robotics/control, and I'm sitting in on a free will seminar this term because it fit my schedule. I can follow the readings okay-ish but I keep feeling like I'm arguing in a language I don't have, so I want to try something.

Here's the position I want translated, as our TA laid it out last week (paraphrase, so blame me if I mangled it):

1. Being mechanical isn't a fact about what you're made of. The wasp and the person are both physical systems; the difference between them isn't a difference in kind of stuff, it's a difference in what happens when the world stops cooperating.
2. The wasp in the example we read — it paralyzes its prey, drags it to the burrow entrance, goes inside to check, comes back out, and if the prey has been moved a few inches it starts the whole routine over, forever — is sphexish. Its rigidity only shows up once the situation leaves the range of situations it was built for.
3. So rigidity and flexibility aren't intrinsic properties of an agent at all. They're relations between an agent and a set of situations. An agent is flexible relative to a distribution D if it does something sensible across D, sphexish relative to D if there's a big chunk of D where it loops or idles.
4. Humans are non-sphexish relative to an enormously wide D — way wider than anything we've built so far.
5. And that's what free will is. No ghost, nothing contra-causal, just being an agent whose policy holds up over a wide enough range of situations, including situations where its own reasons have to be revised. That's also why we hold people responsible and don't hold wasps responsible.
