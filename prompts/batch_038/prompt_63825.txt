I am putting together a session for a graduate seminar on absurdism, and rather than rehashing the stock illustrations I would like a purpose-built thought experiment.

**Design target.** Construct a single scenario concerning one ordinary person such that Thomas Nagel's account in The Absurd entails that this person's life is absurd, while Albert Camus's account in The Myth of Sisyphus entails that it is not. The divergence must be generated by the two accounts operating on the details of the case, not asserted, and it must be located in an ingredient Camus's account requires and Nagel's does not. It may not be located in any feature Nagel argues is irrelevant to absurdity — the brevity of a life, its minuteness relative to the cosmos, or the fact that chains of justification eventually terminate. Both philosophers must be able to hold that the life in question is worth living; the case may not get its force from misery or from the character's suffering.

**Constraints on the scenario.**
- One paragraph. Contemporary and realistic. No metaphysical or science-fictional machinery: no immortality, no deity, no afterlife, no cosmic vantage point, no narratorial access to the character's place in the scheme of things.
- The character is vividly and unironically invested in what they do. The seriousness must be genuine, because one term of the collision depends on it; a person merely going through the motions will not serve.
- The character is also fully capable of stepping outside their own projects and seeing that the grounds for them are arbitrary, and the scenario must show them doing exactly that, articulately and without distress, in response to at least one concrete provocation that would ordinarily be expected to set off a craving for the world to make sense — a bereavement, a diagnosis, the discovery that a life's work has been superseded, or something comparable.
- The absence of that craving must be exhibited in what the character says and does. Asserting a temperament, assigning a philosophical label, or announcing that the character feels no need for meaning fails the brief.
- No pathological shortcuts. The character is not depressed, anhedonic, dissociated, sedated into flatness, or fooling themselves. Nor may the absence of craving be bought by any consolation Camus would treat as a leap: the character holds no religious, metaphysical, or quasi-mystical comfort, and has not resolved the question in advance by an act of faith in reason, in humanity, or in their own work.

**Deliverables, in this order.**
1. The scenario.
2. Nagel's verdict, together with the specific element of his account that produces it and an account of how the details of the case satisfy that element.
3. Camus's verdict, on the same terms, making explicit which of the two relata his account finds missing.
4. Identification of the single feature of the case doing the work. Then a robustness check: state what each verdict becomes when that feature is inverted — the same person, the same life, the same provocation, now with a felt craving for unity and intelligibility — and say whether the divergence survives, closes, or reverses.
5. The strongest attack each defender can mount on the case as described, including whether the objection is that such a character is impossible, that the character has been misdescribed rather than genuinely constructed, or that the scenario covertly supplies what it was supposed to withhold; and an honest estimate of how much damage each attack does.
6. A short reasoned judgment: does the case show two philosophers disagreeing about one thing, or two accounts answering different questions under a single word?

No references, bibliography, or verbatim quotation is needed or wanted; work from the two accounts as you understand them. If any constraint above proves unsatisfiable in combination with another, say so explicitly, identify which one you have relaxed, and explain why — do not violate one silently.
