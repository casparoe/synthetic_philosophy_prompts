I do model validation at a European insurer — mostly I review models other people built and argue about whether they can be trusted, so my working vocabulary is backtesting, effective sample size, prior and posterior precision, ambiguity sets and worst-case-over-the-set optimisation, tail-dependence assumptions, reverse stress tests, model tiering, and the whole apparatus of the "documented expert overlay" you reach for when the data doesn't cover the question. No philosophy background, stats masters, about as much Hume as I picked up incidentally. A colleague who reads more widely than me insists that half of what my function does was already argued about by Keynes — not the macro Keynes, the early book on probability — and I've been picking at it slowly, because the prose predates me by a century. I've come away fairly convinced he's onto something, but I can't tell whether the something is a substantive claim or a bundle of pre-formal intuitions that my machinery has since absorbed and made rigorous, and my partner thinks I'm wasting my evenings on a dead economist's philosophy book, which so far I can't prove her wrong about.

So: take his position on probability and uncertainty and restate it in the vocabulary I just listed, as faithfully as you can. The pieces I want carried over are (a) the idea that probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a proposition, rather than a number attached to an event or a personal degree of belief; (b) the claim that probabilities aren't always comparable to one another, that you can have a genuine partial ordering with no fact of the matter about which of two arguments is the stronger — his umbrella case, where a high barometer and black clouds can leave it unsettled whether to take the umbrella, and where he thinks it's rational to let caprice settle it rather than go on weighing; (c) the separation between the balance of favourable and unfavourable evidence on one hand and the weight of an argument on the other, which he says turns on the absolute amounts of relevant knowledge and relevant ignorance, so that new evidence always increases weight even when it drives the probability down; (d) his attack on the principle of indifference as a machine for manufacturing numerical probabilities out of nothing; and (e) the later material on long-term expectation, the convention of assuming the future resembles the present, and the spontaneous-urge business that carries you into action when there's no calculable probability to go on. Where my vocabulary has no word for something, don't reach for the nearest-looking word and move on — flag it and keep going with a placeholder.

Then give me the residue report, and be as blunt about it as you can. Is weight of argument just effective sample size under another name, or does the "relevant ignorance" half refuse to translate — because my terms count the data I have, and I genuinely don't know whether I have any term for the quantity of relevant evidence I'm missing. On non-comparability: sets of measures and imprecise probabilities look like they express it, but the moment I do minimax over the set I'm back to a total ordering of acts, so tell me whether that scalarising move refutes Keynes, quietly betrays him, or is irrelevant because his claim was never about acts in the first place. On caprice: our governance requires a written rationale for every override and there is no line item anywhere for "we let it be decided arbitrarily and that was the rational thing to do" — is that absence a hole in my framework or a sign that he's dressing up a shrug as an insight? And run the whole translation once against a concrete case, something first-of-kind with essentially no history, say correlated failure across a single shared cloud vendor, where I still have to hand in a capital number at the end — walk through what I'd actually do and mark which of those steps he'd have recognised and which he'd have objected to, and why. What I most want is honesty about the strains: don't Bayesian-ise him and then congratulate him for anticipating imprecise probabilities. For each place the translation has to be forced, say whether the forcing happens because my vocabulary is missing a joint that his cuts reality at, or because the joint he's cutting isn't actually there. End with a verdict — is there anything in his account that would change how I do my job, or is it all recoverable as folklore about model risk that we've simply learned to say more carefully?
