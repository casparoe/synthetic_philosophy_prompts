so I'm drafting a truthfulness code for the press office of a mid-sized city council (elected cabinet, ~600 staff, the usual local-government set-up), and I've been leaning hard on Arendt — mainly the Pentagon Papers piece and the truth-and-politics essay — to stop it turning into another values poster. Before I circulate a draft I want a consistency check on what I actually believe, because I suspect parts of it don't hang together.

The package:

(a) only assertions count. What's impermissible is saying what you believe to be false. Omitting, cherry-picking, framing, technically-true-but-engineered-to-be-misread — that's spin, it's unpleasant, but it's a question of style and judgement, not something the code can reach.

(b) why lying is bad has nothing to do with the liar's character. I take it that lying is a way of acting on the world, and the harm is what it does to the shared factual ground everyone else argues on. So the code should be written in terms of damage to the record, not in terms of officials being dishonest people.

(c) factual truths — what was said at the meeting, what the inspectorate found, what the water test showed — are the fragile ones precisely because they didn't have to be that way. Necessary or philosophical truths are less politically at risk. Protect factual truth first.

(d) truth is in a sense anti-political: it ends discussion rather than opening it, you can't vote on it. All the same the office should be as forthright as it can manage, and I don't think forthrightness is in tension with the fact that politics runs on persuasion.

(e) the deepest institutional failure isn't a liar at a podium, it's an office that has come to believe its own briefings. So the teeth of the code should be about internal epistemics: dissent channels, red-teaming, somebody in the room required to argue the opposite case.

(f) sanctions anyway: any member of staff who knowingly asserts something false in a public statement gets a written warning first time, dismissal second, no discretion, applies to the mayor's office too.

(g) keep some ordinary room to manoeuvre — not revealing your bottom line in a pay negotiation, not confirming the timing of an enforcement raid. Fine, and outside the code entirely.

The case I keep coming back to: suspected contamination in the supply, tests pending for 36 hours, a journalist asks whether the water is safe. The draft line is that the council has no information indicating a risk to public health at this time — literally true, engineered to be read as reassurance, and everyone in the room knows how it'll be read. My view is that this line is permissible under (a) and (g) and doesn't need flagging.

Two bits of machinery I've built in, so you can react to them rather than guess: a two-column log of every public statement, column one what was said, column two what the office expects the audience to take away; and a flag for review whenever column two diverges from column one without column one being false. Plus a reversal test before issue — would we accept this exact sentence from the opposition?

What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, the real ones ordered by severity, and for each what would have to go and what the cheapest fix costs me. Then which single revision cleans up the most. And tell me how sure you are that the ones you call genuine are genuine rather than my labels just being sloppy — mark the close calls, and if you think I'm failing to seperate two things that need seperating, say which.

Push back on the frame if it deserves it. If you think I'm turning Arendt into an institutional-design consultant she'd have no patience for, or that a code like this is beside the point of the problem she's describing, say that plainly instead of humoring me.

British English please.
