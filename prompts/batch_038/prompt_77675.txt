I am revising an early draft of a paper on the norm of assertion and would like a substantive critical commentary on the argument. The draft follows in full. It is early enough along that I care far more about whether the central claim is defensible and interesting than about surface polish, though please also flag passages that are unclear or that move too fast.

Some context before the draft. The paper's target is the debate between knowledge-first accounts, on which one should assert that p only if one knows that p, and alethic accounts of the kind defended by DeRose and by Weiner, on which one should assert that p only if p is true, with justified-belief and evidence accounts (Lackey, Douven) occupying the middle ground. I assume familiarity with that debate and with the standard cases. What the paper is supposed to contribute is the distinction introduced in §2 and the argument built on it in §3; everything else is scaffolding.

---

**DRAFT: Transparent Assertion, or Why the Norm Debate Is Fought on Confounded Cases**

**§1. The standard evidence and its hidden parameter.** Every case deployed in the debate varies three parameters at once: the truth of what is asserted, the speaker's epistemic position, and the hearer's access to the evidence the speaker's claim rests on. The lottery case, the stopped-clock case, the Gettier-style report, and the appeal to the ordinary conversational challenge — how do you know? — all hold truth-value fixed and vary the speaker's epistemic position, and all of them, silently and without comment, set the third parameter to zero. Nobody in these cases is standing in the room with the ticket, with the clock, or with the source of the report. That is not a neutral background condition. It is precisely what makes the case feel like testimony rather than like a remark. My claim is that once the third parameter is varied independently of the other two, the candidate norms come apart in a systematic and predictable way, and the stock cases have all been selected from one end of that variation.

**§2. The distinction.** Call an assertion that p *transparent* relative to an audience A when the evidence on which the speaker's claim rests is available to A as well, and A is aware that it is available. Otherwise the assertion is *opaque*. Two people in front of the same stopped clock, or reading the same printed odds, or looking at the same barn, sit at one pole; a report about a distant city, a summary of a paper one has not read, a claim about someone's childhood sit at the other. Transparency is a property of the conversational situation, not of the proposition asserted, and it plainly comes in degrees — but I shall argue that it functions as a switch with respect to which norm governs.

**§3. The argument.** On every account in play, the norm of assertion does protective work, and the work it does is to protect uptake: the hearer's entitlement to form a belief on another's say-so is what makes assertion a distinctive speech act rather than a noise. In an opaque assertion, uptake is a transfer. The hearer has no independent route to the truth of what is asserted, so the only thing standing between the hearer and error is the speaker's epistemic position. Protecting uptake therefore requires that this position be strong enough to bear the weight placed on it, and that is the rationale for a knowledge requirement — a good rationale, and one I want to keep. In a transparent assertion there is no transfer to protect. The hearer's warrant, if there is any, comes from the shared evidence, and the assertion is at most a prompt to attend to it. The protective function is therefore idle, and whatever governs transparent assertion must be answerable to a different consideration: that the assertion not corrupt the shared record. That consideration is alethic. So the knowledge norm governs opaque assertion, and a truth norm — supplemented by a belief condition, to exclude knowingly insincere true assertions, which the truth norm alone permits — governs transparent assertion.

**§4. What this does to the stock cases.** Two consequences. First, it explains why the lottery intuition is simultaneously so strong and so fragile. When we imagine someone saying that your ticket is a loser, we almost always imagine it said to a hopeful holder: we imagine opacity, or at least a hearer prepared to be moved. Hold transparency fixed instead. Two people look at the same printed odds and one says the ticket is a loser. There is residual oddness, but it is not epistemic. It is the oddness of saying something pointless, and of implying that one's evidence is better than it is — a failure of relevance and self-presentation, not the violation of a knowledge rule. Second, the account yields a prediction. Intuitions tracking knowledge should be strongest in opaque cases and should weaken continuously as transparency increases. Case-construction ought to be able to exhibit the gradient; my suspicion is that the standard cases were chosen from the far end of it and that this went unnoticed because the parameter was never named.

**§5. The obvious objection, and my weak reply.** If the norm is constitutive of the speech act, a norm that switches with the audience's access cannot be constitutive of anything: on this account there are two speech acts, not one assertion governed by two rules. My reply separates the constitutive core from the derived norms. The constitutive core, uniform across all cases, is that assertion presents itself as conveying warrant to the hearer; what varies is how much warrant there is to convey. Where there is none to convey, the presentation is satisfied by anything true, and the derived norm collapses to the truth norm. I think this is the shakiest passage in the paper. It requires the presentation to be capable of vacuous satisfaction, which looks like abandoning the idea that assertion represents the speaker as knowing.

**§6. A datum and a falsifier.** The datum: the challenge how do you know? is apt, indeed expected, in opaque contexts, and infelicitous — pointed, or merely puzzled — in fully transparent ones. If two people are looking at the same clock and one says it is three o'clock, the other's how do you know? does not request a source. It registers a suspicion that the speaker is claiming more than the shared evidence supports. The falsifier: a transparent case in which the assertion is clearly improper although true, and in which nothing about pointlessness, relevance, or self-presentation accounts for the impropriety. If such cases are easy to find, the paper is wrong in its central claim rather than merely incomplete.

**What I would like from you, in this order:**

1. **Fairness of characterization.** Do §1 and §3 describe the knowledge norm's rationale in a form its proponents would recognize, or have I smuggled in a transmission premise about testimony that they need not accept and that they would immediately disown? 2. **The distinction itself.** Is transparency versus opacity a principled joint, or a gerrymander designed to sort the cases the way I want them sorted? Is that coherent, or does the admission of degrees quietly destroy the argument in §3, since a partly transparent assertion seems to permit partly protective uptake?

3. **Novelty, bluntly.** Is §3 just the familiar claim that the norm of assertion is context-sensitive, repackaged with a new label? Would a referee say that the real content is a claim about conversational relevance that has nothing to do with norms of assertion? I would rather hear this from you now than from a journal later.

5. **The falsifier.** Attempt to construct the case described in §6. Do not reach for exotic examples; I want the most ordinary transparent assertion you can find that is improper although true, together with your argument that pointlessness and self-presentation cannot absorb the impropriety. 6. **Terminology.** Transparent is already heavily loaded in this literature — the transparency of belief, epistemic transparency, transparent agents in the sense of being visible from the inside. Which collision is most damaging, and what would you rename the property instead?
