Here's the question they were given:

> Two stipulated cases. **Case A:** a sleeper is woken after a monitored period of REM lasting about nine minutes. Their unprompted narration of the dream takes forty seconds, and they insist the events "felt like hours." In a control condition, the same sleeper is given a drug that doubles REM duration while leaving narrative report length unchanged.
> **Case B:** a sleeper is woken and says nothing about any dream. The experimenter reads them a plausible, generic narrative and asks them to react. Twenty minutes later the sleeper reports that narrative as their own dream, confidently, and adds two new details that were not read to them.
>
> (a) What would Malcolm say about whether the sleeper in each case had a dream *experience*? (b) What would Dennett say, and does his disagreement with Malcolm turn on what the facts are or on what could count as settling the facts? (c) Do sleep-lab results like the one in Case A settle the dispute between them? 400 words.

And here's the student's answer:

> Malcolm's view is that dreams are not experiences at all. For him a dream isn't an occurrence during sleep, so in Case A there was no nine-minute experience, and really no experience of any length: what actually happened is that the sleeper woke up with an impression and then talked. His argument for this is that the only criterion we could have for how long a dream lasts is how long the telling of it lasts, so if the telling is forty seconds the dream can't have been hours long, and any talk of dream time is meaningless. That's why Case A doesn't bother him — it's just a longer version of the point, the drug condition only shows that REM length and telling length can come apart, which for Malcolm is further evidence that neither of them is measuring an experience. In Case B he'd say the same thing, only more so: there's no impression to report, the person is just producing a narrative under suggestion, which is what all dream reports are anyway. So Malcolm comes out denying that anybody in either case dreamt, and denying that dreaming happens in the sense that anything goes on in the sleeper's mind.
>
> Dennett disagrees but not in the naive way. He doesn't just say "of course dreams are experiences, look at the science," he takes Malcolm's conceptual argument seriously and says the received view has to earn its keep. His move is that there are several possible models of how dream content gets into memory and when, and that we shouldn't assume the obvious one. The one he likes is the retroactive model: the sleeper really does have the experience while in REM, but afterwards the memory trace gets edited and expanded, which is why the report can be so much richer and longer than the underlying event. That's why Case B is so interesting for him — the added details show memory being written to after the fact, so the same thing could be happening in ordinary dreams without anybody noticing. And crucially Dennett thinks that in some of these cases there may be no way for us to ever find out whether the experience happened, which is a weaker claim than Malcolm's and leaves the received view standing.
>
> On (c) the Case A results look like they settle it, and I think they mostly do, because they give a measure of dream duration that doesn't depend on the telling at all. Malcolm said the only criterion was the length of the report, but if narrated length tracks REM length in the normal condition, then there is an independent criterion, and once you have an independent criterion of duration you have an event that has a duration, and an event with a duration that the sleeper is the subject of is an experience. So the empirical data refutes Malcolm's argument rather than just ignoring it. The drug condition is a complication because it breaks the correlation, but one could say it just shows the mechanism being interfered with, like a clock that's been tampered with is still a clock.
