Consider the November 2016 U.S. presidential election as a test case for futarchy — Hanson's proposal that a government let prediction markets decide policy, with votes fixing the measure of national welfare and markets estimating its expected value conditional on each candidate option. In the final days, contracts on a Clinton win were trading around eighty cents, give or take a few, and the outcome was a tail event for those markets. Please analyze the episode against the theory. First, be disciplined about the mapping: what is futarchy actually asking a market to do, and is the quantity the 2016 markets got wrong — a marginal probability about which candidate wins — the same quantity futarchy would rely on, or is that a difference the analogy papers over? Second, sort the candidate explanations of the miss (longshot bias and thin liquidity near the tails, uninformed, expressive, or manipulative trading, correlated errors shared with the polls, and the possibility that the markets were simply unlucky rather than wrong) into those that transfer to markets over policy-contingent welfare and those that do not. Third, deliver a verdict: does the episode confirm the futarchist's complaint about pundits and pollsters, constitute an anomaly the view must absorb, or expose that the hard problem was never forecasting but the choice of the welfare measure itself? Commit to one of these rather than hedging across all three, and mark the step in your reasoning you are least sure of. If you think the analogy is too weak to bear this weight — that a bet on a single winner tells us little about decision markets — say so plainly up front instead of performing the analysis anyway. American English, please.
