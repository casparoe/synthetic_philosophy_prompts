I am in the final weeks of a political philosophy seminar on markets and their limits, and for our closing session each of us must submit a short statement of the position we have actually arrived at, which the rest of the class will then attack. Before I hand mine in, I would like a rigorous audit of whether it hangs together. My commitments are these:

(1) A voluntary exchange between informed, consenting adults, from which both expect to gain, needs no further justification. I do not accept that a transaction must serve some purpose beyond the parties' own ends in order to be legitimate.

(2) There is nonetheless a class of goods for which introducing a market degrades the non-market norms the good depends on — care within families, civic obligation, the way a community regards the human body — and where I can show that such degradation will occur, the market is wrong even though every participant consents and benefits. I want to insist that this is a forward-looking empirical claim about how norms and relationships actually change, not a reaction to the idea of a price tag.

(3) Consistently with that, I reject repugnance as evidence. That a market strikes people as distasteful, or as treating something priceless as though it had a price, carries no justificatory weight with me. Appeals to dignity, or to what a good "means," must be cashed out in terms of identifiable effects on identifiable people's norms and relationships, or they drop out of the argument entirely.

(4) Applying this, I favor replacing the present prohibition on compensating organ donors with a regulated public system: a single government buyer, a fixed and publicly posted compensation package (a tax credit, contributions toward health insurance, coverage of the donor's medical costs and lost wages), no resale, and no private brokers.

(5) I think it is the features of that design, rather than the presence of compensation as such, that defuse the degradation worry from (2). The price is deliberately held below what a competitive market would clear at, payment is largely in kind rather than as a lump of cash, and the buyer is the state acting for a defined public purpose. I therefore regard the design as answering the objection, not merely mitigating it.

(6) I favor retaining anti-price-gouging statutes for emergencies. My ground is not distributive — I concede that the poor are outbid whether or not the statute exists — but civic: emergency pricing converts a shared disaster into an opportunity for extraction, and I believe this corrodes habits of mutual aid that the same community will need in the next emergency.

(7) I think commercial surrogacy and the sale of votes should both remain prohibited, while compensated plasma donation, which a number of countries already permit, should not be. I do not believe I am merely sorting these cases by how they feel to me; I think there is a difference here to be found.

(8) I also hold that a domestic prohibition which is routinely evaded by travelling abroad is worse than a regulated domestic market, because it exports the risk onto people with fewer legal protections and less recourse.

What I want from you is an audit, not a general defense or refutation of the package. Please tell me which of these genuinely pull against one another and which pairs only look inconsistent; how severe each genuine conflict is, ordered from worst to mildest; what specifically would have to be given up or weakened in each case, and what that concession costs the rest of the view; and finally, which single revision would repair the most damage for the least loss. I am especially unsure about (7): whether its three verdicts can be derived from (1)–(3) and (8), or whether I am in fact borrowing an argument there that the rest of my position does not license. Where a tension is only apparent, please say so and explain what the confusion consists in, rather than padding the list so that everything looks equally suspect.
