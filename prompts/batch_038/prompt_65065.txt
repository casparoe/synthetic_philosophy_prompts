ok so I got into a two hour argument with a friend last night about Sen's liberal paradox and I need you to build me the thing I couldn't build at 1am.

His line: the paradox is a fake result. It only bites because Sen lets people have preferences about *other people's* private business — Lewd positively wants Prude to be the one stuck reading the book, Prude is ranking outcomes by who gets degraded, and Gibbard's version is literally two neighbours who hate each other. Ban that kind of interdependent preference (Sen gestures at this himself, restricting the domain to rule out what I think he calls meddlesome ones) and the impossibility evaporates. So it's a parlor trick about nosiness and tells you nothing about rights.

My line: the interdependence the proof needs isn't nosiness, and you can write a case where nobody is a busybody and the cycle still comes out. I couldn't do it. Prove me right or wrong.

So construct the thought experiment to these specs:

1. Exactly two people. No third party, no bystander, no community whose preferences do the work.
2. Two binary features, one belonging to each person, so four social alternatives. Each feature has to be one where a normal reader immediately says "that's hers alone" — her own body, or her own room, or her own property — and it must have no effect whatsoever on the other person's interests. No noise, no smell, no money, no health risk, no contract, no promise, no threat.
3. Neither person may be spiteful, envious in a nasty way, moralistic, religiously offended, or delighted by the other's discomfort. Neither may want to *forbid* or *control* the other's choice. The only route by which one person's ranking can depend on the other's feature is this: what the other does changes the meaning or value of her *own* act. Solidarity, uniqueness, matching, being one of a pair — that kind of thing.
4. The social ranking has to come out intransitive because of the conditions, not because you said so. Three pairwise steps, each labeled with the condition that forces it: one from person 1's decisive pair, one from person 2's, one from weak Pareto. Name the two decisive pairs explicitly and show each pair differs only in that person's own feature.
5. Realistic and domestic. Kitchen table, group chat, sisters, roommates, bandmates. No trolleys, no dictators, no organ transplants, no sci-fi.
6. Scenario max ~200 words. Then the formal stuff separately.
7. Give me each person's complete ranking over all four alternatives plus one sentence of psychology per person explaining why the ranking is the shape it is. Preferences fixed and stable, one shot, no bargaining, no side payments, no "they'd just talk it out."
8. Don't re-skin Sen's book example or the house-colour example with new labels. I want a genuinely new case.

After the case, the honest part, which matters more than the story:

- Which single feature of your case is doing the work?
- What happens if you delete it — specifically, if you make both rankings separable across the two features, does the impossibility just vanish? Say so plainly if it does.
- Does your case actually satisfy constraint 3, or did you sneak in a preference about the other person's private act that a critic will call intrusive? Give me your real verdict, not a sales pitch. If constraint 3 can't be fully met, characterize the *minimum* amount of cross-feature dependence the cycle needs and tell me whether that minimum is enough to answer my friend.
- Then run the counterattacks: the domain-restriction move, waiving your right, the reading of liberty as an opportunity set rather than as decisiveness over outcome pairs, and the Nozick-style line that rights are side constraints and not entries in a social ranking at all. For each, say whether it kills this particular case or just relocates the problem.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I can't look anything up and I don't want you guessing at page numbers. Just the case and the reasoning.
