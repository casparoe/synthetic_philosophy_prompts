I sit on the review panel of a private foundation that funds work in the foundations of physics, and this cycle we must decide a single proposal on which the panel is badly split. I have been asked to circulate a written recommendation in advance of next week's meeting.

The proposal comes from a theorist and an experimentalist. It develops a deterministic account of the EPR–Bell arrangement on which the hidden state prepared at the source is not statistically independent of the settings later chosen at the two wings: since the source event, the two setting events, and everything else are all aspects of one prior state of the universe rather than separately specifiable systems, the dependence among them is ordinary common-cause dependence inside the shared past light cone, and treating it as a forbidden correlation is a prejudice about how to carve the world into subsystems rather than a physical result. The applicants claim their model reproduces the standard quantum statistics for the singlet exactly, and that its advantage is a strict spacetime locality — no influence of any kind propagates outside the light cone. The budget is modest: numerical simulation plus one table-top optical run that varies the physical mechanism generating the settings (quantum-random numbers in one arm, settings fixed by a long pre-recorded chain of human choices in the other) and looks for a difference in the measured correlation.

Separately, two panelists want to add a standing condition to our practice: any proposal that claims to escape a no-go theorem must state which assumption of the theorem it rejects, in terms the panel can check, and must identify at least one prediction distinguishing it from quantum mechanics. A third panelist objects that this is gatekeeping dressed as methodology, and points out — correctly, so far as I can tell — that the demand for a distinguishing prediction would have failed Bohm's theory, which is empirically equivalent to ordinary quantum mechanics and was nonetheless a real advance; and that the most famous impossibility proof in this area was eventually evaded precisely because Bell located an additivity assumption that cannot legitimately be imposed on non-commuting observables.

Please draft the recommendation memo I will circulate. It should:

1. Take a definite position on this proposal — fund as requested, fund subject to stated conditions, or decline — with reasoning the applicants could actually reply to rather than a summary of our deliberations.
2. Assess what the central claim buys and what it costs. In particular: does denying statistical independence at the source genuinely preserve locality in the sense the Bell argument requires, or does it relocate the nonlocality into the initial conditions; and how should the panel weigh the fact that the applicants distinguish no-signalling from the stronger condition Bell's derivation actually needs, if they do?
3. Say something definite about the applicants' proposed optical run, including whether a dependence of the correlation on the settings-generation mechanism is the kind of evidence that could bear on their interpretation, and what would make the run worth funding even if the model is false.
4. Recommend whether we should adopt the proposed standing condition, and if so in what revised wording — a formulation a panelist could apply to next cycle's proposal without re-deriving it from first principles. Here you must treat the objecting panelist's case at its strongest, and explain why the Bohm and von Neumann precedents do or do not generalize into a rule.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1,200 words. Write it as a document the panel can act on, not as a survey: no citations, no bibliography, and no background on entanglement that the panel already knows.
