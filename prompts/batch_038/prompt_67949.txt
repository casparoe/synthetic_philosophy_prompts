Fifteen years of multi-round licensing negotiations for a living, plus way too many long-running board-game nights, and there's a pattern I keep running into that I dont have a word for.

Setup, stripped down. Two of us alternate moves over, say, twelve rounds. If we both keep playing nice, the pot grows and we split something much bigger than either of us started with. But somewhere near the end there's always a round where one of us can grab most of the pot and end it right there. I did an executive game-theory course years ago, so I know the standard move: you start from that last round, note the grab is obviously right there, step back one, note it's now obviously right at the second-to-last, and so on until you've concluded nobody should ever have been nice in round one at all. Everyone I've ever negotiated with has heard some version of that argument, everyone ignores it, and mostly the ignoring works out.

What I actually notice is *who* manages to stay nice for all twelve rounds. It isn't the sharpest people at the table. It's the ones everybody believes couldn't pull off the endgame grab — the guy who demonstrably can't hold the whole sequence in his head, who'd be too slow or too proud or too sentimental to take the money in round eleven. Counterparties don't squeeze him, because squeezing him just makes him walk, and he's never going to work out that he should have walked three rounds earlier. The ones who are visibly very good at the arithmetic get squeezed constantly and end up worse off. So an apparent defect in reasoning turns into an asset, both sides know it, and nobody has signed anything.

I know "commitment" is in the neighborhood, and "reputation." But a commitment is something you do and a reputation is something you build, and this is something you *are* — and its the faking part that scrambles it for me, because the moment you fake it you've demonstrated you could have taken the money, which is exactly the thing that destroys it.

Then there's a second flavor and I can't tell whether it's the same phenomenon. Some players don't look like they're restraining themselves at all. They never represent the last round as a decision — they play each round as though it were the only round. No threat in it, nothing being held back, no signal sent. And yet cooperation with them holds up *better* than with the merely stubborn ones.

So: does any of this have an established name, and is it one thing or two? If there's a term, how does it differ from commitment and from reputation — what does it cover that they don't? And if the two flavors really are different, which one does the term actually pick out, and what would you call the other? Also, am I right that the payoff to being bad at this scales with how long the game is, or am I just pattern-matching? And where's the edge — at what point does a genuine incapacity stop being an incapacity and start being a strategy, and could you even tell from the outside?

The label isn't the point, the carving is: what this is, what it isn't, where it runs out. But please finish with a last line of the exact form \answer{X}, where X is the single term you'd tell me to use — an existing one if there's a good existing one, your own coinage if there isn't, or "none" if you think this isn't really a unified thing at all.
