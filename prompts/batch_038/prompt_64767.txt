I am a professor of philosophy; my field is aesthetics, and for the better part of three decades I have written about why people choose to sit through representations of sorrow when they could perfectly well avoid them. Six weeks ago my wife died, aged fifty-eight. She was a mezzo-soprano who made her name in lament repertoire — she first came to notice in Dido's Lament, and she sang Winterreise complete every February as a private ritual that no one was invited to — and she was the least consolable person I have ever known. She detested the phrase at peace, walked out of a memorial service for a colleague because somebody read a poem about a butterfly, and told me in terms what she wanted from her own: no service, no uplift, and an evening of her own choosing in the college chapel, all of it grief, none of it relieved. Her colleagues from the company have honoured that.

I have been asked to give the spoken remarks at the halfway point of the concert, on Saturday evening. Please write them.

The room will divide roughly in two. On one side, musicians from her company and her conservatoire, several of whom read philosophy, and one of whom — a conductor who reviewed my book and thinks my account intellectualises the whole business, that I explain what a lament does as a species of understanding while leaving out what it does to the body — will be sitting in the front row. On the other side, her family: our two children, her brother, and her mother, who is eighty-four, who has told each of us separately that she does not see how she will sit through an evening of this, and who asked me, without hostility but quite plainly, whether we could not have chosen something that celebrated her instead. My mother-in-law's question is the real brief. The remarks must answer it — not by overruling her, and not by conceding that the programme is self-punishment dressed up as tribute.

The difficulty is that the honest answer to her is the thing I have spent my career on, and delivering it as a lecture would be grotesque. The philosophical content has to do the structural work of the speech without ever announcing itself as philosophy: I do not want the phrase the paradox of tragedy uttered in a chapel, and I would prefer that it go unuttered altogether. Nor may the remarks claim that the music compensates for anything, that art makes the loss worth having, that we take pleasure in a character's suffering, or that grief is good for us and will leave us cleansed — all of which she would have called a lie, and two of which I have argued against in print. What the speech may commit to is my own view, that what the room will get from Dido is not enjoyment of anyone's pain but the exercise of a capacity we have reason to value: the capacity to be moved, and moved correctly, by what actually matters. It must also be honest, at least obliquely, about a wrinkle my own field tends to slide past, namely that the puzzle I work on concerns invented sorrows and Saturday's is not invented. If the remarks paper over that, my mother-in-law will hear it and so will the conductor.

Practical requirements. Between six and seven minutes, which for me is about seven hundred words, and speakable by a man who will be exhausted and may need to stop mid-sentence. It should contain one memory of her that is funny, particular and true to how she was, and that memory should carry argumentative weight rather than provide relief from the argument. It should address my mother-in-law directly at some point without putting her on the spot or turning her into a specimen of unphilosophical feeling. It must end by handing the room over to the second half, which is the Winterreise. And please do not put words in my wife's mouth that she did not say, and do not quote poetry at her: she hated being quoted at, and I would rather the speech contained no quotation from any real text than one that I cannot vouch for.

I am not asking for alternatives. Give me one speech, committed: one definite account of why a room full of people who loved her should choose to spend an evening inside her grief rather than step around it, argued for through the shape of the thing rather than asserted, and not a menu of framings for me to select from. If you judge my constraints to be jointly unsatisfiable — if you think, for instance, that no answer to my mother-in-law can avoid both consolation and despair — say so plainly, and then write the speech anyway, on your own best judgement as to which constraint must bend.

After the speech, add a brief note in your own voice rather than mine: which account of the paradox of tragedy the speech has committed itself to, what it has taken for granted that is contested, and where it has bent something for the sake of a room that is not a seminar.
