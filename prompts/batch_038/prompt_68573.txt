A friend and I lost most of a weekend to an argument about the ethics of a referee report, and I would like it reconstructed properly, as a dialogue between two co-editors. The setup: an early-career researcher has sent them a paper both of them think is bad — not borderline, but built on a central argument they agree is unsound. One editor drafts the blunt report: the argument fails, here is where it fails, and no, the project does not look salvageable. The other insists on the hedged report: warm about the ambition, dense with wondering-whether constructions, closing with a suggested alternative direction that neither editor believes in. Each thinks the other's draft would wrong the author.

Their positions, briefly. The hedger holds that his draft is no lie at all — it asserts nothing false, and in a community able to read such a report it conveys the verdict by a route both parties understand; the blunt draft, meanwhile, buys one person's honesty with another person's confidence. The defender of bluntness holds that the hedged draft is the worse of the two precisely because of its deniability: it leaves the author to assemble the bad news herself out of implicatures she may not be competent to decode, and lets the writer later protest that he never said any such thing. Her principle is that an author has a claim not merely to true sentences but to information she can act on.

Have them work through the three points where my friend and I stalled. First: does a community's shared code for reading politeness as refusal make the hedged report non-deceptive, or is that transparency self-undermining, since the code only functions while nobody says out loud what it means? Second: is misleading without asserting anything false a lesser wrong than lying or a greater one — the direction of that comparison kept flipping on us, and I want each editor to take a side and hold it. Third: does withholding the verdict protect the author's autonomy or override it, by substituting the editor's estimate of what she can bear for her own? One of them should also press the cross-cultural point — a colleague of mine working in Kyoto tells me the same hedged sentences would be read there as a clear and considerate refusal and the blunt ones as aggression, so if the moral valence tracks the code, the code rather than the literal content must carry the weight — and the other should explain why that cuts against the hedge rather than for it.

Both editors are equally able and equally well-intentioned; no strawmen, no talking past one another, and every turn should answer something the other actually said. Keep them on this case: no murderer at the door, no stock examples. Do not let them converge or settle on a compromise. Then add a short note marking which premises the two treat as common ground really are shared among people who work on this and which are live disputes — including, if it is one, the comparison between lying and misleading — so that neither character wins a point by asserting a contested view as settled.
