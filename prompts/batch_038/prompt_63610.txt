ok, kind of a weird request but here goes.

three months ago I moved into a six-person housing co-op and somehow ended up as the person writting our charter — the doc that says who has final say over what: quiet hours, the porch light, whether somebody's partner can move in, the compost thing. while drafting it I kept hitting a wall, and then found out the wall has a name: Sen's liberal paradox. I want each of us to have at least one little domain where our own preference just settles the outcome, and I also want the house to respect unanimous preferences, and it turns out those two can be made to contradict each other. I can literally construct the case out of our actual setup: Marta has final say over whether the porch light stays on overnight, Dev has final say over whether his brother becomes a long-term guest, and once everybody's preferences over the four combinations get tallied, the whole thing jams.

the two replies I keep running into are (a) the paradox only bites when preferences are meddlesome, i.e. when people care about what others do inside their own protected sphere, and (b) rights should be treated as waivable or tradeable, so the holders just bargain them away. I want you to argue against both and argue for something stronger instead:

the real source of the trouble is that a social state in Sen's framework is a complete description of the whole situation, so a "protected sphere" can never be cleanly carved out of it. In a shared house every domain bleeds into every other one through the rent split, sleep, the utilities meter, wear on the kitchen. So localizing the rights either fails outright, or — if you force it through by only letting each right-holder's preference count over pairs that differ in their own feature — it quietly gives up Pareto instead of rescuing it.

what I actually want:

- a real argument built up in steps, not a survey of who said what.
- an explicit preference profile over the four co-op outcomes (light on/off × brother stays/goes) where the interdependence is purely about consequences for the person — their share of the bill, whether they sleep, whether the porch gets used for smoking at 2am — and not about prurient interest in what the other person does. Show the exact point where the localized rights and a unanimous strict preference collide, and name which condition has to go to get consistency back.
- the strongest objection to this thesis, stated as charitably as you can, then why it doesn't land. Especially deal with the claim that once you restrict attention to genuinely self-regarding preferences the paradox just evaporates, and explain why that restriction isnt available to someone who has to write rules for a house where six people share one staircase and one meter.
- and honestly: is my charter doomed, or is there a design that dodges this — for instance rights that get exercised through a procedure other people can act inside of (bidding, compensation, a fixed sequence of moves) rather than as a flat personal veto over an outcome? If you think that works, argue why it doesnt just smuggle the same problem back in one level up.

no references or reading list please, I just want the argument. Assume I know what "decisive over a pair" means and what the Pareto condition says; define anything else you lean on.
